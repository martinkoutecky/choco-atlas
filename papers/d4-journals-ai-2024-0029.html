The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the distributional version of the paper’s unrestricted social-cost result. My anchor is Theorem 3.7, proved here: *Median-of-TruncatedAvg has distortion at most \(2\)* for social cost. The paper says this meets an unrestricted-mechanism lower bound of \(2\) from Filos-Ratsikas and Voudouris (2021), but that lower bound is cited prior work, not Theorem 3.7 itself.

A concrete mirror is Continuous Median-of-TruncatedAvg. An instance has \(k\) equal-mass districts. District \(d\) is given by a finite distribution \(\mu_d=\sum_j p_{dj}\delta_{x_{dj}}\) over rational positions, with positive rational masses summing to \(1\). The population types are \((d,x_{dj})\), with mass \(p_{dj}/k\) in society. Let \(Q_d\) be the district’s quantile function. Its representative is the mean of the middle half of its population,
\[ y_d=2\int_{1/4}^{3/4}Q_d(u)\,du, \]
and the facility must be placed at a median \(w\) of the \(y_d\)’s. The underlying task is: given these distributions, compute \(w\), the social cost \(\Phi(w)=\sum_d\int |x-w|\,d\mu_d(x)\), and an optimal benchmark \(o\in\arg\min_z\sum_d\int |x-z|\,d\mu_d(x)\); determine the resulting distortion. A solution is the exact output and costs. Weighted order statistics, quantile integration, and a median are computable in polynomial time in the support size and input bit length.

The regime could be a large set of municipalities, each with many residents but relatively few repeated ideal locations—for example, residents clustered around a small set of established neighborhoods or transit hubs. A type records district and ideal location; the masses express population shares. This keeps the paper’s district structure, local representative rule, permitted outputs, and social-cost objective. The quantile definition is the natural co-modification needed when a fractional share, rather than an integral number of agents, lies in the trimmed range. For rational masses, scaling to a common denominator recovers an equal-sized finite electorate; when each district size is divisible by four, the quantile representative agrees with the paper’s rule. So Theorem 3.7 supports expecting the factor-\(2\) guarantee to survive this mirror. The continuous computation is tractable—Class A-like—though that is not a complexity classification established by the paper.

The authors would likely recognize this as a continuous-population version of their unrestricted social-cost problem. It changes the population representation, not merely the outcome space: the facility’s location was already on \(\mathbb R\). The mirror covers only Theorem 3.7; it does not attempt to continuize the strategyproof results, where unilateral manipulation by a zero-mass agent would be vacuous.

The weakness is decisive for the ChoCo programme’s intended test: the paper contains no named result asserting a computational-complexity classification such as NP-hardness, membership in P, or fixed-parameter tractability. Its named theorems concern distortion and strategyproofness. Theorem 3.7 is the closest useful anchor, but it is not a qualifying complexity anchor. This mirror gives a plausible distributional extension and an efficiently computable rule; it does not pose the discrete-hardness question whose dissolution the programme is meant to investigate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.7 is the closest anchor, but it is a distortion theorem, not a computational-complexity result. It bounds the worst-case ratio of a specified mechanism; the paper does not classify the complexity of computing an outcome or solving an optimization problem. In the proposed mirror, computing the representatives and winner is weighted order-statistic computation, and computing the social-cost benchmark is finding a weighted median. With finite rational support, these are straightforward sorting and arithmetic. Proving the factor-\(2\) bound for all such distributions would be a new distortion theorem, not a complexity result.

The proponent’s model is nevertheless a sound population continuization. A type can be a district and an ideal location, and residents clustered at shared locations are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Replacing the middle-half average by a quantile integral is a natural way to handle fractional mass. So the negative case should not rest on the facility already ranging over \(\mathbb R\), on the model’s lack of multiplicity, or on whether the factor survives. The problem is that this strongest mirror still asks how a known, simple rule behaves over distributions; it does not expose a computational bottleneck in the paper. To get one, we would need to add a different source of difficulty, which would make the result an adjacent problem rather than a mirror of Theorem 3.7.

The paper’s strategyproof results offer no stronger computational anchor. They, too, concern distortion. Moreover, unilateral deviations by zero-mass agents are vacuous in a nonatomic model; allowing a positive mass to deviate changes the question to group manipulation.

I can’t honestly make the universal claim airtight: the distributional distortion question is sensible, and the authors might recognize it as theirs. The negative case is strongest—and fairly decisive for ChoCo’s stated purpose—on scope: this paper supplies no named computational result for continuization to extend. That supports “no worthwhile complexity mirror here,” not “no worthwhile continuous-population question in any scenario.”
