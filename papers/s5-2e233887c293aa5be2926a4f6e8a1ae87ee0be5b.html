The continuous mirror question

Given a finite outcome set \(O\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the honest team’s ranking types with total mass \(1-\rho\), a coalition mass \(\rho>0\), the other party’s ranking \(p_o\), and a target outcome \(p\), does there exist a rational distribution \(x\) over rankings with total mass \(\rho\) such that \(p\) is the VAOV SPE result for both possible initiators? The team order is obtained by Borda-scoring the honest mass and coalition reports together, with the paper’s lexicographic tie-break.

The model it lives in

Ranking types are complete strict rankings of \(O\). The honest team contributes Borda score \(\sum_r \mu_r b_r(c)\) to outcome \(c\), and coalition report mass contributes \(\sum_r x_r b_r(c)\), where \(x_r\ge 0\) and \(\sum_r x_r=\rho\). The resulting score order and the fixed \(p_o\) determine the VAOV outcome.

The objection that survived

The reductions place their combinatorics in the alternatives, so the continuous problem may remain hard and yield no population-driven tractability gain.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Borda coalitional constructive and destructive hardness results, Theorems 3 and 7; it leaves the single-manipulator, \(x\)-approval, and approximation results without continuous counterparts.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the constructive Borda mass-coalition problem polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard?
  • Does the destructive Borda mass-coalition problem have the same complexity as the constructive version?
  • How do continuous solutions relate to discrete manipulation when report masses are restricted to a discrete grid?

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I believe in is continuous coalition manipulation of the negotiating team’s Borda order. The team still chooses a complete ranking of the negotiation outcomes by Borda, the other party still has its fixed ranking \(p_o\), and the bargaining protocol is still VAOV. The change is that the team’s population and its coalition are represented by masses of interchangeable members.

Take a finite outcome set \(O\), with \(|O|=m\), and let \(\mathcal R(O)\) be its strict rankings. An honest team is given by a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal R(O)\), with total mass \(1-\rho\), represented by its \(\tau\) positive-mass rankings. A coordinated coalition has mass \(\rho\), where \(0<\rho<1\). Its members share the paper’s manipulation objective and may report any ranking. A report plan is a rational distribution \(x\) over \(\mathcal R(O)\), with \(\sum_r x_r=\rho\). Thus \(x_r\) is the mass reporting ranking \(r\); every unit of mass casts a complete, integral ballot.

For each ranking \(r\), let \(b_r(c)\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\) be its Borda score for outcome \(c\). The team’s aggregate score is
\[ S_x(c)=\sum_r \mu_r b_r(c)+\sum_r x_r b_r(c). \]
Sort by \(S_x\), using the paper’s fixed lexicographic tie-break, to obtain the team order \(p_t(x)\). This is a population mirror of the paper’s question: an instance specifies \(O,\mu,\rho,p_o\), and a target; a solution is a report distribution \(x\) satisfying the relevant VAOV outcome condition for both possible initiators. For a discrete team with \(n\) honest members and \(k\) manipulators, set \(\mu_r=n_r/(n+k)\), \(\rho=k/(n+k)\), and \(x_r=k_r/(n+k)\). The resulting Borda order is exactly the discrete one. The mirror relaxes the grid of possible masses; it does not change the scoring rule or negotiation question.

A plausible regime is a large member-based association negotiating with a regulator or supplier over a fixed menu of packages. Its many members might fall into relatively few ranking types over that menu, while a substantial caucus shares an objective and coordinates its reported ballots. For example, a federation might have hundreds of thousands of members but only a few dozen observed ranking types over a small policy agenda. The paper’s setting is already about a negotiating team; this scenario makes the team’s population high-multiplicity. The opposing party remains one negotiating party, as in the paper.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: *CC-MaNego is NP-Complete with Borda.* In full, given the honest voters’ preference profile, the other party’s order, a number of manipulators, and a preferred outcome \(p\), it is NP-complete to decide whether the manipulators can report preferences making \(p\) the VAOV SPE result whether the team or the other party initiates. The paper proves this by reduction from Permutation Sum; it relies on an NP-completeness result for that source problem cited elsewhere.

The corresponding continuous question is CC-MaNego\(_\infty\)-Borda: given \(O,\mu,\rho,p_o,p\) as above, does there exist a rational report-mass distribution \(x\) of total mass \(\rho\) such that
\[ N_t(p_t(x),p_o)=N_o(p_t(x),p_o)=p? \]
A successful \(x\) is the solution. I expect this mirror to be tractable, although that is a research hypothesis, not a result in the paper. The aggregate Borda vectors attainable by splitting mass across rankings form a scaled permutahedron: a convex set rather than a choice of a fixed list of whole-voter permutations. Linear optimization over that set is accessible to standard optimization methods. The hard step is whether the exact VAOV target condition, which depends on the induced ranking and its top-set intersections, can be handled efficiently alongside that convex geometry.

The second anchor is Theorem 7, also proved here: *DC-MaNego with Borda is NP-Complete.* In full, given the honest profile, the other party’s order, a number of manipulators, and a disliked outcome \(e\), it is NP-complete to decide whether they can report preferences making \(e\) fail to be the VAOV SPE result for either initiator. The proof again reduces from Permutation Sum.

Its mirror, DC-MaNego\(_\infty\)-Borda, has the same instance format, with disliked outcome \(e\), and asks whether there is a mass distribution \(x\) of total \(\rho\) such that
\[ e\ne N_t(p_t(x),p_o) \quad\text{and}\quad e\ne N_o(p_t(x),p_o). \]
I expect this version to be tractable for the same reason as the constructive one: fractional report mass may remove the integral alignment constraints used in the reduction. The two questions should be studied separately, since the accepted regions in the VAOV outcome rule need not behave identically.

These mirrors cover only the paper’s Borda coalitional constructive and destructive hardness results, Theorems 3 and 7. They do not claim a continuous counterpart to the single-manipulator result, the \(x\)-approval algorithm, or the one-additional-manipulator guarantees.

The weakest point is also central to the classification. The reductions encode combinatorics over alternatives using whole rankings of the candidates, and hardness driven by the agenda often survives continuization. Theorem 3’s reduction even uses only two manipulators, so it does not itself show hardness in a large-coalition regime. Fractional report mass could dissolve its permutation constraints, but neither the paper nor this argument proves that it does. The concrete next questions are whether the VAOV target condition admits an efficient description over the Borda permutahedron, whether either continuous problem has a new continuum-specific hardness reduction, and how continuous solutions relate quantitatively to discrete ones when the coalition mass is restricted to the discrete grid.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about how much ChoCo would gain from this paper, rather than whether its mirrors are legitimate. Theorem 3 and Theorem 7 use the same Permutation Sum reduction, with two manipulators, and both place the hard choices in how Borda points are distributed across alternatives. Under the proposed continuous model, the coalition’s reports affect the result only through an aggregate score vector in a scaled permutahedron. The negotiation then depends on which ranking chamber contains that vector. So the two anchors lead to closely related geometric feasibility questions, rather than opening two distinct population-complexity directions.

That makes these weaker evidence for a *population-driven* tractability gain than a paper where the number of agents is itself the source of the difficulty. It also rules out a direct atomless mirror of the single-manipulator result: an individual of zero mass cannot change the team’s order. But neither point defeats the Borda anchors. The proposed coalition has positive mass, and both the honest team and coalition can be represented by rational masses over complete types. The fact that the reduction uses two manipulators does not prevent asking the corresponding mass-coalition question.

The negotiating-team examples in the paper may not themselves be large populations. Still, a large membership organization choosing a negotiating position over a fixed menu is a plausible regime with repeated ranking types. In that setting, the target, Borda aggregation, and VAOV outcome condition all remain recognizable. The type-level model also loses no individual identity that these two problems use.

So I cannot honestly defeat Theorem 3 or Theorem 7 as anchors. The best negative conclusion is that they may yield a narrow, overlapping mirror whose value to the programme is uncertain. The stronger claim—that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario—does not hold up against the mass-coalition formulation.
