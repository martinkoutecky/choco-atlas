The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is the paper’s subject: it gives stationary equilibrium payoff calculations for three finite weighted voting games. Its 1981 result—that six of nine incumbents gain—is a comparative-static finding, not a computational result. The paper defines no input family, algorithmic task, or complexity bound. There is therefore no named computational result here for ChoCo to continuize.

The best rescue is a large legislature with a few complete delegate types, asking whether adding mass of a new type raises the equilibrium payoff of a majority of incumbent mass. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and it gives the paper’s paradox a natural population-level form. But its central question is how equilibrium payoffs change with the type distribution. To make it a ChoCo problem, one would have to add a new task—such as finding the complexity of computing those payoffs across arbitrary inputs—and specify a continuum bargaining game. That would be a new computational game-theory project, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The bargaining model also makes the continuum formulation consequential. In the paper, every individual can be selected as proposer and retain a proposer’s surplus. A type-level proposer would give a whole bloc that role; an atomless lottery over individuals does not select a positive-mass proposer. A finite-replica limit could provide a principled model, but studying that limit would itself be a new equilibrium-analysis result.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s stated scope, though it cannot establish that no useful research extension exists. A large typed legislature is conceivable, and computing its equilibrium could be worthwhile in a broader programme. The case against is that this paper supplies no computational result for such a mirror to extend.
