The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I believe is a mass version of the paper’s lower bound on strategyproof mechanisms. It is a close mirror of the strategic facility location problem, though a weaker fit for ChoCo’s focus on computational complexity: the anchor below bounds the approximation ratio of mechanisms; it does not prove a runtime lower bound such as NP-hardness.

My anchor is Theorem 2 (Computer-assisted lower bound), proved in this paper: for \(n=4\), every randomized strategyproof mechanism on the circle has approximation ratio at least
\[ \rho_0=\frac{108818734234255}{99999999999758} =1.088187342345\ldots. \]
The paper’s proof also extends this bound to \(n=4k\) by replacing each of four reports with \(k\) identical reports. A deviation by one base agent corresponds to moving those \(k\) copies one at a time; the individual strategyproofness inequalities add, while social cost and optimum both scale by \(k\). That extension is not a separate numbered theorem, but it is the part that gives this anchor its high-multiplicity relevance.

Here is a precise mirror. Fix the paper’s grid \(V=\{j/120:j=0,\ldots,119\}\) of possible preferred locations on the unit circle \(G\). An instance has a population scale \(N\) and a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(V)\). The mechanism \(\mathcal M(N,\mu)\) returns a lottery over facility locations in \(G\). To retain the paper’s individual incentive constraint while making the population a mass distribution, a single resident’s report changes the distribution by \(1/N\): for \(\mu_t\ge 1/N\), strategyproofness requires, for every \(s\in V\),
\[ \mathbb E_{Y\sim\mathcal M(N,\mu)}[d(t,Y)] \le \mathbb E_{Y'\sim\mathcal M(N,\,\mu+\frac1N(\delta_s-\delta_t))}[d(t,Y')]. \]
The objective is the per-capita utilitarian cost
\[ \mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(\mathcal M) = \mathbb E_{Y\sim\mathcal M(N,\mu)} \left[\sum_{t\in V}\mu_t d(t,Y)\right], \qquad \mathrm{OPT}(\mu)=\min_{y\in G}\sum_{t\in V}\mu_t d(t,y). \]

Call this the mass-strategyproof circle facility approximation problem: determine the least worst-case ratio achievable by a family of such mechanisms over \(N\) and \(\mu\), with \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)>0\). A solution is either a strategyproof mechanism family with a proved ratio, or a lower-bound certificate ruling out ratios below a specified value. Theorem 2’s proof uses a finite grid of profiles and deviations, so its certificate applies to this finite-type mirror. The paper’s cloning argument then gives the same obstruction at arbitrarily large \(N=4k\), with at most four positive-mass location types.

A plausible regime is residents choosing a shared public-facility location along a circular route, or a recurring time slot on a cycle. Residents with the same ideal point have identical preferences under the paper’s distance model. The lower-bound family consists of arbitrarily large populations with only a few such preference groups: \(N\) grows while the support stays at most four. This is not an invented high-multiplicity story; the paper’s own cloning argument supplies it.

I expect the lower bound to transfer: the mass domain includes the paper’s repeated-profile instances, and the \(1/N\) mass shift preserves the one-resident deviation on those instances. That is a Class B-style obstruction in the programme’s sense. The mirror covers Theorem 2 and its cloning extension only; it does not claim that the paper’s \(3/2\)-approximation mechanism has a well-defined extension to arbitrary fractional masses. Further questions include whether the lower bound is tight for the mass problem, whether larger supports strengthen it, and whether a useful limit of the \(1/N\) incentive constraints exists as \(N\) grows.

The soft spot is that this is not a purely atomless model: it retains a one-resident deviation of size \(1/N\). Without that granularity, a single agent changes no mass, making strategyproofness vacuous. Keeping the scale and deviation size is a defensible co-modification to preserve the authors’ problem, but it limits the claim to a continuous mass formulation of their mechanism-approximation question. It does not turn their result into a computational-complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is about what ChoCo is trying to classify. This paper gives no named result about the computational complexity of a social-choice problem. Theorem 1 bounds the distortion of a particular mechanism; Theorem 2 lower-bounds the approximation ratio achievable by strategyproof mechanisms. The finite LP in Theorem 2 supplies a certificate for that bound—it is not an algorithm for computing an outcome or solving a population-level intervention problem. The same objection applies to the more tempting mirror of the \(3/2\) guarantee: it remains a mechanism-quality question, not a complexity result.

The proponent’s high-multiplicity example is real. Repeating each of four reported locations \(k\) times gives a population with \(N=4k\) and at most four location types. But the proposed incentive constraint still moves one resident, hence shifts mass by \(1/N\). For each \(N\), this is the discrete profile problem written as a histogram. Taking an atomless limit removes that individual’s effect on the distribution, making the usual strategyproofness comparison vacuous. A nontrivial continuum version would need to retain a mass scale or introduce group or infinitesimal-deviation constraints. Those can be sensible questions, but they are extra modeling choices, and the paper’s certificate does not turn them into a computational-complexity result.

So the cloning argument supports the plausibility of a high-multiplicity facility-location regime; it does not make this a strong ChoCo mirror. I cannot honestly argue that no continuous-population model of the paper’s mechanism-design problem is worthwhile. The negative case is narrower: the one anchor raised—and the paper’s other main guarantee—falls outside the programme’s computational target.
