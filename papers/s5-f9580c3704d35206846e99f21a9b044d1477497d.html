The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is limited: this paper contains no named computational result. Its numbered results are axiomatic characterizations, not claims of membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or similar. Thus it has no fully eligible ChoCo anchor. The strongest structural candidate is Corollary 8, proved in this paper:

A prize allocation rule satisfies anonymity, order preservation, endowment additivity (equivalently, scale invariance), and top consistency if and only if it is a proportional rule. There are nonnegative parameters \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\ldots\), with \(\lambda_1>0\) and \(\lambda_{k+1}\le \lambda_k\), such that a competitor at position \(r\) receives
\[ > \frac{\lambda_r}{\sum_{k=1}^{|N|}\lambda_k}E. > \]

This is not a computational anchor, but it gives the most credible basis for a continuous-population extension.

Consider a large online sales or innovation competition with a small number \(\tau\) of rank-relevant performance types. Type \(t\) comprises a large cohort of interchangeable contestants: they have the same observable score class, eligibility, and prize-relevant characteristics. Let \(\mu_t\) be the cohort's population share, ordered from highest to lowest performance, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The continuum society is \(\mu\), not the prize fund: \(E\) remains a scalar resource, while each type receives a per-capita prize \(p_t\). The cohort's total prize is \(\mu_t p_t\).

A precise problem would be:

Continuous Proportional Prize Design\(_\infty\). Given rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\), a prize fund \(E\), and piecewise-linear concave utilities \(u_t(p)\) for the members of each type, choose a nonincreasing prize schedule \(p_1\ge\cdots\ge p_\tau\ge0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t p_t=E \]
to maximize
\[ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t u_t(p_t). \]
Equivalently, one may parameterize the schedule by nonincreasing \(\lambda_t\) and set
\[ p_t=\frac{\lambda_t}{\sum_j\mu_j\lambda_j}E. \]
A solution is the optimal type-level prize schedule, together with its value.

This is a genuine population continuization: mass replaces the number of contestants, and the action is allocation of prize mass across rank-relevant types. The original authors should recognize it as the computational version of their concluding suggestion that one should choose, among the characterized rule families, the rule maximizing a goal such as effort or participation. Under the stated concavity and piecewise-linearity assumptions, the direct formulation is a linear program, so I would expect a Class A result. It also generates natural questions about convergence from finite competitions, the treatment of Winner-Takes-All atoms at the top rank, and optimization with nonconcave participation or effort objectives.

The regime is plausible but requires a convention absent from the paper: identical contestants may occupy a tied performance block, with the block receiving its average positional prize. This is defensible in a high-multiplicity setting—individual tie-breaking is not a meaningful type distinction—but it is a co-modification of the original strict ranking model.

My weakest point is decisive: the paper never studies this optimization problem, never gives a computational anchor, and its exact finite model assigns potentially different prizes to every position, which can make rank itself a nearly unique type. I would therefore present this as a credible new continuous research question suggested by Corollary 8, not as evidence that the paper already contains a computational continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper supplies no eligible ChoCo anchor. Theorem 1, Theorems 2–3, and Corollaries 1–8 are axiomatic classifications of prize rules. None states a computational problem, an input model, a complexity bound, or an algorithmic task. Corollary 8 therefore cannot itself support a computational mirror; it only describes which rules satisfy certain axioms.

The proposed Continuous Proportional Prize Design is a new optimization problem, not a continuization of Corollary 8. The paper does not give contestants types, utilities, participation costs, or a choice problem over prize schedules. It takes the ranking and endowment as primitives and characterizes admissible rules. Adding \(u_t\), choosing the schedule, and maximizing welfare changes the problem’s objective and its substantive primitives. The resulting LP may be a sensible project, but its computational content comes from those additions, not from the paper’s theorem.

The population interpretation is also less faithful than it first appears. Corollary 8 uses a sequence \((\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\ldots)\) indexed by ordinal positions. A positive-mass performance type occupies a block of positions, whose members generally receive different prizes. Giving that type one common prize requires an additional tie convention, such as averaging the prizes over its block. That is a defensible high-multiplicity model, but it is a new allocation rule rather than the rule characterized in the paper.

The seemingly more direct limit through many finite competitions is not canonical either. If a type occupies ranks \(a_t+1,\ldots,a_t+n_t\), its per-unit-mass prize is

\[ p_{t,n} = E\, \frac{\sum_{r=a_t+1}^{a_t+n_t}\lambda_r} {(n_t/n)\sum_{r=1}^{n}\lambda_r}. \]

For summable \((\lambda_r)\), the limit collapses to a top-block allocation: almost all normalized prize mass goes to the highest positive-mass block. For sequences such as \(\lambda_r=1/r\), the same top concentration occurs. For \(\lambda_r=1\), the limit is equal division; for regularly varying sequences, other nontrivial densities can arise. Thus the family in Corollary 8 has no single continuous population limit. To obtain a chosen density \(\lambda(q)\), one must impose extra asymptotic regularity or make the weights depend on \(n\), which abandons the population-independent positional sequence used by the theorem.

A direct quantile formulation has a related problem. Corollary 8’s consistency uses the same positional sequence after a subset of competitors is removed. A rule indexed by normalized rank \(q\in[0,1]\) rescales ranks whenever the population is restricted, so it does not preserve that consistency without another scaling law or a redefinition of the axiom. Either choice produces a new continuous prize rule rather than a limit of the characterized one.

There is a plausible standalone project here: finite performance cohorts, averaged prizes for tied blocks, and welfare-optimal prize design. The negative case is therefore not that no meaningful continuous competition model can ever be written. It is that, under ChoCo’s requirement of mirroring a computational result from the paper, Corollary 8 cannot carry the case. The proposed LP is an interesting extension inspired by the conclusion, but not a continuous mirror of a result this paper actually proves.
