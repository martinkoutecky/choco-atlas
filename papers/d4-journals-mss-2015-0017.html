The continuous mirror question

Given a finite state space \(S\) with rational probabilities, risks \(k\), types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, and nonnegative rational per-capita endowments \(e_t^k(s)\), determine the exact set of individually rational Pareto-optimal type-homogeneous allocations \(x_t^k(s)\geq 0\). Require \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t^k(s)=\sum_t \mu_t e_t^k(s)\) for each risk and state, and require \(x_t^k\succeq_{SSD}e_t^k\) for every type and risk; Pareto comparisons range over feasible type-homogeneous allocations, with strict improvement for at least one positive-mass type on at least one risk. Represent the resulting set by its extreme points and their convex hull.

The model it lives in

A continuum population of producers or small insurers with distribution \(\mu\) over complete exposure types; each member of type \(t\) receives the same state-contingent allocation \(x_t\). Feasibility conserves aggregate endowments, individual rationality and Pareto comparisons use the paper’s risk-by-risk SSD preferences, and the output is the full IRPO polytope rather than a scalar welfare optimum.

The objection that survived

Requiring members with the same exposure type to receive a common contract excludes allocations the paper allows, though standardized contracts by exposure class make that restriction plausible for a mutual pool.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s rational-probability characterization and computation of individually rational Pareto optima for type-homogeneous allocations; it leaves aside the broader Pareto-optimality results and the paper’s one-dimensional characterization.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of listing every extreme point as a function of \(|S|\), \(\tau\), the number of risks, and the input bit length; is there an output-sensitive algorithm?
  • If members of one type may split their mass across different contracts, does the IRPO set retain a finite polyhedral description?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: Section 4’s individually rational Pareto optima, in a regime with many insurers but few complete endowment types. The paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or similar. Its best computational anchor is structural.

Theorem 3, proved in this paper, says that with a finite state space and rational state probabilities, the set \(P_{IR}\) of individually rational Pareto-optimal allocations is a polytope, hence the convex hull of finitely many extreme points. The authors use vertex identification to derive all such allocations, including in the examples. This is a computational result in the sense that it turns the question into finite exact computation, but it does not establish a polynomial-time bound.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover. Consider a large mutual risk-sharing pool of producers or small insurers, with many members but only \(\tau\) distinct exposure profiles. That scenario is close to the paper’s agricultural-producer example. A type \(t\) records a member’s complete initial endowment \(e_t^k(s)\) across risks \(k\) and states \(s\), along with any preference or policy parameters the problem uses. Members of a type are indistinguishable in those respects. Their population shares are rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to one; typically the number of members is far larger than \(\tau\). The state probabilities remain the paper’s finite, rational probabilities. It is the member population—not the states, outcomes, or uncertainty—that is continuous.

Call the problem Mass-IRPO Enumeration. Given the finite state space \(S\), rational probabilities \(p_s\), types \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\), and nonnegative rational endowments \(e_t^k(s)\), choose a state-contingent wealth allocation \(x_t^k(s)\geq 0\) for each type and risk. Each unit of type \(t\)’s mass receives that type’s allocation. Feasibility requires, for every risk and state,
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t^k(s)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t e_t^k(s). \]
Individual rationality requires \(x_t^k\succeq_{SSD} e_t^k\) for every \(t,k\), using the paper’s second-order stochastic-dominance preference. An allocation is Pareto-optimal in this type-homogeneous problem if there is no other feasible type-homogeneous allocation that weakly improves every type on every risk and strictly improves at least one positive-mass type on at least one risk. The requested answer is the exact set of these allocations, represented by its extreme points and their convex hull. There is no scalar welfare objective in the paper; the object being computed is the whole set.

I expect the natural linear-optimization queries over this set to fall in Class A. The paper’s comonotonicity characterization orders states by aggregate endowment for each risk; with that order fixed, feasibility, monotonicity, and the finite-state SSD conditions give linear constraints. A linear score over allocations can therefore be optimized by an LP. Enumerating every extreme point is a stronger output task: the answer may itself be exponentially large, and Theorem 3 does not prove a polynomial-time listing algorithm. The likely tractability claim is for optimization or an output-sensitive representation, not for a short list of all vertices.

This mirror covers Theorem 3’s rational-probability IRPO computation, rather than the paper’s broader Pareto-optimality results. It also gives the authors a recognizable question: which individually rational, Pareto-efficient risk-sharing allocations are available to a population with repeated exposure profiles?

My weakest point is the type-homogeneous allocation restriction. The paper permits two insurers with identical endowments to receive different contracts; this mirror gives everyone in an exposure type the same contract. A standardized contract per exposure class is plausible for a mutual pool, but it is still a restriction. Allowing mass within a type to split among different contracts would be a more faithful extension, and would raise a further question: whether the paper’s finite-polytope characterization survives that split. The other open question is the complexity of full vertex enumeration as a function of \(m,\tau,p\), and the input bit lengths.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 3 is not a complexity theorem. It proves that the individually rational Pareto optima form a polytope, then points to generic vertex identification. It gives no running-time bound, input encoding, or guarantee that listing the vertices is efficient; the list may be exponentially large. So it does not establish the kind of algorithmic result the programme most directly seeks.

That objection does not defeat the mirror, though. A credible version is a large mutual of producers with a small number of complete exposure profiles. If type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), endowment \(e_t^k(s)\), and receives a common type-level allocation \(x_t^k(s)\), feasibility is
\[ \sum_t \mu_t x_t^k(s)=\sum_t \mu_t e_t^k(s). \]
The resulting question—characterize or optimize over the individually rational Pareto optima—is recognizable risk sharing. Farmers with the same crop and weather exposure provide a plausible repeated-type regime. The quantities are per capita, and a type with positive mass still matters to Pareto comparisons. Neither identity dependence nor a vanishing continuum rules this out.

The best case against is that this version is a weighted, type-compressed form of the paper’s finite-agent model. For rational \(\mu_t\), it corresponds to duplicating each type in proportion to its mass and restricting those duplicates to share a contract. The paper’s comonotonicity argument and finite-state SSD constraints already supply the structure for a finite linear formulation. Thus the mirror may add little beyond compressing repeated agents; full vertex enumeration can still be exponential. But that is a low-yield argument, not a fundamental obstruction.

Allowing members of the same type to receive different contracts would remove the common-contract restriction and more closely match the paper’s full allocation space. That raises a harder question about distributions of contracts within each type; it does not make the population interpretation incoherent.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor in every scenario. The paper offers a weak computational anchor, and the most natural mirror looks like a modest extension of its existing polytope result. But a pooled producer population with repeated exposure types is sufficiently plausible that the universal negative case is weak.
