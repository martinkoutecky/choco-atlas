The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population mirror here, but no qualifying computational anchor. The paper gives no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifying a problem as tractable or hard. Proposition 1 characterizes equilibria in the compulsory-voting game for \(E>6\): candidates have equal winning chances and use strictly mixed balanced-number strategies that never target \(\lfloor E/4\rfloor\) voters or fewer; in the election, non-indifferent voters vote sincerely and indifferent voters choose each option with probability \(1/3\). Proposition 2 gives the voluntary-voting characterization: candidates again have equal winning chances, never make offers below \(2c\) per targeted voter, and have at least one equilibrium in balanced-number strategies; voters below intensity \(2c\) abstain, while those at or above it turn out with positive probability and vote sincerely. Both propositions are proved in the paper’s online supporting information. They are equilibrium results, not computational-complexity results; the numbered experimental results are empirical findings. So neither can honestly serve as an anchor under your rule.

The strongest candidate mirror I see is a High-Multiplicity Polity Equilibrium problem. Its regime would be a large electorate of \(N\) people, typically \(N\gg\tau\), partitioned into a small number \(\tau\) of voter types sharing the same voting cost and other behaviorally relevant characteristics. The instance gives their rational population shares \(\mu_t\), the voting mode, a per-capita budget \(w\), and the office bonus \(\rho\). Each candidate chooses how much mass \(x_{it}\) of each type to target; if candidate \(i\) targets total mass \(x_i=\sum_t x_{it}\), each targeted voter is promised \(w/x_i\), reflecting the paper’s equal division of a fixed budget after scaling the total budget with \(N\). Voters then choose among the available ballots and abstention. The requested output would be a subgame-perfect equilibrium: candidates’ distributions over targeting choices, voters’ type-level response probabilities, and the resulting probability of winning. A realistic setting is national or regional pork-barrel politics aimed at large cohorts—such as residents of districts or workers in industries—rather than a small committee.

That model preserves the paper’s central strategic question: how targeting choices, voting costs, and turnout interact to determine electoral success and redistribution. It is also the kind of setting where a type-mass representation could be useful to the authors. Proposition 2 is the closest substantive lead: a continuous analogue could ask whether an equilibrium imposes a threshold on the mass targeted, just as the finite model imposes a \(2c\) threshold on per-voter promises. But this is a proposed research question, not a result established by the paper, and I cannot assign it a credible complexity class from these propositions.

The soft spot is pivotality. In a literal atomless electorate, one voter cannot change the election result, so voluntary voting at positive cost collapses: no individual has a reason to turn out. Preserving the paper’s participation incentives may require retaining a finite-population scale \(N\), or specifying another model of election uncertainty. That is a substantial modeling choice, not a harmless translation. Thus the population regime is plausible, but the paper supplies no qualifying computational result to anchor a ChoCo case, and its central turnout mechanism makes the continuous limit especially delicate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed high multiplicity polity equilibrium is the strongest candidate, but it does not give ChoCo a computational result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 characterize equilibria in the paper’s finite game; Results 1–7 report experimental findings. Asking for the complexity of computing equilibria in a newly specified continuous game would be an adjacent research question, not a mirror of a computational claim in this paper.

The better version of the proposed mirror does have a plausible population regime: many voters in a few cohorts, with the total budget scaled with population so each targeted voter still receives a finite amount. That scaling is a sensible co-modification. The deeper problem is pivotality. In an atomless electorate, one voter’s ballot cannot change the probability of winning. With positive voting cost, abstention therefore strictly beats voting in the voluntary model, regardless of preference intensity. The \(2c\) turnout threshold in Proposition 2—and the mechanism behind the paper’s comparison of voting modes—vanishes. Under compulsory voting, individual ballots likewise have no effect, leaving voter choices payoff-indifferent rather than sincere in the way the finite model predicts.

Keeping finite \(N\) preserves pivotality, but then the turnout incentives depend on the \(1/N\) scale and the integer vote margin. They do not follow from the population shares alone. Adding aggregate election uncertainty or treating cohorts as strategic voting blocs could restore consequential votes, but either adds a mechanism absent from the paper. The resulting equilibrium-computation problem might be worthwhile; it would be a new model, not a faithful continuous mirror of these propositions. And the paper supplies no computational result to anchor that new problem.

The negative case is weakest as a claim that no interesting continuum model could ever be built. The high multiplicity setting itself is plausible. But every version that preserves the paper’s distinctive costly-turnout mechanism must retain finite pivotality or introduce a new source of it, while the paper’s actual results offer no computational-complexity anchor. That makes the proponent’s mirror a poor fit for this programme.
