The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly supply a qualifying anchor under your rule. The numbered results in the paper concern truthfulness and payment sharing, not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or in a parameterized complexity class. The closest is Theorem 1, proved here: for a given multicast allocation method \(O\), a truthful payment scheme exists if and only if \(O\) has the monotone non-increasing property—holding other agents’ costs fixed, any agent selected at a higher cost must also be selected at every lower cost. The paper’s NP-hardness statements about minimum-cost multicast trees are background claims attributed to earlier work, not numbered results of this paper. The introduction’s claim about hardness when receivers and relay agents are both selfish also has no numbered result establishing it in the supplied text. I would not present any of these as a qualifying complexity anchor.

The closest positive mirror I can defend is therefore unanchored: a high-multiplicity version of Theorem 1’s truthful relay-payment question. Consider many repeated multicast deployments, each with a finite network template. A type specifies a relay’s full role in that template—including connectivity, capacity, and cost—so agents of one type are interchangeable for everything the protocol uses. If there are \(N_t\) agents of type \(t\), mass is \(\mu_t=N_t/N\), with relatively few types compared with agents. The instance would give a finite role template, rational type masses and unit costs, and a specified routing rule \(O\) that selects relay mass. The problem is to decide whether the rule admits incentive-compatible, individually rational per-unit payments, and, if so, return the critical cost threshold for each relay type; each selected unit is paid its threshold. Its solution is a yes/no answer and those thresholds.

This is recognizably related to the paper’s question about truthful payments for a fixed multicast protocol, and for LCPT-like rules the threshold computation might be tractable through shortest-path calculations. But that expected outcome is a conjecture about the proposed mirror, not a result of this paper. Fractional relay mass and replicated network roles would also need to preserve multicast feasibility and the effect of an individual report. The authors might accept that model for repeated, structurally identical deployments; they might reject it if those assumptions erase the topology-specific incentives they study.

That is the weak point, and it limits the positive case: the paper offers a plausible subject for a continuous mechanism-design question, but no numbered computational-complexity result to anchor the required continuization argument.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying anchor. Theorem 1 characterizes when an allocation rule admits truthful payments: it must satisfy the monotone non-increasing property. It does not classify the complexity of a computational problem, or establish an efficient algorithm for checking that property or computing thresholds. The paper’s polynomial-time condition is part of its mechanism-design setup; the NP-hard multicast claims are background attributed to earlier work. The other numbered results concern monotonicity, payment sharing, and fairness. Under the requested anchor standard, there is no computational-complexity result here to continuize.

The proposed repeated-deployment model also has a structural problem. A relay’s role includes its position in the network: which paths it can serve, and how those paths connect to the source and receivers. Its selection and payment threshold depend on that position and on other agents’ costs. A type list with masses records how many relays have each role, but loses which roles occur together in each network. Preserve each deployment’s topology and the copies can be handled independently; the aggregate threshold distribution is then the summary of repeated runs. Let mass move among roles across deployments and the representation can combine relay mass from separate networks without preserving any valid path. Including enough deployment-specific information to prevent that loss removes the proposed compression.

A more promising repair would model divisible traffic or many interchangeable relay units over a fixed topology, with fractional routing and per-unit incentives. That may be a worthwhile continuous networking problem. It changes the allocation and incentive questions, though: the paper studies whether individual relays are selected and paid a critical threshold under a given multicast protocol. It supplies no result showing that its characterization or payment schemes carry over to a fluid routing model.

So the case against is strongest on the absence of any named computational-complexity anchor and the mismatch between aggregate type masses and network structure. The broader claim that no continuous multicast mechanism could ever be worthwhile is harder to defend: a fluid model may be valuable as new work, even though it does not establish a worthwhile mirror of this paper’s results.
