The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper that qualifies as an anchor under the requested standard. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting a complexity result. Definitions 3.1 and 3.2 define utility loss and distance; the decision-tree algorithm and the results in Section 5 are methodological and empirical. The paper’s statement that finding an optimal decision tree is intractable is not a numbered result proved here. So I cannot honestly give an anchor-based case.

The strongest positive lead is an unanchored mirror of Sections 3–5. Call it Population-Bounded Utility Elicitation Tree. Fix a decision model \(M\), a patient history \(h\), and a finite set of strategies \(\mathcal S\). An instance gives normalized utility types \(u_1,\ldots,u_\tau\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a finite set \(\mathcal Q\) of the paper’s preference and fixed-threshold lottery questions, and a question budget \(q\). The question is to choose a binary query tree of depth at most \(q\), with a strategy \(s_\ell\in\mathcal S\) at each leaf, minimizing
\[ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\left[ \max_{s\in\mathcal S} EU_{u_t}(s\mid h) - EU_{u_t}(s_{\ell(t)}\mid h) \right], \]
where \(\ell(t)\) is the leaf reached by type \(u_t\)’s answers. A solution is the tree and its leaf recommendations.

A plausible regime is a large prenatal decision-support service serving many patients within the same clinical-history stratum, where many patients share a small number of complete utility models. Then \(N\), the number of patients, is much larger than \(\tau\), the number of distinct utility types; \(\mu_t\) is the population share of each type. This fits the paper’s assumption that new users’ utilities come from the same distribution as its database, and its use of population prevalence to motivate clustering. The paper’s per-history trees also make fixing \(h\) a natural scope choice.

I think the authors could recognize this as a continuous version of their goal: ask fewer, simpler questions while keeping decision loss low. But the paper does not optimize a shared tree against population-weighted utility loss. It greedily builds trees using cluster-label impurity, then evaluates utility loss empirically. The proposed objective is therefore a natural extension of their method, not a result they establish. If its discrete tree-selection counterpart is hard, I would expect hardness to transfer: an explicit electorate maps to masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and the tree-selection choices remain. That is a conditional prediction, not a complexity conclusion supported by a named result here.

This candidate covers the clustering and classification method and its empirical evaluation in Sections 3–5; it does not mirror a formal complexity theorem or the paper’s decision-theoretic strategy-selection problem. Its weakest point is that moving from classifying a new individual to optimizing one population-wide elicitation policy changes the computational question. Since there is no qualifying anchor, that is as far as an honest positive case can go.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts at the anchor gate: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. It defines utility loss and distance, gives clustering and decision-tree procedures, and reports experiments on a small database. Its remark that finding an optimal decision tree is intractable is not a complexity result established here. The paper therefore offers no theorem whose continuous version could deliver the kind of complexity result ChoCo is tasked with charting.

The proposed Population-Bounded Utility Elicitation Tree is the strongest possible rescue, but it is a new optimization problem, not a mirror of a result in the paper. The paper builds history-specific clusters and uses a greedy tree to classify one new patient’s utility function; the candidate instead asks for a population-wide tree with a question budget, optimized for aggregate utility loss. That could be a sensible extension. Yet its population objective is a weighted learning objective: patients still answer questions individually and receive personalized recommendations, while the weights summarize how often each utility type occurs. Repeating patients changes those weights, but does not create a shared social outcome or a mass-transfer decision of the kind the continuization programme studies. Its computational core is query-tree optimization, not a complexity question the paper formulated and answered.

A more faithful population model would treat the patient database as a distribution over complete utility types and measure the expected loss of the paper’s classifier. That is plausible—the authors explicitly invoke prevalence—but it still gives a distributional evaluation of a heuristic, not a named computational result to mirror. Choosing a type space, or optimizing the shared elicitation policy, may lead to worthwhile research; neither supplies the missing anchor.

This is not a claim that no useful model can be built. The strongest honest negative case is narrower: this paper does not give the required computational foothold, and its most plausible continuous formulation changes the central task from classifying an individual for personalized advice to optimizing a population-level information policy. The case is weaker if the assignment allows a new computational problem inspired by the paper rather than a mirror of one of its results; under that broader standard, the proposed tree deserves consideration.
