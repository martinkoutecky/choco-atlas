The continuous mirror question

Given finite job types \(A\) and machine types \(B\), acceptable type pairs, strict type rankings, rational job masses \(\mu_a\), rational machine masses \(\rho\nu_b\), job sizes \(q_a\), and capacities \(Q_b\), decide whether one can compute a machine-optimal stable distribution of integral local configurations in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length. A configuration \(S\) assigns an integer number \(n_a(S)\) of jobs of each type to one machine; rational masses \(z_{b,S}\) must satisfy \(\sum_S z_{b,S}=\rho\nu_b\) and \(\sum_{b,S}z_{b,S}n_a(S)\le\mu_a\). Each configuration must satisfy the paper’s relaxed capacity condition, and if positive mass of type-\(a\) jobs prefers type \(b\) to its assignment, every configuration used by type-\(b\) machines must have at least \(Q_b\) load from types they rank above \(a\). Among stable outputs, require the machine-type configuration distribution to stochastically dominate every alternative under the stated lexicographic configuration order.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity stable allocation market with job and machine types, rational mass for each type, and integral job bundles at each machine. The decision variables are rational masses \(z_{b,S}\) assigned to local configurations; job mass constraints, the paper’s relaxed capacity condition, and type-level blocking define stability, while machine-type distributions are compared by the proposed lexicographic stochastic dominance.

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous mirror of the paper’s machine-optimal relaxed unsplit stable allocation result. Imagine a large service market with many recurring, standardized jobs and many machines or providers. A job type records its size and ranking of machine types; a machine type records its capacity and ranking of job types. The regime has \(N\) jobs and machines but only \(\tau\) distinct full types, with \(\tau \ll N\). For example, the jobs might be recurring contracts that cannot be split, and the machines might be capacity-limited providers. Every property used by the allocation problem belongs in the type, so agents grouped together really are interchangeable.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: the authors give an \(O(|E|)\)-time algorithm for computing the machine-optimal relaxed unsplit stable assignment. In the paper’s model, each job is assigned whole to one machine or left unassigned; a relaxed assignment permits a machine to exceed capacity, provided removing its least-preferred assigned job puts it below capacity. Stability uses the paper’s stricter blocking rule: a job that prefers another machine can block only if that machine is not filled to capacity with jobs it prefers over the job. Machine-optimality means no machine can get a lexicographically better allocation in another relaxed unsplit stable assignment. Theorem 2 computes such an assignment using the reversed Gale–Shapley algorithm.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Machine-Optimal Relaxed Unsplit Stable Allocation. The input is finite sets of job types \(A\) and machine types \(B\), acceptable type pairs, and strict rankings over the acceptable types. Each \(a\in A\) has job size \(q_a>0\) and rational mass \(\mu_a\); each \(b\in B\) has capacity \(Q_b>0\) and rational mass \(\rho\nu_b\), where \(\sum_a\mu_a=\sum_b\nu_b=1\) and \(\rho\) is the machine-to-job population ratio. All masses are binary rationals. The support size is \(\tau=|A|+|B|\), and \(L\) is the input bit length.

A local machine configuration \(S\) is an integer multiset of jobs assigned to one machine: \(n_a(S)\) jobs of each type \(a\). It uses only acceptable pairs, and it is relaxed if deleting its least-preferred job leaves load strictly below capacity. The output is a list of such configurations and rational masses \(z_{b,S}\), where \(z_{b,S}\) is the mass of type-\(b\) machines receiving configuration \(S\). They must satisfy
\[ \sum_S z_{b,S}=\rho\nu_b \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{b,S}z_{b,S}n_a(S)\le \mu_a. \]
Thus the configuration is integral for each machine, even though a fractional mass of a machine type can receive each configuration. The residual job mass is unassigned.

Stability has a direct type-level definition. Let
\[ u_{a,b}=\sum_S z_{b,S}n_a(S). \]
For each acceptable pair \((a,b)\), consider the mass of type-\(a\) jobs assigned below \(b\), including unassigned jobs:
\[ W_{a,b}=\mu_a-\sum_{b':\,b'\succeq_a b}u_{a,b'}. \]
If \(W_{a,b}>0\), then every configuration \(S\) with \(z_{b,S}>0\) must have at least \(Q_b\) load from job types that \(b\) prefers over \(a\):
\[ \sum_{a':\,a'\succ_b a}q_{a'}n_{a'}(S)\ge Q_b. \]
Finally, among all stable outputs, the returned configuration distribution for each machine type must be machine-optimal: it stochastically dominates every alternative distribution in that type’s lexicographic order over configurations. At the type level, compare configurations by the highest-ranked job type whose multiplicity differs; having more of that type is better. This asks for a machine-optimal assignment as in Theorem 2, not just any stable assignment or a split-job relaxation of it. The computational question is whether it can be produced, or its nonexistence certified, in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), rather than in the expanded population size.

I expect Class A as a working conjecture. The finite theorem’s proposal structure may admit a bulk implementation that processes identical cohorts together, and configuration masses preserve the jobs’ indivisibility. But that is a research question: the expanded finite algorithm takes time in \(|E|\), which may be enormous relative to a binary-rational input. A useful first result would be either a compressed proposal algorithm or a hardness proof in \(\tau,L\). Further questions include whether the paper’s minimum-total-congestion property also holds in this model, and whether the type-level problem has an exact finite realization or useful rounding guarantees.

This mirror covers the paper’s relaxed, stricter-stability model and its machine-optimality result. It does not claim to continuize every result in the paper. The paper’s cited NP-hardness for a different, weaker blocking definition is relevant context, but it is not my anchor: Theorem 2 is the numbered result here, and it concerns the stricter variant.

The weak point is the type boundary. The paper assumes strict preferences over named edges; grouping repeated agents requires preferences over types, so copies of a type are interchangeable. That weakens or coarsens individual identity-level rankings. Also, preserving unsplittability requires retaining local integer configurations: replacing them with fractional flows would change the question. Those configurations may be numerous when capacities and sizes are binary encoded. I think the mirror still merits study because it keeps the paper’s job sizes, machine capacities, stability condition, and unsplit assignments intact while compressing a repeated population. But whether authors would accept type-level preferences as faithful to their strict individual rankings is the case’s soft spot.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that Theorem 2’s machine-optimality is defined for named machines with strict rankings over named jobs. Its proof compares particular edges in the symmetric difference of two assignments. Once machines and jobs are grouped into types, that pointwise comparison disappears. A distribution over configurations with stochastic dominance in a type’s lexicographic order is a new optimality notion; the finite theorem does not establish that it is the limit of machine-optimality in replicated finite instances. If we keep enough identity-specific rankings to preserve the original comparison, the number of types may grow with the population and defeat the compression.

That objection does not defeat the best mirror. The authors could instead define preferences over job and machine types, then ask for a stable unsplit allocation that is optimal under a type-level ordering. The configuration masses preserve whole-job assignments at each machine, and fractional masses across configurations are an ordinary consequence of treating the population as continuous. This is a sensible computational question, even though its relationship to Theorem 2 needs to be proved. The paper’s high-multiplicity stable-allocation setting supports the plausibility of the regime; it is not a novelty collision. Nor does the possibility that configurations are numerous make the question unworthy: that is precisely where the computational work would lie.

So I can challenge the proposed mirror’s claim to faithfully continue machine-optimality, but I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous version. Theorem 2 is a named computational result, and a type-level model with a carefully chosen optimality definition appears to clear the programme’s gate. The negative case is weak at its strongest point: it identifies a real gap between the paper’s individual optimum and the proposed aggregate one, but not a reason to abandon the mirror.
