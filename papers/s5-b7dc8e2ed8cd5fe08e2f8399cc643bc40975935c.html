The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly make an anchor-based case under your rule: this paper has no named computational-complexity result. Its named results concern calibration and population-risk limits, not whether an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally tractable. The closest is Theorem 4, proved here: as the Soft Kemeny temperature tends to zero, any accumulation point of population-risk minimizers induces a ranking that minimizes expected pairwise disagreement among the model’s realizable rankings; if a Kemeny-optimal ranking is realizable, the limit recovers one. Theorem 3, also proved here, establishes that the expected Soft Kemeny loss converges to expected disagreement when ties have probability zero. Neither result analyzes an algorithm’s running time or computes a solution to an input aggregation problem.

The strongest nearby mirror suggested by those results is Continuum Kemeny Aggregation. An instance consists of \(m\) alternatives \(C\), a finite support \(\mathcal T\subseteq S_m\) of \(\tau\) complete ranking types, and binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). Type \(t\) means a voter with ranking \(t\); \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of society. The task is to output a ranking
\[ \pi^\star\in\arg\min_{\pi\in S_m}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t\,d_K(\pi,t), \]
where \(d_K\) is Kendall disagreement. A solution is any minimizing ranking.

A plausible regime is a large evaluation panel judging the same fixed set of candidate responses to a prompt. Many annotators share one of a small number of stable preference types, so the number of annotators is much larger than \(\tau\). This mirrors the paper’s Kemeny target: its Soft Kemeny loss approaches pairwise-disagreement minimization, and Theorem 4 gives the population-optimum connection. The mirror covers that Kemeny result alone.

For this candidate, I expect hardness to transfer: a discrete profile with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\) maps to \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), preserving the optimal rankings. Thus rational masses do not remove the combinatorics of ordering the alternatives; under standard discrete Kemeny hardness, this version is Class B. Questions it raises include the exact complexity in \(m,\tau,L\), whether restrictions on \(\tau\) permit efficient algorithms, and what approximation guarantees are possible.

The weak point is also substantial: the paper’s input is a distribution \(\mathcal D\) over pairwise comparison instances, not a stated finite-support distribution over complete voter rankings. Stable, transitive ranking types are a plausible RLHF setting, but they are an additional modeling bridge. And because the paper already studies population-level expected disagreement, a referee might see this as a computational reframing of its existing objective rather than a genuinely new mirror. That makes it a credible candidate problem, but it does not supply the required named computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 establishes the direction of a pairwise population-risk minimizer; Theorem 3 proves convergence of a loss to disagreement; Theorem 4 relates limiting population minimizers to Kemeny-optimal rankings within a realizable set. None studies the complexity of computing those minimizers or gives an algorithm with a running-time guarantee. So the paper itself leaves no stated complexity result for a continuization programme to classify.

That scope objection does not defeat the proposed Continuum Kemeny Aggregation mirror. Its input—a distribution over complete ranking types—and its weighted Kendall objective are coherent. A panel of many annotators sharing a small set of stable rankings is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the objective follows naturally by averaging pairwise disagreement over those rankings. It is closely connected to the paper’s Soft Kemeny limit, even though the paper does not analyze the computation of its optimum.

I cannot honestly dismiss that mirror on the grounds that rational masses encode discrete profiles, that hardness may transfer, or that ranking types add a modeling step: the programme’s criteria rule those out as objections, and the ranking-type model is a natural reconstruction of the paper’s objective. The defensible negative case is therefore narrow: this paper contains no named computational result to anchor a mirror. I cannot make the stronger claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario; the Kemeny candidate survives that case.
