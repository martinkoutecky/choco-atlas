The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of worker types \(t=(a_t,h_t)\), where \(a_t\) is a truthful answer vector for a fixed task and \(h_t\) is honesty, binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), and the task’s control answers, compute the infimum of \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t R_t(\omega;\mu)\) over \(\omega\in[0,1]\) satisfying \(R_t(\omega;\mu)>R_u(\omega;\mu)\) whenever \(h_t>h_u\). Here \(R_t\) is type \(t\)’s expected total iMET reward under the paper’s report model and aggregation rule; determine whether the infimum is attained and return a minimizing weight if it is.

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete worker types, with each type carrying mass and retaining its truthful response vector and honesty level. Each type receives its per-worker expected iMET reward against the population distribution, while total expected reward is summed by mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 23’s reward-weight selection in iMET, including the expected total reward objective and honesty-ordering constraints; it leaves Propositions 21 and 22 and the thesis’s other mechanisms and robustness results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give a support-sensitive complexity bound in task length, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length, and characterize when the infimum is attained.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a named result that meets the required anchor test: the thesis gives no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifying a problem by computational complexity or algorithmic runtime. Its closest results are strategic. For example, Proposition 21 proves that truthful answers are a dominant strategy in iMET’s truthfulness-reward component; Proposition 22 says this yields a unique Nash equilibrium; Proposition 23 establishes that the reward-weight constraints have a feasible solution. These are real results, but they do not establish that an underlying problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. I can’t honestly present one as a qualifying anchor.

The strongest *unanchored* positive angle is iMET’s reward design. In a large recurring crowdsourcing panel, define a worker type as their truthful answer vector for a short standardized task together with their honesty level. Let \(\mu\) be a rational mass distribution over the types in the panel’s support. The continuous problem is: given \(\mu\), the control questions, and the task, choose \(\omega\in[0,1]\) to minimize expected total rewards, using iMET’s same truthfulness and consistency rewards, subject to workers with different honesty levels receiving strictly ordered expected total rewards. The solution is the minimizing \(\omega\). A panel with many workers and a moderate number of recurring response profiles makes mass a plausible representation, while retaining iMET’s heterogeneous truthful opinions. This looks like a candidate for tractability when the support and task are explicit, since the mechanism chooses a single weight; computing the exact expected-reward coefficients is part of the question.

That candidate mirrors Equation 5.7 and is supported by Proposition 23’s feasibility result, but it is not a valid anchor under the stated requirement. Its weakest point is also substantive: for a long or highly subjective task, complete truthful answer vectors may be nearly unique, so the number of types could approach the number of workers. The thesis’s formal results show a natural incentive-design problem to continuize, but do not provide the required named computational result to anchor a positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

No anchor survives the requested test. The thesis’s named results concern incentive properties: Proposition 21 gives a dominant strategy, Proposition 22 a Nash equilibrium, and Proposition 23 feasibility of the reward constraints. The CONGRESS and iMEMLI results are also strategic or welfare claims. Chapter 6 defines robustness and gives a simulation procedure, but provides no complexity classification or runtime result. Its Algorithm 5 is a procedure, not an analyzed computational result.

The proposed iMET question is the strongest possible repair, and a large panel with recurring answer profiles is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. With types recording truthful answer vectors and honesty, a mass distribution can preserve type-level expected rewards. But Equation 5.7 already specifies the weight-selection optimization and Proposition 23 establishes feasibility. For a fixed short task, the mirror mainly replaces worker counts with type masses and solves the same scalar-weight optimization; it does not open a new complexity landscape. In the operational version, where iMET estimates honesty and similarity from realized answers, preserving those estimates may require types to include response histories, making support grow rapidly with task length. That limits the broader case, though it does not rule out the short-task regime.

Chapter 6 is the closest alternative computational angle, and it already represents populations by strategy proportions and defines robustness for an infinite population. A further mass formulation of that chapter would largely restate its existing model.

So the case against is strong under the required anchor standard: there is no named computational result to mirror, and the proposed iMET formulation is an unanchored extension with limited computational novelty. The universal claim is weaker: the panel regime is plausible, and I cannot honestly argue that continuous population mass is meaningless here.
