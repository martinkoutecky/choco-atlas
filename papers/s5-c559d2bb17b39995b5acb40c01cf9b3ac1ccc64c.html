The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result such as hardness or membership in P. Its closest candidate is the authors’ conjecture that every alternative maximizing proportional majoritarian support belongs to the uncovered set. They state it as a conjecture, backed by simulations, and leave its proof to future work (§§4, 6 and footnote 8). It cannot honestly serve as the named result your anchor rule requires.

The strongest positive case is therefore a proposed mirror of that conjecture, not an anchor-based case. Consider a large election over a fixed set \(C\) of alternatives. A type is a complete strict ranking of \(C\), and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with ranking \(t\). Millions of voters might share a relatively small number \(\tau\) of ballot types. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: the rule depends on the distribution of rankings, not on voters’ names, and population shares are a natural way to express its inputs.

Call the candidate problem Distributional PAMS Containment. Its input is \(C\) and a list of \(\tau\) rankings with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), with no pairwise ties. For each pair, let
\[ p(a,b)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t. \]
Define
\[ q(a)= \frac{\sum_{b:\,p(a,b)>1/2}p(a,b)} {\sum_{b:\,p(a,b)>1/2}p(a,b)+\sum_{b:\,p(a,b)<1/2}p(b,a)}. \]
The PAMS winners are the alternatives maximizing \(q\). The question is whether every such winner belongs to the uncovered set of the strict-majority tournament induced by \(\mu\). A “yes” solution certifies containment; a “no” solution gives a PAMS winner covered by another alternative. This directly continues the paper’s proposed rule and its uncovered-set claim to a society represented by masses.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: their method uses the quantitative distribution of pairwise votes, and the continuous formulation preserves those quantities while allowing arbitrary rational population shares. The expected direction is tractability: all pairwise masses and scores can be computed with polynomially many rational operations in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. Whether containment always holds remains a conjecture, not an established result. Natural next questions include proving or refuting it and specifying what happens when pairwise masses tie.

This mirror covers only the PAMS rule and its proposed uncovered-set guarantee. It does not continuize the paper’s discussions of Rousseau, epistemic voting, Kemeny rankings, or strategic equilibria. The weak point is substantial: the paper proves no computational result for this mirror, and evaluating the rule appears straightforward. So this is a plausible continuous population model, but it does not supply the discrete hardness anchor needed to ask whether continuization removes hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest reason to pass on this paper is that it has no computational result to continuize. It gives a voting rule and conjectures that its maximizers lie in the uncovered set, but proves no complexity theorem or algorithmic bound. The case for acknowledges this: its proposed anchor is a conjecture, not a result.

The proposed Distributional PAMS Containment question does not fill that gap. For a society listed by \(\tau\) rankings, compute the pairwise masses \(p(a,b)\), the scores \(q(a)\), and the majority tournament; then check whether a maximizing alternative is covered. That takes polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the rational input length. It is a simple way to evaluate the rule, not a computational challenge developed in the paper. Asking whether the conjecture holds for *every* profile would instead be a universal mathematical claim, with no society as the input instance.

There is a natural high-multiplicity regime here: large elections with repeated rankings. That is not the problem. The proposed rule uses only pairwise tallies, and normalizing voter counts to masses leaves the calculation unchanged. So a continuous version of winner determination is straightforward, but it offers no apparent new optimization structure or complexity landscape.

A stronger proposal would add an intervention problem—for example, finding the least-cost mass of voters to persuade to make a target a PAMS winner. That could be computationally interesting, but it introduces costs and an optimization objective the authors never study. Their discussion of strategic voting concerns equilibrium outcomes and the uncovered set, not a costed intervention task. It would be a new project attached to the rule, rather than a mirror of a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strongest as a recommendation not to use this paper as a continuization target: its population model is sensible, but its actual rule-evaluation task is easy and its central claim is conjectural. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile follow-on problem; the mass-intervention variant is a plausible one. That leaves the universal “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” claim weaker than the narrower conclusion that this paper offers no strong computational anchor.
