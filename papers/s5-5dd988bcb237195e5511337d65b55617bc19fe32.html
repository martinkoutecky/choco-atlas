The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many agent types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), resource classes \(j\in[q]\) with rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\), a conflict graph \(H\), utilities \(u_t(j)\), threshold \(\eta\), and bundle cap \(s\), does there exist a feasible mass assignment \(x_{t,b}\)? Each bundle \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^q\) must satisfy \(\sum_j b_j\le s\), have support independent in \(H\), and meet \(\sum_j u_t(j)b_j\ge\eta\) for its recipient type \(t\). Require \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for each type; in complete mode require \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_j=\rho_j\) for each class, and in partial mode require \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_j\le\rho_j\).

The model it lives in

A typed continuum of agents with mass \(\mu_t\), supplied with per-capita masses of interchangeable resource classes. Variables \(x_{t,b}\) assign type mass to legal integral bundles; type-mass and resource-capacity constraints form a rational LP with at most \(|T|\binom{q+s}{s}\) variables.

The objection that survived

The reduction’s tailored utility profiles do not establish hardness in a low-support fleet regime; the mirror’s tractability is most directly motivated for recurring resource classes and typed populations.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1, item 3, especially its hardness result for size-bounded complete and partial allocation; it leaves the unbounded-size results and the paper’s other parameterized and kernelization results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a specified finite population size \(N\), when does a feasible rational mass assignment lift to integer bundle counts for the \(N\) agents and their resource copies?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous high-multiplicity version of the paper’s size-bounded conflict-free fair allocation problem. It fits the paper’s own job-scheduling motivation: imagine a large fleet of machines, with many machines sharing one of a small number of complete utility profiles, and recurring jobs supplied in standardized classes. The number of machines \(N\) can be large while the number of machine types \(\tau\) stays small. Each machine still receives an integral bundle of jobs; the continuum lets a type’s population be divided among different bundles.

The anchor is Theorem 1, item 3—proved in this paper. It says that both Sb-C-CFFA and Sb-P-CFFA have polynomial-time algorithms when \(n\) and \(s\) are constants, even if \(\eta\) is large, and when \(s=1\), even if \(\eta\) is large. It also says that both variants are NP-hard for every \(n\ge1\) even when the binary encoding of \(\eta\) is polynomial in \(m\), and are NP-hard for every \(\eta\ge2\) and every \(s\ge2\). I would build the mirror chiefly around that last hardness result: the paper proves it here by reduction from 3-Dimensional Matching, with the hardness already present at \(s=\eta=2\) and an edgeless conflict graph.

Call the mirror Continuous High-Multiplicity Size-Bounded CFFA. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of agent types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\); \(q\) resource classes, with rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\); a conflict graph \(H\) on the classes; a nonnegative utility \(u_t(j)\) for each type and resource class; a threshold \(\eta\); a bundle cap \(s\); and a mode, complete or partial. An agent type is the full utility profile and every other feature the problem uses. A resource class groups interchangeable copies: copies have the same utility for every agent type and the same compatibility relations. In the discrete lift to \(N\) machines, class \(j\) has \(N\rho_j\) copies, for an \(N\) making the counts integral.

A bundle is an integer vector \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^q\), with \(\sum_j b_j\le s\), whose support is independent in \(H\), and whose utility to its recipient type satisfies \(\sum_j u_t(j)b_j\ge\eta\). The question is whether there are nonnegative rational masses \(x_{t,b}\), where \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(b\), such that \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for every type. In the complete mode, require \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_j=\rho_j\) for every resource class \(j\); in the partial mode, require this quantity to be at most \(\rho_j\). A solution is the feasible mass assignment \(x\). Thus every unit of agent mass gets an integral, conflict-free bundle meeting the threshold, while identical agents may be divided across bundle choices.

This is a credible high-multiplicity version of the authors’ question: it keeps their agents, resource utilities, conflicts, size limit, and minimum-satisfaction threshold. The mass variables express how a large population of identical machines is distributed among valid bundles. The scaling of job supplies with population is part of making that fleet regime coherent. In particular, the mirror does not give a machine a fractional job.

For fixed \(s\), this mirror is a Class A candidate. There are at most \(\binom{q+s}{s}\) integer bundle patterns before filtering for conflicts and utility, so the feasibility question is a rational LP with at most \(\tau\binom{q+s}{s}\) variables and \(\tau+q\) main constraints. At \(s=2\), the number of patterns is \(O(q^2)\), and the LP is polynomial in the input size, including the bit lengths of masses, supplies, utilities, and \(\eta\). With \(s\) as an input parameter, this gives an \(q^{O(s)}\)-size formulation. The paper’s NP-hardness would therefore dissolve in this typed mass regime, even on the edgeless-conflict slice used by the reduction.

The scope is the paper’s size-bounded complete and partial allocation variants, anchored at Theorem 1, item 3. I would not stretch this mirror to cover the paper’s kernelization results or its unbounded-size variants. Further questions include when a feasible continuous solution lifts to a discrete allocation at a *specified* finite population size, how much discrepancy remains when it does not lift exactly, and what complexity returns as \(s\) grows.

The weak point is substantial: the 3-Dimensional Matching reduction does not itself describe a low-support machine population. Its utility patterns encode individual choices, so the number of distinct agent types can grow with the hard instance. The LP’s tractability may also reflect the allowed pooling across identical types and repeated job classes, rather than something the paper’s unrestricted hard instances possess. I still think the mirror is worth asking: it describes a plausible large-fleet regime in the authors’ own setting, and the typed, per-capita formulation preserves the paper’s allocation question while making the effect of high multiplicity precise.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1(3)’s hardness is built from finely tailored utility profiles and item identities. The 3-Dimensional Matching reduction does not itself describe a small set of machine types or recurring, interchangeable jobs. In an application with one-off jobs, those identities may be essential, so a fleet-wide model with replenished job classes could be a poor account of that application.

But that objection does not defeat the better mirror. Give each agent type its complete utility profile, and each resource class its utility profile across types, compatibility relations, and within-class multiplicity rule. Let \(x_{t,B}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving a legal integral bundle \(B\) of size at most \(s\). Type-mass equations and resource-capacity equations then form a rational LP. For fixed \(s\), there are at most \(O(q^s)\) bundle patterns per type, so this is polynomial in the number of resource classes \(q\), types \(\tau\), and the input bit lengths. It preserves the threshold and integral-bundle requirements; only members of an identical type may be divided among bundles.

Nor does the reduction’s large number of distinct profiles invalidate the mirror. It can be lifted by repeating each profile and each item class as the population grows. The resulting \(\tau\) may grow with the instance, but the LP remains polynomial for fixed \(s\). If that LP accepts an instance that the discrete reduction rejects, the gap comes from mass splitting across integral bundles—the intended high-multiplicity effect, not a defect in the model.

So my negative case rests on application fit: a particular job-scheduling setting may not have large populations, stable types, and proportionally replenished standardized jobs. That limits where the mirror is useful, but the paper’s fleet and scheduling motivation gives a plausible regime, and scaling supplies is an allowed co-modification. The theorem is a named computational result, and I see no sound “already done” or identity-based objection. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor universally; the case against it is weak.
