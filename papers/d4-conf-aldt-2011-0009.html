The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a result-backed continuous mirror for this paper under the programme’s anchor rule: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result.

The closest candidates are Theorems 3–5, proved in the paper. They characterize fairness and other properties of the online cut-and-choose, moving-knife, and mark-and-choose procedures. Those are substantive results, but they do not establish that a computational problem is tractable or hard. Theorem 1 is an impossibility result about fairness axioms, and Theorem 2 shows that a trivial allocation procedure has several properties; neither supplies a computational anchor either.

There is a plausible population regime: many users arriving and departing while a divisible resource is allocated, with users drawn from a small set of complete types sharing valuation functions and relevant arrival conditions. A fluid model could represent each type by its mass and track mass receiving each slice. But asking whether a fluid version preserves Theorem 3’s fairness guarantees would be an axiomatic question about a new procedure, not a computational mirror of a named result in this paper. The cake is already continuous as an outcome; that alone does not continuize the population.

So the positive case is limited to the possibility that online allocation has a meaningful high-multiplicity regime. Its weakest point is decisive here: the paper provides no named computational result to anchor the requested question, and turning its fairness analysis into one would require inventing a different research problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point is that the paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Theorems 3–5 classify fairness and other properties of three fixed procedures; Theorem 1 gives fairness impossibilities, and Theorem 2 exhibits a trivial allocation. None states the complexity of a computational problem. That leaves no result-anchored computational mirror in the sense ChoCo is studying.

The best repair is to take Theorem 3’s online cut-and-choose procedure and model a large population as cohorts with shared valuations and arrival windows. One could then ask whether its guarantees survive, or optimize a fairness measure over online policies. But the first is a new axiomatic question about a mass-level procedure; the second changes the fixed procedure into a policy-search problem. Either might be interesting, but neither continuizes a computational result in this paper. Theorems 4 and 5 have the same limitation.

Theorem 1 is no stronger as an anchor. A pointwise fairness requirement keeps the individual-player question, but offers no computational problem. A mass-level fairness requirement or an approximation such as “what fraction of the population can be served proportionally?” changes the question. In particular, treating a cohort as one mass changes the role of the final player in the impossibility argument; keeping agents sequential instead preserves the online process but supplies no compression result.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting—many users with a small set of valuation profiles arriving in recurring time windows—so I would not claim that a continuous population model is meaningless here. That is the honest weakness of the negative case: a useful new computational project could be built around it. But under ChoCo’s stated criterion, it would be a new project, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.
