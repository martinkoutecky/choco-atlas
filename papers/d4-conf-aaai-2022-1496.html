The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), public budgets \(b_t\), per-unit values \(v_t\), reserve price \(v_s>0\), and per-capita supply \(q>0\), define \(D_\mu(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[p\le v_t]\left\lfloor b_t/p\right\rfloor\). Find the exact rational price \(p^\star\ge v_s\), if one exists, such that \(D_\mu(p^\star)>q\) and \(D_\mu(p^\star+\varepsilon)\le q\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\). Each unit of mass retains integral per-agent demand, while aggregate demand may be fractional.

The model it lives in

A society of advertisers represented by rational type masses, where a type records its public budget and unit value and the active masses are fixed. Inventory scales with population at density \(q\); the decision is the exact price where aggregate budget-limited demand crosses that density.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the I-critical-price computation in Lemma 2. It leaves the II-critical price and its individual-pivotality condition, as well as SNCA's network activation, incentive-compatibility, and welfare results, unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the threshold be computed in time polynomial in the support size and binary input length, without dependence on the numeric event-count parameter \(K\)?
  • Is there a useful mass-based analogue of the paper's II-critical notion that supports an incentive-compatible clinching mechanism?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest computational anchor is Lemma 2, proved in this paper. It says that, for a fixed set of participants \(N'\), both the I-critical and II-critical prices can be computed in polynomial time. An I-critical price \(p^*\) is one where total demand drops from above supply to at most supply: \(D(N',p^*)>m\ge D(N',p^*+\epsilon)\) for every \(\epsilon>0\). An agent is important when removing their demand would leave demand below supply while demand including them meets it: \(D-D_i<m\le D\). A II-critical price has no important agent at \(p^*\), but has one at \(p^*+\epsilon\) for every \(\epsilon>0\). The proof generates at most \(mn\) demand change points, sorts them, and scans them; its stated running time is \(O(mn^2)\). The paper proves this result here. It does not give a named NP-hardness result, so this algorithmic result is the right anchor.

My lead mirror is Mass-SNCA I-Critical Price. Consider a large sponsored-ad auction run by a platform with public daily spending caps and a referral network. Many advertisers may share a value per impression, a cap, and a referral role—for example, accounts in the same campaign and budget tier occupying the same position in repeated local referral networks. A type \(t\) is that complete record, including its network role; \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the advertiser population. The support has size \(\tau\), with population size \(N\gg\tau\). Inventory scales with the market: there are \(Nq\) units for rational supply density \(q>0\). This uses the public-budget ad-auction setting the authors themselves identify, while making the repeated-cohort assumption explicit.

Given the active type masses, reserve price \(v_s>0\), and supply density \(q\), define per-capita demand at price \(p\) as
\[ D_\mu(p)=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[p\le v_t]\left\lfloor\frac{b_t}{p}\right\rfloor. \]
This is the large-\(N\) demand: for sufficiently large replicated markets, each buyer’s budget-limited demand is below total inventory, so the finite-market cap of \(Nq\) units per buyer does not bind. The problem is to return the exact rational price \(p^*\) at which \(D_\mu(p^*)>q\) but \(D_\mu(p^*+\epsilon)\le q\) for every \(\epsilon>0\). The price is the decision variable; the solution is the threshold, not an allocation or a mechanism.

This mirrors the I-critical-price computation in Lemma 2, not the whole SNCA. With \(K=\sum_t\lfloor b_t/v_s\rfloor\), demand changes only at values \(v_t\) and \(b_t/k\) for relevant positive integers \(k\). Sorting and scanning these events gives an exact algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\), \(K\), and the input bit length \(L\). I expect this problem to be tractable in that parameterization, the Class A direction suggested by the paper’s price computation. If budgets are binary encoded and \(K\) is large, this is only polynomial in a numeric parameter; obtaining a bound polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\) alone is a further question.

I would not claim that Lemma 2’s II-critical-price result transfers unchanged. In the continuum, one buyer has vanishing mass, so individual pivotality—the feature that defines an important agent—disappears. Treating an entire type cohort as one “important agent” would restore pivotality by changing what counts as an agent, and I do not think the paper’s authors should be expected to accept that as the same problem. The continuous mirror covers the I-critical half of Lemma 2; it does not claim a mirror of SNCA’s incentive-compatibility proof, network activation process, or welfare results.

The weakest point is that the price subproblem takes the active type masses as given and therefore leaves the network’s strategic activation rule aside. The repeated referral-motif regime is plausible for platform markets, but it is a constructed instance regime, not a claim that every social-network auction has few types. The natural next questions are whether the price computation can be polynomial in bit length without \(K\), and what replaces individual importance in a genuinely atomless, incentive-compatible clinching mechanism.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot honestly defeat their Lemma 2 anchor. Its I-critical-price question has a coherent population mirror: for type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supply \(q\), and per-agent budgets and values \((b_t,v_t)\), define
\[ D_\mu(p)=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[p\le v_t]\left\lfloor\frac{b_t}{p}\right\rfloor. \]
Finding the price where this aggregate demand crosses \(q\) is a well-posed computational problem. Individual purchases remain integral; their aggregate across a mass of identical buyers need not be. The threshold does not vanish in the atomless limit.

The stronger objection is that this mirror isolates a narrow static calculation. It takes the active population as fixed, so it says nothing about how the network activates buyers or whether a mass-based mechanism can preserve incentives. The II-critical notion is especially ill-suited to an atomless population: no individual has positive mass to be pivotal, and treating a whole type cohort as one important agent would change the incentive problem. Those are real limits on what the mirror says about SNCA.

But they do not dispose of the I-critical-price question. Lemma 2 computes prices for a fixed participant set, and this price subproblem does not use the network beyond that set. The paper also says its method works without a network. A repeated-market scenario with few budget–value types and supply scaling with population is plausible, and the price computation has finitely many rational demand-change points. It may be a narrow or straightforward mirror, but “the answer might be easy” is not enough to make the question unworthy under this programme’s standard. The negative case is therefore weak at its strongest point: the proposed mirror survives.
