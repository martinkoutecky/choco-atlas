The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(C\), a chair order \(\succ\), a finite support \(T\) of ranking types, and a rational polytope \(P\) of possible mass distributions \(\mu\) over \(T\), construct a finite adaptive policy that queries unranked pairs and returns a ranking \(R\) that is \(W_\mu\)-unimprovable for every \(\mu\in P\). Each query reports the pairwise majority under \(\mu\), with ties resolved by a fixed public rule; \(W_\mu\)-unimprovability is defined using rankings reachable under \(W_\mu\) and alignment with \(\succ\).

The model it lives in

A continuum electorate with ranking types \(t\in T\) and masses \(\mu_t\); its pairwise majorities induce \(W_\mu\). The chair adaptively queries pairs and seeks a regret-free agenda policy for every possible society \(\mu\in P\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1, with Lemma 1 as supporting logic. It leaves the characterizations in Theorems 2–4 and Propositions 1–3 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can restrictions on \(P\) reduce the worst-case number of pairwise queries while preserving regret-freeness for every \(\mu\in P\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a mirror of the paper’s constructive regret-free agenda result, but there is an important qualification: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Theorems 1–4 and Propositions 1–3 prove properties of strategies and outcomes; none classifies a computational problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise. So there is no strictly qualifying computational anchor here.

The closest lead is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that insertion sort produces a \(W\)-efficient ranking for every majority will \(W\), and therefore a regret-free strategy. Here “efficient” means that every pair \(x\succ y\) on which the committee agrees, \(xWy\), is ranked \(x\) above \(y\). The implication to regret-freeness follows from Lemma 1: every \(W\)-efficient ranking is \(W\)-unimprovable. This is a constructive result, but “efficient” is an outcome property in the paper, not a claim about running time.

A plausible continuous-population problem is Continuous-Mass Regret-Free Agenda Construction. An instance consists of a finite alternative set \(C\), the chair’s strict order \(\succ\), an allowed set \(T\) of voter-ranking types, and a rational polytope \(P\) describing possible mass vectors on \(T\). The realized society is a hidden rational distribution \(\mu\in P\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of the population of type \(t\). A query on \(\{x,y\}\) reveals which alternative has more than half the mass preferring it; ties follow a fixed public priority order. The chair’s policy may depend on the query history, but not directly on the hidden \(\mu\).

The task is to output a finite adaptive policy that queries unranked pairs and eventually returns a ranking \(R\), such that for every \(\mu\in P\), \(R\) is \(W_\mu\)-unimprovable. That means no other ranking reachable under the induced majority relation \(W_\mu\) is strictly more aligned with \(\succ\). Insertion sort is a solution: the policy inserts alternatives in chair-preference order, using pairwise majority queries. It needs at most \(\binom{|C|}{2}\) queries. Thus the proposed task is tractable, with a policy constructible in polynomial time. That complexity statement is a consequence of applying the paper’s construction to this formulation; it is not a complexity theorem stated by the authors.

There is a credible high-multiplicity regime: a large membership organization or association deciding the order in which a small set of proposals will be considered. Members have complete rankings of those proposals, but many share the same ranking, so the society is represented by a small-support distribution over types. The chair controls the order of pairwise votes, and only learns the majority direction when a pair is voted on. This preserves the paper’s central interaction and objective, and the authors would plausibly recognize it as their agenda-setting problem with a continuum electorate. The population is what becomes continuous; the alternatives, votes, and final ranking remain discrete.

I would cover Theorem 1 only, with Lemma 1 as supporting logic. I would not treat Theorems 2–4 as additional computational anchors: their characterizations of regret-free strategies, errors, and priorities are substantive, but they are not complexity results. The proposed mirror is expected to be tractable. Further questions could ask whether restrictions on the allowed type support or mass vectors permit fewer queries, or how the answer changes under costly queries or strategic voting.

The weakness is that the continuous masses collapse to the signs of pairwise majority margins. Once those signs are known, the rest of the problem is exactly the paper’s tournament model, and insertion sort works regardless of the support size or the bit lengths of the masses. So this is a plausible continuous-population restatement of a named result, but a thin contribution to a programme about the complexity landscape of computational social choice: the paper supplies no hardness result whose behavior under continuization could be investigated.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor raised is Theorem 1, and it is not a computational-complexity result. It constructs insertion sort and proves an outcome property: the resulting ranking is \(W\)-efficient, hence regret-free. “Efficient” here does not mean polynomial time. The other named results characterize outcomes, behavior, and priorities; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem over a society.

The proposed continuous society is plausible: a large association could have many members sharing rankings, with their type masses inducing pairwise majorities. That is not a multiplicity objection. But the paper already takes the majority relation \(W\) as its operative object and allows every tournament \(W\). A distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types reaches the chair only through the pairwise-majority answers it induces. The proposed policy therefore works for every \(\mu\) because it works for every \(W\); support size \(\tau\) and mass bit length \(L\) never enter its computation. This is a possible continuous restatement, but it does not expose a complexity question about a continuous population.

Adding a known uncertainty polytope \(P\) does not change that for the proposed goal: insertion sort guarantees regret-freeness across all possible majority relations, so it need not use \(P\). To make \(P\) matter, one could optimize query cost, impose agenda restrictions, or specify a prior and cardinal utility. Those may be worthwhile research questions, but they add objectives or information absent from Theorem 1; they do not rescue it as a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

The negative case is strongest on the programme’s stated scope: there is no complexity landscape in this paper for continuization to chart. It is weaker if “computational result” is meant broadly enough to include any constructive strategy theorem. On that reading, the association scenario supports a modest continuous corollary, and I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile extension. The case against is that this corollary carries no computation over the population distribution, not that such a population regime is implausible.
