The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of house types \(\mathcal H\), a finite support \(T\) of complete types \(t=(e_t,\succ_t)\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), with supply \(q_h=\sum_{t:e_t=h}\mu_t\), determine whether a strict-core allocation exists and output one if it does. An allocation is \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}=q_h\). A blocking coalition selects assigned masses and feasibly reallocates their endowed supply so all selected mass weakly prefers its reassignment and positive mass strictly prefers it. Does the paper's existence characterization, construction, and uniqueness result hold for this population model, and with what running time in \(|\mathcal H|\), \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over complete house-type and preference types, with per-capita house supplies \(q_h\). The decision variable \(x_{t,h}\) records the mass of type \(t\) receiving house type \(h\); each individual still receives one house, while type-level allocations may be fractional. Strict-core blocking is defined using feasible reassignment of selected masses, with weak improvement throughout and strict improvement for positive mass.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does weighted HTTS characterize existence and produce the unique aggregate strict-core allocation under the stated positive-mass blocking convention for arbitrary real masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make a positive case for one narrow mirror: Strict-Core House-Swapping\(_\infty\), the continuous-population version of the paper’s strict-core existence and construction problem. The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that the strict core exists exactly when Algorithm 1 completes every house top trading segment with a feasible allocation; when it exists, the algorithm finds it, and that allocation is unique. The theorem also gives running time \(O(|H|^2+|H||I|)\).

Here is the version I would hand to a prover. Let \(\mathcal H\) be a finite set of house types. A voter type is a pair \(t=(e_t,\succ_t)\), consisting of an endowed house type \(e_t\in\mathcal H\) and a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) over \(\mathcal H\). The instance gives a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) such types and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). The supply of house type \(h\) is \(q_h=\sum_{t:e_t=h}\mu_t\).

An allocation is a matrix \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\), where \(x_{t,h}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned house type \(h\). It must satisfy \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}=q_h\) for every \(h\). The matrix records aggregate assignments: in an atomless population, each type’s mass can be partitioned so every individual receives one house, even when the type-level mass assigned to a house is fractional.

Call \(x\) blocked if there are selected masses \(z_{t,a}\), with \(0\le z_{t,a}\le x_{t,a}\), and a reassignment \(y_{t,a,h}\ge 0\) such that
\[ \sum_h y_{t,a,h}=z_{t,a}, \qquad y_{t,a,h}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ h\succeq_t a, \]
and, for every house type \(h\),
\[ \sum_{t,a} y_{t,a,h} = \sum_{t,a:e_t=h} z_{t,a}. \]
The last equation preserves the selected coalition’s endowment. The reassignment blocks if it strictly improves a positive mass, meaning \(\sum_{t,a,h:h\succ_t a}y_{t,a,h}>0\). The problem is to decide whether a strict-core allocation exists and, if so, output one. There is no scalar welfare objective in the paper’s problem; the decision criterion is feasibility and absence of a blocking coalition.

This is a natural population regime for a large housing exchange or relocation programme with standardized unit categories: many households, a relatively small set of unit types, and many households sharing both an endowed category and a ranking over categories. For example, a programme might have \(N\) households but only \(\tau\) materially distinct endowment-and-preference profiles. House supplies then scale with population size, while the model still gives each household an integral house. That preserves the paper’s problem: agents exchange endowments under preferences, and coalitions must be able to reallocate their own supply. It does not make outcomes fractional.

The paper’s HTTS construction suggests a direct algorithm for this mirror. On the remaining house types, draw an arc from \(h\) to the favorite remaining type of each positive-mass type endowed with \(h\). Choose a sink strongly connected component, assign its owners to their favorite remaining types, and check supply equals demand in each type. Remove the component and continue. With rational masses, those checks are exact. I would expect this version to be tractable, in Class A: the finite proof’s supply-and-demand argument appears to carry over from counts to masses, and the support list replaces the list of named agents. The natural parameters are \(|\mathcal H|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\); an implementation can process the support across at most \(|\mathcal H|\) rounds, giving polynomial arithmetic and bit complexity.

The mirror covers the paper’s strict-core existence and construction result, including the uniqueness claim at the aggregate-allocation level. It does not purport to mirror every result on housing markets with subjective indifferences. It also does not claim a new algorithmic payoff: Theorem 1 is already polynomial-time. The case here is that population proportions are a sensible way to state this particular exchange problem at scale, not that continuization turns a hard problem easy.

My weakest point is the continuum blocking convention. The paper requires at least one individual to improve strictly; in an atomless population, a literal singleton has zero mass and no resources to exchange. Requiring strict improvement by positive mass is the useful continuum analogue, but an author could reasonably ask for a formal equivalence or a more careful limit argument. The mirror is strongest when the programme really has repeated complete types \((e_t,\succ_t)\); if nearly every household has an idiosyncratic ranking, \(\tau\) approaches the population size and the high-multiplicity case loses force.

The next questions are whether the paper’s uniqueness and existence characterization hold exactly for arbitrary real masses as well as rational inputs, and how to define an approximate strict core when supply and demand match only up to a small mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest skeptical case is that Theorem 1 already has almost exactly the structure a mass version would need. HTTS operates on house types, and its key test is whether supply equals demand within a segment. Replace agent counts by masses, retain one preference-and-endowment type for each distinct cohort, and the same graph and balance checks give the proposed algorithm. For rational input masses, multiplying by a common denominator also produces a finite replicated market with the same HTTS checks. The continuous formulation therefore appears to require a weighted proof of an existing algorithm, rather than a new optimization method or a new complexity analysis.

That makes this a weak fit for ChoCo’s computational gap. There is no exponential-variable LP, pricing problem, or known complexity boundary to investigate here; the paper already gives a simple polynomial algorithm. The remaining modelling choice—allowing an atomless coalition to block when positive mass strictly improves—has a coherent formulation in the proponent’s version. A housing programme with many households sharing endowed unit categories and preference rankings is plausible, so the high-multiplicity objection does not rule it out either.

Those points weaken the case for prioritizing this mirror, but they do not defeat it. An exact correspondence between the discrete and mass models could itself be a worthwhile result, and the programme explicitly treats such identities as valid outcomes. I cannot make an honest case that no worthwhile mirror exists: Theorem 1’s strict-core existence and construction result survives as a narrow, tractable continuous mirror. The negative case is weak precisely where it needs to be strongest.
