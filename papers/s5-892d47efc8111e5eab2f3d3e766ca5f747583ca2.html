The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is plausible at the level of the market regime. A large regional hiring exchange could have many doctors and many comparable hospitals, with only a small number \(\tau\) of complete doctor choice types and hospital preference-and-quota types, where the population size \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\). A continuous version could assign mass to groups of doctors receiving integral bundles of contracts; the mass records what fraction of each type receives each bundle. That keeps each person’s bundle integral while making the population continuous. The authors could plausibly recognize this as a mass-market version of their matching problem, though aggregating their doctor-to-doctor envy condition would need careful treatment.

But the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 proves a lattice structure; Theorem 2 says the operator maps envy-free allocations to envy-free allocations, weakly improves doctors, and has stable allocations exactly as its fixed points; Theorem 3 proves stable allocations are nonempty and form a lattice; and Theorem 4 gives a join formula under the law of aggregate demand. These are structural results, not named complexity or algorithmic results. Theorem 2 is the nearest operational result: iterating the operator reaches stability in the finite market, but the paper gives no complexity bound. The conclusion identifies computing the full set of envy-free allocations as future work.

So there is a credible high-multiplicity setting, but no paper result that can honestly serve as the required computational anchor. I cannot give an anchor-backed continuous problem or classify its expected complexity without turning a new research question into a result the paper actually establishes. My weakest point is that the positive case stops at model plausibility: this paper does not make the computational claim the continuization programme is meant to examine.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is decisive against a *paper-anchored* mirror: the paper proves no named computational result. Theorem 1 is a lattice characterization; Theorem 2 gives properties of an operator and identifies its fixed points; Theorem 3 establishes a lattice of stable allocations; Theorem 4 gives a join formula under LAD. None states a complexity bound or analyzes an algorithm’s running time. Theorem 2 is the closest candidate, since iterating the operator reaches stability in a finite market. But its termination argument is only finiteness: it gives no bound in the input size, and that argument does not carry over directly to a continuum of allocations.

The strongest repair would be to ask how efficiently a high-multiplicity market can compute a stable completion from an envy-free allocation, or represent the full set of envy-free allocations. But that requires specifying a new computational problem and its input encoding; it is not a complexity result established by any of the named theorems. The structural mirrors fare no better for ChoCo’s stated purpose: a continuum version of the lattice or join formula remains a structural question. There is also a real modelling obstacle to preserving those operations: the join uses each named doctor’s choices across two allocations, while aggregate masses over bundles do not say which doctors’ bundles correspond across the allocations. Retaining that correspondence may require extra state beyond the type masses.

That is a strong case against treating this paper as an existing computational result ready to continuize. It does not justify the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The authors themselves name computing the full set of envy-free allocations as future work, and a large market with repeated doctor and hospital types is plausible. A computational study of that high-multiplicity problem could be worthwhile, even if it is a new algorithmic direction rather than a mirror of the paper’s theorems. The honest negative case is therefore weak at the universal claim: it establishes the absence of a qualifying computational anchor, not the absence of a promising continuous research question.
