The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a direct continuous version of the paper’s applicant based least squares ranking. But the paper has no named computational result to anchor it to, so this cannot meet the requested anchor requirement.

The closest named results are about axioms, not complexity: Proposition 3.1, proved here, says row sum violates size invariance while normalised row sum and least squares satisfy it; Proposition 3.3, proved here, says least squares satisfies bridge set independence and bridge set autonomy; Proposition 3.5, proved here, says row sum and normalised row sum violate bridge player independence. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifies its complexity. Definition 3.3 gives the least squares formulation, but it is not a numbered computational result.

A natural, author recognisable mirror would be Applicant-Mass Least-Squares Ranking. An instance has faculties \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the support of trimmed applicant-list types. A type is an applicant’s ordered list of distinct faculties after the paper’s duplicate removal; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the applicant population. Let \(B_t(i,j)=1\) when type \(t\) lists \(X_i\) ahead of \(X_j\), and \(0\) otherwise. Form \(A_\mu=\sum_t\mu_tB_t\), then \(R_\mu=A_\mu-A_\mu^\top\) and \(M_\mu=A_\mu+A_\mu^\top\). The task is to output the paper’s least squares scores \(q\), satisfying \(L(M_\mu)q=R_\mu\mathbf e\), with the paper’s zero sum normalisation on each connected component, and the resulting ranking.

The regime is a national admissions cohort: applicants are the many agents, and applicants with the same trimmed list are indistinguishable for this ranking method. The paper reports more than 150,000 revealed preferences and 179 faculties in 2016, so treating applicants as mass is natural. It does not report the number \(\tau\) of distinct list types, however, so it gives no evidence that the support is small.

This candidate question is tractable by aggregating the \(\tau\) matrices and solving a rational linear system; that is an inference from the formulation, not a result proved in the paper. It covers the computation of the least squares ranking, not the paper’s preference-recovery assumptions, axiomatic comparisons, or empirical conclusions. Its weakest point is substantial: the continuous input largely normalises the same applicant tallies the paper already aggregates, and the paper proves no computational claim about that task. So there is a plausible mirror, but no named complexity anchor supporting a continuization case of the kind requested.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is the missing target: the paper states no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 3.1 is about size invariance; Proposition 3.3 about bridge set independence and autonomy; Proposition 3.5 gives violations of bridge player independence. These are axiomatic claims about the scoring rule, not complexity results or algorithms for a population problem. They already quantify over real-valued preference matrices, so fractional masses would change how the matrix is constructed, not what these propositions say.

The proponent’s applicant-mass model is sensible: applicants with the same trimmed list can be grouped, and the paper’s large cohorts make that regime plausible. But the resulting task, \(\mu\mapsto A_\mu\mapsto q_\mu\), feeds the least-squares method the paper defines. It is a coherent continuous-input formulation, not a continuous counterpart of any of the three propositions. The paper takes the aggregate matrix as its ranking problem; it neither states a computational question about the distribution of applicant types nor proves a result about one.

The strongest repair would be a mass-robustness problem: how much applicant mass must move between list types to change a faculty’s rank? That fits the programme’s population focus and is closer to the paper’s discussion of robustness. But the paper’s robustness evidence compares aggregation conventions; it does not study population perturbations. A mass-shift problem needs a new intervention model, a cost on changes between partial lists, and a target such as changing a rank or winner. It may be a worthwhile follow-up, but it adds the computational question rather than mirroring a result the paper establishes.

So none of the anchors the proponent raised survives as a computational mirror. The negative case is strong for the paper as written, though the universal claim is less secure: the applicant-mass setting is natural, and a new robustness problem could be worth studying. The point is that this would be new work motivated by the paper, not a continuization of its named results.
