No result is covered: Theorems 1 and 2 concern convergence, Proposition 1 gives a cycle, and Appendix B discusses complexity observations rather than a named computational result established by the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best foothold is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large electorate with relatively few complete voter types, where a type records a ranking, an uncertainty model, and decision-rule parameters, and its mass is the fraction of voters of that type. But this only makes the population description sensible; it does not yet give a good mirror of the paper’s strategic question.

The closest numbered anchors are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in the paper. Theorem 1 says that with nested or partitioned decreasing belief functions, voters using the pessimistic rule or the stated mixed rules with \(\alpha\) large enough converge to an equilibrium. Theorem 2 says that with a decreasing belief function around the true score, voters using a Hurwicz criterion with \(\alpha>\tfrac12\) converge to an equilibrium. Proposition 1, also proved there, gives a ten-voter, four-candidate cycle showing that convergence is not guaranteed under a uniform pignistic criterion on a size-one \(\ell_1\) neighborhood. These are results about dynamics, not computational complexity classifications. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.15786)

There is a serious obstacle to continuizing those dynamics: in an atomless population, one voter’s ballot change has zero mass and cannot change the plurality winner, so the strategic incentive the paper studies disappears. Giving a positive mass of voters a joint ballot change could preserve an effect, but that turns the action into collective movement rather than an individual strategic vote. That is the weakest point, and it makes this a poor positive mirror.

So, under the requested anchor rule, this paper has no named computational result to anchor. Appendix B discusses \(\#\mathrm P\)-hard aggregation under pignistic criteria and polynomial-time flow methods for pessimistic criteria, but presents these as prior-work complexity observations, not as a numbered result established in the paper. I therefore can’t honestly give a continuous problem per qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that none of the three anchors is a computational result. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 establish convergence for particular belief and decision rules; Proposition 1 gives a finite cycle under a pignistic rule. They do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Appendix B discusses complexity results from prior work and points toward future questions, but does not establish a numbered computational result of its own. Since ChoCo’s target is the computational landscape of continuous-population problems, these results offer no direct target. A continuum model of the convergence arguments would be a study of dynamics, which is outside that scope.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity electorate here: many voters sharing a ranking, ballot, uncertainty model, and decision-rule parameters. That makes a distribution over voter types sensible. But it does not preserve the strategic action in the theorems. In an atomless population, one voter changing ballot changes the aggregate score by zero. The winner is therefore unchanged, the utility gain is zero in every score state, and the paper’s strict-improvement rule yields no moves. The resulting “convergence” is vacuous: every state is already an equilibrium.

A finite-population approximation does not fix this generically. A single ballot changes a normalized score by \(1/n\), so it matters only at or very near a plurality tie. Under regular uncertainty over normalized scores, the probability of being pivotal vanishes as \(n\) grows. Keeping positive probability on exact pivotal states can retain finite-voter incentives, but then the limit depends on discrete boundary information that the type-mass distribution alone has discarded. That is a singular, finite-size effect, not a robust continuous-population version of Theorem 1 or 2.

One could instead let a positive mass of a type switch ballots together. That avoids the zero-impact problem, but changes the actor from an individual voter to a coordinated bloc. Its incentives and update process are not specified by the paper’s unilateral decision rules, so any convergence theorem would concern a newly chosen model. The same issue defeats Proposition 1 as a mirror: its cycle is built from particular one-voter changes. In an atomless model those changes have no effect; scaling them up to cohort moves does not carry over the stated cycle or its conclusion.

The negative case is strongest against these specific anchors, not against every conceivable follow-on project. A carefully defined bloc-dynamics model, or a hybrid model retaining some finite pivotal voters, might be worth studying. But either would need new strategic assumptions and new computational questions. The paper’s own results do not supply those, and treating its uncertainty distributions over score vectors as continuization of the society would change the wrong object. I therefore would not count this paper as a worthwhile continuous-population mirror for ChoCo, while conceding that the broader universal claim—no plausible scenario could ever make one worthwhile—is more than the text can establish.
