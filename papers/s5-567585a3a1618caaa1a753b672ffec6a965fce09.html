Why no mirror

Theorem 60 and Proposition 62 are genuine algorithmic query-complexity results, but their input is an unknown tournament accessed through oracle calls. With a ChoCo input \(\mu\), the majority tournament can be constructed directly; the proposed panel-oracle version adds a hidden access model that the anchors do not study. The panel is a plausible application, but it does not make these results computational results over the specified continuous society.

What the mirror covers

The proposed reading covers Theorem 60's general-tournament termination bound and Proposition 62's transitive top-one query bound; it does not transfer the empirical reranking results or Conjecture 63 into computation on a distribution input.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is to treat the tournament as the collective preference of a large evaluator population. Take a fixed set \(C\) of \(n\) candidates or documents. A voter type is a complete ranking of \(C\), and the judging society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) such rankings. For each pair \(a,b\), direct an edge \(a\to_\mu b\) when more than half the mass ranks \(a\) above \(b\); assume no pairwise majority ties. The resulting majority tournament is the society’s aggregate preference graph.

The regime is a large crowd or evaluator panel with many people but relatively few distinct ballots: for example, a substantial crowdsourced evaluation panel whose members fall into a few hundred ranking types. A query on up to \(k\) candidates elicits the induced tournament on those candidates from the panel. The decision is to return \(m\) candidates with smallest in-reach in the full majority tournament, breaking ties within a strongly connected component as the paper permits. The output must satisfy the paper’s top-\(m\) rank condition. This keeps the paper’s actual objective—top-\(m\) selection by tournament reachability—while giving its tournament a plausible continuous-population source. The paper explicitly names crowdsourced preference judgments as an application, which helps make this recognizable as their problem.

My lead anchor is Theorem 60, proved in this paper: for any underlying tournament on \(n\) vertices, Algorithm 1 terminates in at most \(\binom n2\) rounds. Each nonterminal round reveals at least one previously unknown edge, and there are only \(\binom n2\) edges to reveal. In the panel mirror, this gives a worst-case query bound for identifying the top-\(m\) candidates under the majority tournament, including when that tournament has cycles. This is the strongest anchor because it covers the paper’s general, non-transitive setting rather than only a special case.

A second, narrower anchor is Proposition 62, also proved here: when the underlying tournament is transitive and \(m=1\), Algorithm 2 identifies the top vertex in at most \(\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{k-1}\right\rceil\) queries. Each query eliminates at least \(k-1\) candidates from being the top vertex. In the mirror, this is the top-candidate problem for a panel whose majority relation is transitive. It is a useful, sharper result for that regime, though weaker as a general mirror than Theorem 60. The paper offers no named NP-hardness or other conventional complexity-class anchor; these are query-complexity results, and I would not present them as Turing-complexity classifications.

I would call the explicit-profile problem Continuous-panel top-\(m\) tournament ranking: given \(C\), a rational support \(\{(t,\mu_t)\}_{t=1}^{\tau}\), and \(m\), construct the majority tournament and output a valid top-\(m\) set under in-reach ranking. Its expected direction is tractable: sum the masses for each candidate pair, then compute the tournament’s strongly connected components and their order. This takes polynomial time in \(n,\tau\), and the rational bit length \(L\). In the operational elicitation version, where the panel is accessed through \(k\)-wise queries, Theorem 60 gives the general query bound, and Proposition 62 gives the transitive top-one bound. The further question is whether support structure or mass sizes allow substantially sharper query bounds.

The main weakness is the access model. ChoCo’s central model supplies \(\mu\) explicitly, while BLITZRANK counts queries to an unknown tournament. If the algorithm has the full support and masses, it can calculate every majority edge directly, so the query bounds no longer capture the central computational cost. The paper’s query problem becomes a close mirror only when the society is encountered through costly elicitation. I think the underlying social-choice question remains credible—the panel generates exactly the kind of tournament the paper ranks—but this access mismatch limits how much of the paper’s algorithmic contribution the continuous mirror preserves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The main problem with the proposed mirror is the access model. ChoCo’s society is an input: its support and rational masses are given. Theorem 60 instead takes an unknown tournament as its object and counts calls to an oracle that reveals edges. The theorem contains no voter types or masses, and its bound is in \(n\), the number of alternatives. If the ranking distribution \(\mu\) is given, its pairwise majorities determine the whole tournament directly; if \(\mu\) is hidden and a panel must be queried, the problem is query complexity over an oracle, not computation on the continuous society ChoCo specifies.

The stronger panel formulation makes the connection as plausible as possible: let a large panel have ranking types distributed according to \(\mu\), and let a query return the exact majority tournament on the selected candidates. Theorem 60 then applies, including when the majority relation has cycles. But its proof counts newly revealed edges. It works for every tournament, regardless of whether that tournament came from a panel, a single LLM, or another source. The population supplies an interpretation of the oracle; it contributes no computational structure to the theorem. Proposition 62 has the same gap. A transitive majority relation lets its elimination argument find the top candidate in \(\left\lceil (n-1)/(k-1)\right\rceil\) queries, but the bound concerns distinct candidate identities and is independent of \(\mu\), its support size, and its masses.

That makes the proponent’s distinction decisive: their explicit-profile problem is continuous, but these query bounds concern a different interface. The claimed polynomial computation from explicit \(\mu\) is not what either named result establishes. A mirror about costly querying of a hidden crowd could be worthwhile, but it would need to make the crowd’s access model and the role of its type distribution part of the problem. The paper’s theorems alone do not do that.

This is the strongest negative case, and it has a real limit: a crowdsourced panel with a fixed distribution over rankings is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and its majority tournament is exactly the kind of object BLITZRANK can rank. Under a broad, application-level reading, Theorem 60 survives as a useful query guarantee for that panel. The case against works only under ChoCo’s stricter requirement that the continuous society itself be the computational input.
