The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. It gives no complexity classification, algorithmic theorem, or hardness result to quote as an anchor. Sections 2 and 3 propose fairness notions and point toward scheduling techniques; the paper says it answers RQ1 and leaves platform evaluation and redesign (RQ2 and RQ3) for future work. The cited scheduling results are prior work, not results proved here. So there are no anchors, and I cannot honestly supply one continuous problem per anchor.

There is nevertheless a plausible *follow-on* problem latent in the paper’s RQ3. Consider a large freelance platform with many providers and recurring job requests. A provider type records all relevant shared attributes—skills, location, availability, benefit, and compatibility with job types. Let \(\mu_p\) be the fraction of providers of type \(p\), and let \(d_{rq}\) be the mass of requests of job type \(q\) in round \(r\). The regime is credible when both populations are large but these type supports are modest.

For a finite, announced horizon, let \(x_{rpq}\) be the mass of type-\(p\) providers assigned to type-\(q\) requests in round \(r\). Given per-match customer utility \(U_{pq}\), provider benefit \(B_{pq}\), and per-provider capacity \(\kappa_p\), require \(\sum_p x_{rpq}\le d_{rq}\) and \(\sum_q x_{rpq}\le \kappa_p\mu_p\). Define realized benefit per provider of type \(p\) as \(A_p=\frac{1}{\mu_p}\sum_{r,q}B_{pq}x_{rpq}\), and deserved benefit per provider as \(D_p=\sum_{r,q}d_{rq}U_{pq}B_{pq}\). The proposed problem is to find a feasible schedule maximizing \(\sum_{r,p,q}U_{pq}x_{rpq}\), subject to \(A_p=\lambda D_p\) for every type and one common \(\lambda\ge0\). The solution is the mass-allocation schedule \(x\) and its achieved utility. This makes provider and request populations continuous; it is not merely continuity in time or in the outcome space.

For this explicit finite-type, finite-horizon formulation, I would expect tractability: the constraints, including the common-factor fairness condition, form a rational linear program. That is a new formalization prompted by the paper, not a result the paper establishes or a classification of its original online matching problem. Further questions include whether the offline allocation can be implemented online, how to round it to indivisible matches while preserving fairness, and how complexity changes when the type space is implicit or grows.

The strongest point for this lead is its direct connection to the paper’s proposed actual/deserved-benefit fairness criterion and its redesign question. Its weakest point is substantial: the paper’s platform makes sequential, indivisible matches, while this formulation plans over a known horizon and allocates fractional mass. The paper itself flags the fluid assumption in processor scheduling as unrealistic for discrete matching events. Authors might recognize this as a high-volume fluid redesign problem, but the required anchor-backed case for a continuous mirror is absent.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no paper results to anchor a continuous mirror on. This is a position paper: it proposes fairness criteria, maps them to processor scheduling, and explicitly leaves platform redesign (RQ3) for future work. It states no computational problem with a complexity result, algorithm, or hardness theorem. The scheduling algorithms it cites are prior work. On the assignment’s terms, that is decisive: the proponent supplied no continuous question for a named result.

The proposed finite-horizon LP is a plausible follow-on, but it is new work prompted by RQ3, not a mirror of something this paper establishes. More seriously, its constraint \(A_p=\lambda D_p\) gives proportionality at the *type-average* level. The paper’s criterion is about every provider’s actual benefit relative to their deserved benefit. A schedule could meet the type-average constraint while repeatedly serving some providers and starving others. A faithful version would need to track providers’ changing benefit balances, along with availability and incoming requests. That becomes a dynamic scheduling or control problem whose state and assumptions the paper never specifies.

There are credible high-volume regimes for this domain, so “providers are individuals” is not a sound blanket objection. But aggregating them into types changes the fairness guarantee; retaining their histories may erase the small-support model that makes the aggregation attractive. One could study that trade-off, but it would be a newly formulated research problem. The proposed LP shows a route to one simplified fluid redesign, not that this paper contains a worthwhile continuous computational mirror.

So the negative case is strong for the paper as presented: it has no computational result to mirror, and the offered candidate is an extrapolation that changes the fairness question. The broader claim that *no scenario* could yield a worthwhile continuous study is weaker; I would not defend that universal claim, only that this paper does not establish such a mirror.
