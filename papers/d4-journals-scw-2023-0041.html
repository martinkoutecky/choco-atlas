What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive opening is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: on a rich domain, for arbitrary numbers of agents and objects, the minimum-price Walrasian rule is the unique ex-post revenue-optimal rule among desirable rules satisfying no subsidy. Theorem 2, also proved here, gives the corresponding uniqueness result when the domain contains all positive-income-effect preferences and the rule satisfies no bankruptcy. Corollary 1 specializes that result to the classical domain.

But none is an eligible anchor for a ChoCo computational mirror. These are mechanism-characterization results: they identify which rule is revenue-optimal. They do not assert that a computational problem is in \(P\), hard, or otherwise classified. The paper studies a finite set of agents and objects, while “continuous” refers to preferences over bundles with real-valued payments—not to a continuous population.

A population version is imaginable: represent agents by a distribution over complete preference types, scale object supplies with population mass, and study revenue-maximizing allocations and payments. But that would be a new economic characterization question, not a computational question anchored by a result in this paper. It would also need a careful account of fractional type-level assignments and what strategy-proofness means when any one agent has negligible mass.

So I cannot honestly state a continuous computational problem per anchor, or predict its complexity class. The paper has no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that Theorems 1 and 2 contain no complexity classification. That leaves the affirmative case weaker than one built on a theorem already proving an algorithmic bound, but it does not rule out a worthwhile computational mirror. Each theorem identifies a concrete optimum to compute: the minimum-price Walrasian outcome that maximizes ex-post revenue within a specified class of rules.

For Theorem 1, take a large school-seat, licence, or housing market where many people share a complete preference type. Represent the society by rational masses \(\mu_t\) over \(\tau\) types, and scale supplies of each object category with population size, giving rational per-capita capacities \(q_a\). A type can be encoded by a rational piecewise-linear utility representation over object-payment bundles. The computational question is: given these types, masses, and capacities, compute the minimum Walrasian prices, a feasible allocation of mass to objects, and the resulting revenue. Its parameters include \(m,\tau\), the encoding length \(L\), and the capacities. Theorem 1 supplies the target: on a rich domain, this is the outcome of the unique ex-post revenue-optimal rule under the stated axioms and no-subsidy constraint.

Fractional type-level allocations fit the model: they can represent portions of a type group receiving different integral bundles, with splits among indifferent agents. Individual incentive constraints can still be stated for each agent: at the common prices, truthful demand must be at least as good as the bundle obtained by reporting another type. Whether the finite-market uniqueness and incentive properties carry over, and how efficiently the mass outcome can be computed, are questions to settle rather than assumptions to make.

Theorem 2 gives a related computational question on positive-income-effect preferences, with the no-bankruptcy bound scaled to population mass. The task is again to compute the minimum-price revenue-optimal outcome, now when individual subsidies are allowed subject to an aggregate deficit bound. This tests whether the same computational treatment handles income effects and aggregate budget constraints; it does not need to be sold as a separate kind of continuization.

The real limitations are specific. The paper leaves preference representations unrestricted, so its model alone does not define a Turing-machine input; a mirror must choose a finite encoding, such as piecewise-linear utilities. Scaling supplies also changes the finite market’s object regime, though it is the natural co-modification for a population limit. And a single agent’s report has negligible effect on aggregate prices in the continuum, so the finite-to-continuous incentive dictionary needs careful work. Those are substantial research choices, not reasons that every plausible mirror fails.

If ChoCo accepts only papers that already state a complexity theorem, this paper misses that entry criterion. Under the broader stated aim—asking computational questions about continuous societies—Theorem 1 gives a recognizable optimization target and a plausible high-multiplicity setting. The case for a mirror is therefore real, though it rests on a newly specified computation problem rather than a complexity result already proved in the paper.
