What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Theorem 2’s next-price mediation result; it does not cover the paper’s general characterization, other auction classes, or impossibility results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a limit on the positive case: the paper has no named theorem that classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized. The closest anchor is Theorem 2, proved here. It is a constructive implementation result, not a complexity result.

Theorem 2 says that in every next-price position auction, there is an individually rational mediator that implements the VCG outcome function. In the paper’s setting, there are \(n>m\) bidders, \(m\) positions with strictly decreasing click-through rates, and positive valuations. The mediator’s full-participation bids are built from the VCG payments: the bidder in position \(j\), for \(2\le j\le m\), bids the VCG payment for position \(j-1\); the top bidder bids slightly above the first-position VCG payment; and the bidders below the last position receive bids arranged around the last-position VCG payment. If only a strict subset uses the mediator, it passes their reports through as bids. The theorem establishes that truthful reporting and using the mediator is an ex-post equilibrium, including against direct bids, and that a participant’s utility stays nonnegative even when others bypass the mediator. The paper gives the mediator and proves these properties.

The most credible mirror is Continuum Next-Price Mediation. Its regime is a large market of near-identical advertising opportunities: \(n\) advertisers and \(m\) available positions both grow, with \(m/n\to q\in(0,1)\). Advertisers share a small set of per-click valuation types, so the type distribution \(\mu\) has support \(\tau\ll n\). Mass means the fraction of advertisers of each valuation type. Position quality becomes a decreasing click-through curve \(\alpha(x)\), where \(x\) is an advertiser’s rank by bid and only ranks below \(q\) receive a position. This is plausible for a platform-level pool of comparable inventory, though less natural for one ordinary search-results page, which has few slots.

For a precise computational version, take an instance consisting of rational \(q\), a piecewise-linear click-through curve \(\alpha\) that drops to zero after rank \(q\), and a finite rational valuation distribution \(\mu\). A solution is a measurable mediator policy: for every measurable set \(S\) of advertisers who authorize it, and their reported values, the policy specifies the bids it submits for them. The induced continuum next-price auction orders advertisers by bid, allocates click-through rate \(\alpha(x)\) at rank \(x<q\), and charges the bid immediately below that rank per click. The target is continuum VCG: if \(v_\mu(y)\) is the value at rank \(y\), its per-click price at a winning rank \(x\) is
\[ P_\mu(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha(x)}\int_{(x,1]}v_\mu(y)\,(-d\bar\alpha(y)), \]
where \(\bar\alpha\) is the click-through curve extended by zero beyond the supply cutoff. The question is to construct a mediator whose truthful full-participation outcome equals this allocation and price, whose truthful strategy resists both false reports and direct bids, and whose participants remain individually rational for every \(S\). In the continuum, an individual’s deviation has zero effect on the aggregate distribution, but it can still change that individual’s rank and payment; defining rank by inserting the advertiser into the reported population preserves the incentive question.

The natural bid schedule is the continuum limit of the paper’s one-rank shift: the bid immediately below rank \(x\) should equal \(P_\mu(x)\). For a type distribution with finite support and a piecewise-linear click-through curve, the required integrals break into finitely many pieces. I would expect this synthesis problem to be Class A, with a finite exact description computable from the support and curve. The bridge to the discrete auction is also concrete: VCG payment sums become the displayed integral as the empirical type distributions and position curves converge. Further questions include the exact encoding and running time of that construction, and which other payment rules admit an analogous continuous characterization.

This case covers Theorem 2 alone. Its weakest point is the regime change: the paper studies finitely many positions, while this mirror scales the supply of positions with the advertiser population. Also, in a nonatomic population unilateral deviations leave the distribution unchanged, so the mediator’s strategic task may be weaker than in the finite game. The individual rank-insertion model keeps deviations meaningful, but authors might still regard the resulting market as a standard nonatomic auction extension rather than a continuization of their central contribution. No discrete hardness result in the paper anchors a hardness-dissolution claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest objection is that Theorem 2 proves an implementation result, not a computational one. It gives a mediator and proves that truthful participation is an ex-post equilibrium with individual rationality. It does not define an algorithmic problem or analyze its complexity. The same is true of the paper’s stronger characterizations and impossibility results. A continuous version could be worthwhile mechanism-design research, but it would not fill ChoCo’s stated gap in the computational complexity of population interventions.

The proposed market is the strongest version of the mirror, and scaling both advertisers and positions so \(m/n\to q\) avoids the objection that only a vanishing fraction of the population gets a slot. With finite-support valuations, a click-through curve, and continuum VCG prices, the target has a sensible aggregate description. That establishes a plausible continuous auction question. It does not, by itself, make the question computational: the paper asks whether a mediator sustains particular individual deviations, while the proposed input is a distribution over types.

There is a sharper mismatch in the paper’s actual mediator. With full participation, Mediator 1 submits specially constructed VCG-equivalent bids. For every strict subset of participants, it simply forwards their reports as bids. In an atomless society, full participation and participation by everyone except one advertiser have the same distribution \(\mu\). A policy determined only by that distribution cannot distinguish the two cases. Keeping the paper’s distinction requires a tagged individual or information about membership changes of measure zero. Rank insertion can define that individual’s auction payoff, but it does not tell an aggregate mediator when to switch between those two policies. An alternative policy might avoid the switch, but its incentive and individual-rationality properties would be a new continuum implementation result.

A more computational formulation could encode payment rules and ask which admit such a continuum mediator. That would be a newly chosen decision problem, rather than a complexity question established by this paper’s results. My negative case is therefore strongest on fit with ChoCo’s programme and weaker as a claim that the continuous auction extension lacks research value. If the programme includes continuum mechanism design, the proponent’s scaled-market scenario remains credible.
