Why no mirror

The three-type cohort version is a recognizable restatement of the division domain, so the population mirror is plausible. But Theorems 4 and 5, like the other results, establish axiomatic or structural properties rather than a computational task, algorithm, or hardness result; the paper fails bit (a).

fails bit a — no named computational result to mirror

What the mirror covers

The cohort restatement translates the paper's choice and revealed-preference results, including Theorems 4 and 5. It covers no computational result because the paper states none.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is a high-multiplicity division setting. Suppose many people share one of finitely many entitlement types \(t\in T\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of the population of each type. If type \(t\) has per-person minimum entitlement \(a_t\), a division gives that type a per-person amount \(y_t\), subject to
\[ y_t\ge a_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t y_t=1,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t<1. \]
A choice rule maps entitlement levels to feasible divisions. This is a coherent population-continuous version of the paper’s setting: with fixed masses, the type totals \(z_t=\mu_t y_t\) lie on a simplex and have minimums \(\mu_t a_t\). Large cohorts with shared entitlements make a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror to. The closest result is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: for \(n=3\), if \(C=C_\Pi\) for a weakly monotonic \(N\)-path, then \(C\) satisfies the strong axiom of revealed preference if and only if it has no revealed-preference cycle of length three. This is a rationalizability characterization, not a complexity result. Theorem 4, also proved here, says that for every \(K\), there is a choice function with a cycle of length \(K+1\) and no shorter cycle. The other numbered theorems characterize choice rules. None establishes a problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

The cohort model is therefore a possible extension of the paper’s division problem, but it does not supply the requested continuous problem per anchor. Turning Theorem 5 into one would require adding a finite representation of the choice rule and a computational task that the paper neither specifies nor studies. The weakest point is decisive: the paper’s results are axiomatic, while the population-cohort interpretation would be new. The simplex is an allocation domain, not a distribution describing the society; fractional allocations alone do not make the paper a continuous-population computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cohort interpretation is coherent. With three positive-mass entitlement groups, let \(\mu_i\) be group \(i\)’s population share, \(a_i\) its per-person minimum, and \(y_i\) its per-person allocation. Setting \(x_i=\mu_i y_i\) and \(b_i=\mu_i a_i\) turns feasibility into \(x_i\ge b_i\) and \(\sum_i x_i=1\). This is a bijective change of coordinates on the paper’s simplex domain. It preserves continuity and revealed-preference cycles, so Theorems 4 and 5 carry over to the cohort model. That is a legitimate population story, not a mistake about fractional outcomes.

But those theorems are axiomatic results, not computational ones. Theorem 5 characterizes rationalizability for choice functions; Theorem 4 constructs functions with cycles of specified lengths. Theorems 1–3 characterize classes of choice functions. Their input is an arbitrary continuous map \(C\) over an uncountable domain, with no finite encoding specified. Continuity alone does not make that map computable. The paper defines no finite-instance decision problem, algorithm, running-time bound, or hardness result for the cohort model—or for its original finite-agent model.

The strongest repair would restrict the choice rules to, say, rational piecewise-linear paths, specify their encoding, and ask whether the represented rule is rationalizable. That could be a worthwhile new computational question. But the representation and task would have to be added from outside the paper; they are not a computational mirror of Theorem 5. The same gap applies to Theorem 4. The cohort change of coordinates confirms that these results survive population weighting, but it does not supply the missing computational problem. I would reject this paper as a ChoCo computational mirror, while leaving open whether a separately formulated finite-representation project is worth pursuing.
