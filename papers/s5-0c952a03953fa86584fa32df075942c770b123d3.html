The continuous mirror question

Given \(M\), rational conditional type masses \(\mu^\theta\) on \(T=\{U,I_1,\ldots,I_M\}\), prior \(\pi\), and threshold \(q\), apply the Proposition \(1\) equilibrium rule to a uniformly random ordering of the high-multiplicity population, compute the limiting expected correct-action fraction \(W_d=\sum_\theta \pi(\theta)\nu_d^\theta(\theta)\), and decide whether \(W_d\ge q\).

The model it lives in

A finite-\(M\) sequential Bayesian model with types \(U,I_j\), mass vector \(\mu^\theta\), discrete actions \(\{\bot,1,\ldots,M\}\), random order, and equilibrium computation over limiting action masses \(\nu^\theta\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow but genuine mirror for equilibrium and welfare computation. It is not a complexity-class mirror: Banerjee’s paper contains no named theorem saying that a problem is NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar. Its named results are equilibrium characterizations. The best anchor is therefore Proposition 1.

Proposition 1, proved in this paper rather than cited from elsewhere, states that under Assumptions A, B, and C there is a unique Nash-equilibrium decision rule \(D\). The rule is:

  • The first agent follows her signal if she has one and chooses \(i=0\) otherwise.
  • For \(k>1\), an informed agent follows her own signal if either her signal matches an option already chosen, or no nonzero option has yet been chosen more than once.
  • If some non-highest previously chosen option has been chosen more than once, an informed agent follows that option, unless her signal matches another previously chosen option.
  • If the highest previously chosen option has been chosen more than once and no other nonzero option has been chosen more than once, she follows the highest option, unless her signal matches an option already chosen.
  • An uninformed agent chooses \(i=0\) exactly when everyone before her chose \(i=0\); otherwise she follows the highest previously chosen option, unless another nonzero option has been chosen more than once, in which case she follows that repeated option.

The printed proof is partly informal; Banerjee says that a complete formal proof was available in an earlier version. The result is nevertheless established as a result of this paper.

My lead mirror would be Continuous Herd-Equilibrium Evaluation.

Keep the alternatives discrete. Let \(A=\{\bot,1,\ldots,M\}\), where \(\bot\) is the paper’s default action \(i=0\), and let the hidden correct option be \(\theta\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\). A type is

\[ T=\{U\}\cup\{I_j:j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\}, \]

where \(U\) means “uninformed” and \(I_j\) means “informed with signal \(j\).” The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\). In the symmetric signal model, conditional on \(\theta\),

\[ \mu_U=1-\alpha,\qquad \mu_{I_\theta}=\alpha\beta,\qquad \mu_{I_j}=\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)}{M-1}\quad(j\neq\theta). \]

More generally, the instance may provide an arbitrary rational conditional mass vector \(\mu^\theta\), together with a prior \(\pi\) over \(\theta\). Agents of the same type have the same information and payoff; there are \(n\mu_t^\theta\) copies of type \(t\) in the \(n\)-agent realization. Their order is uniformly random, exactly as in Banerjee’s model.

The continuous instance is the mass vector \(\mu\), not \(n\). Each agent still chooses one discrete option. A decision rule is a map

\[ d(h,t)\in A, \]

from the ordered public history \(h\) and the agent’s private type \(t\) to an action. The solution is the unique equilibrium rule \(d\) corresponding to Proposition 1, together with its induced limiting action masses

\[ \nu^\theta_d(a) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} \mathbb{E}_\theta \left[ \#\{r\le n:a_r=a\} \right]. \]

The welfare objective is the limiting expected fraction of agents choosing correctly,

\[ W_d = z\sum_{\theta}\pi(\theta)\nu^\theta_d(\theta). \]

Thus the concrete computational question is: given \(M\), the signal likelihoods or mass vectors \(\mu^\theta\), the prior \(\pi\), and a rational threshold \(q\), compute the equilibrium action rule and decide whether \(W_d\ge q\).

This is a plausible continuization because it preserves the paper’s essential structure: a large population, finitely many repeated information types, private noisy signals, sequential actions, public observation of actions but not signals, and a payoff from selecting the correct option. The paper’s continuum of alternatives is not being used as the mirror; alternatives remain finite. The continuous object is the population distribution.

A natural regime is a very large market of customers choosing among \(M\) restaurants or technologies. Their private information is coarse—“no signal,” or a signal naming one of the \(M\) options—so \(M+1\) types occur with macroscopic frequencies while \(n\gg M+1\). This is exactly a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s setting, not an idiosyncratic reinterpretation.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A for fixed \(M\). Proposition 1 reduces the strategic problem to a finite collection of public-history summaries involving repeated options, the current highest option, and signal matches. A dynamic program over those summaries can work with masses rather than individual identities. If \(M\) is part of the input, the number of possible histories or option subsets may create a harder evaluation problem; the paper gives no basis for claiming polynomial time in unrestricted \(M\). The natural further questions are the exact complexity as \(M\) varies, quantitative convergence from finite \(n\) to \(\mu\), and the optimal policy for hiding the first few actions to prevent inefficient herding.

The weakest point is serious: Banerjee’s mechanism is triggered by the first few individually observed actions. In a completely nonatomic simultaneous population, every individual has zero mass and cannot create such an informational cascade. My mirror therefore retains a finite ordered “seed” of individually visible decisions and continuizes the high-multiplicity population around it. That is a defensible boundary-layer interpretation of the paper, but a referee who insists on a wholly atomless sequential process could reject it. The positive claim should therefore be narrow: Proposition 1 admits a credible high-multiplicity continuous-population analogue, not every possible mean-field interpretation of herd behavior.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strong, and its decisive point is that Banerjee’s paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Proposition 1 is an equilibrium decision-rule characterization, not a theorem about complexity, algorithms, optimization, approximation, or parameterized computation. Proposition 2 has the same status. The numbered welfare claims are probabilistic economic results, not computational results. Thus “Continuous Herd-Equilibrium Evaluation” would be a newly invented computational problem inspired by the paper, not a continuization of one of its computational contributions. Under ChoCo’s anchor rule, that is already fatal.

Even granting that new problem, the proposed mirror does not preserve the paper’s essential population-level object. Herding is triggered by a few individually observed early actions. A type distribution \(\mu\) records how much mass has each signal, but it does not itself record the ordered public history on which later posteriors depend. Under the proponent’s random-order construction, the first \(O(1)\) agents remain a finite random seed even as \(n\to\infty\). The limiting action distribution is therefore not determined by \(\mu\) alone: it depends on the particular early sample and can remain random. The purported continuum is a continuum tail attached to a discrete cascade trigger.

The only rescue is the proposed “boundary layer”: retain a finite sequence of individually visible pioneers and let the rest of the population be high-multiplicity. But then the entire herd externality is carried by the retained discrete pioneers. Their order, identities, and observation protocol remain part of the model, while the continuum population merely amplifies their signal. This is a hybrid finite-seed stochastic process, not a society represented by a continuous distribution. If instead one makes the whole process genuinely nonatomic, each individual action changes zero population mass, so no action can create the repeated-choice event that drives Proposition 1. If one scales the publicly visible prefix to a positive population mass, the public information becomes an aggregate frequency rather than the paper’s finite history, and one has defined a new mean-field information model.

There is also a type-space dilemma. In Banerjee’s actual model, an informed type includes an exact signal \(i'\in[0,1]\). Generic signal types have zero mass, so the exact model has no high multiplicity of complete types. The proposed repair discretizes the alternatives and signals into \(M\) values. That may be a sensible restaurant model, but it changes the equality and repetition events on which the equilibrium rule is built. It is not merely expressing the same population at higher multiplicity; it creates a finite-signal cascade model that the paper explicitly does not analyze formally.

The same objection defeats stronger variants based on Proposition 2 or the welfare intervention. The threshold \(k^*\), the decision rule \(D^*\), and the proposal to hide the first \(r\) decisions all concern a finite ordered prefix. Keeping \(r\) finite gives the same discrete-seed hybrid; making \(r\) a positive fraction of the population changes the information structure. There is no canonical population-continuous limit in which the paper’s mechanism survives unchanged.

A finite-\(M\) high-multiplicity version may be an interesting new economics or stochastic-process project. It is not, however, a worthwhile ChoCo mirror of this paper: the source contributes no computational result, and its central phenomenon is generated by order-sensitive individual events that either remain discrete or disappear under population continuization.
