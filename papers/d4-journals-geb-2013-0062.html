What the mirror covers

The paper covers existence and axiomatic characterizations of the generalized Nash solution on classes of choice problems; it provides no computational result for a continuous-population mirror to cover.

The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make an anchored positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a related complexity classification. The central Theorem 3.1, proved here, characterizes the Nash solution on uniformly \(p\)-smooth choice problems by seven axioms. Corollary 2.7, also proved here, establishes that this solution exists on that domain. Neither is a computational result.

The obstacle is also about scope. The paper’s feasible alternatives are continuous sets \(P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^N\), but the set of players \(N\) is finite; it does not model a population distribution over agent types. A continuous-agent bargaining model might be worth studying, but it would not mirror a named computational result of this paper. Under the requested anchor rule, there is therefore no valid per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the paper’s contents: Theorem 3.1 characterizes the Nash solution by axioms, and Corollary 2.7 proves existence. Neither gives a complexity result. That is the strongest reason to keep this paper out of a programme mapping computational continuizations. A complexity analysis needs an input representation for \(P\), yet the paper allows arbitrary feasible sets. Choosing inequalities, a vertex description, or a separation oracle would supply a model the paper never specifies. For a continuum of agents, one would also need to define type utilities, feasible allocations, and how the solution is represented. Those choices would determine the resulting computational problem.

Still, the absence of a named complexity theorem does not rule out a continuous computational question. The main theorem supplies a natural object to compute, \(\phi(P,r)\), and the finite-player model does not rule out high multiplicity in a suitable bargaining setting. For example, a large population could contain cohorts with identical utility profiles. A type-mass model could then use a weighted Nash objective such as \(\int \log(u(t)-r(t))\,d\mu(t)\).

That is the strongest repair to the proponent’s case, and it is coherent. Its weakness as a *mirror of this paper* is that the computational target, representation, and continuum solution all have to be supplied from outside the article. The resulting work may be worth doing, but its complexity would be a property of those additions; Theorem 3.1 itself offers no computational question for the programme to classify. I would therefore argue against treating this as a worthwhile paper-specific mirror. The broader claim that no plausible continuous bargaining scenario exists is much weaker: the cohort model is plausible, and the paper alone cannot rule it out.
