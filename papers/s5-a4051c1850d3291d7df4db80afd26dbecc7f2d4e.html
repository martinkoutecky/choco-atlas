The continuous mirror question

Given rational finite agent types \(T\) with masses \(\mu_t\), finite good types \(G\) with per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and additive utilities \(u_t(g)\), choose masses \(x_{t,b}\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\), satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}b_gx_{t,b}=q_g\), to maximize \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}u_t(b)\), subject to every positive-mass bundle being \(\mathrm{PROP1}\): \(u_t(b)+\max_{g:q_g>0}u_t(g)\ge R_t:=\sum_g q_g u_t(g)\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity course-allocation market with finitely many complete valuation types, fractional masses of agents, proportional supplies of good types, integral bundles for each individual, configuration variables \(x_{t,b}\), and per-capita utilitarian welfare as the objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6.8's two-agent \(\mathrm{ComputeUMwithinPROP1}\) optimization problem in a high-multiplicity market; it leaves the EF1 results, existence results, fixed-agent pseudopolynomial algorithms, and empirical comparisons untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the exact configuration problem be solved in polynomial time by column generation, or is its pricing problem NP-hard?
  • Does a rigorous two-way dictionary exist between rational mass allocations and sufficiently replicated finite allocations with individual \(\mathrm{PROP1}\)?
  • How does the exact configuration optimum compare with the aggregate fractional-knapsack relaxation suggested by Theorem 6.8?

The case FOR (proponent)

I think this paper admits a credible continuous mirror, with Theorem 6.8 as the strongest anchor.

The natural regime is a large repeated course-allocation or school-seat market. There are \(N\) students but only a small number \(\tau\) of complete utility types: students of the same type have the same additive valuation for every course-seat type. Each seat type also has supply proportional to \(N\). Thus \(N\gg \tau\), while every student still receives an integral bundle of indivisible seats. This is a genuine high-multiplicity regime, not fractional allocation of goods. The paper itself recognises this setting through course allocation and cites Bredereck et al.'s high-multiplicity fair-allocation work; that prior work supports the mirror rather than colliding with it.

Formally, let \(T\) be the finite set of student types, with population masses \(\mu_t\), and let \(G\) be the finite set of good types, with \(q_g\) copies per unit population. A bundle is an integral vector \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{G}\), and \(u_t(b)=\sum_g b_g u_t(g)\). The allocation variable is \(x_{t,b}\ge 0\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(b\), satisfying \( \sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t \) and \( \sum_{t,b}b_gx_{t,b}=q_g \) for every good type \(g\). The objective is per-capita utilitarian welfare \(W(x)=\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}u_t(b)\).

For PROP1, define the type-\(t\) per-capita entitlement \(R_t=\sum_g q_g u_t(g)\). Since every supplied good has many copies outside any one individual’s bundle, a bundle \(b\) is PROP1 for type \(t\) when \(u_t(b)+\max_{g:q_g>0}u_t(g)\ge R_t\). The continuous problem is:

\(\mathrm{HM\text{-}ComputeUMwithinPROP1}_{\infty}\): given \((\mu,q,u)\), output a feasible mass allocation \(x\) maximizing \(W(x)\) subject to every bundle receiving positive mass being PROP1 for its type.

Every \(b\) remains integral; only the mass \(x_{t,b}\) is fractional. Rational \(x\) has the expected high-multiplicity dictionary: after clearing denominators, \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Nq_g\) copies of good \(g\) realize it as an ordinary finite allocation.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6.8, proved in this paper. It states that, even with two agents, \(\mathrm{ComputeUMwithinPROP1}\) is NP-hard. The proof reduces Knapsack. For items \(i\), with values \(v_i\), weights \(w_i\), threshold \(T\), \(W=\sum_iw_i\), \(V=\sum_iv_i\), and \(w^*=\max_iw_i\), the constructed instance has usual items and two large items when \(T\ge W/2\). Alice values a usual item at \(w_i\), while Bob values it at \(w_i+v_i\). Alice values the two large items at \(2T-W+w^*\) and \(w^*\); Bob values them at \(2T+V+w^*\) and \(W+V+w^*\). Every item is worth more to Bob, so the unique unconstrained utilitarian optimum gives everything to Bob. Any welfare-maximizing PROP1 allocation must keep the large items with Bob and is determined by a subset \(S\) of usual items sent to Bob. Its welfare is \(C+v(S)\), while PROP1 holds exactly when \(w(S)\le T\). Thus an optimum fair allocation solves Knapsack. When \(T<W/2\), the paper uses three large items with Alice-values \(W-2T+2w^*\), \(W-2T+w^*\), \(W-2T+w^*\), and Bob-values obtained by adding \(V\) to each; the same equivalence with Knapsack follows.

The continuous version uses two student types, \(A\) and \(B\), with \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\), and per-capita supply \(q_g=1/2\) for each good type in the construction. If \(\rho_i\in[0,1]\) is the fraction of copies of usual good \(i\) assigned to type \(B\), then the aggregate welfare contribution is a constant plus \(\frac12\sum_i v_i\rho_i\). In a balanced mass allocation, the PROP1 condition becomes \(\sum_iw_i\rho_i\le T\). The discrete Knapsack subset has become a mass-transfer vector, and the first relaxation is exactly the fractional-knapsack LP.

For the exact integral-bundle problem, however, one must still distribute those masses into whole bundles, with every individual bundle PROP1. Hence the real continuous question is whether a configuration LP, together with a tractable pricing or balancing argument, captures the optimum. My forecast is that the aggregate two-type core is Class A, while the unrestricted exact mirror is a plausible Class C boundary problem: the original \(0/1\) subset hardness may dissolve, but configuration pricing can retain bin-packing or knapsack structure. Either outcome would be informative and recognisable to the authors as their problem, not a simplified welfare relaxation.

This mirror covers only Theorem 6.8 and its two-agent PROP1 optimization problem. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s EF1 results or its pseudopolynomial algorithms.

The weakest point is that Theorem 6.8’s hardness is driven by item-selection combinatorics, not by a large number of agents. Therefore hardness might survive continuization through integral bundle configurations. Also, normalized valuations remain an open issue in the paper, so the first mirror should preserve the theorem’s unnormalized valuations and treat normalization as a follow-up question. Those are limitations of the predicted complexity result, not reasons to reject the continuous-population model itself.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that it does not actually preserve the paper’s PROP1 problem. The vector \(\rho\) records only how much of each good type is assigned to type \(B\). The claimed condition \(w(\rho)\le T\) follows from an average-utility calculation, but PROP1 is an individual condition. The correct high-multiplicity formulation must require, for every integral bundle \(b\) receiving positive mass,

\[ u_t(b)+\max_{g:q_g>0}u_t(g)\ge R_t, \qquad R_t=\sum_g q_g u_t(g). \]

Thus the decision variable is not merely \(\rho\), but a distribution over integral bundle configurations. Two allocations with the same \(\rho\) can differ in whether every individual bundle is PROP1. If one imposes the condition only on type averages, the result is fractional knapsack, but that is no longer the paper’s PROP1 notion. If one retains individual PROP1, the clean subset-to-\(\rho\) correspondence in Theorem 6.8 disappears and the proposed reduction has not established a mirror of that theorem.

There is also a genuine degeneracy on the other natural route. If the \(m\) original items remain single copies while the number of agents tends to infinity, each agent’s proportional entitlement tends to zero. PROP1 then becomes essentially automatic, and the problem collapses to unconstrained welfare maximization. To avoid that collapse one must scale every item’s supply with the population. But doing so changes the paper’s one-copy indivisible-goods problem into a multi-unit configuration-allocation problem with typed seats or courses. That may be interesting, but it is a new model whose central difficulty is bundle configuration, not the computational phenomenon studied by Theorem 6.8.

This is the best negative argument, but it is not decisive. A repeated course-allocation market with two valuation types, proportional seat supplies, integral bundles, and a configuration LP is a perfectly coherent high-multiplicity regime. The paper’s course-allocation motivation makes it plausible, and the fact that the discrete Knapsack hardness may disappear is not an objection under the continuization programme. Existing high-multiplicity fair-allocation work supports rather than defeats it.

So the honest conclusion is that the proponent’s specific \(\rho\)-based explanation is incomplete: it conflates average feasibility with individual PROP1. But after replacing it with the exact configuration formulation, a worthwhile continuous mirror remains. I cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no scenario produces one; this anchor should probably survive.
