The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no qualifying named computational anchor. It gives a bidding algorithm in §3.5 and reports empirical findings in Figures 1–4, but it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. I therefore can’t honestly state a continuous problem *per anchor* without inventing one.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large logistics fleet with many drivers sharing complete operational types—capabilities, availability, starting and ending locations, task preferences, and pricing parameters—allocates recurring standardized transport work. A continuous version could assign mass of each driver type to feasible task bundles, minimize total bid cost, and satisfy shipment demand and scheduling constraints. That is a possible research question, but it is not anchored by a named result in this paper, and the paper’s task-specific scheduling experiments do not establish that the authors would recognize this formulation as their problem.

So the positive case is limited to plausibility of the setting. Its weakest point is decisive for the requested format: the paper offers an algorithm and empirical trade-offs, but no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Section 3.5 gives a greedy bidding procedure; Section 4 reports empirical tradeoffs for synthetic instances and one trucking dataset. The paper states no complexity result or algorithmic guarantee for that procedure or for winner determination. Its measured finding—that changing \(p\) affects preferences and task duration—could motivate a study of population-level behavior, but that would be an analytic or empirical limit question, not the computational question ChoCo is set up to pursue.

The best rescue is a fleet with many drivers sharing complete operational types, handling repeated shipment types. A continuous model could use feasible driver schedules as configurations and allocate driver mass to cover shipment demand at minimum bid cost or duration. This is a coherent high-multiplicity problem: identical drivers can be grouped, and fractional mass can represent a continuum of them. It could even yield an LP whose pricing problem is computationally interesting.

But that formulation supplies a new optimization objective and decision process. The paper’s contribution is a preference-sensitive bidding rule and experiments on its effects. A planner choosing fleet-wide schedules and bundles would study a different algorithmic problem; keeping the greedy rule would leave a population-level behavior question without a computational result in this paper to mirror. The trucking data also has route and time details that make repeated complete types a constructed regime, though that alone would not disqualify it.

So the negative case is strong against calling the proposed fleet LP a mirror of a named result in this paper. It is weak against the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario: a standardized high-multiplicity fleet model is plausible and may be worth ChoCo’s time as a related problem. I can defend the narrower verdict—no anchored computational result here—more firmly than the universal one.
