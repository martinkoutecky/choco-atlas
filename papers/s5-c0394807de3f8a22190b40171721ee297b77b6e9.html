The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a genuine population version of Proposition F.3, which I would use as the lead anchor.

Proposition F.3 is proved in the paper. It states that there is a truthful combinatorial-auction mechanism \(A\) with \(cc(A)=\exp(m,n)\), \(tie(A)=m\), and \(price(A)=1\). The underlying social choice function has two players. Player \(1\) receives nothing. Player \(2\) receives a maximum-value bundle \(S\) satisfying \(|S|=m/2\), \(v_1(S)\ge 1\), and \(v_2(S)\ge 1\), with lexicographic tie-breaking. Truthfulness follows from the menu in which \(S\) costs \(1\) when \(v_1(S)\ge1\), and costs \(+\infty\) otherwise. The paper proves the exponential communication lower bound by reducing set disjointness on the \(\binom{m}{m/2}\) bundles of size \(m/2\).

My continuous mirror is Continuous Eligibility-Constrained Menu Allocation.

Let \(M\) be a set of \(m\) items, with \(m\) even, and let

\[ \mathcal S_m=\{S\subseteq M:|S|=m/2\}. \]

A population type \(t\) is a complete pair \(t=(a_t,b_t)\), where \(a_t\) is the valuation of a certifying role and \(b_t\) is the valuation of a requesting role. The pair is complete: agents of the same type agree on every bundle value relevant to the problem. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many such types.

For type \(t\), define its menu by

\[ p_t(S)= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if }S\in\mathcal S_m\text{ and }a_t(S)\ge1,\\ +\infty & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

The decision variable is \(x_{t,S}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(S\). Every individual receives an integral bundle; only the aggregate assignment is fractional. With normalized per-capita item supplies \(q_j\), a feasible allocation satisfies

\[ \sum_{S\in\mathcal S_m}x_{t,S}\le \mu_t \]

and

\[ \sum_{t}\sum_{S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j. \]

The outside option has value \(0\). The problem is to compute an optimal mass allocation

\[ \max_x \sum_{t}\sum_{S\in\mathcal S_m} x_{t,S}\bigl(b_t(S)-p_t(S)\bigr), \]

with a fixed lexicographic rule used if a canonical optimum is required. Equivalently, one may ask only whether the optimum is positive.

This is the continuous form of the paper’s question: which eligible bundles should requesting agents receive when eligibility is encoded by another side’s menu? The point-mass special case \(\mu_t=1\) recovers Proposition F.3 exactly: the chosen bundle is a maximum-value \(S\) with \(a_t(S)\ge1\) and \(b_t(S)\ge1\). But the intended regime is not one pair copied pointlessly. It is a large population of applicants, employers, projects, or service units, with perhaps millions of agents but only a small catalogue of repeated certifier–requester types. Item supplies scale per capita, as they must when repeated agents receive integral bundles.

The authors should recognise this as their problem rather than as an unrelated welfare LP. The mirror preserves the same \(m/2\)-bundle agenda, the same menu-price rule, the same positive-surplus condition, and the same max-profit choice. What changes is only the population representation: named players become masses of interchangeable type-pairs, and the allocation becomes a mass transfer.

I would expect two regimes. With explicitly listed bundle values, the continuous problem is a linear program and can be solved in time polynomial in \(|T|\binom{m}{m/2}\) and the encoding length. In that representation, the paper’s communication lower bound dissolves because the relevant bits are exposed as explicit coordinates.

In the natural succinct or oracle representation, however, I expect Class B: hardness transfers in the programme’s broader sense. Proposition F.3’s combinatorics live in the bundle agenda \(\mathcal S_m\), not in the number of named agents. A point-mass instance already embeds the paper’s hard case, and a demand-oracle version can retain the exponential query barrier. Thus continuization removes population multiplicity, but it does not automatically remove hardness caused by exponentially many possible bundles.

This yields several useful follow-up questions: when the number of types is small, is there a polynomial pricing or separation algorithm for the eligibility-constrained bundle problem? Can column generation exploit structure in \(a_t\) and \(b_t\)? Under demand-only access, does a continuous menu-reconstruction problem retain the phenomenon of Theorem B.9, which proves that polynomially many demand queries may suffice to allocate a bundle while exponentially many are needed to reconstruct the menu? And when rational masses are expanded into finite populations, what approximation and rounding guarantees preserve the allocation?

The mirror covers Proposition F.3 only. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s entire communication-complexity framework.

The weakest point is that the paper has two named players, whereas my population model introduces a certifier–requester type-pair and per-capita item supplies. That is a genuine co-modification, not something forced by the original statement. It is nevertheless a natural one: without it, cloning the two players would merely pad the instance and would not create a meaningful continuous society. The other limitation is that Proposition F.3 gives a communication lower bound, not an NP-hardness theorem, so the mirror should not be advertised as proving a new NP-hard continuous problem. Its honest contribution is to separate the part of the difficulty caused by population representation from the part caused by the combinatorics of bundle alternatives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s construction does not actually continuize Proposition F.3. It replaces the paper’s two independent players by one population of agents whose type contains a pair \((a_t,b_t)\). That is a new allocation problem.

The exponential lower bound in Proposition F.3 comes from two independent valuation vectors: player \(1\) holds bits \(A_S\), player \(2\) holds independent bits \(B_S\), and the mechanism must detect whether some \(S\) satisfies \(A_S=B_S=1\). The proposed type \(t=(a_t,b_t)\) puts both vectors inside one complete type. Once the type data are available to the allocation procedure, the crucial two-party information split has disappeared. If the pairs are kept correlated, that correlation is an additional modelling assumption; if they are not, a distribution over pairs does not preserve the original problem.

Cloning the actual players does not repair this. With the original supply of \(m\) items, \(N\) copies can still produce only one winning bundle, whose mass is \(O(1/N)\); the normalized continuum limit loses the outcome. Scaling the item supply by \(N\) makes every clone receive a copy of the same bundle, so the result is merely a rescaling of the two-player choice. Giving each type its own menu and introducing per-capita capacities produces a packing problem that is not the social choice function in F.3.

The more faithful alternative would use two populations: certifier types \(a\) and requester types \(b\). But then the paper’s menu rule has no canonical population lift. Eligibility could mean that some positive mass of certifiers accepts \(S\), that the average certification value exceeds a threshold, that every certifier accepts it, or that a certifier must be matched to each requester. These choices are materially different. The existential version depends only on support, not on mass; the average and threshold versions change the rule; and matching creates a new two-sided market. None follows from Proposition F.3 itself.

The proposed \(p_t(S)\) also exposes the mismatch. In F.3, \(v_1\) generates one common menu for player \(2\). In the mirror, every type generates its own menu from its own \(a_t\). The certifier and requester roles have been fused, so the mechanism’s taxation interpretation and its communication problem are gone. Calling \(a_t\) a “certifying role” does not preserve that structure.

A single repeated certifier type with a continuum of requester types is more coherent, but then the certifier’s menu is fixed and the population problem is simply allocation under an exogenous eligibility constraint. That may be an interesting configuration LP, but it does not mirror the paper’s communication result: the hard part of F.3 is communicating the menu-generating valuation and the requesting valuation held by different players. A centrally specified population distribution has no natural analogue of that communication task.

This is not the forbidden objection that the continuous answer might be easy, hard, or unchanged. The issue is more basic: Proposition F.3 has no genuine population axis. Its difficulty is carried by two individualized, exponentially rich valuation functions, not by repeated agents. A point mass in the proposed model merely embeds the original instance as a Dirac case; it does not establish a high-multiplicity regime or a discrete–continuous dictionary.

The negative case is therefore strong but not airtight. One could deliberately study a new certifier–requester matching market inspired by F.3, and that market might be worthwhile. But it would need to be defended as a new problem, not as the continuous mirror of Proposition F.3. Under the programme’s stricter standard, I would reject this anchor: every nondegenerate version either loses the paper’s two-player semantics or introduces an unforced matching, aggregation, and supply model.
