The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-backed case under the brief’s rules: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result. It gives no algorithm, runtime bound, or hardness reduction. Its numbered results concern equilibrium existence and properties, which are analytic game-theoretic claims.

The nearest results are Theorem 3.4, which the paper says follows from Britz, Herings, and Predtetchinski (2010, 2014), and Proposition 6.2, proved here. Theorem 3.4 says that under Assumptions A and B, or A and C, a pure stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium exists; every such equilibrium has immediate agreement and is efficient; there is a unique limit equilibrium; and all limit proposals equal the weighted asymmetric Nash bargaining solution. Proposition 6.2 says that the specific three-player game in Example 6.1, with \(\delta=3/4\), has no pure stationary subgame-perfect equilibrium. Neither result asserts that a problem is tractable or hard. Proposition 4.2’s delay result is likewise about equilibrium behavior, not computational complexity.

A possible *future computational question* would be: given rational masses \(\mu_t\) for finitely many constituency types, a finite state space and rejection-transition rules, a discount factor, and a rational feasible set of per-capita payoffs, determine whether a pure stationary equilibrium exists and, if so, output its proposals and acceptance rules. Here a type would specify a constituency’s preferences, response position, and transition rule; mass would be its share of the represented population. The decision variable would be a proposal at each state, with acceptance rules for each type. This has a plausible constituency-bargaining story, but it is not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper, and its complexity is not established here.

That candidate also has a serious plausibility problem. In the paper, each of three named players is strategically pivotal: one person’s rejection determines the next state. In a continuum of actual agents, a single person has zero mass, while unanimity makes even one rejection decisive. Treating each type as a bloc preserves the bargaining logic, but makes the bloc—not its members—the strategic player. So the strongest honest conclusion is that the paper may motivate a later computational study of a continuous constituency-bargaining model, but it supplies no eligible anchor for the continuous-computational-social-choice case requested here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 3.4 classifies equilibrium properties under two protocol assumptions; Proposition 4.2 shows delay in a particular game; Proposition 6.2 proves nonexistence in another particular game. None gives an algorithm, runtime, or complexity classification. Turning any of them into a computational question would add a new contribution rather than continuize a result of this paper.

Theorem 3.4 is the closest candidate, but its bargaining weights \(\mu\) are the stationary distribution of the protocol’s state transitions, not a distribution of agents. Computing the resulting finite-dimensional Nash bargaining solution might be a computational task, but population mass has no natural part in it. Likewise, Proposition 4.2’s threshold \(\delta>1/2\) and Proposition 6.2’s nonexistence proof are equilibrium facts about fixed games. Asking an algorithm to detect such behavior across a family of games is possible, but the paper supplies no population-based problem for that algorithm to solve.

The proposed constituency version does not repair this gap. In the paper, each player has a separate payoff coordinate, a veto, and a possible effect on the next state: the transition depends on *who* rejects. A mass distribution over types either leaves those strategic players intact—in which case constituency mass is extraneous—or replaces players with blocs, requiring new rules for bloc payoffs, proposer selection, and rejection. With an atomless population, a person has zero mass but can still veto under unanimity; replacing that veto with a positive-mass threshold changes the bargaining protocol. A model of large-committee bargaining could be worthwhile, but it would be a new model whose connection to this paper would need to be established.

So the negative case is strong for the proposed ChoCo programme: this paper offers neither a computational anchor nor an evident way to make population mass matter while retaining its defining strategic mechanism. The remaining weakness is that this cannot rule out a useful continuous bargaining model motivated by the paper; it only shows that such a model would need its own question and justification, rather than serving as a continuous mirror of these results.
