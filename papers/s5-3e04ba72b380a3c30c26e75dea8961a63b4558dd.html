The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many programs and a finite support \(T\) of applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), where each type specifies preferences, a grade vector, and an eligibility vector, plus rational per-capita capacities and reserve masses, compute a feasible assignment \(x_{tp}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_p x_{tp}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tp}\leq\kappa_p\). The assignment must be stable under the paper's multidimensional-reserve priorities, meaning no positive mass can obtain a preferred program through a blocking contract, and fair under its grade-and-privilege criterion, with equal-grade mass allowed to split at cutoffs.

The model it lives in

Represent applicants by complete types carrying mass \(\mu_t\), and represent assignments by \(x_{tp}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to program \(p\). Each individual receives one program or remains unmatched; programs apply the paper's slot-specific reserve priorities to the mass, splitting indifferent equal-grade cohorts at cutoffs. The task is to compute a stable and fair assignment, with capacities and reserve quantities scaled per capita.

The objection that survived

The paper proves the outcome guarantee only for finite applicants and gives no runtime or bit-complexity bound for a weighted, type-compressed cumulative-offer procedure with tied cutoff mass; those parts remain to be established.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. The strongest computational anchor is Proposition 4, proved in the paper: the student-proposing stable mechanism, using the authors’ multidimensional-privileges choice functions, produces an allocation that is stable under students’ preferences and those choice functions, and is fair. Here, stability means individual rationality and no student–program contract that the student prefers and the program would choose; fairness means that when a student misses a more-preferred program, each student assigned there either has a higher grade or claims privileges the rejected student does not. The paper constructs the mechanism using cumulative offers. Proposition 4 is an algorithmic outcome guarantee, not a runtime result, so the complexity expectation below is a question for the mirror, not a theorem of this paper.

My lead mirror is Continuous Multidimensional-Reserve Stable Assignment. An instance has finitely many programs and a finite support of student types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type specifies everything relevant to assignment: a strict preference order over programs and being unmatched, an exam-grade vector \((\theta_{tp})_p\), and an eligibility vector for public-school, minority, and low-income privileges. The instance also gives each program a rational per-capita capacity and reserve masses, obtained by scaling the paper’s reserve proportions with the population.

The question is to compute a stable, fair assignment of student mass to programs under the paper’s multidimensional-privileges priorities. Let \(x_{tp}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to program \(p\); require \(\sum_p x_{tp}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tp}\leq\kappa_p\), where \(\kappa_p\) is the program’s capacity. Each unit of mass represents an individual assigned to one program; \(x_{tp}\) records how much of a cohort receives each assignment. Programs choose mass using the paper’s slot priorities: a reserve first considers students claiming its privilege vector or a superset, then the lower-priority groups, with grades ordering students within a priority class. A mass version of cumulative offers repeatedly sends unheld mass to its next preferred program and lets programs retain the highest-priority mass up to capacity. A solution is the resulting assignment: no positive mass can block with a more-preferred program under that program’s choice rule, and rejected mass does not violate the paper’s grade-and-privilege fairness condition.

The natural regime is a large centralized admissions round with many applicants but repeated combinations of exact exam scores, preference lists, and eligibility profiles. For example, a system with standardized scores, a restricted program menu, and the three binary privilege dimensions can have \(N\gg\tau\), where \(N\) is the applicant count and \(\tau\) the number of distinct complete types. The paper’s Brazilian admissions setting makes this a plausible regime to investigate, though it does not establish that its observed data compresses this way. The scenario passes the high-multiplicity test only when applicants sharing a type are truly interchangeable in all the features the mechanism uses.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: with finite support, a type-compressed cumulative-offer procedure should avoid processing every individual, with running time depending on the number of programs, \(\tau\), and the rational input length. That is a prediction to prove, including termination and bit-growth bounds—not a result supplied by Proposition 4. It would be a population-continuous matching question, not continuity of seats or lotteries. Its scope is the paper’s stable, fair allocation under multidimensional reserves; it does not claim to mirror the empirical analysis or the separate incentive-compatibility result.

The weak point is that the paper assumes no ties in exam grades, while repeated high-multiplicity types necessarily create equal-grade cohorts. The mirror therefore needs an explicit tie rule, such as splitting cutoff mass anonymously, and a corresponding weak fairness condition for tied grades. That is a real co-modification, and the strongest paper result does not directly establish it. The case survives because the core problem remains the same: how to assign applicants under multidimensional reserve priorities so that the allocation is stable and fair.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 4 is the wrong kind of anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme. It proves that a specified mechanism produces stable, fair assignments; it gives no complexity classification or algorithmic bottleneck to carry over. Recasting its outcome guarantee as “compute a stable, fair mass assignment” changes the question from a mechanism-design theorem to a new computational problem. That may be sensible, but it does not fill the gap this programme is designed to chart.

The strongest version of the mirror would give each type a complete preference order, grade vector, and eligibility profile, then let rational masses compete for per-capita seats. In finite support, a mass version of cumulative offers can use the paper’s slot priorities, cut a cohort at a capacity threshold, and pass rejected mass to its next choice. The paper’s no-ties assumption means positive mass at an identical grade needs a tie-splitting rule; the proponent is right that this is a reasonable co-modification. But once made, the mirror is a fractional implementation of the paper’s existing procedure, not a new optimization structure or a complexity result anchored in the paper.

A continuous score distribution avoids tied cohorts, but then exact computation depends on how that distribution is encoded. A piecewise-rational representation gives a finite cutoff computation; a general measure needs additional representation and access assumptions that the paper does not motivate. Continuum college matching already has a standard analytic framework in which stable outcomes are described through admission cutoffs, though that literature is not itself a computational collision or an answer to the proposed complexity question ([Azevedo and Leshno](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2013/01/azevedo-leshno-supply-demand-matching.pdf)). So the honest negative is limited: a mass version is coherent and the exact multidimensional-reserve variant could be worth studying. What fails is the claim that Proposition 4 supplies a worthwhile *computational mirror* of this paper.
