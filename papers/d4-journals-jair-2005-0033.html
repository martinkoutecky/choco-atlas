The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case: the paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result.

The closest candidates are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in the paper. Theorem 1 gives conditions under which, for arguments other than a distinguished argument \(A\), acceptance under preferred or stable semantics is equivalent to being well-defended under the tuple valuation; it also gives one-way conditions for \(A\). Theorem 2 says that, on finite acyclic systems, a condition on a generic local valuation makes acceptance equivalent to being well-defended. These are structural equivalences, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifies its complexity.

Appendix B’s Algorithm 2 proposes propagating tuple values through graphs, with bounded propagation for cycles, but gives no complexity result. The conclusion itself says that the computational study of well-defended arguments remains for further investigation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no named computational anchor, so there is no continuous question from their case that survives by default. Their central point is decisive: Theorems 1 and 2 characterize when acceptance agrees with being well-defended under specified valuations; they do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Algorithm 2 is a propagation procedure, but the paper gives it no complexity result, and its conclusion leaves the computational study of well-defended arguments for future work.

The strongest possible mirror would define an algorithmic problem around those results—for example, deciding whether an argument is well-defended, or computing its valuation, in a society of argument types. But that requires adding the computational question the paper never asks. It also requires a new population model: arguments are nodes in an attack graph, and the paper permits arbitrary relationships among them. A useful type would have to preserve the attack structure relevant to paths, cycles, and extensions; in general, that structure can make almost every argument a different type. Replacing it with type-level attack densities instead changes the semantics: the paper’s definitions depend on whether particular attackers and branches exist, not just on their mass.

One could certainly study continuous argumentation models, or computational interventions on large argumentation graphs. Those may be worthwhile projects in their own right. But neither supplies a result of this paper to continuize, and either would require choosing new semantics and a new computational problem. On the material given, there is no worthwhile continuous *mirror* to recommend.
