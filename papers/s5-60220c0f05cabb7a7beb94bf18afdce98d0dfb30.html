The anchor — hardness

Lemma EC.2

Every anchor argued

  • Lemma EC.2 — survived: The opponent shows that hardness may not transfer when seed choices become fractional, but does not displace the concrete, computational type-mass analogue.

The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many roles \(t\in\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), symmetric nonnegative interaction weights \(K_{tu}\), group signs \(r_t\in\{-1,+1\}\), rational opinions \(s_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t s_t=0\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t s_t^2=1\), and a rational seed-mass budget \(\beta\), consider an atomless population partitioned into role blocks of masses \(\mu_t\), with a role-\(t\) user interacting with each role-\(u\) user at weight \(K_{tu}/N\) in an \(N\)-user approximation. Choose a measurable seed set \(S\) of mass at most \(\beta\) and a real opinion shift \(\delta\) supported on \(S\) to maximize the reduction in disparity \(\|(I+L)^{-1}(r(s+\delta))\|_{L^2}^2\); given a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether the maximum reduction is at least \(q\). Equivalently, determine the complexity of minimizing the proponent’s displayed role-mass objective over \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_t\le\beta\), and aggregate shifts \(v_t\), with \(v_t=0\) when \(x_t=0\).

The model it lives in

An atomless population with rational mass \(\mu_t\) in each of finitely many complete interaction roles, block interactions determined by \(K\), fixed group signs and initial opinions, and decisions comprising seeded mass \(x_t\) and aggregate opinion shift \(v_t\) per role.

The objection that survived

The paper’s reduction chooses named vertices, while the mirror permits partial seeding within each role, and no rounding or gap argument shows that the reduction survives.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the fixed-network, fixed-correlation opinion-seeding problem underlying Lemma EC.2; it leaves robust seeding under uncertain group structure and recommendation reweighing aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does allowing fractional seeded mass within each role make the stated optimization tractable, or can the paper’s Maximum \(k\)-Coverage hardness be preserved by a gap or rounding argument?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s opinion-seeding problem in a large platform whose users fall into a small number of repeated interaction roles. A role records a user’s initial opinion, group label, and interaction intensity with every other role. If there are \(N\) users and \(\tau\) such roles, the regime is \(N\gg\tau\). To keep each user’s total exposure finite as \(N\) grows, a user in role \(t\) has interaction weight \(K_{tu}/N\) with each user in role \(u\), for a symmetric nonnegative matrix \(K\). This is a plausible model for cohort-based recommendation, though a generic follower graph may have too many distinct interaction profiles for it.

My anchor is Lemma EC.2, proved in the paper’s online companion. It says that, for fixed network \(L\) and group-correlation matrix \(C\), maximizing the opinion-seeding benefit \(F(S,L,C)\) over sets \(S\) with \(|S|\le b\) is NP-hard. It also says \(F\) is monotone and weakly submodular, with ratio \(\gamma=1/\kappa(Z)\), where \(Z=M\odot C\) and \(M=(I+L)^{-2}\); consequently, greedy hill-climbing achieves a \((1-e^{-\gamma})\)-approximation. The companion proves the hardness by reduction from Maximum \(k\)-Coverage. This is the right anchor because it is an explicit hardness result about the underlying intervention problem, rather than about an encoding.

Call the mirror Type-Mass Opinion Seeding. Its instance has finitely many roles \(t\in\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), the symmetric interaction matrix \(K\), a rational initial opinion \(s_t\) for each role with \(\sum_t\mu_t s_t=0\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t s_t^2=1\), group labels \(r_t\in\{-1,+1\}\), and a rational seed-mass budget \(\beta\). The population is an atomless space \(\Omega\), with a measurable block \(\Omega_t\) of mass \(\mu_t\) for each role. The group structure is fixed at \(C(u,v)=r(u)r(v)\), where \(r(u)=r_t\) on \(\Omega_t\). The platform chooses a measurable seeded set \(S\subseteq\Omega\) with \(\mu(S)\le\beta\), and an opinion shift \(\delta\) supported on \(S\). It seeks to maximize the reduction in disparity, \(g(s,L,C)-g(s+\delta,L,C)\), where \(g(s+\delta,L,C)=\|(I+L)^{-1}(r(s+\delta))\|_2^2\). A solution is a seed set and shift attaining the optimum, or a rational-threshold decision: is the optimum at least \(q\)?

This is a concrete finite-input optimization problem, not just a population story. Put \(d_t=\sum_u\mu_uK_{tu}\), and let \((L_{\mathcal T}a)_t=\sum_u\mu_uK_{tu}(a_t-a_u)\). For a seeded mass \(x_t=\mu(S\cap\Omega_t)\) and total shifted opinion \(v_t=\int_{S\cap\Omega_t}r\delta\,d\mu\), the mean opinion in role \(t\) after the intervention is \(a_t=r_ts_t+v_t/\mu_t\). The continuum objective reduces exactly to minimizing

\[ \sum_t\mu_t\bigl[(I+L_{\mathcal T})^{-1}a\bigr]_t^2 +\sum_t\frac{1}{(1+d_t)^2}\left(\frac{v_t^2}{x_t}-\frac{v_t^2}{\mu_t}\right) \]

over \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_t\le\beta\), with \(v_t=0\) when \(x_t=0\). For fixed \(x_t,v_t\), the best shift is constant on the selected mass in that role; the formula accounts for both the change in its type average and the within-role variation. Thus the continuous decision variables are seeded mass and opinion change by role, and the input size is measured in \(\tau\) and the rational bit length.

I expect the hardness to transfer (Class B) in this regime: the candidate-to-target incidence that drives the Maximum \(k\)-Coverage reduction can live in the interaction matrix over roles, while each role is replicated arbitrarily many times. Increasing \(N\) need not erase the combinatorial structure in \(\tau\). The key proof obligation is to show that allowing partial seeding within a role does not defeat the reduction—for example, by proving suitable endpoint rounding or a gap-preserving embedding. The type-mass formulation makes that a precise question.

The mirror covers only the fixed-network opinion-seeding selection problem behind Lemma EC.2. It does not claim to mirror robust seeding under uncertain group structure, recommendation reweighing, or the paper as a whole. My weakest point is that the paper’s stated hardness reduction uses \(C=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\), where disparity reduces to polarization. The anchor therefore supports a faithful mirror of the paper’s seeding objective, but does not establish hardness driven by group uncertainty. The block-network assumption is also substantive: it is convincing for repeated platform cohorts, but much less so for networks whose users have mostly unique neighborhoods.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Lemma EC.2’s hardness depends on choosing a discrete set of named users. Its reduction from Maximum \(k\)-Coverage encodes each set and element in the finite network, then asks whether to seed each candidate vertex. In the proposed mirror, the decisions are instead seeded masses \(x_t\) and aggregate shifts \(v_t\). Nothing in the lemma shows that an optimum has \(x_t\in\{0,\mu_t\}\), or that partial seeding can be rounded without losing the reduction. The coverage argument therefore does not establish hardness for the mass problem. The displayed role-level objective makes that a precise proof obligation, but does not settle it.

That gap matters: fractional allocation can remove the discrete choice that made a set-cover reduction work. The mirror needs its own complexity result—perhaps a gap-preserving reduction, perhaps an algorithm showing the relaxation is tractable. Until then, citing the finite-user NP-hardness as evidence that hardness transfers overstates what the anchor establishes.

There is also a scaling issue. If the seed budget \(b\) stays fixed as the population grows, its mass tends to zero; with the paper’s bounded intervention norm, its aggregate effect tends to zero as well. A nondegenerate mirror must scale the budget with population, as the proposed per-capita budget \(\beta\) does. That is a defensible co-modification, but it means the mirror asks a different intervention question from the finite theorem.

These points weaken the claimed transfer, but they do not defeat the best mirror. A platform with many users in repeated exposure roles is plausible, and its type-mass seeding problem is well specified. The set-cover structure could still survive partial seeding, or role-wide interventions could make roles the natural decision units. Even if hardness then lives in the number of roles, that remains a meaningful computational question under ChoCo’s scope. I can make a case that this anchor’s hardness has not yet been mirrored; I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
