The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that the paper studies a genuinely high-multiplicity regime: voters share a distribution of private values over bundles, and the authors analyze what happens as the electorate grows. A plausible type is a voter’s complete utility vector \(\theta=(\theta_\emptyset,\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_{12})\); mass records the fraction of voters with each type. A ballot is a subset of issues, and the aggregate outcome passes each issue receiving more than half the ballot mass. This fits settings such as large electorates voting on a fixed set of referenda, where many voters may have the same relevant values.

The nearest result to anchor a mirror is Proposition 4, proved in this paper: for every density in the open class \(C\) defined in Example 1, both issues are unpredictable along every sequence of equilibria—each issue’s probability of passing stays bounded away from both \(0\) and \(1\) as the electorate grows. That is a substantive large-population result, but it is not a qualifying computational anchor. The paper proves no named result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another computational complexity class. Its existence, limit, and implementation propositions answer analytic game-theoretic questions, not computational-complexity questions.

A computational question suggested by Proposition 4 would be: given a succinctly represented type distribution, determine whether every sequence of symmetric weakly undominated equilibria has both issues unpredictable in the large-electorate limit. But this is a proposed new problem, not a result the paper establishes or classifies. It also needs a careful formal input model before it could be handed to a prover.

The main difficulty for the mirror is that the strategic effect driving the paper is pivotality in a finite election. In an ordinary atomless electorate, one voter has no effect on the outcome, so the pivotal conditioning that creates the exposure problem disappears. Defining the mirror through limits of finite-electorate equilibria could preserve the authors’ question, but it makes the finite approximation central and does not supply the missing computational anchor.

So the paper has a strong high-multiplicity setting and a natural candidate for a computational follow-up, but no named computational result to mirror under the stated anchor rule. That is the limiting weakness of the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 4 is the strongest anchor, and it establishes a limit result, not a computational one: for distributions in \(C\), issues remain unpredictable along every sequence of equilibria. Propositions 2–3 concern equilibrium existence and monotonicity; Propositions 5–8 concern outcomes and implementation. None states a complexity result or gives an algorithm. Under the programme’s anchor rule, there is no computational result here to mirror.

The population setting is natural: the voters are i.i.d. draws from a common distribution of value types, and the paper studies growing electorates. But the distribution \(\mu\) is a prior over types, while each election still has finitely many voters. That finiteness supplies the pivotal events driving the paper’s strategic exposure problem. In an atomless population, one voter’s ballot cannot change whether an issue passes, so the ballot no longer affects that voter’s payoff. The resulting equilibrium question loses the strategic feature Proposition 4 analyzes. Taking limits of finite-election equilibria preserves that feature, but keeps the finite game central.

The proponent’s proposed decision problem—given a distribution, determine whether every equilibrium sequence has unpredictable outcomes—is a possible new project, not a result of this paper. Proposition 4 gives a sufficient condition on an open class \(C\), rather than a classification of all distributions. With two issues and a finite encoding, checking the defining mass inequalities for \(C\) would be straightforward. A broader equilibrium-sequence problem would need a specified representation for the full-support density and a precise output and approximation model; choosing those would define new computational questions rather than supply a mirror of the paper’s results.

So the negative case is strongest on the absence of a computational anchor and the loss of pivotal incentives in a literal atomless model. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile follow-up exists. High multiplicity is plainly sensible for large electorates, and computational questions about finite-election equilibria or their limits could be valuable. The narrower conclusion is that this paper offers an analytic limit theory to build on, not a computational result for ChoCo to continuize.
