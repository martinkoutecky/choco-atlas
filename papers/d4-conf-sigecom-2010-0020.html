The continuous mirror question

Fix a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) advertiser classes, each with a common value \(v_\theta\) and click-reward law \(F_\theta\). For population sizes \(N\) with \(N\mu_\theta\) integral, each advertiser submits an individual bid; a mechanism assigns one impression per round for \(T\) rounds and pays advertisers afterward. Among deterministic and randomized mechanism families that are ex-post truthful and normalized for every individual report and fixed click realization, determine the best regret rates as \(N\) grows, uniformly over these populations, against the truthful-profile benchmark \(T\max_\theta b_\theta p_\theta\), where \(p_\theta\) is the mean of \(F_\theta\). Characterize whether type-level pooling yields a nondegenerate limit of scaled per-advertiser impressions and payments, such as \(N\mathbb{E}[X_i]\) and \(N\mathbb{E}[P_i]\), while preserving those individual incentive constraints.

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over advertiser classes supplies the population description; each class shares a value and click law, while a tagged advertiser remains individually strategic. A mechanism learns from clicks, assigns one impression per round, and seeks low regret with ex-post truthfulness and normalized payments. The central question is whether pooling by class changes the deterministic and randomized regret frontier.

The objection that survived

The limiting mass description does not itself encode a tagged advertiser’s unilateral deviation, and with fixed \(T\) an individual’s expected allocation vanishes as \(N\) grows; the case does not fully specify limiting scaled payments and incentives.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the stochastic MAB regret and truthfulness frontier in Theorem 7.14, with Theorem 7.9’s mechanism as an algorithmic reference point; it leaves the generic payment transformation, offline applications, and multi-parameter extension aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can pooled click observations support individual ex-post truthful mechanisms with regret governed by \(\tau\) rather than \(N\)?
  • Does the deterministic exponent lower bound persist, or can high multiplicity and pooling improve the frontier?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is in the paper’s stochastic multi-armed bandit section. My lead anchor is Theorem 7.14. It concerns stochastic MAB mechanism design with two agents and an unknown upper bound \(b_{\max}\) on bids. It states that any ex-post truthful, ex-post normalized mechanism with regret \(\widetilde O(b_{\max}T^\gamma)\), for every \(b_{\max}\), must have \(\gamma\ge 2/3\) if it is deterministic; that lower bound is cited from Babaioff et al. (2014). The theorem also states that a randomized mechanism with \(\gamma=1/2\) exists; that part is proved here. This is a regret lower bound rather than an NP-hardness result, but it is the paper’s clearest named hardness-style result, and its contrast between deterministic and randomized mechanisms makes a useful continuization question.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large online advertising market with many small campaigns but relatively few recurring campaign classes. A type \(\theta\) records everything relevant here: its value \(v_\theta\) per click and its click-reward distribution \(F_\theta\) on \([0,1]\), with mean \(p_\theta\). The mechanism does not know \(F_\theta\); it learns from clicks. Let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution over the \(\tau\) types, with \(N\mu_\theta\) advertisers of type \(\theta\) in a finite population of size \(N\). Consider \(N\) growing while \(\tau\) stays small. Members of a type share the same value and reward law, but their click outcomes are independent. At each of the paper’s \(T\) discrete rounds, the mechanism still assigns one impression to one advertiser.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover, the two-type truthful bandit frontier. Given a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) supported on two types, a horizon \(T\), and hidden click laws \(F_\theta\), determine the best regret exponents for deterministic and randomized mechanism families as \(N\) grows. Each family must give every advertiser an individual bid and payment, be ex-post truthful for every fixed click realization, and be normalized as in the paper. Regret is measured against \(T\max_\theta b_\theta p_\theta\) at truthful profiles, and the bounds must hold uniformly over the finite populations \(N\mu_\theta\). The continuous description records the limiting per-capita expected impressions \(x_\theta=\lim_{N\to\infty}N\,\mathbb E[X_i]\) and payments for a typical member of each type; these averages may be fractional, with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta x_\theta=T\). A solution is a construction and guarantee for each mechanism family, together with matching lower bounds if they exist.

I would expect the randomized side to remain tractable, with a \(\widetilde O(b_{\max}\sqrt T)\) regret guarantee, and I would expect the deterministic lower bound to persist at exponent \(2/3\). That is a conjectured Class B boundary for deterministic mechanisms, not a consequence of Theorem 7.14: the theorem’s two-agent lower bound does not automatically prove a lower bound for a population of advertisers. The main follow-up question is whether pooling click observations across identical types changes the deterministic frontier.

A second, supporting anchor is Theorem 7.9, proved in this paper. It states that in stochastic MAB there is an ex-post truthful mechanism that is ex-post no-positive-transfers and universally ex-post individually rational, with regret \(O(\sqrt{nT\log T})\) and gap-dependent regret \(O((n/\delta)\log T)\). Its continuous counterpart is the type-compressed NewCB problem: for a rational type distribution with support size \(\tau\), construct such a mechanism family with regret \(O(\sqrt{\tau T\log T})\) and gap-dependent regret \(O((\tau/\delta)\log T)\), with core computation polynomial in \(\tau,T,L\) apart from the cost of producing per-advertiser outputs. I would expect this to be Class A: repeated observations can be pooled by type, so \(\tau\), rather than the number of advertisers, should govern learning. The hard part is preserving individual truthfulness when an advertiser can deviate from the common type report; the paper’s NewCB monotonicity argument is for individually tracked agents, not pooled cohorts.

These mirrors cover only the paper’s stochastic MAB regret and truthfulness results. The authors should recognize them as continuations of their problem: they retain advertisers as strategic agents, click observations as the learning signal, and the same regret benchmark, while asking what changes when there are many agents but few complete types.

The weakest point is that a distribution over types does not by itself retain the identity of a unilateral deviator. My proposed model therefore keeps exact finite-population incentive constraints and takes a limit of per-capita outcomes, rather than claiming that a type-mass mechanism already implements payments. If that limit cannot be characterized without preserving nearly all individual-level information, the continuous mirror loses much of its value. That is a substantial caveat, but it is also a concrete question the paper’s authors could assess.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the MAB results make each advertiser an individually tracked strategic arm. A mass distribution over advertiser types does not retain that structure while also preserving the paper’s truthfulness guarantees.

For Theorem 7.14, the deterministic lower bound is for exactly two strategic agents. Replacing each with a large cohort changes the mechanism’s inputs and benchmark. At a truthful profile, advertisers in a cohort may share a value and click law. But dominant-strategy truthfulness must also cover a unilateral report change. That advertiser then has a distinct bid, and the mechanism must handle its individual allocation and payment. A distribution over truthful types cannot encode all those deviations with support size \(\tau\).

There are two standard ways to define the limit, and each drops part of the theorem’s subject. Treat a cohort as one strategic player, and the incentive constraint is for a group or representative rather than for each advertiser. Treat individuals as infinitesimal members of a continuum, and one person’s deviation has zero effect on the aggregate learning state. That can define a sensible price-taking model, but it removes the finite-agent interaction the theorem studies. Keeping exact finite-\(N\) dominant-strategy constraints preserves that interaction, but requires accounting for individual reports and states. The proposed \(\tau\)-type frontier then has no direct basis in Theorem 7.14.

Horizon scaling adds a second difficulty. With fixed \(T\), a symmetric advertiser receives at most \(T/N\) expected impressions on average as \(N\) grows, so its utility and feasible individually rational payment vanish. Scaling payments and allocations to restore per-advertiser utility would change the per-realization impression and payment guarantees. If instead \(T\) grows with \(N\), pooled click data can yield a useful learning problem, but the per-capita regret of efficient methods vanishes. Preserving the regret frontier then requires studying lower-order stochastic terms alongside individual incentive constraints. That may be a worthwhile asymptotic problem, but the two-agent exponent in Theorem 7.14 does not establish its frontier.

Theorem 7.9 has the same obstacle in a more visible form. Pooling clicks across advertisers with a shared click law is a plausible improvement, but NewCB’s ex-post monotonicity argument tracks each advertiser’s bid, confidence bounds, and designated observations. Pooling those observations creates a different allocation rule whose individual monotonicity and payment guarantees need new proofs. Requiring every advertiser in a cohort to submit one common bid would make compression easier, while changing the incentive question to a cohort-level one. Keeping individual bids means a deviator can split off from the cohort; an exact finite-population model must represent that split and its effect on the learning process.

The paper does contain named algorithmic results, so absence of computational content is not a strong objection. The narrower point is that its central computational guarantee concerns calls to an arbitrary allocation rule, while its MAB results concern regret and individual incentives. Replacing advertiser-level state with type-level state does not follow from those guarantees. It changes the mechanism being analyzed.

This leaves a plausible but limited negative case: the proposed mirrors do not yet preserve the paper’s named incentive problems while delivering the claimed type compression. A carefully designed nonatomic model with a tagged individual and pooled learning could still be interesting, and the paper’s authors might recognize it as a useful extension. That possibility is the weakness in claiming that no continuous mirror is worthwhile in any scenario.
