The case FOR (proponent)

I can see a plausible population-continuum model here, but the paper has no numbered computational-complexity result to anchor it to. Its numbered theorems concern incentive properties, so I can’t honestly give the requested per-anchor complexity questions or predict whether they are tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that under Assumptions 1–3 there are linear-transformation coefficients for the Composite Elicitation Mechanism that make it strictly posterior truthful. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that the Mutual-Information-Based Mechanism is strictly posterior truthful under Assumptions 1 and 2, at least three agents in every report group, and the stated invariance condition on its coefficients. Neither theorem classifies a computational problem as being in P, NP-hard, or otherwise.

The modeling intuition is strong: a large crowdsourcing pool could have a small number of expertise classes, with each class sharing a signal-generation law and \(\mu_\ell\) denoting its share. A continuum version could seek a payment rule based on an agent’s report and aggregate report distributions that makes truthful reporting a strict posterior Bayesian Nash equilibrium. But that would mirror the paper’s mechanism-design results without meeting your anchor requirement. The main weakness is that a single agent’s deviation in an atomless population cannot change the report-group masses, which may remove part of the finite-crowd strategic issue the mechanisms address.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the mirror is that neither named result is computational. Theorem 1 establishes the existence of payment coefficients for CEM; Theorem 2 gives a sufficient condition for MIBM to be strictly posterior truthful. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem. A continuous version could ask whether these truthfulness guarantees hold in a continuum-agent game, but that would be an analytic mechanism-design result, not the complexity landscape ChoCo is set up to chart.

The best mirror of Theorem 1 would put mass \(\lambda_\ell\) on each signal format and ask whether CEM remains strictly posterior truthful. For positive-mass groups, the answer appears to follow directly from the paper’s per-agent expected-payment argument: replace the random peer’s finite sum by an expectation over that group’s signal distribution. The coefficients do not depend on the population shares. This is a sensible continuum model, but it does not create a computational question whose tractability depends on multiplicity.

For Theorem 2, one could replace report-group counts with masses and ask which coefficient functions preserve truthfulness. But a single agent’s deviation does not change masses in an atomless population, so the theorem’s count-invariance condition loses its role. Constant coefficients, as in the paper’s example, already avoid that issue; the proper-scoring-rule argument still supplies the incentive comparison. One could instead study profitable deviations by positive-mass coalitions, or optimize accuracy over unknown crowd compositions. Those are possible research questions, but they add a new strategic model or objective rather than continuize either theorem’s computational content.

There is also a fit problem for ChoCo’s finite-support type model. An expert’s realized signal ranges over a continuous interval, and informative signals lead to different posterior beliefs and incentives. Treating signal formats as the finite set of types omits that distinction; representing it exactly requires a continuous signal-type distribution. Moreover, the paper’s premise is that the mechanism does not know the population shares, whereas a society specified by masses supplies them as input. A robust question over unknown shares might be worthwhile, but it would need to be formulated and justified separately.

So the negative case is strong against this paper as a ChoCo complexity mirror, but not airtight against every related research direction. The two theorems admit natural continuum-agent analogues, and a broader mechanism-design project could make those analogues useful. What the paper does not offer is a named computational result for population continuization to explain or classify.
