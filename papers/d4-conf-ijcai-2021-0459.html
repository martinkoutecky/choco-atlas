The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of doctor types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), preferences, eligibility, and hospital and regional priority tiers, together with hospitals partitioned into regions, rational per-capita capacities \(q_h\), regional quotas \(\delta_r\), and regional weights \(w(h)\), can one compute a direct mechanism returning assignment masses \(x_{t,h}\) and unmatched masses \(x_{t,\emptyset}\)? Require \(\sum_{h\in H}x_{t,h}+x_{t,\emptyset}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,h}\le q_h\), and \(\sum_{t\in T,\,h\in r}x_{t,h}\le\delta_r\). The induced lottery for a member of type \(t\) assigns hospital \(h\) with probability \(x_{t,h}/\mu_t\); require truthful preference reporting to be weakly preferred in stochastic dominance to any misreport by an infinitesimal member, and require mass-adapted versions of the paper's fairness and non-wastefulness conditions under regional weights.

The model it lives in

A finite-support population distribution over complete types, where each type shares preferences, eligibility, and priority tiers. The decision variables are mass assignments \(x_{t,h}\) and \(x_{t,\emptyset}\); feasibility uses per-capita hospital capacities and regional quotas. There is no optimization objective in Theorem 1: the task is to compute an allocation whose induced lotteries satisfy strategyproofness, fairness, and non-wastefulness.

The objection that survived

With the paper's strict individual priorities, complete types may be nearly as numerous as doctors; moreover, Theorem 1 does not establish strategyproofness for shared priority tiers and an atomless assignment-lottery notion.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can make rests on Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that GDA-RO is strategyproof for doctors and returns an outcome that is non-wasteful and fair under regional weights. In GDA-RO, hospitals pass proposals to the regions, which choose first among contracts at higher-weight hospitals and then use regional priority, subject to hospital capacities and regional quotas. The paper states no named result that classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard, and it gives no running-time bound. So this is a constructive algorithmic anchor, not a complexity-classification anchor.

A plausible mirror is Weighted Regional Mass Matching. Consider a large residency cohort divided into types \(T\). A type records everything relevant to matching: doctors in it have the same hospital preferences, eligibility, and priority positions. The instance gives each type a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), hospitals partitioned into regions, per-capita hospital capacities \(q_h\), per-capita regional quotas \(\delta_r\), hospital and regional priorities over type–hospital contracts, and regional weights \(w(h)\). These are the paper’s matching ingredients, with doctors represented by population shares and capacities and quotas scaled to the population.

The requested solution is an exact rational allocation \(x_{t,h}\) of each type’s mass to hospitals, plus \(x_{t,\emptyset}\) left unmatched, where
\[ \sum_{h\in H}x_{t,h}+x_{t,\emptyset}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{t,h}\le q_h,\qquad \sum_{t\in T,\,h\in r}x_{t,h}\le\delta_r. \]
The computational question is to produce such an allocation by a direct mechanism that is strategyproof for an individual doctor and whose allocation is fair and non-wasteful under the paper’s regional weights. A type’s mass may be split across hospitals; that is the aggregate of integral assignments to individual doctors, not a change to the one-doctor/one-hospital matching rule.

For the fairness and non-wastefulness conditions, take the paper’s definitions and replace a doctor by an infinitesimal member of a positive-mass type. A type \(t\) has a claim to a better hospital \(h\) if an infinitesimal member assigned to a less-preferred option can move to \(h\) while preserving capacities and quotas, and the paper’s weight-and-priority test favors that move. For fairness, compare such a member with positive mass assigned to a contract \((t',h')\), using the paper’s three cases: same-hospital priority, equal regional weight with higher regional priority, or strictly greater weight at \(h\). After removing the infinitesimal recipient, define the candidate hospital’s \(\alpha\) as \(w(h)\) if it has a vacancy (or is that recipient’s hospital, which the removal frees), and \(-\infty\) otherwise. The allocation must have no such justified envy and no such claim to a vacant position. Strategyproofness means a doctor cannot improve by reporting another preference; for split mass, compare assignment lotteries by stochastic dominance under the doctor’s true ranking.

The regime I find credible is a high-volume residency clearinghouse where many graduates share preference lists and fall into common eligibility and priority bands: for example, a cohort of roughly \(10^4\) doctors with \(\tau\) in the hundreds. The paper itself reports about \(10{,}000\) doctors and more than \(1{,}000\) hospitals annually, but it does not establish that the real market has only hundreds of complete types. The scenario needs repeated preference and priority profiles, not just many doctors. It is still recognizably the authors’ problem: the same doctor–hospital matching, regional quotas, hospital capacities, weights, fairness, and non-wastefulness. The changes are the population representation and per-capita supplies.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable, as a conjecture for a prover rather than a result of this paper. GDA-RO already processes proposals by preferences, weights, and priorities; with finite support, a mass version could accept or reject portions of a type at quota boundaries. That suggests an exact algorithm polynomial in the number of hospitals, regions, supported types, and input bit length. The discrete high-multiplicity bridge would then ask whether the mass allocation can be implemented or rounded for counts \(n_t/N\), and with what error. Further questions include whether strategyproofness survives fractional acceptance, and whether it holds only for individual deviations or also for coordinated deviations by a positive mass of a type. Theorem 1 does not answer either.

The weak point is the type regime. The paper permits strict, potentially individualized priorities. If each doctor’s priority position is part of their complete type, the number of types may approach the number of doctors, removing the computational gain. Shared priority bands are plausible in some markets, but the paper does not establish that JRMP has them. I would limit the claim to Theorem 1’s weighted regional matching guarantee; Theorems 2 and 3, the empirical welfare comparison, and the paper’s wider GDA-R framework need not be mirrored to make this case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the right anchor, but its guarantees depend on information that the proposed mass model cannot compress. Fairness compares particular doctors using strict hospital and regional priorities; strategyproofness concerns what an individual doctor gets after a report. Under the paper’s strict priorities, two doctors with different priority positions are different types under the programme’s definition. So in a market with \(N\) doctors, preserving the theorem’s inputs can require \(\tau\) on the order of \(N\). The paper’s large market does not by itself supply the moderate-support regime the mirror needs.

There are plausible ways to group doctors, but each changes what the theorem is about. If a type is a shared preference and priority tier, the model needs ties, and the fairness test must be rewritten for them. If types instead include a continuous priority score, the model must represent a distribution over scores and retain a tagged individual’s outcome to make strategyproofness meaningful. A finite list of rational type masses alone gives neither the strict priority order nor the individual outcome. The proponent’s comparison with an infinitesimal recipient exposes this gap: removing zero mass does not free a positive-capacity position, so the paper’s discrete replacement test needs a new limiting definition.

That leaves a worthwhile-looking extension—fluid GDA with priority tiers or score distributions—but it is not yet a continuous mirror of Theorem 1’s stated model. It asks whether the guarantees survive a change to the priority domain and a new definition of individual incentives. And Theorem 1 is an algorithmic guarantee, not a complexity classification: the paper poses no named hardness or running-time question for a continuous mirror to answer. The high-multiplicity literature supports the general regime, but it does not resolve this particular loss of priority information.

This negative case has a real weakness: priority tiers are plausible in some matching markets, and authors could recognize a tied-priority mass version as their problem. If they do, a continuous GDA theorem may be worth proving. The case against is strongest for the paper as written, whose strict individual priorities are central to the guarantee the proponent chose.
