The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) alternatives, \(k\), a panel of \(M\) top-\(k\) ballots encoded by its empirical distribution \(\mu\) over supported ballot types \(t\), and the promise that the ballots were independently drawn from a two-component concentric Mallows mixture with shared center \(\sigma_0\), ask whether sorting \(B_\mu(c)=\sum_t\mu_t b_t(c)\) recovers \(\sigma_0\) with the probability guaranteed by Corollary 8 when the non-expert draw count meets its stated sample-size condition.

The model it lives in

Each type \(t\) is a top-\(k\) ballot with mass \(\mu_t=N_t/M\); the task is to sort alternatives by their mass-weighted top-\(k\) Borda totals \(B_\mu(c)=\sum_t\mu_t b_t(c)\). The recovery guarantee remains over the random panel drawn from the promised mixture.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 8’s Borda recovery guarantee for the common center. It leaves the component-separation result, the sampling algorithms, and the other results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the sample-size guarantee be sharpened when the empirical distribution has small support \(\tau\), with complexity stated in \(n\), \(\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s common-center recovery: a high-multiplicity panel of raters, not a claim that the paper’s whole mixture-identification programme is already a continuous computational theory.

Take, for example, millions of people ranking the same eight films, each giving only their top five. A type is one complete top-five ballot; there are at most \(8\cdot7\cdot6\cdot5\cdot4=6{,}720\) such types, so the number of people can greatly exceed the number of distinct types. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the panel giving ballot \(t\). For consensus recovery, people with the same ballot are indistinguishable: the task uses their submitted ranking, not their hidden expert status.

I would ask the prover to study High-Multiplicity Top-\(k\) Concentric Consensus Recovery. An instance is the support of a rational distribution \(\mu\) over top-\(k\) rankings, together with \(n,k\); \(\tau\) is the support size and \(L\) the mass bit length. The promise is that the panel was sampled from two Mallows components with a shared center \(\sigma_0\), and that the non-expert sample count meets the paper’s stated condition. For each item \(c\), let \(b_t(c)\) be its contribution under the paper’s top-\(k\) Borda rule. The task is to return the complete ranking obtained by sorting the weighted totals
\[ B_\mu(c)=\sum_t \mu_t b_t(c). \]
That ranking is the solution; under the promise, the intended answer is \(\sigma_0\). Weighted Borda can be computed in polynomial time in \(n,\tau,L\), for example in \(O(k\tau+n\log n)\) arithmetic operations.

The anchor is Corollary 8, proved in this paper. It states that Borda returns the correct central ranking \(\sigma_0\) when the number of samples in the non-expert component satisfies the paper’s sample-complexity bound specialized to \(i=n\) and \(\theta=\theta_b\). In the notation of the displayed bound in Theorem 7, this is the threshold
\[ N_b \geq \mathcal{O}\!\left( n^2\log(\epsilon^{-1}) \left( \frac{k^2(1-\exp(-\theta_b))^2}{1-\exp(-\theta_b n)} -n\Delta^{1k}(\theta_b) \right)^{-2} \right), \]
where \(\Delta^{1k}\) is the adjacent-item top-\(k\) probability gap defined in the paper. The continuous formulation keeps the same recovery target and aggregate statistic, while representing repeated ballots by their masses.

This is a Class A expectation for that recovery problem. The paper’s guarantee is statistical, not a worst-case polynomial-time classification theorem: it says when Borda recovers the center from a random panel, while the mass representation lets an algorithm process repeated ballots without iterating over every person. The paper’s NP-hardness statement for Kemeny appears as a citation in the preliminaries, not as a numbered result proved here, so I would not use it as the anchor. A useful follow-up is whether high-multiplicity Kemeny maximum-likelihood recovery remains hard, and how far Borda’s recovery guarantee can be extended.

The weakest point is the boundary between an empirical mass vector and the mixture distribution used as a sampling law. Corollary 8 does not establish a new sample-complexity result for an exact population law; in this mirror, the theorem’s probability guarantee still concerns the panel that produced \(\mu\). That limitation does not erase the narrower case: the panel’s observed types and the center-recovery objective are exactly those of the paper, with multiplicities encoded as population mass.

I would not extend this anchor to Theorem 4’s expert/non-expert separation. A ballot type can arise from either component, so assigning each type’s mass wholly to one hidden source would change the task. The proposed mirror covers Corollary 8’s Borda recovery result, not the paper’s full identifiability claims.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is narrow because repeated ballots are a sensible high-multiplicity regime here. A large panel rating the same films could contain millions of people but only thousands of distinct top-\(k\) ballots. For consensus recovery, the ballot can be a complete type: the task does not use the rater’s identity or hidden expert status.

The difficulty is that Corollary 8 is a sample-complexity result. Its guarantee concerns a finite random sample drawn from a mixture, and its threshold refers to the number of observations from the non-expert component. If the input is the sample’s histogram \(\mu\), weighted Borda gives exactly the same ranking as ordinary Borda on that sample. This is a valid compressed high-multiplicity representation, but it does not turn the theorem’s finite-sample guarantee into a new complexity result about a population distribution.

Taking \(\mu\) to be the exact mixture law changes the question. The paper already formulates that law and studies identifiability; Corollary 8 does not give an algorithmic complexity result for receiving the exact law as input. The encoding also matters: an exact Mallows mixture gives positive mass to every top-\(k\) ballot, so listing its support can require \(\tau=(n)_k\) entries. A compact parametric encoding would need to specify how the unknown center is represented, and that becomes a different recovery problem.

That is the strongest objection I can support: the proposed mirror is either a useful compression of the paper’s finite-sample Borda computation, or an exact-law problem whose input and computational content are not supplied by Corollary 8. It is not an airtight case against a mirror. The empirical-histogram version passes the programme’s high-multiplicity test, and the other analyst’s weighted-Borda formulation is a credible mirror; a reader could reasonably decide that this alone is worthwhile.
