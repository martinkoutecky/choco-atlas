The continuous mirror question

Given finite providers \(N\), channel sets \(C_i\), a finite state space \(\Omega\) with rational probabilities \(p_\omega\), and a finite support \(T\) of rational subscriber masses \(\mu_t\), where each type \(t\) has provider \(i(t)\), rates \(R_{\ell t\omega}\), concave utility \(u_t\), and minimum expected rate \(m_t\), find an efficient schedule and a core imputation. For each coalition \(S\), choose schedules \(a_{\ell t\omega}\in[0,1]\) for types with \(i(t)\in S\) and channels \(\ell\in C_S\), subject to \(\sum_\ell a_{\ell t\omega}\le 1\), \(\sum_{t:i(t)\in S}\mu_t a_{\ell t\omega}\le q_\ell\), and \(\sum_\omega p_\omega\sum_\ell a_{\ell t\omega}R_{\ell t\omega}\ge m_t\). Set \(r_t(\omega)=\sum_\ell a_{\ell t\omega}R_{\ell t\omega}\) and \(z_\ell(\omega)=\sum_t\mu_ta_{\ell t\omega}\). Define \(v(S)\) as the maximum expected \(\sum_{t:i(t)\in S}\mu_tu_t(r_t(\omega))\) minus the providers’ convex costs of channel use \(z_\ell(\omega)\). Return a grand-coalition optimizer and \(x\) with \(x(N)=v(N)\) and \(x(S)\ge v(S)\) for every coalition \(S\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of subscriber mass over recurring customer types, with state-contingent fractional schedules and channel capacity density \(q_\ell\) per unit of population. Coalition values are concave welfare optimizations; a grand-coalition dual solution supplies stable payoff shares.

The objection that survived

The mirror largely aggregates a convex optimization the paper already uses, so it may expose little new computational structure; this bounds its novelty but does not defeat the well-posed population question.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s transferable-payoff spectrum-pooling result in Section III. The paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, or in a parameterized class. Its best computational anchor is Theorem III.1, proved here: for the fixed-deployment spectrum-pooling game, the set \(I\) of payoff vectors induced by optimal dual solutions of the grand coalition’s problem is nonempty and contained in the game’s core \(C\). Thus an optimal dual solution gives a stable way to share the grand coalition’s maximum payoff. The authors also say the relevant convex optimization yields such an imputation in polynomial time; that runtime claim is in the discussion, not in the theorem statement.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a set of wireless providers with millions of subscribers but relatively few recurring service and coverage profiles. A type records everything relevant to the model: the customer’s provider, coverage area, rate distribution across channels, utility, and minimum-rate guarantee. Subscribers of a type are interchangeable, and \(\mu_t\) is their fraction of the population. A type’s schedule may be fractional, as in the paper; the aggregate channel load is then the type’s mass times its per-member schedule. This is a population mirror: the paper’s fractional time allocations make resource use continuous, but its customers are still individually indexed.

Here is the problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Continuous Spectrum-Pooling Core Computation, and take it as the lead mirror. The input is a finite set of providers \(N\); channels with provider owners; a finite state set \(\Omega\) with rational probabilities; and a finite support \(T\) of customer types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to one. Each type \(t\) has an owner \(i(t)\), channel rates \(R_{lt}(\omega)\), an increasing concave utility \(u_t\), and a minimum expected rate \(m_t\). Each provider has a convex cost function \(C_i\) for its channel use. The instance also gives each channel a capacity density \(q_l\): available channel capacity per unit of population. This scaling describes a regime in which radio capacity grows with subscriber population, for example through additional cells and spatial reuse.

For a coalition \(S\), let \(a_{lt\omega}\in[0,1]\) be the share of type \(t\)’s members scheduled on channel \(l\) in state \(\omega\), for channels and types owned by \(S\). Require \(\sum_l a_{lt\omega}\leq 1\) for each type and state, \(\sum_t\mu_t a_{lt\omega}\leq q_l\) for each channel and state, and \(\sum_\omega p_\omega\sum_l a_{lt\omega}R_{lt}(\omega)\geq m_t\) for each type. Define \(v(S)\) as the maximum expected total utility of its types minus its providers’ convex channel-use costs, using \(r_t(\omega)=\sum_l a_{lt\omega}R_{lt}(\omega)\) and \(z_l(\omega)=\sum_t\mu_ta_{lt\omega}\). The requested solution is a grand-coalition schedule attaining \(v(N)\) and a payoff vector \(x\) such that \(x(N)=v(N)\) and \(x(S)\geq v(S)\) for every coalition \(S\). In other words, return both an efficient schedule and a core imputation.

I expect this version to be tractable when utilities and costs have explicit rational piecewise-linear descriptions: the finite-type scheduling problem becomes a linear optimization problem, and Theorem III.1’s dual-price construction is the reason to expect the core shares to be obtainable without separately searching over every possible payoff division. For general convex functions, tractability depends on how those functions are represented and optimized. Further questions include whether the dual construction remains efficient when the type set or state space is implicit, and how accurately the continuous solution approximates a finite network when channel capacity does not scale smoothly with subscriber count.

This mirrors the fixed-deployment, transferable-payoff spectrum-pooling result; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s base-station deployment decisions, multi-hop extension, or nontransferable-payoff game. The weakest point is the capacity-density assumption: with fixed total radio capacity, per-capita supply vanishes as the population grows. That assumption is a co-modification of the resource model, but it fits settings where capacity is provisioned as networks grow. If real subscribers have sufficiently idiosyncratic contracts or rate profiles, the number of types may approach the number of customers and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem III.1 is the strongest anchor, but it is a core-existence theorem: an optimal dual solution for the grand coalition induces a payoff vector in the core. The paper’s polynomial-time statement appears in the discussion, not as a numbered complexity result, and it does not specify an input model for the concave utility and convex cost functions. That leaves the mirror without a clear computational guarantee to extend.

The best population version is still easy to describe: group interchangeable subscribers by provider, coverage and rate profile, then give each type a rational mass. With per-capita capacity scaled to keep service levels meaningful, a schedule and stable provider payoff shares form a coherent problem. But for symmetric, separable utilities and costs, the type-level version is just the paper’s existing convex optimization with repeated customer coordinates compressed into \(\tau\) types. With explicit piecewise-linear functions it is an LP; the dual argument for the core carries over directly. The population change removes redundant input, but it does not expose a new computational bottleneck or require the continuous-optimization machinery that the paper itself is already using.

Making the mirror substantially harder would require adding richer interactions between subscribers or more elaborate resource-scaling rules. Then the difficulty would come from those added primitives and their representation, not from continuizing the population. Alternatively, one could make providers the large population. That is a poor fit to this theorem: the core is over provider coalitions, and each provider’s customers, channels and infrastructure help determine which coalitions are feasible. Treating many such providers as interchangeable risks changing the coalition game rather than compressing its population.

The negative case is weakest at the subscriber scenario. Large subscriber bases with repeated profiles and capacity that grows with demand are plausible, and the resulting mass model is mathematically sound. A reader who counts a direct, tractable high-multiplicity reformulation as worthwhile can keep this mirror. The narrower case against it is that this paper offers no numbered complexity result for that reformulation, and its most natural version appears to be routine aggregation of an optimization problem that is already convex.
