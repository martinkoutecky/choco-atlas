The case FOR (proponent)

There is one important limit to the positive case: this paper gives no named computational-complexity result. Its numbered results concern stability and welfare, not whether finding an equilibrium is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. I can’t honestly use it to ask whether a known hardness result dissolves in the continuum. The strongest result to mirror is Theorem 3, which the paper presents as its own result, without attributing it to an external source: for hedonic project games with universal concave, monotonic decreasing preferences and identical project rewards, \(\mathrm{PoA}_{NS}(\mathcal G)=\mathrm{PoS}_{JS}(\mathcal G)=1\). In full, the worst Nash-stable outcome is socially optimal, and the best joining-stable outcome is socially optimal. This is an efficiency anchor, not a complexity anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large, standardized project platform with many workers and relatively few equal-reward projects. Workers have identical eligibility and parameters, and share the same preference for coalition size; the number of workers is much larger than the number of projects, while the number of types is \(\tau=1\). That is a narrow regime, but it matches the preference class in Theorem 3. A type here is the complete shared worker profile, and mass is the fraction of workers assigned to each project.

Call the mirror Continuous HPG Stable-Welfare Computation. An instance gives \(m\) projects with common reward \(r>0\), total population mass \(1\), and a common preference function \(q:[0,1]\to\mathbb Q_{>0}\), represented as a rational piecewise-linear, concave, non-increasing function. A feasible decision is an allocation \(x\in\mathbb R_{\ge 0}^m\) with \(\sum_j x_j=1\), where \(x_j\) is the population share on project \(j\). A worker on project \(j\), whose mass is \(x_j>0\), receives utility \(u_j(x)=r q(x_j)/x_j\). This retains the paper’s product of coalition preference and equal reward share: the size-based preference depends on the project’s population share, and the project reward is divided among its members. The factor \(1/x_j\) is the finite per-person share \(1/(n x_j)\) after a common rescaling of utilities, which preserves deviation comparisons and welfare ratios.

The task is to compute, for this instance, a welfare-maximizing allocation, the worst and best welfare among stable allocations, and the corresponding \(\mathrm{PoA}_{NS}\) and \(\mathrm{PoS}_{JS}\). Stability is the nonatomic, or Wardrop, limit: positive mass can sit only on projects offering maximal utility. An infinitesimal entrant to an empty project has limiting utility \(+\infty\), since \(q(0)>0\); so every project must be occupied at equilibrium. Since \(q(x)/x\) is strictly decreasing, equal utilities then require \(x_j=1/m\) for every project. Aggregate welfare is \(W(x)=r\sum_{j:x_j>0}q(x_j)\). Concavity makes the equal allocation welfare-maximizing as well. Thus the mirror predicts both ratios are \(1\), and the exact allocation is immediate. In this nonatomic limit, the joining-consent check is also unchanged by an infinitesimal entrant, so joining stability coincides with Nash stability here. I expect this formulation to be tractable—Class A—with an exact algorithm given by the equal split. That is my conclusion about the mirror, not an algorithmic claim made by Theorem 3.

The authors should recognize the model as a continuous version of the particular problem in Theorem 3: it replaces the count of agents in a project by their population share while keeping the same size-based preference and reward-sharing trade-off. The anchor is especially well suited to a population mirror because the theorem’s universal preference class needs only one type. The mirror covers Theorem 3 alone; it does not extend the paper’s arbitrary-preference results, its unbounded-PoA result, or its other stability theorems. The rewards being divisible already is not the reason this is a continuous mirror—the population mass is.

The main follow-up questions are whether the guarantee survives heterogeneous rewards, whether it extends to several types whose preferences depend on coalition composition, and how the nonatomic stability notion compares with finite one-agent deviations as population grows. The weakest point is that infinitesimal deviations erase the effects on existing coalition members, so the paper’s joining-stability refinement loses force in this mirror. And because Theorem 3 is not a complexity result, this tractable case does not establish that continuization has removed any discrete hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no numbered computational result to mirror. Its theorems concern stability existence and welfare ratios; its conclusion identifies the complexity of finding stable outcomes as future work. That leaves no proved complexity boundary whose change under continuization this paper could reveal.

The proponent’s Theorem 3 is the best anchor, and its proposed model is coherent. But in its one-type, equal-reward version, the continuous problem collapses: an infinitesimal agent prefers any empty project under the proposed \(q(x)/x\) utility, so every project must be occupied. Since that utility decreases with load, stability forces \(x_j=1/m\) for every project. Concavity makes the same split welfare-maximizing by Jensen’s inequality. The algorithm is therefore just an equal split; the function \(q\) does not affect the answer. This is a valid continuous counterpart to the welfare statement, but it supplies no computational landscape. The paper’s finite joining-stability check also concerns the effect of an entrant on existing members; in the atomless model that effect vanishes. In this particular preference class the theorem’s conclusions survive anyway, but the mirror does not preserve a nontrivial consent question.

A richer mirror—with several worker types whose preferences depend on the composition of their project teams—could retain the paper’s central collaboration-versus-reward trade-off and pose interesting questions about stable allocations. That is the strongest objection to a universal negative. But it leaves Theorem 3’s homogeneous preference assumptions behind, and the paper gives no computational result to anchor the new problem. Such a project may be worthwhile as new continuum game theory; the paper itself does not make it a computational mirror in ChoCo’s sense.

So the negative case is strong against prioritizing this paper for ChoCo’s computational programme, but weak as a claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The homogeneous mirror is mathematically sound; its weakness is that it answers the theorem by an immediate equal split rather than opening a substantive computational question.
