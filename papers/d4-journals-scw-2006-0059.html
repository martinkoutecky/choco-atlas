The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a named computational result in this paper that can anchor a continuous mirror. Propositions 1 and 4 show that under positive argumentation, or under \(D^*\), Free Disposal and Procedural Invariance force the persuasion rule to be constant. Proposition 2 characterizes the nonconstant rules permitted under negative argumentation using a unique decisive attribute set \(M^*\). Proposition 3 says that, when both positive and negative argument types are available, the plenary winner depends only on the negative arguments. These are axiomatic characterizations; none asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classifies its complexity.

The nearest computational result is the paper’s report that finding analogies is NP-complete, attributed to Aragones et al. (2001). It is neither a numbered result of this paper nor a complexity result about Spiegler’s formal debate model, so it cannot serve as an anchor here.

A possible extension would give an audience a distribution \(\mu\) over types of debate judges, where each type completely specifies how a listener evaluates argument sets. One could ask which party wins a majority in each session, or how much audience mass must change type to alter a plenary outcome. The audience in the paper’s motivation makes this imaginable, but the formal model has a single persuasion rule and does not aggregate listeners. That extension changes the problem rather than continuizing a result of the paper. I therefore cannot give a continuous problem per anchor, or credibly predict its place in the tractable/hardness trichotomy.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Propositions 1 and 4 characterize persuasion rules under axioms; Proposition 2 characterizes the permitted bias under negative argumentation; Proposition 3 says which argument type determines the plenary winner. None defines an instance of a computational problem or studies its complexity. The cited NP-completeness result for finding analogies belongs to a different model and a different paper.

The paper also has no population hidden in its formal objects. It has two role-labeled parties and a state assigning attributes to four possible worldviews. The attributes are not people, and the four worldviews are not voter types. Repeating a worldview or attribute does not create mass or change the argument sets. The parallel and plenary sessions are three procedures applied to the same state, not three populations to aggregate. Turning the number of issues, attributes, or debates into a continuous quantity would continuize something other than the population.

The audience extension is the most plausible proposal, since the paper mentions audiences in its motivation. Give each listener a complete evaluator type, distribute the audience over those types, and ask how much mass must change type to flip the majority’s verdict. That is a coherent audience-aggregation model. Its new primitives, though, are the individual evaluators, the aggregation rule, and the costs of changing audience types. The paper supplies none of them: its persuasion rule is one common convention that maps argument sets to a winner. If each evaluator votes on every session, even Procedural Invariance becomes a new collective property: majorities in the two parallel sessions may consist of different listeners. With evaluator types and conversion costs specified, the resulting control or robustness problem could be interesting, but its computational questions arise from those additions rather than from the paper’s characterizations.

So none of the propositions offers an anchor, and the audience proposal does not rescue one. The negative case is strong for this paper as a continuization target. Its limit is the universal claim: a researcher could build a worthwhile continuous model of heterogeneous debate audiences that the authors might recognize as adjacent to their subject. The text does not rule out that possibility; it gives little reason to treat such a model as a continuous mirror of any result here.
