The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. The closest is Theorem 1, proved here: for a closed convex feasible set and a symmetric benchmark allocation, the uniform-gains rule is efficient, symmetric, envy-free, strongly group-strategyproof, and benchmark-guaranteeing; it is also continuous under the stated conditions. Proposition 2, also proved here, characterizes that rule as the unique continuous focal rule for a specified class of symmetric division problems. These are mechanism-design possibility and characterization results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, hard, or belongs to another complexity class. The paper gives no running-time analysis or encoded computational problem to anchor a continuous complexity question to.

A plausible population regime would be a large rationing economy: many workers or consumers share complete single-peaked preference types, the input is a rational distribution over those types, and a per-capita supply constraint scales with the population. But turning the paper’s finite-agent rule into a weighted continuum rule, then asking for its computational complexity, would be a new extrapolation. The paper does not establish that this is the continuous analogue of a named computational result. Since there are no eligible anchors, there is no per-anchor prover question I can responsibly offer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case for “no qualifying mirror” is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 proves that the uniform gains rule has specified incentive, efficiency, fairness, and continuity properties. Proposition 2 characterizes that rule uniquely for a class of division problems. Neither states a complexity bound for an encoded problem, and the paper supplies no input model or algorithmic analysis. The fact that the rule is defined by a leximin maximization does not fill that gap: with an arbitrary closed convex feasible set \(X\), the paper does not say how \(X\) is represented or how to compute the maximizer.

There are plausible high-multiplicity settings here. A large rationing economy could have many consumers sharing single-peaked preference types, with supplies scaling per capita. That makes the population regime sensible; it does not make either theorem a computational result. A continuum version of the existence or characterization claim would be analytic mechanism design. Asking how efficiently to compute a rule from type masses would be a new computational question, requiring a specified representation of feasibility, the mass distribution, and the desired output. It might be worthwhile on its own, but it would not mirror a computational claim made in this paper.

The most favorable candidate is the symmetric division result in Proposition 2, since its uniform rationing rule offers a concrete object to compute. Yet uniqueness among continuous focal rules is still an axiomatic characterization, not a complexity statement. A high-multiplicity treatment could build on it, but it cannot supply the missing computational anchor. The paper’s other examples and its continuity results do not change that assessment.

So I would defend the negative verdict for ChoCo’s stated programme: no anchor raised here survives the requirement to mirror a named computational result. The limit of that verdict is the word “any.” This paper could inspire an adjacent computational project; the material does not establish that no such project would be worthwhile. The stronger, supportable claim is that such a project would begin beyond the paper’s results, rather than continuize their computational landscape.
