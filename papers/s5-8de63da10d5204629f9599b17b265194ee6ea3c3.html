What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a continuous computational mirror; deciding the welfare relation or aggregating payoffs over normalized positions would introduce a new computational question.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the paper’s repeated-buyer setting. A large online marketplace could have many buyers sharing the same preferences, payoff problem, and signal technology, with each buyer learning from earlier purchase actions. In that regime, the number of buyers could be large while the number of intrinsic agent types stays at one. That makes a population-scale version plausible as a *scenario*.

The difficulty is that the order of buyers is doing essential work. A buyer’s position determines the history they observe, and the paper’s results concern each agent’s payoff at each position. Collapsing buyers into type masses risks erasing that sequence. The paper has an infinite countable stream, but it does not model society as a distribution of population types or study a population-level computational choice.

I find no named computational result that meets the requested anchor rule. The closest results are analytical:

  • Theorem 1 characterizes \(\pi \succeq_S \pi'\) by comparing every agent’s payoff under every equilibrium for \(\pi\) with the payoff from repeated observable signals under \(\pi'\).
  • Theorem 2 gives a sufficient condition for that relation using an intermediate mixture of full and no information; Proposition 2 gives an equivalent inequality for checking that condition.
  • Theorem 4 shows that in the specified binary environments, a Blackwell improvement weakly raises each agent’s payoff through a finite horizon \(N\).

These results characterize welfare comparisons, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. Proposition 2’s finite inequality is checkable, but the paper does not present it as a complexity result.

A natural *follow-up* would be: given two finite rational signal structures, decide whether \(\pi \succeq_S \pi'\). Theorem 1 gives a characterization, and Theorem 2 with Proposition 2 gives a sufficient certificate. But that is not a continuous-population mirror: the input is a pair of signal structures, and the population masses do no work in the problem. So the positive case is that the paper supplies a credible large, homogeneous-agent scenario and a natural computational follow-up. Under the stated ChoCo anchor requirement, it supplies no valid anchor for a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes the welfare order \(\succeq_S\); Theorem 2 and Proposition 2 give a sufficient comparison criterion; Theorem 4 proves a finite-horizon payoff monotonicity result. None classifies an algorithmic problem or gives an algorithm, hardness result, or parameterized bound. Turning any of them into a question about deciding \(\succeq_S\) would require specifying a finite input, restricting the universal quantification over decision problems and horizons, and asking for complexity. That could be a worthwhile new project, but it would supply the computational question the paper does not ask.

The population story is plausible: many marketplace buyers may share the same preferences and signal technology. But the buyers in this paper are not interchangeable units for the results it proves. Agent \(i\) observes \(i-1\) actions, and payoffs can depend on that position. Collapsing them into one mass type loses the sequence. Treating normalized position as a continuum of types preserves the order only by making rank the central coordinate; then an aggregate payoff question is a new formulation, while the original results still concern each indexed agent. In an atomless sequential population, one agent’s action also has zero mass, so preserving its effect on later beliefs needs an added cohort or signal-aggregation mechanism.

Theorem 1 is the best candidate for a computational follow-up, but its characterization quantifies over every decision problem, every agent, and every equilibrium. The paper does not turn that into a finite computational task. Theorem 2 with Proposition 2 gets closer: their inequality gives a finite check for a sufficient condition on two signal structures. Yet that check is about the information structures, not population masses, and the paper does not establish that the condition characterizes \(\succeq_S\) in general. An algorithm for checking the inequality would compute the stated certificate; it would not mirror a computational result of the paper.

Theorem 4 does not rescue the case. Its \(N\) is a finite horizon, and its conclusion compares payoffs agent by agent under binary signals and actions. Integrating those payoffs over normalized positions might define a continuous aggregate-welfare question, but that changes the object being compared and remains an analytical extension, not a complexity problem. The paper’s own extension to dynamic signals also underscores the modelling burden: marginal signal types alone can miss cross-agent dependence that changes social value.

I would concede that repeated-buyer cohorts could motivate adjacent work, and I cannot rule out every future computational model one might build from the setting. The negative case is narrower: none of the three proposed anchors currently gives ChoCo a computational population question to mirror. Making one do so requires adding the computational problem and the population formulation, rather than continuing an existing result.
