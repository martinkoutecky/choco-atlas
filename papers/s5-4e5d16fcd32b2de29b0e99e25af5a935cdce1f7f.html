The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\ge k\ge3\), a finite \(P\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\), and rational distributions \(\nu_g:P\to[0,1]\) with \(\sum_{p\in P}\nu_g(p)=1\), form types \(t=(g,p)\) of mass \(\mu_{g,p}=\nu_g(p)/m\). Choose \(r_g\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu_g)\) and a \(k\)-multiset \(y\) of entries of \((r_g)_{g=1}^m\) to minimize \(\operatorname{SC}_\nu(y)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{g=1}^m\sum_{p\in P}\nu_g(p)\max_{\ell\in[k]}|p-y_\ell|\); determine the best worst-case approximation ratio, including whether every algorithm has ratio at least \(2k\) and whether the central-median mechanism achieves at most \(2(k+1)\).

The model it lives in

A finite-type high-multiplicity model with types \(t=(g,p)\), district masses \(\nu_g(p)\), representative choices \(r_g\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu_g)\), and a \(k\)-multiset of representative locations minimizing the mass-weighted max-distance social cost.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the unrestricted max-variant bounds in Theorems \(6.3\) and \(6.4\), including the \(2(k+1)\) upper and \(2k\) lower bounds; it leaves the strategyproof mechanism-design results, all sum-variant results, and exact high-multiplicity complexity unresolved.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(2k\) lower bound and \(2(k+1)\) upper bound gap close for continuous finite-type populations?
  • Can the continuous optimum be computed exactly or approximated more closely in polynomial time?
  • How does the ratio change when district masses, support sizes, or within-district distributions vary?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity, mass-based version of the paper’s unrestricted max-variant problem. I would not use the paper’s strategyproofness theorems as anchors: with genuinely nonatomic agents, an individual report has zero effect on the aggregate distribution, so strategyproofness risks becoming vacuous.

Take \(m\) fixed administrative districts and a finite set \(P\subseteq\mathbb{Q}\) of possible positions. A type is \(t=(g,p)\), recording both district \(g\) and position \(p\); the district label is part of the type because the representative constraint depends on it. For each district \(g\), let \(\nu_g(p)\) be the fraction of its residents at \(p\), with \(\sum_{p\in P}\nu_g(p)=1\). The whole society is the distribution \(\mu_{(g,p)}=\nu_g(p)/m\). Thus \(\tau\le m|P|\), while each type may represent arbitrarily many residents.

This is a credible regime for regional facility planning: many residents belong to one of a few districts, and residents within a district share an ideal access point or location type. The district must nominate one representative location, and facilities can only be opened at nominated locations. The continuous object is the population mass, not the facility outcome: there are still exactly \(k\) facilities, and they are still chosen as a \(k\)-multiset of representative locations.

A feasible solution chooses \(r_g\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu_g)\) for every district and then chooses \(k\) entries from the multiset \((r_g)_{g\in[m]}\). For the max variant, its cost is \(c_{\max}(p,y)=\max_{\ell\in[k]}|p-y_\ell|\), and its social cost is

\[ \operatorname{SC}_\nu(y)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{g=1}^{m}\sum_{p\in P}\nu_g(p)c_{\max}(p,y). \]

The underlying continuous problem is therefore:

“Given \(m\), \(k\), the finite type distribution \(\mu\), and the line positions, choose all district representatives and then \(k\) feasible facility locations minimizing \(\operatorname{SC}_\nu(y)\). Determine the best worst-case approximation ratio achievable by algorithms for this problem.”

My lead anchor is Theorem 6.4, proved in this paper. It states that, in the max variant, for every \(k\ge2\), every algorithm has approximation ratio at least \(2k\). The proof uses one group with one agent at \(0\) and one at \(1\), plus \(k-1\) groups whose agents are all at \(1\). By symmetry, the first group’s representative can be assumed to be \(0\), forcing facilities at \(0\) and \(1\). The algorithm’s total cost is \(2k\), whereas opening all \(k\) facilities at \(1\) has cost \(1\).

The same witness is a genuine continuous-population instance. Let the first group have \(\nu_1(0)=\nu_1(1)=1/2\), and let every other group have \(\nu_g(1)=1\). There are only \(2k\) possible types, but each can represent an arbitrarily large mass of residents. If the first group nominates \(0\), every resident has max-cost \(1\), so \(\operatorname{SC}_\nu=1\). If it nominates \(1\), all facilities can be placed at \(1\); only half of the first group’s mass pays cost \(1\), giving \(\operatorname{SC}_\nu=1/(2k)\). Hence the ratio is exactly \(2k\). The discrete lower-bound construction survives unchanged after replacing counts by masses.

The corresponding prover-ready question is:

“Does every algorithm for Continuous Distributed Max-\(k\)-Facility Optimization have worst-case ratio at least \(2k\), even when \(P=\{0,1\}\), \(m=k\), and all group masses are rational?”

The answer should be yes, by the direct mass version of Theorem 6.4. This is not an NP-hardness anchor—the paper contains no named NP-hardness result—but it is a computational approximation boundary that survives continuization. It is also not continuum-specific: the obstruction comes from the representative constraint and the max objective, not from the number of individual agents.

A second, constructive anchor is Theorem 6.3, also proved here. It states that, for \(k\ge3\) in the max variant, the mechanism \(M_k^{\max}\) has approximation ratio at most \(2(k+1)\). The mechanism chooses the leftmost median position of each group as its representative, sorts those representatives, and opens facilities at the \(k\) central representatives, namely the representatives with indices

\[ \left\lceil\frac{m}{2}\right\rceil+\ell-\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil, \qquad \ell\in[k]. \]

Its continuous mirror is exactly the same rule with “median” interpreted as the leftmost support position whose cumulative group mass reaches \(1/2\). The prover-ready question is:

“Given the distributions \(\nu_1,\ldots,\nu_m\), does the population-median/central-representative mechanism just defined always return a feasible solution with \(\operatorname{SC}_\nu(y)\le 2(k+1)\operatorname{OPT}_\nu\)?”

The expected answer is yes. The proof uses only ordering and lower bounds on mass lying to the left and right of representative locations, so the finite-agent counts can be replaced by masses. The mechanism is computable in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length of the rational masses: sort the support positions and compute cumulative sums.

Together these mirrors give a meaningful continuous approximation landscape: a polynomial-time \(2(k+1)\)-approximation and an unavoidable \(2k\) lower bound. The natural follow-up is whether the continuous optimum has ratio exactly \(2k\), whether the small gap closes, and whether an exact high-multiplicity algorithm exists. Unequal district masses, continuous within-district distributions, and a nonvacuous block-truthfulness notion are further questions.

This case covers only the paper’s unrestricted max-variant results, specifically Theorems 6.3 and 6.4. It does not claim to mirror the whole strategyproof mechanism-design programme or the sum variant.

The weakest point is that these anchors are approximation bounds, not complexity classifications. The paper gives no NP-hardness result whose discrete hardness might dissolve. Also, the continuous mirror has to retain district labels and finite support; allowing arbitrary nonatomic distributions while preserving individual strategyproofness would require a more careful model. But those are limitations of the extension, not reasons to reject this mirror: for the paper’s unrestricted distributed facility-location problem, the mass formulation preserves the exact phases, feasibility constraints, objective, and extremal witnesses while placing the population itself in the high-multiplicity regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The requested universal negative case is not credible here. The proponent has identified a genuine high-multiplicity regime, and both anchors survive it.

The strongest possible objection is that a nonatomic population has no individual whose position can be selected as a group representative. That objection applies to an arbitrary continuous-density model, and it also makes individual strategyproofness vacuous. But it does not defeat the better finite-type version. Let each district have rational mass over finitely many positions, with type \((g,p)\). A representative is then a position type in the district’s support; since agents of that type are interchangeable, no identity information is lost. This is exactly the programme’s high-multiplicity notion.

Theorem 6.4 transfers particularly cleanly. Take \(m=k\), let the first district have masses \(1/2\) at \(0\) and \(1/2\) at \(1\), and let every other district have all its mass at \(1\). If the first district nominates \(0\), every feasible distributed outcome contains both \(0\) and \(1\), so every unit of mass has max-cost \(1\), giving social cost \(1\). The unrestricted optimum places all \(k\) facilities at \(1\), with cost only \(1/(2k)\), hence ratio \(2k\). If the representative is \(1\), put the other districts at \(0\) instead. This is a valid rational finite-type society with arbitrarily many agents per type, not a merely formal limit.

Theorem 6.3 also survives. For each district, define its representative as the leftmost support position whose cumulative mass reaches \(1/2\), then choose the \(k\) central representatives. The proof’s counting statements become mass statements: at least half of each relevant district’s mass lies on the required side of its representative. Replacing agent counts by these masses leaves the approximation inequalities unchanged, yielding the \(2(k+1)\) bound. With explicitly listed rational supports, the mechanism is computable by sorting and cumulative sums.

One could object that these are approximation bounds rather than NP-hardness results, or that the transferred proofs do not produce a continuum-specific phenomenon. Neither is a valid rejection under the programme: approximation boundaries are computational results, and “continuization does not improve the ratio” is itself a legitimate answer. Moreover, the compressed rational-distribution formulation creates further questions about exact optimization and high-multiplicity complexity that the finite-agent paper does not answer.

Thus the only honest reservation is that this mirror does not preserve the paper’s strategyproofness programme; nonatomic individual manipulation would indeed be vacuous. But the proponent deliberately anchors on unrestricted computational results, where that issue is irrelevant. I cannot defeat these anchors without relying on the forbidden claim that a surviving answer is “just the same.” The negative case is therefore weak.
