What the mirror covers

The proposed mass analogue addresses Theorem 2's replication limit; Theorem 1's PCPNE–FRP-core equivalence and the example results remain unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a population-limit mirror of the paper’s replication result. It has a natural regime: a large industry with many small firms and a fixed set of willingness-to-pay types. Type \(t\) is a firm’s full willingness-to-pay function; mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of firms of that type. Replicating each of finitely many base types makes the number of firms grow while the number of distinct types stays fixed. That is unusually close to the economy the authors themselves study.

There is a strict limitation, though: the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 1, proved here, says that an allocation is in the free-riding-proof core exactly when some PCPNE produces it. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that under its stated smoothness, convexity, and concavity assumptions, PCPNE public-good provision converges to zero as the economy is replicated. Both are equilibrium and limit results; neither classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. So Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor-like foothold for a continuous-population argument, but it does not meet the requested computational-anchor criterion.

A concrete continuous question suggested by Theorem 2 is this. Let the population have finite type support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and type \(t\)’s willingness to pay be \(v_t(a)=\theta_t a\), with rational \(\theta_t>0\). Let production cost be \(C(a)=a^2/2\), the paper’s linear-utility, quadratic-cost case. A lobby is a measurable set \(A\) of agents, with type masses \(x_t\leq\mu_t\). Its efficient provision level is \(a=\sum_t x_t\theta_t\), and its surplus is \(V(x)=a^2/2\).

Ask for the greatest \(a\) supported by a continuous free-riding-proof core allocation. Such a solution consists of \(A\), its provision level \(a\), and a nonnegative payoff allocation \(u\) to lobby members, with \(\int_A u=V(x)\); every measurable subcoalition \(B\subseteq A\) must receive at least its worth, \(\int_B u\geq \tfrac12(\sum_t\theta_t\,|B\cap\Omega_t|)^2\); and almost every lobby member of type \(t\) must receive at least \(\theta_t a\), their limiting free-riding payoff. Here \(\Omega_t\) is the population of type \(t\). Payoffs are measured in per-unit-mass terms, matching the paper’s replication convention in which each individual’s willingness to pay is scaled down as the population grows.

This continuous problem has only the zero-provision outcome: the free-riding constraints imply \(\int_A u\geq a\sum_t x_t\theta_t=a^2\), while core efficiency requires \(\int_A u=a^2/2\). Thus \(a>0\) is impossible. Theorem 2 supports the plausibility of this limit, though it does not itself prove that this exact atomless formulation characterizes a continuous equilibrium. If forced into the programme’s trichotomy, the specified benchmark is trivially tractable, but it offers no computational landscape. It raises natural follow-ups about the rate of convergence in finite replicas and how the conclusion changes with excludability or atomic agents.

The main soft spot is also the reason to keep the claim modest: the paper studies equilibrium theory, not computational questions, and the continuous version collapses to zero provision. The authors would likely recognize it as a continuous analogue of their replication analysis; they would have much less reason to recognize it as a continuous computational social-choice problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 2. I would grant the proponent’s strongest modelling point: replication gives this paper a credible high-multiplicity regime. Firms can share a full willingness-to-pay type, and the paper explicitly studies economies replicated from a finite set of such types. There is no honest case that mass is meaningless here.

The problem is that Theorems 1 and 2 are equilibrium and limit results. Neither states the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic task with an input encoding and a running time. One could invent a problem of testing continuous FRP-core membership or finding the greatest supported provision level, but that would be a new computational question attached to an economics paper, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result.

The proposed mass version also exposes a deeper limit. Let \(x_t\) be the lobby mass of type \(t\), with linear values \(\theta_t a\), and let \(a=\sum_t x_t\theta_t\). Efficiency leaves \(a^2/2\) to allocate. But a lobby member’s normalized payoff must be at least \(\theta_t a\), the payoff from free-riding when removing one atomless member leaves provision unchanged. Integrating that floor over the lobby requires total payoffs of at least
\[ \sum_t x_t\theta_t a=a^2, \]
which exceeds the available \(a^2/2\) whenever \(a>0\). So the proposed continuous FRP-core has no positive-mass lobby in this benchmark. This is more than a dull answer: atomlessness removes the individual’s effect on provision while preserving a per-capita free-riding incentive, leaving no feasible allocation for a positive lobby.

The same problem remains in the strongest direct extension to smooth type-dependent valuations. At an atomless limit, a member’s departure leaves provision unchanged, so the free-riding floor is their full value \(v_t(a)\). Summed over the lobby, those floors equal gross benefits, while efficiency leaves only gross benefits minus \(C(a)\). Positive provision therefore cannot satisfy both conditions when \(C(a)>0\). Keeping departures consequential requires retaining a positive atom size; as that size vanishes, the model either collapses under normalized incentives or makes individual deviations vacuous under ordinary nonatomic payoffs. A minimum coalition size or other granularity could restore a nontrivial game, but then the atom size is doing essential work.

The paper’s replication theorem remains a natural and recognizable limit question, and the zero-provision limit is a valid economic finding. The strongest negative case is narrower: the continuous formulation either degenerates as an equilibrium object or requires new stability assumptions, and the paper supplies no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous economic model could be built from this paper; I can claim that the proposed anchor does not yield a worthwhile continuous computational mirror.
