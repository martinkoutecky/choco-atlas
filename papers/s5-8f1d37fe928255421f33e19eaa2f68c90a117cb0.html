The continuous mirror question

Given finite complete agent types \(T\), chore types \(E\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita supplies \(\lambda_e\), type entitlements \(r_t\), and additive costs \(v_t(e)\), define \(M_t^\infty=r_t\inf\{\rho:\exists y_{s,b}\ge0,\ \sum_b y_{s,b}=\mu_s,\ \sum_{s,b}b_e y_{s,b}=\lambda_e,\ y_{s,b}>0\Rightarrow v_t(b)\le\rho r_s\}\), where \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{E}\). Decide, or optimize, whether there exist \(x_{t,b}\ge0\) with \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}b_e x_{t,b}=\lambda_e\), and \(x_{t,b}>0\Rightarrow v_t(b)\le(2-\epsilon)M_t^\infty\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity weighted chore-allocation model: \(\mu_t\) is mass of complete worker type \(t\), \(\lambda_e\) is recurring chore supply, and \(x_{t,b}\) assigns mass to whole integral bundles \(b\); minimize the worst ratio \(\max_{t,b:x_{t,b}>0}v_t(b)/M_t^\infty\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Theorem 4’s \(2-\epsilon\) barrier and poses a compressed high-multiplicity analogue of Theorem 2. It leaves Theorems 1, 3, 5, and 6, together with Corollary 1, without separate continuous formulations.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the type-level mass formulation be formalized so that Theorem 4’s \(2-\epsilon\) lower bound is preserved under the continuous WMMS benchmark?
  • Does the compressed configuration problem corresponding to Theorem 2 admit polynomial separation, benchmark computation, and rounding in the number of types and chore types, independent of the clone count?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of weighted chore allocation in which both agents and recurring chore copies are represented by masses. The chores remain indivisible for every individual agent; only the aggregate assignment is continuous.

Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete agent types and \(E\) a finite set of chore types. A type \(t\) specifies an additive cost vector \(v_t(e)\) and a scaled entitlement \(r_t>0\). The population is \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the mass of type \(t\), and \(\sum_t\mu_t r_t=1\). Let \(\lambda_e\) be the per-capita supply of chore type \(e\).

This is exactly the high-multiplicity interpretation: for any sufficiently large \(N\), create \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), each with weight \(r_t/N\), and \(N\lambda_e\) copies of chore \(e\). Thus agents sharing a type have identical valuations and weights, while \(N\) may be enormous compared with \(|T|\) and \(|E|\).

An individual receives an integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{E}\), with cost \(v_t(b)=\sum_e v_t(e)b_e\). An aggregate allocation is a collection of masses \(x_{t,b}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_bx_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}b_ex_{t,b}=\lambda_e\). Hence type-level averages may be fractional, but no individual receives a fractional chore.

To preserve WMMS rather than replace it by average cost, define the continuous benchmark of type \(t\) by \(M_t^\infty=r_t\rho_t^\infty\), where \(\rho_t^\infty\) is the infimum, over all such mass partitions \(y\), of \(\sup_{s,b:y_{s,b}>0}v_t(b)/r_s\). This is the direct limit of the finite WMMS definition: the observer has type \(t\), while the recipient of bundle \(b\) has type \(s\). A feasible allocation \(x\) has ratio \(\alpha\) if every bundle assigned to type \(t\) satisfies \(v_t(b)\le\alpha M_t^\infty\). The objective is to minimize this worst ratio.

A natural scenario is a large service organisation with recurring task copies and many workers in a small number of contract or role types. Workers in the same role have the same disutility vector and workload entitlement; task copies are individually indivisible, but the organisation has many repetitions of each task. This is not an arbitrary relaxation: the paper itself repeatedly groups agents and items by identical weights, valuations, and costs. The new ingredient is making those groups the primary input.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states:

“For any \(0<\epsilon<1\), there is an instance for which no allocation is better than \((2-\epsilon)\)-WMMS.”

The instance uses \(k>4/\epsilon^2\) geometric agent groups, with \(\Delta=2^k\), \(n_i=\Delta^{i-1}\) agents in group \(G_i\), and \(m_1=1\), \(m_i=\frac32\Delta^{i-1}\) chores for \(i\ge2\). Agents in a group share a weight and valuation; chore groups are divided into homogeneous subgroups with carefully chosen geometric costs. Thus the construction already has only \(O(k)\) agent types and \(O(k)\) chore types, while the number of agents and chores is exponentially larger.

The relevant continuous question is:

\(\mathsf{HM\mbox{-}WMMS\mbox{-}OPT}\): given \((T,E,\mu,\lambda,r,v)\), compute the minimum \(\alpha\) for which an aggregate allocation \(x_{t,b}\) exists satisfying the mass, supply, and per-agent WMMS inequalities; in particular, decide whether \(\operatorname{OPT}_\infty<2-\epsilon\).

I expect the lower bound in Theorem 4 to transfer. Its proof does not merely rule out integral allocations: Appendix D.1 replaces an allocation by a fractional assignment \(f(a,e)\in[0,1]\) and proves that even this more permissive object cannot achieve a ratio below \(2-\epsilon\). A continuous mass allocation with integral bundles is weaker than that fractional relaxation, so it cannot evade the obstruction. The combinatorics live in the geometric sequence of agent and chore types, not in the identities of individual agents. This is therefore a Class-B-style transferred approximation barrier, although it is important not to mislabel it as NP-hardness: Theorem 4 is an inapproximability/existence lower bound.

The paper’s second useful anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here, though its proof invokes the cited PTAS of Hochbaum and Shmoys–Tardos rounding. It states:

“Given any instance and a constant \(\epsilon>0\), if there exists an \(\alpha\)-WMMS allocation, then a \((2\alpha+\epsilon)\)-WMMS allocation can be computed in polynomial time.”

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\(\mathsf{HM\mbox{-}WMMS\mbox{-}Approx}\): given rational \((T,E,\mu,\lambda,r,v)\), a constant \(\epsilon>0\), and the promise that \(\operatorname{OPT}_\infty\le\alpha\), output an aggregate integral-bundle allocation \(x\) with ratio at most \(2\alpha+\epsilon\), in time polynomial in the type and chore-type descriptions rather than in the implicit population size \(N\).

I expect this version to be tractable in the natural fixed-chore-catalogue regime. The individual-agent LP in Theorem 2 becomes a configuration LP whose columns are integral bundles \(b\). Column generation can price bundles using the additive cost structure; with a fixed number of chore types, this is a plausible Class-A continuous-optimization problem. The high-multiplicity formulation is valuable precisely because it asks for a running time independent of the enormous number of repeated agents and chores.

The unrestricted version is a useful boundary question. If the number of chore types is part of the input, configuration pricing becomes knapsack-like, and continuum-specific hardness may appear even though population multiplicity has been removed. Thus the mirror does not promise that every version becomes easy; it exposes exactly where the continuous optimization machinery stops helping.

The authors should recognise both questions as their problem. The fairness benchmark, asymmetric weights, additive chore costs, integral bundles, and approximation objective are unchanged. What changes is that repeated agent and chore identities are represented by masses. The paper’s fractional LP and fractional lower-bound argument are not a novelty collision: they relax allocations inside a finite society, whereas this mirror makes the society itself high-multiplicity and continuous.

The weakest point is the forced co-modification of chore supply. With a fixed finite set of chores and infinitely many agents, most agents would receive nothing and WMMS would degenerate. Scaling chore copies with population is therefore necessary, but it makes the mirror less literal than simply replacing \(n\) agents by a distribution. I would also not claim a numbered NP-hardness anchor, because this paper has none for WMMS itself; the strongest honest case rests on Theorem 4’s robust approximation barrier and Theorem 2’s algorithmic result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the proposed Theorem 2 mirror, not to the programme as a whole. The paper’s polynomial-time guarantee is measured in the explicitly listed numbers of agents and chores. After compression, the natural formulation is a configuration problem over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{E}\). Its columns can be exponentially numerous in the binary description of chore supplies, and pricing requires solving

\[ \max\{\text{dual value of }b : v_t(b)\leq \alpha M_t^\infty,\ b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{E}\}. \]

With variable \(|E|\), this is knapsack-like. Even with fixed \(|E|\), computing \(M_t^\infty\) and proving a polynomial separation and rounding procedure are new obligations. Theorem 2 supplies none of them: its finite LP, PTAS computation of WMMS, and Shmoys–Tardos rounding do not automatically become algorithms polynomial in the compressed type description. If the mirror instead allows fractional bundles, it ceases to be WMMS for indivisible chores and erases precisely the indivisibility responsible for the paper’s lower bounds. Thus the proponent has proposed a credible open extension, but not a transferred algorithmic result.

That objection does not defeat Theorem 4, however. The proposed continuous version of its lower bound is essentially exact. For a finite instance with \(N\) agents, assign type \(t\) mass \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), entitlement \(r_t=Nw_t\), and chore-type supply \(\lambda_e=q_e/N\). A mass distribution over integral bundles can be cleared of denominators to produce a finite allocation of cloned agents and chore copies, and every such finite high-multiplicity allocation induces the corresponding mass distribution. The normalized benchmark \(M_t^\infty\) is therefore not an average-cost substitute: it is precisely the clone-limit of WMMS.

Theorem 4’s geometric construction already has repeated agent and chore types, so its obstruction lives in type-level masses, weights, and supplies rather than named identities. Moreover, Appendix D.1 proves the lower bound even after relaxing integral allocations to fractional item assignments. Any aggregate allocation using integral bundles maps to that fractional relaxation, so it cannot evade the \(2-\epsilon\) barrier. The continuous question “can the high-multiplicity instance attain ratio below \(2-\epsilon\)?” is consequently well-posed and inherits the obstruction.

Calling this a mere restatement would not be a valid objection under the programme’s rules. It is exactly the desired negative boundary case: continuization does not dissolve a fairness barrier whose combinatorics survive cloning. The geometric parameters may be contrived, but they are a legitimate high-multiplicity regime, and the service-organisation interpretation is at least coherent.

So the honest negative case fails. Theorem 2 is not yet a continuous algorithmic result, only a worthwhile open compressed-configuration problem. But Theorem 4 survives as a faithful continuous mirror and is enough to prevent the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
