The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.1 is the only plausible anchor, and it establishes existence, not computation: under its economic assumptions, a competitive equilibrium under \(G_Q\) exists. It gives no runtime, algorithm, or complexity classification. Asking how to compute such an equilibrium from a finite type description might be worthwhile in its own right, but it would be a new computational problem, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The best population version would group consumers by complete type—preferences, endowments, and profit shares—and describe the economy by masses \(\mu_t\). That regime is sensible. But the theorem’s strategic mechanism depends on each consumer’s message affecting public-good provision and taxes. In a nonatomic population, one consumer has mass zero and cannot affect aggregate provision. Treating each type’s mass as a coordinated strategic bloc could preserve influence, but changes individual Nash behavior into a coalition problem. Either way, Theorem 3.1 does not carry over as stated.

The paper already uses continuous commodity and price spaces, but that is continuity of outcomes, not of the population. So it supplies no named computational result for ChoCo to continuize. I would reject it as an anchored mirror. The broader claim that no worthwhile high-multiplicity equilibrium-computation project could be built around this model is stronger than the evidence supports; such a project would need a separate computational motivation and result to study.
