The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 5 and 7 characterize when a positional correspondence satisfies a majority property; Proposition 12 characterizes when it fails the minimal majority property. None defines an algorithmic problem or proves a complexity bound. For Propositions 5 and 7, replacing the integer threshold \(\nu\) by a population share \(\alpha\) gives the direct analogue
\[ F_w(\mu)\subseteq M_\alpha(\mu) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha>\frac{\Gamma(w)}{\Gamma(w)+\gamma(w)}. \]
That is a continuous version of an axiomatic characterization, with no computational task for ChoCo to classify. Proposition 12 has the same limitation: a mass-based minimal-majority property can be studied, but it remains an axiomatic question.

Section 5 offers the strongest possible foothold. Give each ranking type a coordinate in the population simplex, sample societies uniformly from that simplex—the continuous limit suggested by IAC—and ask what fraction satisfy \(F_w(\mu)\subseteq M_\alpha(\mu)\). The event is describable through linear score and pairwise-majority inequalities, so an exact version becomes a polyhedral-volume problem. That is well-posed, and the paper’s discussion of difficult exact probability calculations makes it a natural extension of its simulations.

It still does not anchor the kind of complexity mirror ChoCo is seeking. The paper estimates probabilities for finite electorates under IC and IAC; it states no complexity result for computing those probabilities. The proposed volume problem adds a new computational objective, and its central difficulty is volume computation over the society simplex. The continuous population is present, but the result would classify that new geometric calculation, not a computational social-choice problem established by this paper.

A stronger intervention-style mirror could ask for the least mass transfer that creates a conflict between positional scores and a majority relation. That would be a plausible robustness problem over continuous societies. It requires choosing an intervention cost and a perturbation model that the paper does not supply, so it is an analyst-designed extension rather than a mirror of one of its results.

The negative case is strong against an anchored complexity mirror: none of the named propositions qualifies, and the Monte Carlo section leaves a gap to fill by inventing a new problem. It is weak against the broader claim that *no* worthwhile continuous question exists. I cannot honestly dismiss the IAC-limit volume question as meaningless or inherently out of scope. The paper fails to provide a computational anchor, but that alone does not establish the universal negative claim.
