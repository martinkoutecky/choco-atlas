What the mirror covers

The proposed analogue touches the impossibility themes of Theorems 2 and 3 only; it leaves the other results unmirrored, and none of the covered results is computational.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive seed is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for some profiles of separable preferences, no exchange rule is simultaneously efficient, individually rational, and destruction-proof. The counterexample uses two agents and extends to any larger number by adding agents who do not trade. Theorem 3, also proved here, gives a related impossibility: with at least three agents and some separable preference profiles, no rule is efficient, individually rational, and weakly transfer-proof.

Neither is a qualifying anchor for this task. They are impossibility results about the existence of rules, not claims about computational complexity or algorithms. The paper has no named result saying that a problem is hard, tractable, or parameterized-tractable. So I can’t honestly supply a ChoCo mirror *per qualifying anchor*.

There is a plausible continuous axiomatic analogue of Theorem 2. Consider a large exchange market where many participants share a type \(\theta=(R,E)\): the same preferences over bundles and the same endowed bundle. Let \(\mu_\theta\) be the fraction of participants of each type. To accommodate repeated participants, goods would need to be represented by replicated classes with per-capita supplies; an allocation could specify \(x_{\theta,S}\), the mass of type \(\theta\) receiving each integral bundle \(S\). The question would be whether a rule defined for every finite-support \(\mu\) can always produce efficient, individually rational allocations while preventing a positive mass of agents from improving by destroying part of their endowment.

A convincing regime would be a large organization exchanging repeated equipment or task types, with many agents but relatively few preference-and-endowment types. The difficulty is that the paper’s manipulation comparisons concern individual agents and deterministic bundles. A continuum version needs an explicit way to compare the outcomes of a mass of agents, and the replicated-goods model is an additional co-modification. More fundamentally, the proposed question asks whether such a rule exists; it does not ask a computational question about finding or verifying an outcome. That makes it a credible continuous extension of the paper’s economics, but a poor fit for this programme’s computational brief.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 are not computational results. They say that, for some preference profiles, no rule can satisfy specified axioms. They do not classify the complexity of finding an outcome or give an algorithm to mirror. Theorem 1 is also an axiomatic impossibility, while Proposition 1 establishes existence of a rule. The paper contains no named complexity result for ChoCo to continuize. The proponent’s suggested analogue of Theorem 2 is explicit about this: it asks whether an axiomatic rule exists, which is outside the programme’s computational brief.

The best version of that analogue could use a large organization with repeated equipment or task types. Let a type include preferences and an endowed bundle, let \(\mu_\theta\) be its population share, and represent replicated goods by per-capita supplies. Type-level variables \(x_{\theta,S}\) could record the mass receiving each integral bundle. That is a coherent co-modification, and the paper’s story can support it. But it does not preserve the paper’s manipulation questions. In an atomless population, one person destroying an object changes no mass in \(\mu\); the aggregate allocation records no outcome for that singular person. A positive-mass change is a group deviation, so a “destruction-proof” condition against it asks a different question. Theorem 3’s transfer condition is still harder to preserve: it compares the outcomes of a named donor and recipient. Aggregate bundle masses do not identify either person or track whether that recipient is better off. Retaining those comparisons requires individual tags or atomic agents, which loses the proposed low-support population description.

Replicating goods also needs care. In the paper, endowments are disjoint sets of heterogeneous objects, so no two agents own the same exact bundle of objects. Making many agents the same type requires treating distinct copies as interchangeable and replacing exclusive, named objects with per-capita stocks. That modification is admissible, but it does not fix the mismatch between individual deviations and mass changes. Nor does simply asking for an efficient, individually rational allocation provide a computational mirror: Pareto efficiency is a property of an allocation, while Theorems 2 and 3 concern whether a rule can satisfy axioms across markets. To get a per-instance optimization problem, one would need to add a selection objective or a mass-level manipulation notion that the paper does not supply.

So the negative case is strong for the proposed anchors: their direct continuizations are axiomatic, and the natural aggregate reformulation changes the unit of manipulation. It is weaker as a literal claim that no worthwhile continuous problem could be built from this market model. One could formulate a new algorithmic problem for replicated resources, but it would need its own computational objective and deviation concept; it would be a follow-on problem, not a mirror of the paper’s results.
