The continuous mirror question

Given finite house classes \(H\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_h\), a finite set of complete utility types \(T\) with rational utilities \(u_t(h)\), and rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, compute the minimum subsidy per capita for an envy-free allocation. Each individual receives one whole house, while type mass may be split across house classes: choose \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}\le q_h\). For each active type-house pair choose a common subsidy \(z_{t,h}\ge0\), require \(u_t(h)+z_{t,h}\ge u_t(h')+z_{t',h'}\) for every two active pairs \((t,h),(t',h')\), and minimize \(\sum_{t,h}x_{t,h}z_{t,h}\).

The model it lives in

A continuous population with rational type masses \(\mu_t\), complete utility vectors \(u_t\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_h\); the decision variables are type-to-house mass assignments \(x_{t,h}\) and cohort subsidies \(z_{t,h}\), with total subsidy per capita as the objective.

The objection that survived

The reduction's graph-bearing edge types each have mass \(1/N\), so it gives weak evidence for hardness when relevant types retain substantial mass; the proof also does not rule out escapes enabled by fractional splits.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is the paper’s main hardness result, Theorem 3.1. Proved in this paper, it says that computing a minimum-subsidy envy-free outcome is NP-hard. The proof establishes hardness of the threshold question too: given an instance and \(\gamma>0\), is there an envy-free allocation and nonnegative subsidy vector with total subsidy at most \(\gamma\)? It reduces from VERTEX COVER. For a graph \(G=(V,E)\) and bound \(k<|V|-1\), the construction has \(|V|^4\) special agents and houses; for each vertex \(v\), \(|V|^2\) agents, \(|V|\) \(v_{\mathrm{good}}\) houses, and \(|V|^2\) \(v_{\mathrm{bad}}\) houses; and one agent per edge. Special agents value special houses at \(1\). An edge agent for \(\{x,y\}\) values special houses and \(x_{\mathrm{good}},y_{\mathrm{good}}\) at \(1\). A vertex-\(v\) agent values \(v_{\mathrm{good}}\) at \(1+|V|^{-3}\) and \(v_{\mathrm{bad}}\) at \(1\); the other values are \(0\). The instance has an envy-free outcome with total subsidy at most \(k/|V|\) exactly when \(G\) has a vertex cover of size at most \(k\). The vertex-cover hardness is cited from Karp; Theorem 3.1’s reduction is the paper’s own.

I would mirror it with Minimum-Subsidy Envy-Free House Allocation over a Continuous Population. An instance gives finite house classes \(H\), with per-capita supplies \(q_h\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) satisfying \(\sum_h q_h\ge1\); a finite set \(T\) of complete utility types, where \(u_t(h)\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\); and rational type masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\). A solution assigns each individual one house and a nonnegative subsidy. In its finite cohort description, it specifies active type-house pairs \(S\), masses \(x_{t,h}>0\) for \((t,h)\in S\), and subsidies \(z_{t,h}\ge0\), with
\[ \sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{t,h}\le q_h. \]
For every two active pairs \((t,h),(t',h')\), envy-freeness requires
\[ u_t(h)+z_{t,h}\ \ge\ u_t(h')+z_{t',h'}. \]
The objective is to minimize \(\sum_{(t,h)\in S}x_{t,h}z_{t,h}\), the total subsidy per unit of population. Giving a type-house cohort one subsidy loses no generality under envy-freeness: people of that same type receiving the same house must receive equal subsidies, or they would envy one another. The computational input is the explicit rational data, measured by \(r=|H|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and bit length \(L\); the question is to compute the minimum and an attaining assignment, or decide whether it is at most a given rational threshold.

The setting is a large housing pool with repeated, interchangeable room classes—for example, a regional dormitory or temporary-housing system. Agents share a type when they have the same utility vector over those classes. Supplies scale with the population, while every individual still gets one whole house. This co-scaling of supply is part of the mirror: the paper itself allows \(m>n\), and its hardness construction has excess supply. The construction also makes the high-multiplicity regime unusually concrete. With \(N=|V|^4+|V|^3+|E|\), it has \(N\) agents but only \(1+|V|+|E|\) utility types and \(1+2|V|\) house classes. Normalize each agent count and house-class count by \(N\), and the paper’s instance becomes a continuous instance with threshold \(k/(|V|N)\).

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness should transfer. The reduction’s central structure is the graph incidence encoded in which utility types value which house classes. It does not rely only on choosing a particular one of many otherwise identical people. Fractional allocation of a type’s mass across house classes is allowed, but the vertex-cover argument appears suited to it: positive flow from an edge type to an endpoint’s good-house class identifies a cover, and the limited supply of that good class forces the remaining mass of that vertex type to absorb the same utility-gap subsidy. The mass inequalities scale with the instance; they are not tied to indivisible counts. Thus the continuous version has a credible route to the same NP-hardness, though the paper does not prove it. Further questions include whether approximation remains hard, and whether restrictions on the number of types or house classes make the problem tractable.

I would keep the scope to Theorem 3.1. The paper’s polynomial results—Theorem 4.1 for \(m=n+c\) with constant \(c\), and Theorem 4.6 for identical utilities—are not needed to support this hardness mirror and raise separate questions about which restrictions remain meaningful after scaling.

The weakest point is the fractional split itself: the original theorem allocates individual agents, whereas the mirror can divide each type’s mass among house classes and subsidy cohorts. That may create an escape from the vertex-cover lower bound. The normalized reduction and its support-based cover argument make the proposed hardness transfer plausible, but a careful proof must rule out subsidy or allocation tricks involving arbitrarily small positive masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.1 is a real anchor: it is a named computational hardness result, and the mirror of minimum-subsidy envy-free allocation is a sensible question. A large housing pool with repeated room or office types gives a plausible population regime. I cannot honestly rule out every such mirror.

The best objection is to the proposed hardness transfer. In the reduction, \(N=|V|^4+|V|^3+|E|\). The special type has mass \(|V|^4/N\to1\); all vertex types together have mass \(|V|^3/N\to0\), and all edge types together have mass \(|E|/N=O(1/|V|^2)\). Yet the graph’s incidence information sits in \(|E|\) distinct edge types, each of mass \(1/N\). Under normalization, the society approaches one dominant type while the graph-bearing population vanishes. That makes this a poor witness that a genuinely repeated housing population has the same difficulty: the reduction preserves the graph as a growing list of rare atoms.

There is also a proof gap in the proposed transfer. Normalizing the discrete construction gives a feasible continuous allocation, but the continuous problem also permits arbitrary fractional splits. The discrete proof’s counting argument does not by itself show that those splits cannot beat the threshold. The proposed positive-flow cover argument may be repairable, but it needs to handle tiny cohorts and their subsidies explicitly. This weakens the claim that the paper already supplies a robust Class B mirror; it does not make the continuous question unworthy.

A stronger, application-led mirror could use a housing pool with a stable set of utility types and substantial mass in the relevant types. But Theorem 3.1’s construction does not establish hardness for that regime. So the negative case is narrow: the paper’s specific reduction supports a degenerate, rare-type version of the mirror more clearly than it supports the natural high-multiplicity housing story. That is a meaningful reservation, but it falls short of the requested universal claim.
