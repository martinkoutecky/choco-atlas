Its main claims characterize mechanisms by strategyproofness, non-bossiness, neutrality, and efficiency, or prove fairness properties of RSDQ. The closest candidate is the theorem that RSDQ is strategyproof, ex post efficient, and envy-free. A generous mirror would take a large course-allocation market with many students sharing preference rankings and ask for type-level expected assignments. But the theorem concerns a lottery over orders of named agents, with quotas attached to positions in each order. Aggregating students by ranking does not by itself define the corresponding lottery over a continuum. One could specify a cohort limit, or scale objects and quotas with population, but either choice introduces a new model. And asking whether its mechanism remains envy-free or strategyproof would still be an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational focus. Asking how to compute its outcomes could be worthwhile, but that computational question is not asserted or classified by this paper.

The deterministic characterization theorems have the same problem: they say which mechanisms satisfy properties, not how hard it is to compute anything. Any algorithmic project built around these mechanisms would be new work inspired by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its results.
