The continuous mirror question

Given household types \(i\in[\tau]\), each with positive rational population mass \(\alpha_i\), weight \(w_i>0\), and nonnegative utility \(u_i(h)\) for each house type \(h\in[\sigma]\), and per-capita house capacities \(\beta_h\geq 0\) with \(\sum_h\beta_h\geq 1\), decide whether there are assignment masses \(x_{ih}\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_h x_{ih}=\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ih}\leq\beta_h\), such that for every pair of positive-mass assignments \(x_{ih}>0\) and \(x_{jg}>0\), \(u_i(h)\geq u_i(g)\) or \(u_i(h)/w_i\geq u_i(g)/w_j\). Determine the complexity in \(\tau\), \(\sigma\), and the binary input length \(L\), including when there are at most two weight values and three utility values. Each household still receives one whole house; the assignment masses describe cohorts receiving each house type.

The model it lives in

A feasibility problem over rational household-type masses and per-capita house capacities, with variables \(x_{ih}\) specifying how much of type \(i\) receives house type \(h\). Row sums meet population masses, column sums do not exceed capacity, and every pair of occupied type-house assignments must satisfy the paper's SumAvg-envy condition.

What the mirror covers

This mirror covers Theorem 3's hardness result for SAEF-House-Allocation. It leaves Theorems 1, 2, and 4, the observations, and the experiments unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the mass-assignment feasibility problem admit a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\), \(\sigma\), and \(L\)?
  • Does NP-hardness persist with at most two weight values and three utility values?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a high-multiplicity version of SAEF-House-Allocation, the paper’s one-house-per-agent problem. The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper by a polynomial-time reduction from 3-SAT: deciding whether a SumAvg-envy-free house allocation exists is NP-hard even with additive monotonic utilities, only two weight values, and only three utility values, which the construction takes to be \(0,1,M\). The paper’s fairness condition is that for every pair of agents \(a_i,a_j\), either \(u_i(\pi(a_i))\ge u_i(\pi(a_j))\) or \(u_i(\pi(a_i))/w_i\ge u_i(\pi(a_j))/w_j\).

A plausible regime is a large public housing allocation across a city or region. The agents are households; their weights can reflect household size or entitlement. A type records the household’s weight and its utility for each housing category. House types group houses that give the same utility to every household type. For example, a city might have hundreds of thousands of households, dozens or hundreds of distinct household types, and a modest set of standardized housing categories. This is the kind of regime in which mass is a natural description of demand and supply.

I would ask:

Mass-SAEF House Allocation. An instance has finitely many household types \(i\in[\tau]\), each with weight \(w_i>0\), utility \(u_i(h)\ge 0\) for each house type \(h\in[\sigma]\), and population mass \(\alpha_i\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), with \(\sum_i\alpha_i=1\). Each house type has per-capita supply \(\beta_h\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), with \(\sum_h\beta_h=1\). Determine whether there are assignment masses \(x_{ih}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_h x_{ih}=\alpha_i\) for each \(i\), and \(\sum_i x_{ih}=\beta_h\) for each \(h\), with the following fairness condition: for every pair of positive-mass assignments \(x_{ih}>0\) and \(x_{jg}>0\),
\[ u_i(h)\ge u_i(g) \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{u_i(h)}{w_i}\ge\frac{u_i(g)}{w_j}. \]
The output is any such assignment-mass matrix \(x\); this is a feasibility problem, as in the paper. A solution can be implemented by dividing each household type’s population into cohorts of the indicated masses and giving each household one whole house. The mass describes how many households get each category; no household receives a fractional house. The key test is whether feasibility can be decided in time polynomial in \(\tau,\sigma\), and the binary input length \(L\), including under the anchor’s two-weight, three-utility-value restrictions.

This is a close mirror of the underlying question in Theorem 3: find an allocation in which every agent receives exactly one house and no agent envies another under the paper’s SumAvg criterion. It preserves complete types: households grouped together share their weight and all utilities the model uses; houses grouped together share their utilities to all household types. The per-capita supply of houses is the needed co-modification when the population becomes a continuum. I think the authors would recognize it as their allocation problem in a high-multiplicity regime.

My tentative prediction is Class C, continuum-specific hardness. Once an assignment support is fixed, the mass-balance conditions are linear, but choosing a support whose assignment types are pairwise envy-compatible is still a combinatorial problem. Theorem 3 motivates the question but does not establish hardness for this mirror: a continuum of households of one type can be divided among house types, so the discrete reduction’s choices may become fractional. The useful follow-ups are whether that support choice is polynomial-time solvable, whether it is FPT in \(\tau+\sigma\), and whether hardness persists with two weights and three utility values.

This case covers Theorem 3 alone; it does not claim that the paper’s other results or its experiments have the same mirror. Its weakest point is that Theorem 3’s reduction has a number of agents and houses that grows with the 3-SAT instance, and the theorem does not show that its gadgets survive population fractionalization. That leaves the predicted complexity open—but it is also the central question a faithful continuous mirror should pose.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 3’s reduction builds a separate role for each agent and house. Its hardness result gives no evidence that the gadgets survive when a household type can divide its mass among houses. A continuous mirror would therefore need its own argument that the relevant housing regime has repeated types and that fractional assignments capture it faithfully.

The proposed formulation also needs a small repair: house allocation gives each household one house, but need not use every house. A mass version should require \(\sum_h x_{ih}=\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ih}\leq\beta_h\), where \(\beta_h\) is the per-capita supply of house type \(h\). That repair is straightforward. In a large public-housing system with standardized house categories and recurring household profiles, the masses have a clear interpretation: they describe cohorts of households, each still receiving one whole house. Grouping households by weight and their utilities for each category preserves everything the envy test uses.

So the reduction’s failure to transfer directly is a real uncertainty about the likely result, but it does not defeat the mirror. The corrected question is a well-posed computational problem in a plausible high-multiplicity regime. I cannot make an honest case that no worthwhile mirror exists for this anchor; the negative case is weak.
