What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make an anchor-based positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational complexity result. Its numbered results establish existence and structural bounds, but do not classify an algorithmic problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized.

The closest candidate is Theorem 3.7, proved here: for a necklace with two kinds of beads and \(k\) thieves, there is a binary necklace splitting using \(2(k-1)\) cuts. That is a combinatorial existence theorem, not a complexity result; treating it as an “in P” anchor would overstate what the authors prove. The paper says this distinction plainly for the fair-division result too: Corollary 4.7 is an existence result that “does not inform algorithmic aspects.”

A divisible-necklace mass-splitting question would make continuity a property of the *goods* being divided, while the thieves remain finite. That is outside ChoCo’s population-continuization scope. So there is no qualifying anchor here, and no continuous computational problem per anchor to propose honestly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no anchors to defeat: the appended “case for” names no computational result and argues that none of the paper’s results supplies one. That is the strongest objection here. The numbered results establish existence of fair divisions and lower bounds on colorful facets; none states the complexity of a computational problem. The paper itself says Corollary 4.7 is an existence result that “does not inform algorithmic aspects.”

The closest alternatives do not repair that gap. In necklace splitting, \(q\) counts kinds of beads, while the \(k\) thieves are recipients. Treating bead mass as the continuous population would continuize the goods, not the society. The binary-splitting condition also tracks which thieves receive adjacent pieces. In cake and rent division, the \(d+1\) people have individual preference sets and must be matched bijectively to \(d+1\) pieces. A continuum of preference types could motivate a new model, but it would need a measure-valued allocation and a computational input model; neither is specified by the paper’s existence theorem. In the quantitative Sperner results, vertices, faces, and labels describe a pseudomanifold’s structure, not a population of agents that can be grouped into types.

One could still propose a continuum-agent fair-division problem inspired by these topics. That would be a new computational question, not a mirror anchored in a named result of this paper. The universal claim that no such scenario could ever be worthwhile is hard to establish; the stronger, well-supported conclusion is that this paper gives no computational result from which ChoCo’s population-continuization programme can build a mirror.
