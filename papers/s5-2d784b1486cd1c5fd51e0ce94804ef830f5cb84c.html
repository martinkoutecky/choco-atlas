The case FOR (proponent)

On the standard you set, this paper has no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror on. Its sole in-paper theorem is unnumbered and proves a structural implication: divisions satisfying one set of degressive-proportionality conditions also satisfy another. It makes no claim about complexity, an algorithm, or a decision problem. The apportionment procedures discussed are proposals or results attributed to other works, not computational-complexity results established here. So there is no valid anchor number to quote and no per-anchor problem to hand to a prover.

There is a plausible follow-on for a large federation with many jurisdictions and relatively few repeated population and legal-constraint profiles. A type could specify a jurisdiction’s population and seat bounds, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of jurisdictions of type \(t\). The decision would be a distribution \(q_t(k)\) over legal integer seat allotments \(k\) for each type, giving a fractional type-level average \(x_t=\sum_k q_t(k)k\). One could ask whether these distributions meet a seat budget and the degressive-proportionality conditions on the type averages. This puts the jurisdiction population into the model and allows fractional averages while each jurisdiction receives whole seats. The paper’s authors might recognize the question as related to their apportionment problem, but it adds a feasibility question the paper does not analyze; its complexity class is open on the evidence provided.

That is only a speculative future mirror, not an anchor-based positive case. The soft spot is substantial: the paper studies apportionment among 27 Member States and presents a policy problem, while a convincing high-multiplicity regime and computational objective would need to be supplied from outside its results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no computational result to mirror. The paper’s theorem proves an implication between two formulations of degressive proportionality; it gives no complexity claim, algorithmic problem, or decision problem. The Cambridge Compromise and other allocation methods are discussed as proposals or prior work. A continuous study of their algorithms might be worthwhile, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The proposed jurisdiction model is the strongest way to rescue a mirror, but it changes the object. In this paper, countries are the recipients of seats; their citizens’ populations are inputs to constraints. Turning citizens into continuous mass leaves the 27 recipients and the indivisible seat allocation intact. Turning the *jurisdictions* into a continuous society instead requires many jurisdictions with repeated, fully specified population and legal profiles. For the actual European Parliament, there is no such multiplicity. In a larger federation, exact population is part of a jurisdiction’s type, so grouping near-equal populations would change the comparisons the rule is meant to enforce.

There is also a gap between the proposed type averages and the paper’s country-by-country guarantee. Average seat counts can satisfy degressive-proportionality inequalities even when some individual assignments violate them. One can repair that by constraining the distributions’ supports and scaling the mandate pool as the number of jurisdictions grows, but then one has defined a new high-multiplicity apportionment problem. It could be sensible in its own setting; it is not a continuous version of the paper’s theorem or of the 27-country policy problem.

So the negative case is strong against an *anchored mirror of this paper*: none is available. It is weaker against the broader claim that no related apportionment problem could ever merit continuization. The repeated-jurisdiction model is a plausible research direction, and I cannot honestly rule out its value; the case against it is that it is new work adjacent to this paper, not a mirror the paper itself motivates.
