The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of role types \(T\), a strict ranking \(\succ_r\) of \(T\) for each \(r\in T\), rational population masses \(\mu_r\), and a proposed community profile \(s\) with \(0\le s_r\le\mu_r\) and \(\sum_{r\in T}s_r>0\), decide whether the community is self-approving. It is not self-approving exactly when there are outsider masses \(y_b\) and, for each \(r\) with \(s_r>0\), a transport plan \(z^{(r)}_{ab}\ge0\) such that \(0\le y_b\le\mu_b-s_b\), \(\sum_b y_b=\sum_a s_a\), \(\sum_b z^{(r)}_{ab}=s_a\), \(\sum_a z^{(r)}_{ab}=y_b\), and \(z^{(r)}_{ab}=0\) unless \(b\succ_r a\). Decide whether no such common \(y\) and collection of plans exist.

The model it lives in

A society has rational mass \(\mu_r\) of each role type \(r\), and a proposed community selects mass \(s_r\) from each type. Each type ranks role categories; a replacement uses one shared outsider mass profile \(y\), while each evaluator type has its own feasible transport plan matching community mass to outsiders it strictly prefers. Recognition asks whether these matching plans do not exist, an LP feasibility or infeasibility problem with size polynomial in \(|T|\) and the input bit length.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 8’s self-approval recognition problem. It does not cover the group-stability hardness of Theorem 9, combined comprehensive-rule recognition in Theorem 10, or the paper’s structural and other algorithmic results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite-to-continuum dictionary be formalized so rational mass matchings correspond to matchings in replicated finite instances with the same category rankings?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a mirror of Theorem 8: this paper proves that deciding whether a subset \(S\) of a finite preference network is self-approving is coNP-complete. In full, self-approval fails if there is an equal-size outsider set \(G'\) such that each member \(i\in S\) has a bijection \(f_i:S\to G'\) that maps every \(u\in S\) to someone \(f_i(u)\) whom \(i\) ranks above \(u\). Theorem 8 proves the result here by reducing from 3-SAT; it is not merely a citation. The question is whether such a unanimously improving replacement exists for every member’s view of the community.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large platform or professional network with many people but relatively few recurring affiliation profiles. People in the same profile have the same preferences over profile categories, and treat members of a category alike. Let \(T\) be the finite set of categories, with \(|T|=\tau\). Each category \(r\in T\) has a strict ranking \(\succ_r\) over the categories. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) with \(\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r=1\); \(\mu_r\) is the fraction of the population in category \(r\). A proposed community is a mass profile \(s\) with \(0\le s_r\le\mu_r\) and \(\sum_r s_r>0\). The regime of interest has a very large population and modest \(\tau\). Here categories are complete types for the question: two people in a category are indistinguishable in the preferences and membership role the model uses.

Call the resulting problem Mass Self-Approval Recognition. Given \(T\), its rankings, rational \(\mu\), and a rational community profile \(s\), decide whether \(s\) is self-approving. A witness against self-approval is an outside mass profile \(y\), with
\[ 0\le y_b\le\mu_b-s_b,\qquad \sum_b y_b=\sum_a s_a, \]
and, for each type \(r\) with \(s_r>0\), a transport plan \(z^{(r)}\) satisfying
\[ \sum_b z^{(r)}_{ab}=s_a,\qquad \sum_a z^{(r)}_{ab}=y_b,\qquad z^{(r)}_{ab}=0\ \text{unless } b\succ_r a. \]
The same outside mass \(y\) must work for every type in the community, while each type may have its own transport plan. Such a plan is the mass version of the paper’s bijection: it matches all community mass to equally large outsider mass that the evaluator strictly prefers. The answer is yes exactly when no such \(y\) and collection of plans exist.

This is a concrete tractable mirror of the paper’s hard recognition problem. Witness existence is a linear-program feasibility problem with polynomially many variables and constraints in the number of categories and supported types, and rational input. Thus recognition is in P by LP infeasibility, giving a Class A prediction: the discrete coNP-hardness dissolves when interchangeable members and replacements can be represented by divisible mass. The paper itself notes that its framework can be extended to preferences with indifferences or ordered partitions; that makes category-level preferences a more credible extension than imposing an unrelated continuous objective. For rational inputs, a feasible rational transport plan can also be scaled to integer counts in a replicated finite population with the same category ties, giving a useful high-multiplicity dictionary for this mirror.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Theorem 8’s self-approval test, and therefore the self-approval component of comprehensive-rule membership. It does not claim to mirror the group-stability hardness in Theorem 9 or the combined membership result in Theorem 10.

The weakest point is that the paper’s main model ranks named individuals using strict orders, whereas this mirror ranks categories and makes people within a category interchangeable. The paper’s discussion of ordered partitions supports that co-modification, but Theorem 8’s reduction uses individual names as part of its combinatorics. Authors could reasonably say the category-level question is a close extension of their problem rather than its literal continuous limit. That is the point the positive case has to defend: the self-approval test itself remains intact, with bijections replaced by measure-preserving matches.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror changes what the candidates are. In the paper, \(V\) is both the population and the set being ranked: Theorem 8 asks whether named members of a network can be replaced by named outsiders, with each member’s own ranking determining what counts as an improvement. Its reduction uses those identities to encode clauses and variables. The mass LP instead ranks types and permits ties among all people of a type. It asks about replacing bundles of interchangeable people, not the paper’s original identity-sensitive replacement problem.

That distinction limits the mirror’s reach. If a type must preserve each member’s strict ranking over every named person, growing the population also grows the ranked universe. A fixed, modest set of types then requires either repeated rankings over a changing set of named candidates or a coarsening that treats candidates as type categories. The latter is the LP model; it is also the substantive modeling choice that needs defending.

But this objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. The paper explicitly allows extensions to ordered-partition preferences, and a large platform or professional network could plausibly have recurring role types whose members share preferences over role categories. In that setting, a community can still be a mass of people, and the common outsider mass \(y\) correctly represents the same replacement group for every evaluator type. The separate transport plans allow different evaluator types to match that group differently. With finitely many types, feasibility is a rational linear program of polynomial size. This is a coherent population-continuous question the authors could recognize.

Nor can I rule it out by saying the LP is too easy, that only one theorem is mirrored, or that a known high-multiplicity result has already settled it. The paper’s own programme treats structural insight plus LP as a meaningful outcome, and the supplied text gives no existing continuous computational treatment of self-approval recognition.

So the identity issue is a real caveat about how close the mirror is to Theorem 8, but it is not a decisive objection: the paper’s discussion of ties and a plausible category-based regime substantially answers it. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The negative case is weak here, and this anchor likely survives.
