The continuous mirror question

Given finite-support distributions μ_x and μ_y over welfare levels in a common interval \(X\), with rational levels and masses, equal mean welfare, and continuous Lorenz dominance \(\int_0^r Q_{μ_x}(s)\,ds \leq \int_0^r Q_{μ_y}(s)\,ds\) for every \(r\in[0,1]\), can μ_y be reached from μ_x in at most \(k\) moves? Each move chooses submeasures α,β of the current distribution μ with \(α+β\leqμ\), equal positive mass, and recipient welfare levels below donor welfare levels; for some \(t>0\) that keeps the shifted levels in \(X\), it replaces μ by \(μ-α-β+(z\mapsto z+t)_\#α+(z\mapsto z-t)_\#β.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population whose types are welfare levels on the paper’s common scale; the state is a finite-support distribution μ. A move transfers the same per-person amount \(t\) from an equal mass of higher-welfare donors to lower-welfare recipients, and the objective is to minimize the number of moves.

What the mirror covers

This mirror covers Theorem 2’s shortest-explanation problem for restricted Lorenz dominance. It leaves the paper’s other results, including its robust OWA characterizations, explanation-completeness results, and heuristic algorithms, without a population-level mirror here.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding reachability in at most \(k\) cohort moves as a function of support size τ, input bit length \(L\), and \(k\)?
  • Does allowing divisible cohorts make the minimum number of moves tractable, or is there a hardness reduction specific to this mass-transfer model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is Mass-Transfer Explanation. Picture a public authority comparing two ways of distributing the same total welfare among a very large population—for example, residents receiving a public benefit, measured on the common utility scale assumed by the paper. Agents with the same welfare level and transfer technology are interchangeable. A unit of mass represents a fraction of the population; the input lists the finitely many welfare levels in each alternative and their rational masses. In the intended regime, the number of people \(N\) is enormous while the number of distinct levels \(\tau\) is much smaller.

Here is the problem. An instance is two finite-support distributions \(\mu_x,\mu_y\) with rational masses and welfare levels, plus an integer \(k\). Require equal mean welfare and continuous Lorenz dominance: writing \(Q_\mu\) for a distribution’s nondecreasing quantile function, require
\[ \int_0^r Q_{\mu_x}(s)\,ds \leq \int_0^r Q_{\mu_y}(s)\,ds \quad\text{for every }r\in[0,1], \qquad \int_0^1 Q_{\mu_x}(s)\,ds=\int_0^1 Q_{\mu_y}(s)\,ds. \]
A move chooses equal masses \(q>0\) of recipients and donors, with the recipients lower in the welfare ranking. It gives each recipient \(t>0\) welfare and takes \(t\) from each donor, then re-sorts the population. In measure notation, choose submeasures \(\alpha,\beta\leq\mu\) of equal mass and replace the current state \(\mu\) by
\[ \mu'=\mu-\alpha-\beta+(z\mapsto z+t)_\#\alpha+(z\mapsto z-t)_\#\beta. \]
Mass-Transfer Explanation asks whether \(\mu_y\) can be reached from \(\mu_x\) in at most \(k\) such moves; its optimization version minimizes the number of moves. The moves are the action variables, and the objective is exactly explanation length. One may specify rational mass amounts and transfer sizes in a certificate. Rational input masses can be expanded to a finite equal-mass population, so the usual finite transfer construction supplies a finite chain whenever the Lorenz condition holds.

My anchor is Theorem 2, “Hardness of finding short explanations,” proved in this paper. It states: given \((x,y)\in L^*\) and a positive integer \(k\), deciding whether there is a \(T\)-ATX of length at most \(k\) for \((x,y)\) is NP-hard. Here \(L^*\) is restricted Lorenz dominance: \(x\) and \(y\) have equal total welfare and every cumulative sum of the sorted \(x\) is at most the corresponding cumulative sum of the sorted \(y\). A \(T\)-ATX is a chain from the sorted \(x\) to the sorted \(y\), with each link a redistributive transfer from a richer coordinate to a poorer one. The paper proves the hardness by reduction from 3-Partition. My proposed problem replaces the finite population of coordinates by welfare mass and generalizes a single donor-recipient transfer to the same transfer applied to equal-mass cohorts.

This is a plausible object for these authors because their comparison is already anonymous: reorderings are accepted freely, and the explanation’s substantive steps are redistributive transfers. The paper also explicitly motivates its framework with distributing investment revenues among agents. In the mirror, a type at a given state is its welfare level on the common scale; agents at that level are indistinguishable for this explanation task. If a setting has different costs or transfer rules, those must be part of the type. The coordinated cohort move is the continuous counterpart of a single transfer argument applied to indistinguishable mass; it keeps the paper’s step-count objective while making a move meaningful for a nonatomic population.

I would provisionally expect this to be Class C, continuum-specific hardness, though with low confidence. Theorem 2 does not establish hardness for the mirror: divisible mass can be split, so its 3-Partition reduction does not automatically survive. But minimizing the number of moves is still a minimum-support problem, which may remain hard even when mass is divisible. The main follow-up is whether the problem is polynomial in \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(k\), or whether a new hardness reduction works for divisible cohorts. If it is tractable, that would be a Class A result; the paper’s discrete hardness would then have dissolved. I do not see a basis for calling it hardness transfer without that work.

This case covers Theorem 2’s shortest-explanation result only. The paper’s continuous utility scale is outcome-space continuity; by itself it does not continuize the population. My proposed mirror does that separately. The weakest point is the move semantics: the paper counts a transfer between two coordinates, while this mirror counts one coordinated transfer applied to equal-mass cohorts as one step. That is a real model adaptation, and the authors or a referee might reject it as changing what “one step” means. I think it remains defensible because the paper’s steps are explanatory claims about redistributive changes, not operational counts of individual transactions, and applying the same claim to an indistinguishable cohort preserves that explanatory role.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, and it defeats the easiest negative case: this paper does contain a named NP-hardness result for a computational problem. The question is whether that problem has a faithful population-level version.

Its primitive is a \(T\)-ATX link: one transfer of amount \(t\) from one coordinate to another, followed by re-sorting. If the coordinates become a nonatomic population, an individual has zero mass, so the faithful limit of a single-coordinate transfer changes nothing. To get a nontrivial explanation, one must replace that link with a cohort operation: choose equal masses of recipients and donors and apply the same per-person transfer to both groups.

That is a plausible extension, but it changes what “one step” counts. A cohort link bundles many individual transfers into a single explanatory claim. The same redistribution might also be represented by splitting the cohorts into several links, or by pairing different portions of mass. The paper’s minimum-length objective therefore does not pass to the continuum on its own; the analyst must choose a new rule for what counts as one link. Restricting transfers to a fixed population quantum preserves the original operation, but then the problem is a high-multiplicity discrete instance. Allowing arbitrary rational masses gives a genuine mass-level problem, but its minimum number of steps measures the compression permitted by the new cohort syntax.

The strongest proposed mirror has a sensible setting: explaining why one welfare distribution Lorenz-dominates another for a large population. It could ask for the fewest equal-mass cohort transfers connecting the distributions. The paper’s focus on anonymity and redistributive arguments makes it credible that the authors would recognize this question. But Theorem 2’s 3-Partition reduction establishes hardness for coordinate transfers; it does not establish hardness for divisible cohorts. Nor does the paper tell us which cohort syntax preserves the intended explanatory meaning. That leaves a real modelling and complexity problem, but it is a new explanation calculus whose connection to the theorem’s step count must be justified.

So the negative case is limited. I can argue that the proposed mirror does not inherit Theorem 2 merely by replacing vectors with measures. I cannot honestly argue that no useful mirror exists: large-population welfare comparisons are plausible, and a carefully specified cohort explanation problem could be worth studying. The case against is strongest on fidelity to the paper’s computational object, and weak against the best population-level reformulation.
