What the mirror covers

The proposed threshold concerns Theorem 6's restricted-domain majority outcome; it does not cover the paper's general characterizations or its laboratory findings.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is the majority rule result, but there is an important qualification: this paper contains no numbered computational-complexity result. It does not prove that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest named anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper. It is an implementation result, not a complexity result; the computational problem below is a new mirror of it, not a result the authors claim.

Theorem 6 considers an odd number \(n\) of agents choosing between \(0\) and \(1\), with each agent strictly preferring one alternative. For each \(k\in\{2,\ldots,(n+1)/2\}\), it defines a domain containing profiles where either \(0\) has at least \((n+1)/2+(k-1)\) supporters or \(1\) has at least that many. On this domain, majority rule is \(k\)-securely implementable: truthful reports implement the socially optimal outcome, and every \((n-k)\)-dominant-strategy Nash equilibrium—where at most \(k\) agents may use a best response different from their dominant strategy—produces that same outcome.

Lead mirror: Continuous Mass-\(k\)-Secure Majority Robustness. The regime is a large referendum or membership vote with many people but only two relevant preference types: those who strictly prefer \(0\), and those who strictly prefer \(1\). People of a given type share the same preferences and voting circumstances. The population is represented by rational masses \(\mu_0,\mu_1\), with \(\mu_0+\mu_1=1\), rather than named voters. A bound \(\alpha\le 1/2\) is the maximum share allowed to submit nontruthful best-response reports.

Given \((\mu_0,\mu_1)\) with a unique truthful majority winner \(w\), ask for the minimum mass of \(w\)-preferring voters who can change their reports so that \(w\) loses its strict majority lead. The action variable is \(x\), the mass shifted from reporting \(w\) to reporting \(\bar w\). The objective is to minimize \(x\), subject to \(0\le x\le\mu_w\) and \(\mu_w-x\le\mu_{\bar w}+x\). The solution is \(x=(\mu_w-\mu_{\bar w})/2\). This is an exact linear program over binary-rational data; the result is also a certificate that any deviation budget below \(x\) preserves the unique winner, while a budget of \(x\) can at least erase the strict lead.

The best-response condition has a clear continuum interpretation, with a cost: in an atomless society, one voter’s report has zero effect on the aggregate outcome. Either report therefore gives that voter the same utility and is a best response. So the boundedly rational mass may shift reports freely. This is why the continuous robustness threshold matches the normalized domain condition in Theorem 6: setting \(k/n\to\alpha\), the theorem’s supporter threshold divided by \(n\) tends to \(1/2+\alpha\), up to a vanishing finite-electorate correction. The continuous margin condition says the same thing: the winner’s share must exceed \(1/2\) by at least the mass that might switch reports. Ties count as erasing the strict lead; if the question instead requires the target to remain the unique winner, the budget must be strictly below the threshold.

This is a plausible problem for these authors because it retains the paper’s binary voting rule, preference types, bounded-rationality budget, and central concern: when does a majority outcome survive departures from truthful dominant-strategy play? Theorem 6 is proved here, though it builds on the paper’s earlier characterization of \(k\)-secure implementation and the standard fact that majority rule is strategy-proof in this setting. The mirror covers that majority robustness result, not the paper’s general characterization theorems or its laboratory experiment. Its expected complexity class is straightforwardly tractable, by the one-variable LP; that is a prediction for the mirror, not a complexity claim in the paper.

The weak point is also the reason the proposed problem is computationally simple: atomlessness makes individual best responses unrestricted. The paper’s finite-agent theorem constrains deviations through strategic pivotality, and the continuum removes that feature. I think the mirror remains credible as a population-scale robustness question, but it is not an exact limit of the paper’s equilibrium behavior in every detail. The natural follow-ups are whether a measure-theoretic version of the paper’s general \(k\)-rectangular characterization exists, and whether richer preference types or multiple alternatives produce a nontrivial pricing or optimization problem. The mirror continues the population, not the outcome space, probability model, or time dimension.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The first problem is that Theorem 6 is not a computational result. It says majority rule has a particular implementation property on a restricted domain; it gives no decision problem, algorithm, or complexity bound. The paper’s other named theorems characterize implementation properties too. The proposed mass-transfer optimization is therefore a new problem inspired by Theorem 6, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational question the paper asks.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here: a referendum with many people and two preference types. So I would not reject the mirror on grounds that such voters cannot be grouped into masses. The harder issue is that the paper’s equilibrium concept depends on each person’s ability to affect a discrete tally. In an atomless population, one person’s report has zero effect on the aggregate outcome. For binary voting, either report then gives that person the same outcome after a unilateral deviation. Both reports are best responses—and dominant strategies. The distinction between a truthful rational agent and a boundedly rational agent choosing a different best response has collapsed.

The strongest faithful continuous version would define \(\alpha\)-secure implementation over a distribution of the two preference types, requiring the truthful majority outcome to survive every equilibrium in which at most mass \(\alpha\) reports differently. But under atomlessness, every report pattern within that mass bound is an equilibrium: each individual is indifferent to their own report. That condition is exactly the proposed worst-case mass-perturbation question. Its threshold is the winner’s excess over one half. This is a coherent population robustness question, and its threshold matches the large-\(n\) form of Theorem 6’s domain condition. But it gets that match by replacing the paper’s best-response restrictions with an unrestricted mass-shift budget. It no longer studies the equilibrium behavior that motivates \(k\)-secure implementation.

Keeping individual pivotality would preserve more of the paper’s object, but it requires agents to retain nonzero influence as the population grows. A distribution over atomless voter mass does not do that; preserving it means retaining finite or positive-mass agents as distinct strategic actors. Conversely, richer mechanisms that give a person a report-dependent private payoff could restore meaningful individual incentives, but they would change the binary majority-rule environment of Theorem 6.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope argument: the paper offers an implementation result, not a computational result, and its most natural continuous analogue sheds the equilibrium feature that gives the result its content. I would not claim that no worthwhile mass-based robustness question exists—the referendum scenario is credible, and the asymptotic threshold is a real correspondence. The case against is that this is a new, simpler robustness problem, not a continuous computational mirror of the paper’s result.
