The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: mass-aggregated logarithmic least-squares weighting. The anchor is Theorem 4 of Bozóki, Fülöp & Rónyai (2010), cited in §3 rather than proved in this chapter. The chapter invokes it for the result that the logarithmic least-squares optimum for an incomplete pairwise comparison matrix can be obtained by solving a system of linear equations. For a connected comparison graph, the solution determines the weights, and each missing entry is completed by the ratio of the corresponding weights. The chapter contains no named hardness result to anchor a second case.

Here is the population version. A large group of assessors compares the same alternatives using the same connected set \(E\) of questions. A type \(t\) is a complete response profile on those questions, including its comparison values \(a^t_{ij}\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of assessors with that profile. Given the finite support and rational masses, find \(z\), with \(\sum_i z_i=0\), minimizing
\[ \sum_t \mu_t \sum_{(i,j)\in E} \left[\log a^t_{ij}-(z_i-z_j)\right]^2. \]
Return the priorities \(w_i=e^{z_i}\) and fill each unasked comparison with \(e^{z_i-z_j}\). A solution is a global minimizer and its induced weights and completion.

This is plausible for a large, standardized assessment—for example, hundreds of thousands of residents rating four transport proposals on the same five pairwise questions using a small reciprocal rating scale. If many respondents share exact response profiles, the population is large while the observed type support stays much smaller. The objective is the expected squared log error for one shared group weight vector. Equivalently, it is ordinary LLSM on the geometric-mean pooled comparisons, since \(\bar a_{ij}=\prod_t(a^t_{ij})^{\mu_t}\). That connection gives the authors a direct reason to recognize this as their weighting problem with a population-derived input.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: aggregate the log comparisons by type mass and solve the resulting graph-Laplacian system. Its scope is §3’s LLSM weighting and completion, not the chapter’s ordinal-violation results, inconsistency thresholds, or comparison-order design. Further questions include whether the same approach works when types have different missing-comparison graphs, and whether the eigenvalue and lexicographic methods admit similarly tractable population versions.

The weak point is that the anchor is a linear-system method for a single incomplete matrix, not a complexity classification about populations. The proposed extension is clean and recognizable, but it is likely a straightforward tractable lift rather than a new complexity boundary. It supports a modest continuous mirror of the chapter’s weighting problem; it does not support a broader claim about the chapter’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that the anchor changes the object being studied. In the chapter, an incomplete comparison matrix records judgments about a set of alternatives; LLSM turns that one matrix into a weight vector. The chapter does not model a population of assessors or define a collective objective over their matrices. The proposed mirror adds both: it makes assessors the agents and chooses a group weight vector by minimizing their average log error. That is a plausible group-decision problem, but its connection to a continuous society comes from this added layer, rather than from continuing the chapter’s own computational problem.

The stated model is mathematically coherent. With a shared comparison graph and a finite rating scale, complete response profiles make sensible types, and a large assessment panel is a plausible population. The average-loss objective also has a clear interpretation. So the type-space and multiplicity objections do not defeat it.

The strongest remaining concern is that the distribution matters only through the mean log judgment on each compared pair, \(\sum_t \mu_t\log a^t_{ij}\). The proposed population problem therefore reduces to ordinary LLSM on one pooled matrix. That supports the view that this is a new group-aggregation application of an existing method, rather than a high-multiplicity version of a problem the chapter studies. But reduction to pooled statistics alone cannot rule it out: the programme accepts tractable mirrors, and the chapter’s survey of LLSM gives the authors a reasonable basis to recognize the extension.

I cannot honestly defeat this anchor under the programme’s stated gate. The chapter does not present a population-of-assessors problem, which makes the mirror’s claim to be *its* continuous mirror weak. Yet the proposed scenario is credible, and the chapter’s focus on weighting methods makes author recognition plausible. The negative case is therefore weak at its decisive point: it identifies a change of modeling scope, but cannot establish that the resulting population question falls outside the authors’ problem.
