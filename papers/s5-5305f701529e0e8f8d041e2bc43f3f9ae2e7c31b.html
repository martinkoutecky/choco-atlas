What the mirror covers

The proposed direction draws on Propositions 4 and 5 and mentions Proposition 8 as motivation; it leaves the paper’s other analytical results without a continuous computational formulation.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a numbered computational-complexity result in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror. The closest results are analytical: Proposition 4 gives the probability that agent \(n\) favors the true state from the ratio of weighted path-count norms; Proposition 5 gives the exact mislearning probability on a \(q\)-uniform network, \(\Phi\!\left(-\frac{1}{\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{q+2}{q}}\right)\), and shows it increases with \(q\). Both are proved here, but neither claims that a problem is in P, hard, or belongs to another complexity class.

A possible research direction would be a large platform with recurring community and communication-role types: \(\mu_t\) is each type’s population share, and the decision variables track the mass choosing each action. One could ask for the asymptotic probability of incorrect consensus or persistent disagreement under a continuous interaction model. Proposition 8, which proves positive probability of persistent disagreement between two groups with binary actions, is a motivation for that question—not a qualifying computational anchor.

The obstacle is that the paper’s results depend on sequential order and weighted paths. If those details are part of an agent’s complete type, many agents may have unique types; if they are replaced by group averages, the model may lose the mechanism behind the results. So I can’t honestly state a prover-ready continuous problem per anchor here: the paper supplies no anchor of the required kind, and the proposed direction would be a new analytic model rather than a demonstrated computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper gives no computational problem whose complexity a continuous mirror could chart. Propositions 4 and 5 are probability calculations; Proposition 8 is an existence result about persistent disagreement. The paper asks how network structure affects learning, and derives analytic answers. A new problem about computing, optimizing, or deciding a property of a continuous population could be worthwhile, but it would add a computational objective the paper never studies.

Proposition 4 is the strongest candidate for a rewrite: ask for an agent’s accuracy from the distribution of path weights in a continuous network. But its quantity depends on the full vector of weighted paths from that agent to earlier agents. A distribution over community or role types does not generally retain which paths share ancestors, or how their weights depend on arrival order. Types rich enough to preserve that information would encode each agent’s network position and potentially a distinct path profile. A finite role model could still be a reasonable new model, but computing accuracy in it would be a new analysis, not the mirror of Proposition 4.

Proposition 5 is even more tightly tied to the discrete sequence. Its exact mislearning probability is for infinitely many agents, each receiving weight \(q\) from every predecessor. In a population limit, summing a fixed weight over a growing population requires a scaling choice. Scaling links by population size gives a finite aggregate interaction, but changes the path weights and the stochastic contribution of individual signals. Keeping the original scale leaves the aggregate interaction unbounded. One can choose a coherent continuum model, but the paper supplies no computational question about either choice.

Proposition 8’s positive-probability event is that every odd agent takes one action and every even agent the other, despite each agent’s private signal. That event relies on a countable sequence of independent signal draws and summable probabilities of contrarian actions. In a continuum population, the direct analogue—every member of each group choosing the prescribed action—does not preserve that event structure. Asking instead whether positive mass in each group holds opposing actions may be a natural new question, but it changes the event and leads toward analysis of a time-indexed mean-field process. Again, the paper gives no algorithmic result to mirror.

The best case for a continuum model is a platform with repeated community roles and a population share for each role. That could support a useful study of learning in a new interaction model. The negative case is strongest on the programme’s actual criterion: the paper offers no named computational result, and its attractive continuous reformulations require supplying both a new population dynamics model and a new computational objective. I cannot rule out that such a project would be valuable; I do not see a worthwhile *computational mirror of this paper* in it.
