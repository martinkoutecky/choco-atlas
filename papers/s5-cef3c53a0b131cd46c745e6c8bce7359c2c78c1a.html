The continuous mirror question

Given a dynamic federation with parent assembly size \(n\), a stream of membership changes, and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete membership-signature types, compute an integral colored assembly at every update so each child \(v\) receives at least \(\left\lfloor n\sum_{\theta:v\in S_\theta(f)}\mu_\theta/|S_\theta(f)|\right\rfloor\) seats, where \(S_\theta(f)\) is the set of children of \(f\) containing type \(\theta\).

The model it lives in

Use a finite-support type-mass distribution \(\mu\) over membership signatures. Choose integer seat counts \(x_{\theta,v}\ge 0\), with colors \(v\in S_\theta(f)\) or an uncolored option \(\bot\); require \(\sum_{\theta,v}x_{\theta,v}=n\) and \(\sum_{\theta:v\in S_\theta(f)}x_{\theta,v}\ge\left\lfloor n\sum_{\theta:v\in S_\theta(f)}\mu_\theta/|S_\theta(f)|\right\rfloor\) for each child. A positive-mass type can supply any finite number of distinct people in the continuum.

The objection that survived

The paper certifies GFP itself, while the mirror uses a type-based policy that meets the same floors; no equivalence between their update choices is established.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 8.1’s persistent representation invariant and offers a type-level expected-service analogue of Proposition 8.6. It does not reproduce GFP’s guarantee for each named person’s realized service history or analyze convergence time.

Open questions for a prover

  • For the rotation extension, when does a distribution over quota-feasible integral assemblies exist with \(\mathbb{E}[y_\theta]=n\mu_\theta\), and can one be found efficiently?
  • What finite-population guarantees connect type-level service density to realized long-run service for named individuals?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror here, especially for the assembly representation and rotation guarantees. Its limit is that the paper proves protocol correctness, not a complexity classification: it gives no named result placing a problem in P, proving hardness, or analyzing a pricing problem.

The two best anchors are both proved in this paper. Proposition 8.1 says that, in every admissible run of GFP, at every time, each child \(v\) of each parent \(f\) has at least \(\lfloor \mathrm{share}_t(v,f)\rfloor\) seats in \(f\). Proposition 8.6 says that in every GFP run that eventually stabilizes, each person in each persistent community \(f\) has long-run average seats equal to their fair share, \(\min\{\lim_{t\to\infty} n/|P_{t,f}|,1\}\). Together these are components of the paper’s broader fairness claim, Theorem 8.8.

A natural regime is a large digital federation: billions of members, but relatively few distinct patterns of community membership. A type records a person’s complete membership signature across the federation, including which overlapping child communities they belong to; its mass is their fraction of the relevant population. With \(N\) people and \(\tau\) supported types, this is the high-multiplicity regime \(N\gg\tau\). The type is complete for the representation question: members with the same signature count toward the same communities and have the same eligibility. Their accumulated service history matters to GFP’s individual-level priority rule, so a mass-only model must treat individual rotation as a separate issue.

For a parent \(f\), write \(S_t(f)\) for the children of \(f\) containing type \(t\), and let \(\mu_t\) be its mass within \(f\)’s population. The mass-based weight and share of child \(v\) are

\[ W(v,f)=\sum_{t:v\in S_t(f)}\frac{\mu_t}{|S_t(f)|}, \qquad \sigma(v,f)=nW(v,f). \]

The denominator accounts for the paper’s rule that a person’s weight is split across the parent’s children they belong to.

The first mirror, Continuous Persistent Representation, takes a federation DAG, target assembly size \(n\), a finite support of binary-rational type masses, and an online stream of membership and federation changes. At each assembly update, the policy must select \(n\) distinct people and assign each seat to its occupant’s type and, where applicable, a child community. Every realized assembly must give each child at least \(\lfloor\sigma(v,f)\rfloor\) seats. Its solution is an online allocation policy that preserves those floors at every observable state. This directly mirrors Proposition 8.1. The population is continuous; the assembly remains integral.

The second mirror, Continuous Fair Rotation Decomposition, asks for a policy that also balances participation once the federation and masses stabilize. For a stabilized parent, its compact form is to find a distribution \(\pi\) over integral type-count vectors \(y\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\tau}\), where each vector describes one \(n\)-seat assembly, satisfying

\[ \sum_t y_t=n,\qquad \sum_{t:v\in S_t(f)}y_t\ge\lfloor\sigma(v,f)\rfloor, \qquad \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}[y_t]=n\mu_t. \]

The first two conditions make every sampled assembly persistently fair. The last gives every unit of population mass the same expected service intensity: \(\mathbb E[y_t]/\mu_t=n\). For a finite population with \(N\mu_t\) members of type \(t\), this corresponds to expected participation \(n/N\) per person per term. Repeated use of the distribution gives the mass-level rotation analogue of Proposition 8.6. It also implies eventual-fair representation: a child’s expected seats are \(n\sum_{t:v\in S_t(f)}\mu_t\), at least its weighted share. The requested output is a policy or a succinct lottery over integral assemblies, not a fractional assembly.

These are feasibility and construction questions, as in the paper; it has no scalar objective to optimize. For Continuous Persistent Representation, a feasible assembly is easy to build: color \(\lfloor\sigma(v,f)\rfloor\) seats for each child, then fill the remaining seats. For the rotation decomposition, the finite-support formulation is a genuine computational question. The lottery can be represented with at most \(\tau+1\) outcomes if a suitable decomposition exists, but finding it may involve a pricing problem over integral seat compositions subject to overlapping child quotas. I would expect tractability for fixed, small \(n\), by enumerating compositions of \(n\) across \(\tau\) types; with \(n\) variable, the pricing problem deserves separate analysis. The relevant parameters are \(|V|,\tau,n\), and the bit length \(L\) of the masses and quotas.

The continuous population is the central object in these mirrors, not the passage to continuous time or fractional seats. The dynamic update stream and the integer committee remain. A finite society with counts \(N_t\) maps to masses \(\mu_t=N_t/N\); conversely, a rational mass profile can be realized by sufficiently many people of each type. That provides a natural high-multiplicity dictionary. Existing high-multiplicity work on federated assemblies supports the plausibility of this regime: it shows that large populations with repeated structure are already a sensible object for this problem family.

The soft spot is the rotation requirement. In a nonatomic population, individual empirical service histories disappear; the condition \(\mathbb E[y_t]=n\mu_t\) captures equal expected participation density, not GFP’s pathwise guarantee for every named person. A finite-population approximation can restore named-person rotation, but proving a precise two-way correspondence—including how much finite-population error remains in a compressed algorithm—is a further result, not automatic. This mirror therefore covers GFP’s persistent representation and mass-level fair rotation, rather than claiming to reproduce every individual-level guarantee of Theorem 8.8.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The high-multiplicity setting is plausible here: a large federation could have many people sharing the same community-membership signature. The prior work on federated assemblies supports that regime; it is not a collision. So I would not argue that the paper’s population cannot sensibly be aggregated.

The gap is that the paper’s named results are protocol-correctness claims, not complexity results for computational problems. Proposition 8.1 proves that GFP’s updates preserve representation floors. Its mass analogue can use the proposed \(W(v,f)\) and \(\sigma(v,f)\), but that gives a new temporal verification question, not a complexity mirror of a problem the paper formulates. The paper supplies no finite-instance query, objective, or runtime bound for this task. One could add a policy-synthesis problem, specify its input and computational parameters, and study it; that may be worthwhile work, but it would be a new problem rather than a continuous version of Proposition 8.1’s result.

Proposition 8.6 is a weaker anchor for continuization. Its guarantee concerns the realized long-run service of each named person, and GFP’s choices depend on personal service histories and term lengths. Membership signature alone does not capture that state. Include histories in the type and the support can grow with the population; omit them and the proposed condition \(\mathbb E[y_t]=n\mu_t\) guarantees only aggregate, ex ante service by type. That is a different fairness guarantee.

There is also a continuum-specific collapse. With a nonatomic population and fixed finite assembly size \(n\), each assembly selects a zero-mass set. A particular person’s limiting selection rate is therefore zero, matching the literal limiting fair share \(n/|P|\to 0\); individual fairness becomes vacuous. The type-level lottery can retain a nonzero service density across a group, but it does not recover the paper’s person-by-person realized guarantee. Recovering that guarantee requires individual identities and a schedule, which gives back the historical state that the type compression removed.

So I would not spend a ChoCo mirror slot on Proposition 8.6 as stated: the aggregate lottery is a plausible adjacent apportionment problem, but it is not the paper’s temporal result. The case against Proposition 8.1 is less decisive: its mass formulation is coherent, and a broader programme that includes protocol synthesis could adopt it. The negative case is strongest on the mismatch with ChoCo’s computational scope, and weakest on whether dynamic representation-floor maintenance might deserve study on its own.
