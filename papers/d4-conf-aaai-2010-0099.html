Why no mirror

The proposed client cohort is a coherent population model, and the trust inference can be restated over its type masses. But Theorems 1–4 state properties of the certainty function, while the procedure and experiments are not presented as a numbered computational result. The mirror therefore fails bit (a), even though the cohort formulation is recognizable.

What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation reuses the component trust estimates, invocation-conditioned probabilities, and global trust calculation in Sections 4.1–4.3; it does not turn Theorems 1–4 into computational results or cover the future service-selection problem.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous population of service clients. In a high traffic platform running a fixed composite service, many clients could submit reports using the same finite rating scale and encounter the same invocation patterns. A type would be a client’s complete rating and invocation record for the service components under study; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of the client cohort with that record. The regime is plausible when the cohort is very large but standardized workflows and rating scales keep the number of observed types \(\tau\) comparatively small.

There is an important limit to this case: the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind required for an anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says certainty increases with the total number of ratings when the ratio of two rating counts and the other counts are fixed. Theorem 2, proved here, says that with the sum of two counts fixed, certainty increases with one count below the midpoint and decreases above it. Theorem 3, proved here, gives a symmetry of the certainty function under a permutation of the rating counts. Theorem 4, proved here, says certainty increases with a rating count when the other counts are fixed. These are mathematical properties of the trust estimate, not results classifying a problem as tractable or hard. The paper gives a computation method and an experiment, but no numbered theorem establishes its complexity. So none of these can honestly serve as one of the required anchors.

As an unanchored candidate, I would propose Continuous-Cohort Composite-Service Trust Inference. An instance gives a finite service invocation graph, an execution flow, a finite rating alphabet, an effective cohort size \(N\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the cohort’s complete report types. For component \(v\) and rating \(r\), its effective count is
\[ x_{v,r}=N\sum_{t:\,t\text{ reports }r\text{ for }v}\mu_t. \]
The task is to compute the paper’s component trust estimates and invocation-conditioned trust probabilities from these masses, then return a rational value within \(\varepsilon\) of the global trust obtained by its joint-probability formula. Require positive mass in every conditioning stratum used by the instance. This preserves the paper’s trust inference question and makes the clients themselves the continuous object; \(N\) remains explicit because rating proportions alone do not capture the amount of evidence.

I would initially expect this version to be tractable when the rating alphabet and service graph are fixed and the support is modest: most inputs reduce to aggregating masses and computing conditional proportions. The certainty integral is the main unresolved computational step, and the paper supplies no complexity result for it. This candidate covers the component trust estimation and global trust inference in Sections 4.1–4.3, but does not mirror the paper’s theorems as complexity results or its future service-selection problem.

The weak point is that the paper treats ratings as evidence to aggregate, not as agents making strategic choices. Grouping repeated client records is plausible for a standardized, high-volume service, but the paper does not establish that such cohorts have small support or that its authors would regard fractional evidence as their intended model. The mirror is therefore a sensible research question to offer, but it cannot meet the requested anchor requirement for a positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational-complexity result to mirror. Theorems 1–4 characterize the certainty function: Theorem 1 its growth with sample size at a fixed ratio, Theorem 2 its change as two counts are reallocated, Theorem 3 its symmetry, and Theorem 4 its increase with a count when the others are fixed. They establish no running-time bound, hardness result, or algorithm for a computational problem. The paper proposes a trust estimator and illustrates it experimentally.

The proposed continuous cohort is coherent, but its core calculation is just a re-encoding of the paper’s inputs. Given \(N\) and \(\mu\), it forms counts such as \(x_{v,r}=N\sum_{t:\,t\text{ reports }r\text{ for }v}\mu_t\). The estimator then uses those counts and the corresponding conditional masses. The paper already allows fractional counts because of time discounting, so these real-valued statistics do not create a new computational object. At the paper’s fixed rating scale, the full cohort can be reduced to the relevant rating counts and contingency tables. That is ordinary aggregation of observed evidence, not a new complexity question about a continuous society.

One could make a more algorithmic version by asking for exact or approximate evaluation of the certainty integral when the rating alphabet varies. But that would be a new Bayesian-numerics problem. The paper does not establish its computational complexity, and any added difficulty would come from evaluating a posterior functional as the rating dimension grows, not from treating clients as a continuum. Likewise, optimizing over execution flows is a plausible adjacent problem—the paper mentions it as future work—but it optimizes service compositions using trust values as inputs. It is not a computational result of this paper about the client population. To make clients themselves the objects of an intervention, such as changing or targeting their reports, would add a problem the paper does not study.

This negative case is strongest against calling any of these a continuous *mirror* of the paper’s results. It is weaker as a claim that no useful continuous trust-inference project could ever be formulated: a large, standardized client cohort is plausible, and a new algorithmic study of trust estimation or service selection might be worthwhile. But that possibility does not supply an anchor here. The proposed case for identifies a candidate research direction, not a computational result of the paper whose continuous counterpart it has established.
