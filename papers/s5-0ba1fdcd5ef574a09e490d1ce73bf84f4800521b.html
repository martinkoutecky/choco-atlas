The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population mirror here, but the thesis supplies no eligible numbered computational anchor: I found no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. The closest claim is in §3.1.3: finding a core in a coalition is NP-complete, attributed to Faigle et al. (1997). §3.2.1.1 similarly attributes NP-completeness to checking core stability. Neither is numbered as a result in this thesis. Its own complexity discussion in §6.2.2 gives operation counts and claims polynomial time for the CBM when the number of providers is bounded; that is also unnumbered, and the discussion appears internally inconsistent about the CBM’s asymptotic bound. So these claims can motivate a mirror, but they cannot honestly serve as the required anchors.

The strongest plausible mirror would be Continuous Core Search for Typed Group Trading. Consider a marketplace with finitely many goods and buyer and provider types. A buyer type records its per-person order and minimum acceptable discount for each good. A provider type records its per-person supply, price, cost, and discount schedule. These are complete types: anyone whose relevant order or commercial terms differ belongs to a different type. The instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\) of buyer types and \(\nu_u\) of provider types. A variable \(x_{tug}\) records how much mass of type \(t\)’s demand for good \(g\) is served by type \(u\), subject to demand, stock, and the stated discount rules.

For any subpopulation masses \(a\leq\mu\) and \(b\leq\nu\), let \(V(a,b)\) be the maximum combined buyer savings and provider profit achievable by those participants under the same trading rules. The question is: given the typed marketplace, find an efficient allocation and per-person payoff for each type such that no subpopulation can form a coalition and obtain a greater total payoff than its members receive; or report that no such allocation exists. A solution is the allocation and payoff vector, or a certificate that the continuous core is empty. This targets the thesis’s central concern—stable, mutually beneficial buyer–provider coalitions—not the fact that it uses brokers or runs a website.

A plausible regime is recurring bulk purchasing by thousands of retailers, hospitals, or hotel operators buying standard goods through a marketplace. Many participants can share an order type, while a smaller support records the distinct baskets, discount thresholds, and supplier offers. Aggregated mass is natural there: each unit of mass still receives an integral order, while the type-level allocation can be fractional. The mirror covers the thesis’s core-finding and stability discussion, not its full CBM architecture, fees system, or empirical simulation. With a fixed catalogue and suitable concave piecewise-linear trade rules, the continuous problem looks like a candidate for Class A continuous optimization; the thesis’s exact stepwise discounts and variable product catalogue could preserve hard combinatorics, so that tractability prediction needs to be tested rather than assumed.

The soft spot is the anchor gap, compounded by the thesis’s lack of a rigorous complexity theorem for its own algorithm. That prevents this from being an assignment-compliant anchored case, even though the population model itself is credible. The missing bridge is whether the continuous core and the thesis’s discrete coalition-selection procedure coincide on high-multiplicity instances, and whether the proposed mass solution can be converted into a discrete solution with a controlled loss.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make a convincing case that the typed procurement scenario is inherently unsuitable for continuization. Recurring purchases by many retailers or hospitals are a credible high-multiplicity regime. A type can include an order, discount threshold, or supplier terms, so aggregation need not erase relevant differences. Nor do step discounts, integral orders, or FCFS by themselves rule out a mass version: the programme allows those features to be restated where the population model requires it.

The strongest case against is narrower: this thesis supplies no eligible computational result to mirror. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing the complexity of a problem. Its NP-completeness statements in §§3.1.3 and 3.2.1.1 are attributed to earlier work; its own complexity and simulation claims are unnumbered. The proposed “Continuous Core Search” therefore cannot be anchored to a computational result of this paper. A more plausible market story does not fix that gap.

There is also a real distance between that question and the thesis’s formal contribution. The proposed mirror defines a typed transferable-utility game and asks about its continuous core. The thesis describes a brokered trading procedure, but does not give a formal discrete input model or a proved complexity result for that procedure. Its stability check samples balanced weights, and its “core” search selects a coalition with maximum total benefit; neither gives a theorem that can simply be carried over to the typed model. A coherent continuous-core problem could be worthwhile research in its own right, but it would be a newly formalized problem, not a continuization of a computational result established here.

So the negative case is strong on the paper-selection criterion, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous problem exists. The proponent’s scenario survives the substantive high-multiplicity test; what fails is its status as an anchored mirror of this thesis.
