The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives ChoCo no computational result to continue. Propositions 1 and 2 are existence and impossibility claims about anonymous core solutions; Proposition 3 is a closure property; Propositions 4 and 5 establish merging and splitting properties of solution rules. None defines an encoded computational problem or classifies its complexity. That is a serious boundary for a programme whose object is the computational landscape.

The best candidate is Proposition 4: for a game described by a value function \(V(q)\) over vectors of worker types, ask whether one can compute the Dutta–Ray allocation and test its merger property. A workforce with repeated skill and asset profiles could make that a sensible high-multiplicity setting. But it does not rescue this paper as a mirror. To pose the computational question, one must choose how \(V\) is represented, define coalitions of fractional type mass, and specify how a merged bloc participates in later coalitions. Those choices create a new input family and a new computational problem. Proposition 4 itself is a theorem about a solution’s axiom, not about computing that solution. Proposition 5 has the same gap: constructing a splitting-proof rule for a continuous game would be a new existence question, while computing a rule on a specified instance would be a new algorithmic question. The same distinction disposes of the other propositions.

There is also a real modeling strain beneath those choices. In the paper, players are indivisible strategic units, and a merger replaces several units with one while preserving the worth of coalitions containing them. In an atomless population, an individual has zero mass and can be split without changing the society; a positive-mass bloc that acts as one player, by contrast, is an atom. One can define a continuum game with such blocs, or define coalitions by fractional masses, but those are different ways to model the very merger and splitting operations at the paper’s center. Neither supplies a computational mirror automatically.

The workers example therefore shows that a high-multiplicity cooperative-game model might be worth studying; it does not make this paper a computational anchor. A continuous axiomatic treatment could be interesting in its own right, and a computational study of a specified continuous game could be valuable. The negative case is weaker if “mirror” is stretched to include any such follow-on.
