The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem establishing NP-hardness, membership in P, or a parameterized complexity classification. Its strongest computationally relevant anchors are structural and approximation results. My positive case is therefore for a continuous analogue of its equilibrium and approximation analysis, not a claim that it already charts a complexity landscape.

A plausible regime is public services along a one-dimensional transit corridor: millions of residents, two under-capacity clinics or service centers, and residents grouped by a modest number \(\tau\) of location types, such as census-area centroids. The type is a resident’s location; its mass is that location’s fraction of the population. Facility capacities become fractions \(q_1,q_2\), with \(q_1+q_2<1\). The facilities are placed at population quantiles, residents choose which facility to approach, and each serves its \(q_j\) mass of closest entrants. At a distance tie, service can split fractionally. This is a population model: residents’ locations and capacities are continuous in the relevant sense, while fractional service at a cutoff is the co-modification needed to represent mass.

Here is the corresponding question. Given a finite-support location distribution \(\mu\) and capacities \(q_1,q_2\), define \(Q_\mu(v)\) as its \(v\)-quantile and place facilities at \(Q_\mu(v_1),Q_\mu(v_2)\). A mass-routing profile sends each location type’s mass to facilities; each facility admits the nearest routed mass up to its capacity, splitting ties. Call a profile an equilibrium when almost every resident chooses a facility maximizing their expected utility, accounting for the chance of admission. Welfare is the total served mass weighted by \(1-\text{distance}\). A rule is equilibrium stable (ES) when all such equilibria yield the same welfare.

The first anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. For two facilities with \(k_1\ge k_2\), \(k_1+k_2<n\), and percentile indices \(i_j=\lfloor v_j(n-1)\rfloor+1\), it says that, provided \(i_2-i_1>1\), the percentile mechanism is ES if and only if
\[ \lfloor v_2(n-1)\rfloor-\lfloor v_1(n-1)\rfloor\ge k_1+k_2-1. \]
The continuous question is Mass-ES-2Q: given rational \(q_1\ge q_2>0\) with \(q_1+q_2<1\) and quantile levels \(v_1\le v_2\), decide whether this rule is ES for every finite-support location distribution. The limit of Theorem 3 predicts the clean condition \(v_2-v_1\ge q_1+q_2\). A solution is a yes/no answer, with a distribution witness for “no.” This looks tractable: the proposed test is a rational inequality, though its equivalence to ES in the mass game must be proved.

My lead is Optimal ES Two-Quantile Design, anchored in Theorem 5, also proved here. The theorem identifies the best ES *wide-gap* percentile mechanism for given \(n,k_1,k_2\). Write \(\Delta=n-(k_1+k_2)\) and \(\alpha=\lceil(\Delta-(k_1-k_2))/2\rceil\). Its three cases are:

  • If \(\Delta\ge\lceil(k_1+k_2)/2\rceil\), choose \(i_1=\lceil k_1/2\rceil\), \(i_2=n-\lfloor k_2/2\rfloor\); the ratio is

\[ \frac{k_1+k_2}{(k_1+1)/2+k_2}. \]

  • If \(k_1-k_2\le\Delta\le\lfloor(k_1+k_2)/2\rfloor+1\), choose \(i_1=k_1-k_2+\alpha\), \(i_2=n-\alpha\); the ratio is

\[ \frac{k_1+k_2}{i_1+k_2}. \]

  • Otherwise choose \(i_1=\Delta+1\), \(i_2=n\); the ratio is

\[ \frac{k_1+k_2}{\Delta+k_2+1}. \]

The continuous design problem takes rational capacity shares \(q_1\ge q_2>0\) as input and returns quantile levels minimizing the worst-case ratio \(SW^*(\mu)/SW_v(\mu)\) over finite-support societies, subject to ES. Here \(SW^*\) is the best welfare over facility placements and their equilibria, and \(SW_v\) is the rule’s equilibrium welfare, well-defined by ES. Theorem 5 predicts the answer by scaling \(k_j/n\to q_j\): with \(\delta=1-q_1-q_2\), choose
\[ (v_1,v_2)= \begin{cases} (q_1/2,\ 1-q_2/2), & \delta\ge(q_1+q_2)/2,\\ ((q_1-q_2+\delta)/2,\ 1-(\delta-q_1+q_2)/2), & q_1-q_2\le\delta\le(q_1+q_2)/2,\\ (\delta,\ 1), & \delta<q_1-q_2. \end{cases} \]
The corresponding limiting ratios are respectively
\[ \frac{q_1+q_2}{q_1/2+q_2},\qquad \frac{q_1+q_2}{v_1+q_2},\qquad \frac{q_1+q_2}{\delta+q_2}. \]
For equal capacities \(q_1=q_2=q\le 1/3\), this gives ratio \(4/3\). A solution is the optimal quantile pair and a proof of its worst-case guarantee. I expect a tractable, piecewise-rational result, but proving that the mass-game ratio is exactly the limit of the paper’s finite-agent ratios is part of the work. Further questions include whether the optimum survives beyond wide-gap quantile rules and whether the same limit holds for arbitrary finite-support distributions, including large atoms.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: the population is what becomes continuous, the same residents choose among the same capacity-constrained facilities, FCFS admission still creates the equilibrium issue, and the objective is the same welfare guarantee. The paper’s experiments draw finite populations from a distribution; that is useful motivation, but it is not itself a continuous-population result.

My weakest point is the equilibrium bridge. In a nonatomic model, an individual report or choice has zero mass, so the paper’s absolute truthfulness result, Theorem 2, does not carry over automatically; individual truthfulness can become vacuous. And Wardrop equilibria with fractional tie admission need not reproduce the paper’s atomic Nash equilibria without proof. I would not claim that they do. The proposed mirror deliberately centers on the ES and approximation results, while asking the prover to establish the finite-to-continuum correspondence. That leaves a real, bounded question rather than assuming away the main modeling difficulty.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is about fit with ChoCo’s computational remit. The paper has no result on the complexity of a computational problem, nor a runtime analysis of an algorithm. Theorem 3 characterizes when a percentile mechanism is equilibrium stable; Theorem 5 optimizes its worst-case approximation ratio within the wide-gap percentile family. Those are useful mechanism-design results, but their continuous analogues would be new equilibrium and approximation theorems, not a complexity landscape.

That is a real limitation for the proposed Mass-ES-2Q question. As stated, its input is a few rational parameters, and it asks for a property to hold over every finite-support society. That is a universal characterization, much like the theorem it mirrors. If instead the input is a particular distribution and the task is to compute its equilibria or check stability for that instance, it becomes a computational problem, but no longer the direct mirror of Theorem 3.

There is also a deeper bridge problem: the equilibrium notion is not determined just by replacing counts with mass. In the finite game, a named agent can change their strategy and affect admission. In a nonatomic Wardrop model, an individual has zero mass, so that deviation does not affect admission; redirecting an entire type’s mass would be a coalition deviation. Fractional service at a cutoff gives a coherent nonatomic model, but it does not establish that its equilibrium-stability predicate is the limit of the paper’s pure-Nash predicate. Theorem 2’s absolute truthfulness is likewise vacuous for a measure-zero individual unless the model adds a different notion of manipulation. This is not an objection to fractional mass itself; it is a warning that the equilibrium and incentive questions change together.

Theorem 5 has the same scope problem. Optimizing quantile levels from capacity shares is a direct continuous design theorem, but it does not ask for an algorithm on a society input or classify the complexity of one. A stronger computational question—say, computing worst-equilibrium welfare for a given rational finite-support distribution—would be worth formulating, but it would need its own equilibrium model and would not follow from the theorem’s index optimization.

I would not argue that no high-multiplicity scenario is plausible. The service-center example is credible, and the paper’s distributional experiments support asking how the mechanism behaves for populations. Nor do those experiments count as an existing continuous treatment: they study finite samples. So the negative case is weak against the broader claim that the paper merits a continuous mechanism-design mirror. It is stronger only under ChoCo’s narrower goal of charting computational complexity: neither proposed anchor, as stated, supplies that kind of computational question.
