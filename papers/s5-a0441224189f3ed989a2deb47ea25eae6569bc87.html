The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest positive case that meets the anchor requirement. The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Its numbered Results 1–4 report experimental findings.

The closest possible anchor is Result 3: among agreements, the winner received an average share of 60.5%; winners’ and losers’ fairness judgments were both positively correlated with the agreed share; and the paper suggests fairness judgments relate to disagreements indirectly, through opening offers and concessions. That is an empirical result about two-person bargaining, not a result about the complexity of a computational problem. So there are no qualifying anchors, and no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover or classify as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

A plausible population setting would be many similar salary negotiations across firms, with episode types defined by claims and bargaining conditions and mass representing the fraction of negotiations of each type. The paper motivates those settings, but an aggregate account of their outcomes would be a behavioral or statistical model. The study supplies no computational decision problem or objective for that model. Its divisible salary pie is continuity of the outcome space; the society in each experiment remains a pair.

The weakest point is decisive for this task: any algorithmic mirror would need a new problem that the paper neither formulates nor computes. The paper may inspire a separate ChoCo question, but its named results do not anchor one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Results 1–4 are findings from bargaining experiments: they report fairness judgments, offers, concessions, duration, and agreements. None defines a computational problem with an input, objective, feasible solutions, and output, or states an algorithmic or complexity result. Result 3—the 60.5% average winner share and its correlations with fairness judgments—is an empirical association, not a rule that computes an outcome from a society.

The best population version is easy to imagine: many repeated wage negotiations, grouped by the complete type of bargaining episode, including both parties’ claims, beliefs, and bargaining conditions. That could be a sensible high-multiplicity setting. But to ask a computational question, one would still have to specify an intervention, its costs, and a target such as an agreement rate or aggregate share. The paper specifies none of these. Its free-form bargaining setup permits many equilibrium outcomes, and its evidence does not establish a response rule from episode types to outcomes. A mass-transfer or control problem built on such a rule would need to supply the central model itself.

So Result 3 does not survive as an anchor, even in its stronger cohort interpretation. The paper could inspire a new computational study of large-scale bargaining, but that study would add the decision problem and behavioral model rather than continuize a computational question this paper already poses. The universal claim is harder to defend: I cannot rule out a worthwhile future model of repeated negotiations. The negative case is strongest on the narrower point that this paper’s results do not ground such a mirror.
