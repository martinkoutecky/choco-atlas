The strongest continuous-mirror candidate, though, is Kemeny Consensus over a Rational Population. An instance is a candidate set \(C\), a finite support \(S\subseteq \operatorname{Perm}(C)\), and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_\pi\) summing to \(1\). Each ranking \(\pi\) is a voter type; \(\mu_\pi\) is the fraction of the population with that ranking. The task is to return a consensus ranking \(\sigma\in\operatorname{Perm}(C)\) minimizing
\[ K_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_{\pi\in S}\mu_\pi\,\mathrm{dist}_{KT}(\sigma,\pi), \]
and its exact score. Equivalently, ask whether some \(\sigma\) has score at most a given rational threshold.

A plausible regime is a large platform or survey in which hundreds of thousands of people rank the same universities, but many give identical rankings: \(n\) is very large while the number of distinct rankings \(\tau=|S|\) is much smaller. The ranking fully describes each voter for this problem, and the decision variable remains a single consensus ranking. This is a credible mirror of the paper’s central question: the objective is exactly its sum of Kendall–Tau distances, normalized by population size. The paper’s own experiments, however, use five rankings and at most twelve universities, so they do not demonstrate that high-multiplicity regime.

I expect this mirror to be Class B, with hardness transferring. For a discrete profile with \(n_\pi\) voters of each type, set \(\mu_\pi=n_\pi/n\). Then \(K_\mu(\sigma)\) is the discrete Kemeny score divided by \(n\), so the optimal consensus is unchanged. The NP-hardness cited by the paper therefore carries over to these rational-mass instances. This still yields a meaningful continuous computational question, though it does not suggest that continuization makes Kemeny tractable. Further questions include whether the cited dynamic program extends to rational masses, and whether it can be parameterized by support size or by the expected Kendall–Tau distance between two independent draws from \(\mu\).

The main weakness is twofold: there is no eligible named result to anchor this case, and the paper’s actual data regime is far from a large population with repeated voter types. The mirror is nevertheless a faithful formulation of the paper’s central optimization problem; it should be presented as a proposed mirror, not as a result established by this paper.

That is a strong objection to the paper-level anchor, but it does not defeat the proposed mirror itself. A complete ranking captures everything the Kemeny objective uses, and the consensus objective does not track individual identities. For a discrete profile with \(n_\pi\) ballots of each ranking \(\pi\), setting \(\mu_\pi=n_\pi/n\) gives
\[ K_\mu(\sigma)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_\pi n_\pi\,\mathrm{dist}_{KT}(\sigma,\pi). \]
That is a clean, recognizable mass formulation. The paper’s five organization or year rankings are weak evidence for this regime, but they do not rule out the respondent scenario.
