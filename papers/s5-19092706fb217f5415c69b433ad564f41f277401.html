What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns per-type allocations inspired by the elevator example, but the paper's characterizations in Theorems 3.2 and 4.4–4.6 are axiomatic; the numerical tables provide no computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible setting is a large housing cooperative with many owners but few recurring apartment types. A type could specify an owner’s floor, quota or apartment size, and cost contribution; \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s fraction of owners. One could ask for the per-type cost allocation under a continuous analogue of \(ED^U\), \(ESD1^U\), or \(ESD2^U\). That is a credible population regime for the paper’s elevator example.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor that mirror. Theorem 3.2 proves that \(ED^U\) is the unique value satisfying ADD, SWU, SAU, and NPP. Theorems 4.4, 4.5, and 4.6 prove analogous uniqueness characterizations for \(ESD1^U\), \(ESD2^U\), and \(ESD3^U\), respectively, under their stated axioms. These are axiomatic results, not claims about an underlying problem being in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The elevator tables report numerical allocations, but do not supply a named computational result either.

So I cannot honestly give a continuous problem per qualifying anchor. Turning the candidate above into one would require adding a computational task and a representation for the characteristic-function game; the paper neither specifies nor analyzes those. Its central contribution is axiomatic characterization, and continuizing that contribution while the population stays finite would be out of scope. The apartment-owner scenario is a possible starting point for new work, but it does not make a positive anchored case for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The large housing cooperative is a plausible high-multiplicity setting if many owners share a small number of apartment, floor, quota, and cost types. The proponent’s proposed per-type allocation is therefore a reasonable continuous model. The gap is that it mirrors no computational result in this paper.

The named results are Theorems 3.2, 4.4, 4.5, and 4.6. Each characterizes a value by axioms; none studies an algorithmic problem or its complexity. Extending the axioms to allocations by type would be an axiomatic continuization, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. The numerical elevator tables do not supply a computational problem either.

A natural density formulation is possible. For example, if union \(P_k\) has mass \(\alpha_k\), \(ED^U\) could assign it density \(v(N)/(m\alpha_k)\), so its total allocation is \(v(N)/m\). That makes the housing example more coherent as a continuous allocation model, but it still leaves the computational question unspecified. The paper gives no representation of a characteristic-function game over a continuous society. With a chosen finite representation, these value formulas require only a few worth evaluations; with a succinct representation, the complexity would depend on that new encoding and its assumptions.

The mirror case is therefore weak on the high-multiplicity question and strong on the programme-fit question. A continuous allocation model may be worth studying on its own, but making it computational would require adding a problem and representation the paper does not provide. That would be a new computational project prompted by the paper, rather than a computational mirror of one of its results.
