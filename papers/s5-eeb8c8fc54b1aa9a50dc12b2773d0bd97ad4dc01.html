The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is an aggregate version of the paper’s equilibrium-selection problem. Its main anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For a finite collection \(A\) of two-player utility vectors and \(0<\delta\le1\), it exactly characterizes the pure Nash equilibrium outcomes of \(AV_\delta\) as \(AG(A)\cup DIS(A)\). Agreement outcomes are \((1-\delta)a+\delta x\), where \(a\in PE(A)\), \(x\in AFP(A)\), and \(a\geq x\) coordinatewise. Disagreement outcomes are average fixed points \(x\in AFP(A)\) that no \(a\in A\) strictly Pareto dominates. Here an average fixed point is an average of the utility vectors outside the strictly lower-left quadrant of \(x\), with a choice about which boundary points to include. Corollary 1, also proved here, says that \(AV_\delta\) always has a pure Nash equilibrium and is anonymous and \(\delta\)-Pareto efficient in every equilibrium.

Neither result is a complexity classification of the kind the brief requires. The paper gives no named result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. These are the closest named anchors, but they do not qualify as complexity anchors.

A charitable continuous question would treat many independent bargaining pairs as a cohort. An instance would give a finite support \(\Theta\) of bargaining-environment types, rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), and for each type a finite set \(A_\theta\subseteq[0,1]^2\) of rational utility vectors. Each type describes the complete game faced by pairs in that cohort. Choose an equilibrium outcome \(x_\theta\in AG(A_\theta)\cup DIS(A_\theta)\) for each type to maximize \(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta(x_{\theta,1}+x_{\theta,2})\). A solution is the type-by-type equilibrium selection and its aggregate welfare. The regime would be many repeated bargaining pairs but relatively few distinct games. Theorem 1 supplies the feasible outcomes for each type.

This is the strongest case because it retains the paper’s actual equilibrium concept and uses mass for a population-level objective. But it is a weak mirror: the “type” is an entire two-player game, not an individual agent, and the aggregate objective is added by the analyst. The paper studies neither a population of bargaining pairs nor their aggregate welfare. The new optimization also separates by type, so its population scale adds little computational structure; the paper establishes no complexity bound for optimizing over \(AFP(A_\theta)\). I would not assign it a defensible Class A, B, or C classification.

The obstacle is sharper for the paper’s actual model: it has exactly two strategic players. Treating the alternatives as a continuum would continue the outcome space, which the programme excludes. A many-voter approval model changes the mechanism and equilibrium problem; the paper itself gives a counterexample to its proposed extension beyond two players in Section 4.2. Proposition 2’s approximation to the Pareto frontier likewise changes the outcome space, not the population.

So the cohort problem is a precise, charitable adjacent question, but I do not think the authors or a referee should accept it as a continuous-population mirror of their result. The paper has named equilibrium results, but no qualifying computational-complexity anchor for a strong positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the closest anchor, but it characterizes pure Nash equilibrium outcomes for a game with exactly two players; it gives no complexity classification for a computational problem. Corollary 1’s existence and efficiency guarantee is likewise a mechanism property, not an algorithmic result. Under the programme’s anchor criterion, neither supplies a computational result to continuize. The paper’s “average fixed point” does not change that: its average is over alternatives’ utility vectors, not over agents. Proposition 2’s \(k\)-uniform lotteries refine the outcome space, which also leaves the population axis untouched.

The strongest population-level reinterpretation is the proposed cohort of repeated bargaining pairs. Give each game type \(\theta\) mass \(\mu_\theta\), use Theorem 1 to specify its equilibrium outcomes, and maximize aggregate welfare \(\sum_\theta \mu_\theta(x_{\theta,1}+x_{\theta,2})\). This is a coherent follow-on question, but its types are whole two-player games, not agents in a shared society. With no constraint or mechanism linking pairs, the optimization separates by type: the masses weight the chosen outcomes after each game is solved. Adding a common resource, coordinated outcome, or cross-pair constraint could make the population matter computationally, but that coupling would be new to the paper.

Making the participants themselves a continuum fares worse. In an atomless approval vote, an individual deviation has zero effect on the outcome, so the paper’s unilateral Nash condition loses its strategic force. Treating positive-mass groups as players instead changes the game and its deviation structure; the paper’s own Section 4.2 gives a counterexample to its proposed extension beyond two players. The case against a *worthwhile ChoCo mirror* is therefore strong: the paper’s central results concern two-player implementation, while its named theorems provide no computational-complexity anchor. A repeated-bargaining study might still be worthwhile on its own, but its contribution would come from the added population model and coupling, not from continuizing this paper’s results.
