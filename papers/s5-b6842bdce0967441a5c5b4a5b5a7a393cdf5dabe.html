What the mirror covers

The weighted extension covers the hierarchy rule in Theorem 5.1; it leaves the broader axiomatic characterizations and the strong quota pair count unmirrored as computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There are no eligible computational anchors in this dissertation. Its numbered results are axiomatic characterizations and a counting formula; none establishes that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 5.1, the closest fit, is proved here as an application of Theorem 4.3: it says that a hierarchy consensus function satisfies anonymity, competitive decisive neutrality, monotonicity, and tie-breaking if and only if it is \(\mathrm{Maj}^+\). That is a characterization of a rule, not a complexity result. Theorem 4.3 likewise characterizes consensus functions on finite median semilattices, and Theorem 2.7 counts strong quota pair systems. None can honestly serve as the required anchor for a computational mirror.

The strongest positive lead is an unanchored extension of Theorem 5.1’s hierarchy rule. Let \(S\) be a fixed set of at least three items, and let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution with support \(\mathcal H_1,\ldots,\mathcal H_\tau\) over hierarchies on \(S\). Define \(p_A\) as the mass of hierarchies containing a nontrivial cluster \(A\), and \(q_A\) as the mass of hierarchies to which \(A\) cannot be added while preserving the hierarchy property. The problem would be: compute

\[ \mathcal H_\mu = \{\text{trivial clusters}\} \cup \{A : p_A>q_A\}. \]

The solution is the resulting hierarchy \(\mathcal H_\mu\). It is well-defined: if \(A\) and \(B\) are incompatible, then \(p_A\le q_B\) and \(p_B\le q_A\), so they cannot both satisfy their strict-majority conditions. For an empirical distribution with \(\mu_i=n_i/n\), this rule reduces exactly to \(\mathrm{Maj}^+\). A plausible regime is a large panel classifying the same fixed items into hierarchies under a shared codebook, where many contributors produce the same complete hierarchy and \(n\gg\tau\).

If treated as a computation problem with the supported hierarchies listed explicitly, this looks tractable by summing masses for candidate clusters and testing compatibility. But that is a new, likely straightforward algorithmic question—not a result of the dissertation. The authors might recognize the weighted rule as a natural population version of their consensus function; there is no paper result showing that its computation, or any other computational task, is the question they studied.

So the positive case is real but limited: hierarchy consensus has a natural continuous-population extension, and Theorem 5.1 gives it a close rule-level analogue. Its weakest point is decisive under the requested standard: because the paper contains no named computational result, there is no qualifying anchor, no hardness whose fate in the continuum can be tested, and no fully compliant problem-per-anchor case to make.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the dissertation gives no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 4.3 characterizes consensus functions by axioms; Theorem 5.1 applies that characterization to hierarchies; and Theorem 2.7 counts strong quota pair systems. None studies an algorithmic problem or establishes a complexity bound. The closest result, Theorem 5.1, is still a rule characterization: the stated axioms hold exactly for \(\mathrm{Maj}^+\). Under ChoCo’s computational remit, there is no paper result here whose high multiplicity version could be the mirror.

The proposed weighted \(\mathrm{Maj}^+\) rule is nevertheless a credible population model. Contributors classifying the same items under a shared codebook could form a large population with repeated hierarchy types. Existing work on hierarchy consensus also supports the regime’s plausibility. Those points make the extension sensible; they do not supply a computational anchor.

For the proposed input, the computation is direct. Any cluster that can enter the output must appear in at least one supported hierarchy, since its support mass \(p_A\) must be positive. For each such cluster, sum the rational masses of hierarchies containing it and of hierarchies to which it cannot be added. Comparing \(p_A\) and \(q_A\) gives the output. With the supported hierarchies listed explicitly, this takes polynomial time in their total representation size and the masses’ bit lengths. That may be useful as an implementation, but the dissertation neither poses this task nor studies its complexity. The natural weighted extension is therefore a new computation attached to the rule, rather than a continuization of a computational result in the paper.

A stronger computational proposal might ask how much mass must change, or at what cost, to make a specified cluster enter or leave the consensus. That would need a target, permitted changes, and a cost on hierarchies; the dissertation defines none of them. Such robustness or intervention problems could be worthwhile research, but the paper supplies no basis for choosing among them. They are additional problems built around its rule, not mirrors of its results.

The axiomatic results do have possible continuous counterparts: one could define mass versions of the axioms and characterize the resulting aggregation rules. But that would continuize the axiomatic question itself, which falls outside the programme’s stated scope. The paper’s discussion of clustering and legislative bills makes population scenarios imaginable; it does not turn those characterizations into computational questions.

So the negative case is strong for the dissertation as written: it offers no eligible computational anchor, and its most natural population extension does not uncover a computational problem studied by the authors. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror could ever be found is weaker. Someone could develop a useful costed robustness problem for hierarchy consensus. On the evidence here, though, that would be a new project inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of its computational contribution.
