The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(U\), an initial distribution \(\mu_0\) of binary-rational masses over complete rankings, and a stream of rational mass increments \((t,\delta)\), can an online algorithm output after every update a permutation \(\omega\) satisfying \(\mathbb{E}[F_\mu(\omega)]\le 2\min_{\pi\in S_U}F_\mu(\pi)\), where \(F_\mu(\omega)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{e\in U}|\omega(e)-t(e)|\), using \(O(n\log n)\) arithmetic operations per update and bit complexity polynomial in the rational input length?

The model it lives in

The alternatives form the fixed set \(U\); each type \(t\) is a complete ranking, and \(\mu_t\) is its share of the population. The output remains a permutation \(\omega\), with objective \(F_\mu(\omega)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{e\in U}|\omega(e)-t(e)|\). Updates add mass to a ranking type; a mass-weighted sample supplies the expected \(2\)-approximation.

The objection that survived

The \(O(n\log n)\) update and bit-complexity claims for rational mass updates are inferred extensions, not results proved in the paper; the proponent acknowledges this, so it bounds the dynamic claim without defeating the mirror.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4's expected \(2\)-approximation and the continuous counterparts of Theorems 3 and 6's update and cost-evaluation bounds. It does not establish a quality guarantee for LR-Aggregation alone or address the cited Kemeny hardness result.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does LR-Aggregation alone have a provable approximation guarantee for arbitrary rational distributions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a dynamic, high-multiplicity version of footrule rank aggregation. A type is a complete ranking of the same \(n\) alternatives for every person of that type; its mass is that type’s share of the population. For a distribution \(\mu\), the cost of an aggregate ranking \(\omega\) is the average per-person footrule distance
\[ F_\mu(\omega)=\sum_t \mu_t\sum_{e\in U}|\omega(e)-t(e)|. \]
The decision is still a permutation. It is the population that becomes continuous.

A plausible regime is a large electorate or user population ranking a fixed slate of options: there may be millions of people but only \(\tau\) distinct ballot types in the observed population, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the headcount. This is a sensible high-multiplicity model because people with the same ranking are indistinguishable under the paper’s objective. The paper’s small classroom example is not such a regime, but its broader rank-aggregation and social-choice framing supports one.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that the Dynamic Rank Aggregation algorithm, which runs LR-Aggregation and Pick-A-Perm and returns the one with lower footrule cost, is an expected \(2\)-approximation to optimal footrule aggregation. The paper proves the relevant footrule guarantee in Proposition 2; its earlier \(2\)-approximation result for Kemeny aggregation is cited from Ailon et al., not used as the footrule result. The paper also proves in Theorem 3 that LR-Aggregation updates its aggregate in \(O(n\log n)\) time after a new ranking arrives, and in Theorem 6 that a candidate’s footrule cost can be evaluated in \(O(n\log n)\). These bounds support the dynamic implementation of the lead result.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover:

Dynamic Continuous Footrule Aggregation with Mass Updates. An instance gives a finite alternative set \(U\), an initial distribution \(\mu_0\) supported on \(\tau\) complete rankings with binary-rational masses, and a stream of updates \((t,\delta)\), where each update adds rational mass \(\delta\ge 0\) of ranking type \(t\). At every prefix, the algorithm must output a ranking \(\omega\) whose expected population-average footrule cost is at most twice the optimum for the current normalized distribution:
\[ \mathbb{E}[F_{\mu}(\omega)]\le 2\min_{\pi\in S_n}F_{\mu}(\pi). \]
A solution is an online algorithm meeting that guarantee after each update. The target is \(O(n\log n)\) arithmetic operations per mass update, with bit complexity polynomial in the rational input length \(L\); initialization can process the \(\tau\) support rankings.

This is a continuous extension of the paper’s current-multiset objective: replace each occurrence count by its type mass, maintain a weighted sample from the population for Pick-A-Perm, and use weighted presence and cost totals in the LR and rank trees. The extension is not stated or proved in the paper, so the time bound for rational mass updates is an inference from its update procedures and the linearity of the footrule objective. In the terminology of the programme, I expect this question to be Class A. Moreover, the one-shot exact weighted problem is itself polynomial: set the cost of assigning item \(e\) to position \(j\) to \(\sum_t\mu_t|j-t(e)|\), then solve the resulting minimum-cost assignment problem. The interesting dynamic question is whether the paper’s fast update approach and approximation guarantee carry over to mass updates.

The scope is the paper’s dynamic Spearman footrule aggregation and its approximation guarantee. It does not continuize the ranking outcome, and the updates remain dynamic; the continuous object is the population distribution. There is no numbered hardness result in this paper to anchor a hardness-dissolving mirror. Its cited NP-hardness concerns Kemeny aggregation under Kendall distance, a different objective, and is not a numbered result proved here.

The weakest point is that the authors study rankings arriving one at a time, not rational-mass updates. The weighted version is a close extension, but the paper does not establish its bit-complexity bounds. Also, the expected \(2\)-approximation transfers cleanly, while the paper’s empirically strong LR output has no proved quality guarantee. So this is a credible mirror of the dynamic aggregation problem, but not a case that turns a known hardness result into a continuous tractability gain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper’s contribution is about handling a stream of individual rankings, while a rational-mass update is a new input operation. But that distinction does not defeat the mirror: a large population of users with repeated ranking types is a plausible regime, and adding mass to a type is a natural way to model population growth or changing composition. The classroom example is small, but the paper’s social-choice and recommendation settings supply better scenarios. Rankings also contain all the information the objective uses, so grouping identical rankings loses nothing.

Theorem 4 is a real computational anchor. For a distribution \(\mu\), draw a ranking \(t\) with probability \(\mu_t\) and return it as the aggregate. The same weighted-median argument gives an expected \(2\)-approximation to optimal footrule aggregation. For mass arrivals, weighted reservoir sampling maintains such a draw; the paper’s LR and rank-tree counts can likewise be replaced by weighted sums. Theorem 3’s update bound and Theorem 6’s cost-evaluation bound therefore have direct continuous counterparts. These extensions look straightforward, which weakens the case for a substantial new complexity result, but ease of solution is not a reason to reject a well-posed mirror.

A stronger mirror would ask for the exact weighted optimum. For each item \(e\) and position \(j\), set \(w_{e,j}=\sum_t\mu_t|j-t(e)|\), then solve the minimum-cost assignment problem. That makes the static continuous problem polynomial-time solvable, but it remains a valid population-continuous computational question. It also does not settle the dynamic mass-update problem: maintaining an exact optimum as weights change is a different task from computing it once.

So I cannot honestly defeat every anchor. The paper proves algorithmic results, the population can naturally have many repeated ranking types, and neither identity dependence nor a degenerate continuum blocks the model. The best objection is that the advertised \(2\)-approximation and update bounds may transfer with little new work. Under the programme’s stated standard, that is a concern about how much the mirror teaches, not a decisive reason that no worthwhile mirror exists.
