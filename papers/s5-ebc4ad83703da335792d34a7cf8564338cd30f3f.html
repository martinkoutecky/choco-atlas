The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest lead is PAM-Canon\(_\infty\): a high-multiplicity version of the payoff-preserving rearrangement in Proposition 1(i). One caveat comes first: the paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Its propositions are economic statements about matching patterns; none classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized. Proposition 1 is proved here, but it is not a complexity theorem. So this is a plausible computational follow-up, not a qualifying computational anchor.

Proposition 1(i) says that if, for every \(p>p'\), \(a>a'\), and \(u\in[0,\phi(p',a,0)]\),
\[ \phi\bigl(p,a,\psi(a,p',u)\bigr)\ge \phi\bigl(p,a',\psi(a',p',u)\bigr), \]
then every equilibrium is payoff-equivalent to an equilibrium with positive assortative matching (PAM). Proposition 1(ii) gives the analogous result for negative assortative matching under generalized decreasing differences. The first result is the better lead here: its proof explicitly rearranges crossed matches while keeping the agents’ payoffs fixed.

The mirror problem, PAM-Canon\(_\infty\). An instance gives two ordered finite supports of complete agent types, \(P=\{p_1<\cdots<p_r\}\) and \(A=\{a_1<\cdots<a_s\}\), with rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to one on each side. It gives rational piecewise-linear representations of the pairwise utility frontiers, assumes GID, and supplies an existing stable mass matching \(x\) and its type payoffs \((\pi_i,\omega_j)\). The task is to output a mass flow \(x'\) with the same row and column totals, feasible matches at those same payoffs, and no blocking pair, whose support is monotone: there are no crossed positive-flow pairs \(x'_{ij}>0\) and \(x'_{i'j'}>0\) with \(i<i'\) and \(j>j'\). A solution is that flow \(x'\). This is a feasibility and rearrangement problem, not an optimization problem: the paper’s equilibrium notion has no objective to minimize or maximize.

The intended regime is a large two-sided labor market with many workers and firms but relatively few payoff-relevant types—for example, repeated project types matched with cohorts of workers sharing the same wealth, preferences, and incentive-relevant characteristics. Mass is population share, and the matching decision is how much of each type is paired with each other type. Each person still receives one partner; only the aggregate flow can be fractional. If the number of agents is \(N\), the relevant input measures are the support sizes \(r,s\), not \(N\).

I would expect PAM-Canon\(_\infty\) to be tractable (Class A). Proposition 1’s proof suggests uncrossing matches while preserving payoffs; in the mass model, the natural operation is to reroute the largest feasible amount of crossed flow at once. The algorithmic question is whether this can be implemented in time polynomial in the support sizes and the binary encoding length of the masses and frontiers, without expanding the population into individual agents. That expectation is an inference from the proof, not a result established by the paper. Further questions include whether one can compute an equilibrium from market primitives rather than from a supplied equilibrium, and how the problem changes when GID fails.

This mirror is plausible because the paper’s agents’ payoffs depend on type rather than identity, and its result concerns the matching pattern itself. Its weakest point is also substantial: supplying an equilibrium leaves the harder equilibrium-search question unanswered. And population continuity is not new to this paper: Section 7.4 already discusses continuum economies, with the stronger weak-GID result attributed to Legros and Newman (2006b). The defensible computational contribution would therefore be the complexity of the finite-support mass rearrangement, not the discovery of a continuous-population matching model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 is a theorem about stable matching patterns, not a complexity result: it defines no encoded computational input, algorithm, or resource bound. That gap does not rule out a follow-up, but it makes the paper a weak anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme.

PAM-Canon\(_\infty\), the proposed anchor, appears to reduce to a direct calculation. Let \(P_i=\sum_{k\le i}\mu_k\) and \(Q_j=\sum_{\ell\le j}\nu_\ell\). The unique mass matching with noncrossing support is

\[ x'_{ij}=\left|[P_{i-1},P_i]\cap[Q_{j-1},Q_j]\right|. \]

A sweep through the two lists of cumulative masses computes it in \(O(r+s)\) rational operations, with polynomial bit complexity. The supplied equilibrium and payoffs do not guide that calculation. Under GID, Proposition 1 and the continuum discussion in Section 7.4 establish that the resulting monotone matching can preserve the payoffs; the calculation itself just aligns the mass intervals. The NAM analogue under GDD has the same direct construction with one ordering reversed. This leaves no pricing problem, optimization problem, or meaningful search task in the proposed canonicalization.

The stronger candidate is to compute a stable mass equilibrium from market primitives. A large labor market with repeated worker and firm types is a credible high-multiplicity regime; the paper’s type-based, identity-independent payoffs support that interpretation. But computing equilibrium would be a new algorithmic question, not a computational result of this paper. The paper permits arbitrary continuous frontiers, so a finite computational problem first needs an added encoding—such as rational piecewise-linear frontiers—and the complexity would depend on that choice. Such a project could be worthwhile in algorithmic matching, but the paper supplies no discrete computational problem whose continuous version it would answer.

Section 7.4 already establishes a continuum-population matching result analytically. That is not prior computational work, and it should not count as a novelty collision. It does mean that the proposed contribution would need to come from a separately specified equilibrium-computation problem, rather than from continuizing Proposition 1.

The negative case is therefore strongest against PAM-Canon\(_\infty\): its proposed output is a simple quantile coupling, and the paper states no computational question behind it. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario is weaker. A carefully encoded stable-equilibrium computation problem may be worth studying; the paper alone does not make that case.
