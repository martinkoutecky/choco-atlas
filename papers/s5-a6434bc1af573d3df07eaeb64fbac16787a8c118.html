The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying numbered anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. The closest candidate is an unnumbered statement in Section 2, attributed to Abraham et al. (2007): static kidney exchange with cycles of bounded length is polynomial-time solvable for \(k=2\), but NP-complete for each fixed \(k\ge 3\). That is cited background, not a result proved here, and the paper prints no theorem number for it. So this can only be a provisional case, not one that meets your anchor rule.

The most convincing mirror of that cited problem is Continuous Static Kidney Exchange. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of complete patient–donor types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a directed compatibility relation \(E\subseteq T\times T\), and a fixed cycle bound \(k\). A type must capture the properties the matching problem uses, including its compatibility relations. Let \(\mathcal C_k\) be the allowed cycle patterns of length at most \(k\); a pattern may use the same type more than once, with each occurrence representing distinct members of that type. For each \(Q\in\mathcal C_k\), choose \(x_Q\ge 0\), the mass of cycles run with that pattern. The question is to maximize
\[ \sum_{Q\in\mathcal C_k}|Q|x_Q \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{Q\in\mathcal C_k}a_{tQ}x_Q\le \mu_t \quad\text{for every }t\in T, \]
where \(a_{tQ}\) counts occurrences of type \(t\) in \(Q\). A solution is the vector of cycle masses \(x\).

This preserves the underlying choice: assign compatible patient–donor pairs to exchange cycles to maximize transplants. Mass counts agents in repeated classes; each infinitesimal unit assigned to a cycle still receives a whole, integral exchange. In a large exchange program, plausible shared types could be clinical and operational cohorts with repeated compatibility profiles, while \(\tau=|T|\) is much smaller than the number of pairs. The paper’s optimization module computes this cycle-packing core, so this seems a question its authors could recognize.

For fixed \(k\), there are at most \(O(\tau^k)\) cycle patterns, giving a polynomial-size LP. I would expect this mirror to be tractable—Class A—and the discrete NP-completeness to dissolve when a cohort’s mass can be split among cycle patterns. A useful next question is the high-multiplicity bridge: when \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), does solving the LP give a discrete solution within additive \(O(k\tau)\) transplants? Also worth studying are unbounded \(k\), and extensions that include dynamic arrivals, dropouts, and uncertain crossmatch outcomes.

This case covers only the static cycle-packing problem, not the paper’s dynamic simulation results. Its weakest point is the type regime: tissue compatibility can vary pair by pair, so a type that captures every compatibility relation may be nearly unique to each patient–donor pair. Grouping only by blood type or PRA would not address that. The positive case depends on whether repeated, sufficiently homogeneous compatibility classes are plausible in an exchange program; without them, the LP may describe a different regime from the paper’s instances.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a green verdict is that the proponent supplies no qualifying result from this paper to mirror. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. Its NP-completeness statement for bounded-cycle kidney exchange is background attributed to Abraham et al. (2007). The proposed continuous LP therefore mirrors a problem the paper cites, rather than a computational result it establishes.

The LP is a coherent model, and it is the proponent’s strongest point: for fixed \(k\), a finite set of exact types yields polynomially many cycle patterns. I would not dismiss it because its answer may be easy, or because fractional masses might change the discrete answer. The difficulty is that this model strips away the dynamic features that define the paper’s contribution. The simulator follows individual pair arrivals and departures, realized compatibility arcs, and failures discovered after matching. KEP compatibility depends on donor and recipient characteristics, including HLA, and is represented by pairwise edges in the compatibility graph.[The paper on KEP instance generation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305054822000107)

A faithful type model could include complete clinical profiles and preserve compatibility between types. If those exact profiles recur often, that could be a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But the paper does not establish that regime: its population data generate individual pairs and their realized arcs. Coarse types with compatibility probabilities would instead define a stochastic graph model whose cycle availability depends on the realized edges. And extending the typed LP to arrivals, dropouts, and crossmatch failures would require formulating a new dynamic optimization problem; the paper compares policies in a simulator rather than defining a computational problem of choosing an optimal policy.

So the negative case is strong on eligibility: the fallback is not an anchor to a result of this paper. It is weaker on the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The exact-type static LP may be worth studying as a separate ChoCo problem, and I cannot honestly rule out useful dynamic continuizations. If the third reader accepts a model-derived question as an anchor, the proponent’s LP survives; under the stated requirement to anchor on a result of the paper, it does not.
