The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a compliant computational anchor: the paper contains no named result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity. The closest hooks are theoretical. Theorem 5, proved here, characterizes implementable outcome sets as exactly the consistent choice sets. Theorem 15, also proved here, says the ultimate quasi-uncovered set \(UQU\) is a consistent choice set and contains every consistent choice set. Corollary 16 says \(UQU\) is implementable by a terminating policy program satisfying the one-deviation property, and contains every outcome implementable by any such program. These are existence and characterization results, not computational anchors.

The strongest honest case for a *research mirror* is a continuous electorate for the paper’s agenda-formation problem. Imagine a large party or electorate choosing from a finite slate of policies. There are \(N\) voters but only \(\tau\) distinct preference types, with \(\tau \ll N\); a type is a complete ranking of the slate, and \(\mu_t\) is its population share. Define \(x M_\mu y\) when more than half the population strictly prefers \(x\) to \(y\), and use the paper’s completion \(R_\mu=\bar M_\mu\). This is a direct high-multiplicity version of Section 5, which derives a majority relation from a finite electorate. The authors should recognize the agenda process and one-deviation question as theirs.

A concrete follow-up would be Continuous-Population Ultimate Quasi-Uncovered Set. Its input is a finite alternative set \(X\), a support of \(\tau\) ranking types, and binary-rational masses \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) summing to one. Its output is the exact set \(UQU(R_\mu)\), the maximal set of terminal alternatives implementable by a terminating policy program satisfying the one-deviation property. A solution is that subset of \(X\); one could also ask for a finite-state policy implementing it. The policy’s action remains the paper’s: after a history of status quos, propose another alternative or stop. The task is to identify the maximal implementable terminal set, not to optimize a new welfare objective.

For finite \(X\), I would expect this follow-up to be tractable: compute the majority relation from the \(\tau\) types, then iterate the paper’s quasi-uncovered-set operation until it stabilizes. This is a proposed algorithmic consequence of the paper’s characterization, not a complexity result stated in it. The mirror covers the population-aggregation version of the one-deviation agenda problem and the structure in Theorems 5 and 15; it does not mirror a computational finding by the authors.

The weak point is decisive under the requested anchor rule: the computable question is a new extension, not a continuous analogue of a named complexity result in this paper. So the paper offers a plausible population-continuum setting and strong structural guidance, but no qualifying computational anchor for a positive mirror case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper proves no computational complexity result. Theorem 5 characterizes implementable sets as consistent choice sets; Theorem 15 identifies the maximal such set; Corollary 16 shows that set can be implemented. They are structural results about an arbitrary complete social preference relation. Section 5 gives a noncooperative interpretation, but no computational problem with an input encoding, algorithm, or complexity bound. None qualifies as the required computational anchor.

The strongest repaired mirror would use a finite policy slate \(X\), \(\tau\) ranking types, and rational population masses \(\mu_t\). For each pair of policies, compute the mass preferring one to the other, form the completed majority relation, and ask for \(UQU(R_\mu)\) or a policy implementing it. That is a natural high-multiplicity question, and the authors would likely recognize the agenda-formation problem. It still creates a new computational study: the paper’s results already apply once \(R_\mu\) is formed, while the paper itself neither studies the complexity of computing \(UQU\) nor provides a discrete computational result whose high-multiplicity version could be compared. Its arbitrary compact outcome spaces also have no computational representation specified.

I would not reject this mirror on the grounds that the population is too individualized or that the continuum changes the answer. The type-mass model is sensible. The narrower conclusion is that it is a plausible independent research project, not a continuous mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. If “worthwhile mirror” includes launching new computational questions around theoretical results, the negative case is weaker; the requested anchor standard rules that out here.
