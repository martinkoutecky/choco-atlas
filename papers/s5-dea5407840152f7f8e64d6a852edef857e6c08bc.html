The continuous mirror question

Given chore categories \(O\), a supported type distribution \(\mu\) over types \(t\in T\) specified by binary supermodular costs \(c_t:2^O\to\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) and positive rational weights \(w_t\), and feasible rational per-capita supplies \(q_o\), choose masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) for integral bundles \(S\subseteq O\), with \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:o\in S}x_{t,S}=q_o\). Compute a feasible assignment whose histogram \((\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_K)\) is lexicographically minimum, where \(a_1<\cdots<a_K\) are the possible utility levels \(-c_t(S)/w_t\) and \(\rho_j=\sum_{t,S:-c_t(S)/w_t=a_j}x_{t,S}\). Determine its computational complexity in \(m=|O|\), support size \(\tau\), rational input length, and the specified cost-access model.

The model it lives in

Normalize a large workforce to unit mass, group workers by complete type \((c_t,w_t)\), and distribute each type’s mass among integral bundles of recurring chore categories; \(q_o\) is the per-capita supply of category \(o\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1’s weighted leximin criterion; Theorem 4.2 supplies a second surviving mirror for weighted \(p\)-mean malfare. It does not establish continuous algorithms for either criterion or cover the full General Yankee Swap framework and its other results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under a compact cost-function encoding, or an exact marginal-cost oracle with query costs counted, what is the complexity of computing the lexicographically optimal mass assignment?
  • Can pricing over bundle patterns \(S\subseteq O\) for binary supermodular costs be solved efficiently enough to yield a column-generation algorithm?

The case FOR (proponent)

I think the strongest mirror is a population of workers with recurring chore categories. Each worker still receives an integral bundle; only the measure of workers receiving each bundle is fractional. That preserves the paper’s individual cost functions and fairness questions while making a large workforce into the continuous object.

The lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: running General Yankee Swap for Chores with \(\phi(X,i)=(-c_i(X_i)/w_i,w_i)\) computes a weighted leximin allocation. Weighted leximin means lexicographically maximizing the ascending-sorted vector of individual utilities \(-c_i(X_i)/w_i\). A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 4.2, also proved here: for every \(p\ge 1\), running that algorithm with \(\phi(X,i)=w_i[c_i(X_i)^p-(c_i(X_i)+1)^p]\) computes an allocation minimizing \(\sum_i w_i c_i(X_i)^p\), the paper’s min weighted \(p\)-mean malfare objective. The supplied paper has no named hardness result; these are algorithmic anchors.

Here is a concrete regime: a large hospital or firm assigns recurring categories of chores to workers. Many workers have exactly the same cost function over those categories and the same entitlement weight. For instance, \(N\) workers might fall into \(\tau\) role-and-entitlement types, with \(\tau\ll N\), while each chore category has a supply proportional to \(N\). This is a high-multiplicity regime: type is the complete pair \((c_t,w_t)\), and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of workers of type \(t\).

For both mirrors, take a finite set \(O\) of chore categories and rational per-capita supplies \(q_o\in[0,1]\). The upper bound means each worker receives at most one chore of each category; repeated slots can be represented as separate categories. Each type \(t\) has a positive rational weight \(w_t\) and a binary supermodular cost function \(c_t:2^O\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\). An allocation is a rational mass \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) for each type \(t\) and integral bundle \(S\subseteq O\), satisfying \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:o\in S}x_{t,S}=q_o\). Thus all chore supply is assigned, and workers of the same type may receive different bundles. One can give costs by exact marginal-cost oracles; the paper does not specify a compact cost-function encoding, so the encoding belongs in the formal problem statement.

The lead problem is Continuous Weighted Leximin for Binary Supermodular Chores: on that instance, compute a feasible \(x\) whose distribution of individual weighted utilities is leximin-maximal. Precisely, list the distinct possible values \(a_1<\cdots<a_K\) among \(-c_t(S)/w_t\), and let \(\rho_j=\sum_{t,S:\,-c_t(S)/w_t=a_j}x_{t,S}\). The question is to lexicographically minimize \((\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_K)\). This is the continuous extension of the sorted finite utility vector: first reduce the population mass at the worst utility level, then at the next-worst, and so on. If all masses and supplies are multiples of \(1/N\), this is exactly the histogram induced by an \(N\)-worker allocation. The solution is the bundle-mass vector \(x\), not a fractional bundle for any worker.

The second problem, Continuous Weighted \(p\)-Malfare for Binary Supermodular Chores, uses the same instance and asks for a feasible \(x\) minimizing \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}w_t c_t(S)^p\). For a finite population with these type masses, this objective is the discrete objective divided by \(N\). For a bit-encoded version, one can fix an integer \(p\ge1\); Theorem 4.2 itself states the result for every real \(p\ge1\).

I would expect both mirrors to be Class A targets. With bundle patterns as columns, the malfare objective is linear in \(x\); leximin is a sequence of linear objectives over the utility levels. The number of possible utility levels is at most \(\tau(m+1)\), since each binary marginal cost is an integer from \(0\) to \(m\). For compact cost oracles, however, the bundle-pattern LP has exponentially many columns in \(m\), and a polynomial algorithm needs an efficient pricing method. The continuous problem makes that a clear question for the paper’s supermodular structure rather than assuming the finite-agent algorithm automatically answers it.

The mirror covers the paper’s weighted leximin and weighted \(p\)-mean malfare results, not every result or the whole General Yankee Swap framework. Its main weakness is that the paper proves an algorithm for finite agents and one-off chores; it does not prove the required mass algorithm or its pricing complexity. The scenario also assumes recurring chore categories with identical costs across copies. If the application depends on every named chore being unique and every worker having a distinct cost profile, this mirror loses force.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make a convincing case against either anchor. The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror scales chore supplies along with the population. With a fixed set of chores, letting the number of workers grow would make chores per worker vanish; the useful version instead treats chores as recurring categories and assigns each worker an integral bundle from them. That is a real modeling choice, and it makes the mirror a mass-assignment problem over worker types and bundles.

But it is also a reasonable high-multiplicity regime. A type can be the complete pair \((c_t,w_t)\), and the fairness criteria do not track named workers. For rational type masses and supplies, the bundle-mass solution corresponds to assigning whole bundles to cohorts; finite instances with quantities in multiples of \(1/N\) give the direct replication. The prompt explicitly allows supply scaling when it accompanies population continuization, so this objection does not defeat the mirror.

Theorem 4.1 has a clean continuous question: maximize weighted leximin over the distribution of individual utilities. Since there are finitely many possible utility levels, minimizing the mass at the lowest level, then the next-lowest, gives a well-defined extension of the finite sorted-vector objective. Splitting a type’s mass across bundle patterns does not give any worker a fractional bundle. The paper’s tie-breaking by agent index concerns its algorithm; the fairness objective itself does not depend on identities.

Theorem 4.2 likewise has a direct mass version, minimizing \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}w_t c_t(S)^p\). For integer \(p\), this is a linear objective over bundle masses. The paper does not establish that either continuous problem has a polynomial-time solution: the bundle formulation has exponentially many patterns, and its pricing problem needs analysis. That is unfinished computational work, not a reason the question lacks value.

The most substantial caveat is that the paper specifies no compact encoding for its cost functions, so a complexity result for either mirror must state its cost-access model carefully. That is a formalization burden shared with the paper’s own efficiency claims. It does not invalidate the mirrors: the authors could choose a representation or oracle model and ask the resulting computational question.

So the negative case is weak. Both named algorithmic results survive as coherent continuous-population questions. The application may be less persuasive when chores are unique and workers have idiosyncratic cost functions, but recurring chore categories and repeated worker types provide a plausible regime for this model.
