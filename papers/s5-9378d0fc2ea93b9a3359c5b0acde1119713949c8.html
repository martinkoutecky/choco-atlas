What the mirror covers

The proposed extension concerns mass popular matching; it does not cover the random-instance threshold in Theorem 5 or its bounds in Theorems 3 and 4. The finite structural criterion in Lemma 4 also remains without a computational claim by this paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest positive case that meets your anchor requirement. The closest result is Theorem 5, proved here: for random strict \(k\)-incomplete preference lists, when \(k\ge 4\), let \(\alpha_k\) be the root of
\[ \alpha_k e^{-1/(2\alpha_k)}=1-(1-e^{-1/\alpha_k})^{k-1}. \]
If \(\alpha>\alpha_k\), a popular matching exists with probability \(1-o(1)\); if \(\alpha<\alpha_k\), it exists with probability \(o(1)\). For \(k\le 3\), it exists with probability \(1-o(1)\) for every \(\alpha\ge1\). This is a sharp result, but it concerns probabilities in a random finite model, not an algorithm, hardness classification, or other computational complexity result. Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 establish its bounds; Theorems 1 and 2 are cited results for complete lists.

Lemma 4 gives a structural criterion: an \(A\)-perfect matching exists exactly when the top-choice graph has no complex component. Together with Lemma 1, that can support a polynomial-time test, but the paper does not state a named complexity result of that form; Lemma 1 is also cited from elsewhere.

A large-cohort version of popular matching could be sensible: represent graduates by a distribution over preference types and positions by supplies that scale with the cohort, then ask whether a mass assignment is popular by majority mass. But that would be a new deterministic existence question, and the paper’s threshold theorem does not answer it. In the theorem’s own regime, with fixed \(k\ge4\) and \(m=\Theta(n)\), there are \(\Theta(m^k)\) possible ordered preference lists, so random agents almost surely have distinct lists. A repeated-type cohort scenario is possible, but it would not anchor a mirror of Theorem 5.

So I have no continuous problem to state per a qualifying anchor. The paper offers a plausible matching setting, but no named computational result on which to build the required positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no new computational result to continuize. Its main result, Theorem 5, is a phase transition for the *probability* that a popular matching exists in a randomly generated finite instance. Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 prove its bounds; Theorems 1 and 2 are cited results for complete lists. None classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithm. Keeping the random experiment and taking a limit would study a random-graph or mean-field question; replacing the random electorate by a society \(\mu\) would instead ask a different, deterministic question. The latter may be useful, but it does not mirror the paper’s threshold theorem.

The threshold’s own regime also resists a high-multiplicity reading. With fixed \(k\ge4\) and \(m=\alpha n\), there are \((m)_k\) possible ordered preference lists, and the expected number of pairs of people with the same list is \(\binom n2/(m)_k=\Theta(n^{2-k})\). Thus with high probability almost every person has a distinct type, while the type universe grows with \(n\). The support size is essentially \(n\), not moderate. One could build a different model with a fixed set of ranking types and repeated cohorts, but that changes the regime driving the theorem. The frequencies of those types also do not by themselves retain the random top-choice graph whose complex components determine the event.

Lemma 4 is the other plausible anchor: an \(A\)-perfect matching exists exactly when the top-choice graph has no complex component. But this is a structural characterization, not a complexity result established by the paper. Its graph has one edge per person, and its condition concerns finite cycles and orientations. A mass-flow version would need to say how a positive mass edge is assigned and whether it can split. It would also need to preserve popularity comparisons: a type-level flow records how much mass receives each item, but comparing two such flows requires specifying which same-type people receive which items under both plans. A worst-case coupling is a defensible choice, but it is a new definition with its own computational question.

The graduates-and-positions scenario is plausible as a high-multiplicity application of popular matching. It does not rescue an anchor here: the paper’s random model has individually labelled items and, with high probability, no repeated preference lists, while the cohort model would have repeated types and scaled supplies by construction. A mass popular-matching problem could be worth studying on its own, but it would be a new extension rather than a continuization of a named result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong on fit: this paper supplies no computational-complexity anchor, and its central random regime has almost no repeated voter types. It is weaker as the universal claim the assignment demands. A carefully defined mass version of popular matching may still be a worthwhile research problem; what I cannot defend is presenting it as a mirror of this paper’s phase-transition result.
