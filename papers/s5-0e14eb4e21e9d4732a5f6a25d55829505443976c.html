The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.1 proves existence of an MSS, Theorem 4.6 proves uniqueness under lower hemi-continuity, and Theorem 4.12 characterizes MSSs through closed cycles when the state space is finite. Corollary 5.7 identifies the MSS with stable matchings. None gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or even an encoded computational problem.

The best reformulation of Theorems 4.1 and 4.6 would give a society as rational masses of finitely many agent types and ask for its MSS. That could become an interesting computation problem, but the paper’s results offer existence and uniqueness, not a way to compute the set. With arbitrary preferences and effectivity correspondences, the reformulation also lacks a finite input specification until additional restrictions are imposed. Those restrictions would define a new algorithmic project.

Theorem 4.12 is the closest candidate to an algorithmic anchor: in a finite state space, finding closed cycles resembles finding strongly connected components in a deviation graph. But the theorem’s finiteness concerns states, not population size. For a continuous state space, it establishes only that the union of closed cycles lies in the MSS; it does not provide the finite characterization. A graph over mass allocations would require its own representation and algorithm.

The matching result gives the strongest plausible population story. One could represent men and women by preference types and study stable matchings between their masses. That is a sensible high-multiplicity problem, and prior high-multiplicity work would support the regime. Still, Corollary 5.7 establishes an equivalence of solution concepts in the finite matching model; it makes no computational claim. Computing a mass matching would be a new problem prompted by the paper, rather than a continuous version of a result it proves.

The Bertrand application is also tempting, but it is a two-firm model. Extending it to a distribution of firms would require specifying how mass, capacity, and market allocation affect payoffs. That may yield worthwhile economics, but the paper’s characterization does not anchor a complexity question about that model.

So the case against a *mirror of this paper’s results* is strong: none of the proposed anchors meets ChoCo’s computational standard. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous computational project could grow from the paper is weaker. Continuous matching and coalition formation are plausible research directions; their plausibility does not supply the missing computational result here.
