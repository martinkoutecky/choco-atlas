The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results concern equilibrium welfare and communication rounds, not whether an algorithmic problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. In particular, communication rounds here count strategic messages between a buyer and seller; they are not running time.

The closest result is Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper: when the buyer has two possible values, \(\Theta_B=\{v_0,v_1\}\), and the seller has any finite set of costs, the agents can achieve efficient allocation in two rounds, for any priors on those types. Theorem 3.2, also proved here, says that in bilateral trade, if the equilibrium message complexity is finite, then the equilibrium allocation at that complexity has welfare equal to the efficient benchmark \(W^*(\mathcal D_S,\mathcal D_B)\). Both are substantive results, but neither classifies the computational complexity of a problem. Theorem 3.5 is the stronger near-miss: its proof constructs refinements, but the theorem does not claim a running-time bound.

The best positive scenario I can make is a platform running many independent procurement negotiations. Suppose a fraction \(\mu_j\) of its negotiations belong to cohort \(j\), where each cohort has the same local trade environment: a buyer with two possible values, a seller with finitely many possible costs, and specified priors. A solution would give each cohort a Bayes-plausible, voluntary, two-round equilibrium protocol and the resulting prices and allocations. The question is whether the aggregate welfare reaches \(\sum_j \mu_j W_j^*\). Theorem 3.5 implies yes, cohort by cohort. The paper’s indifference-distribution construction suggests how to build the protocols, though it does not establish a bit-complexity bound for doing so.

That is a possible *population* extension: \(\mu_j\) is the share of negotiations in each cohort, and the regime could have many negotiations but few distinct cohort types. Its weakness is substantial. The mass is over independent negotiations, not the agents in the paper’s game, and aggregating their outcomes leaves the strategic problem unchanged. The theorem applies separately to every negotiation; the continuum adds bookkeeping, not a new computational question. Treating \(\mathcal D_S\) and \(\mathcal D_B\) themselves as the continuous population would be less defensible still: they are beliefs about two players’ private types, not population masses.

So this is the strongest honest positive lead I see, but it does not satisfy the required anchor rule. Under that rule, the paper has no named computational-complexity result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts at the programme’s entry gate: this paper names no computational problem whose complexity it classifies. Theorem 3.2 is an equilibrium welfare statement, and Theorem 3.5 says when two rounds of strategic communication suffice. Here “message complexity” counts rounds between the players; it is not algorithmic running time. The exact concavification implementation makes computation a plausible follow-up topic, but the paper states no complexity result for that computation to mirror.

Theorem 3.5 is the closest candidate. A better computational question would be: given rational values, costs, and priors, how efficiently can one construct the two-round equilibrium protocol? But its distributions describe uncertainty about the private type of one buyer or one seller. Turning those distributions into population masses changes the object: a continuum of buyers and sellers would be a market, and adding matching, shared supply, or other coupling would change the game and the theorem. If instead many independent negotiations are grouped into cohorts, Theorem 3.5 applies separately to each cohort; aggregate welfare is just the mass-weighted sum of their welfare. That gives bookkeeping, not a new computational problem.

Theorem 3.2 has the same obstacle. Aggregating independent instances can restate its welfare conclusion pointwise or integrate it over a population of negotiation environments. Requiring a uniform round bound across that population could raise a further question, but it would concern strategic protocol depth across many games, not computational complexity over a continuous society.

So none of the proponent’s candidates survives as a continuous computational mirror. The case is strongest on this paper’s stated results; it is weaker as a universal claim about every possible extension. One could introduce a new population model and study the complexity of computing equilibria in it. But that would be a new computational research question built around the paper’s ideas, not a continuization of one of its named results.
