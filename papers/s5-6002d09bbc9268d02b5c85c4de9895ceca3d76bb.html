The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The absence of a numbered complexity theorem is real, but it doesn’t leave this paper without a computational result to mirror. Its contribution is to build CP models for the four relaxation problems and evaluate how efficiently they find optimal relaxations. The paper reports that the models handle many instances with little overhead; its larger-instance experiments solve several relaxation variants on instances with up to 90 agents per side. That is an empirical algorithmic result. A continuous version can ask whether the same optimization problems admit exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithms when the input is a distribution over types.

A natural mirror keeps the three roles and changes the population representation. Let each side have unit mass distributed over finitely many types, where a type includes its ranking over counterpart types and its per-unit compensation costs. A variable \(x_{ijk}\) records the mass assigned to a triple of types, with its marginals matching the input distributions. Each unit of mass still receives one integral triple; only the aggregate assignment is fractional. Blocking coalitions then have measurable capacity: each type contributes the mass willing to leave its current assignments for the proposed partners. One can ask for the minimum per-capita cost of blocked or accommodating mass needed to eliminate positive-mass blocking coalitions, corresponding to the paper’s TAS, AAS, MAS, and MPAS objectives. The computational question is how the exact or approximate complexity depends on the support sizes and input bit length.

This is plausible in the applications the paper itself discusses. A large network may contain many users, data sources, and servers in a few preference and cost classes; a housing market may contain many households and apartments of recurring types. In those regimes, the objective concerns instability and compensation, not the identity of a particular household or server. Defining types by complete preferences and costs preserves the distinctions the model uses. A mass-based relaxation cost also prevents the result from being an artifact of counting one vanishingly small agent.

The mirror does require preferences to be expressed over counterpart types and the relaxation objectives to be scaled as masses or per-capita costs. That is a substantive model choice, but it fits the programme’s stated scope. The paper’s lack of numbered complexity theorems is a limitation on what its continuous mirror could claim to extend; it does not show that the computational question is absent.
