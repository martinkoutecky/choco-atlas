The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many listed types \(t\), each with values \(v_{tj}\in\{1,p/q\}\) for \(r\) goods cohorts, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\) with \(\sum_j b_j\ge1\), compute a maximizing allocation. A type-\(t\) recipient receives an integral nonzero bundle \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^r\); choose masses \(x_{t,z}\ge0\) such that \(\sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_j=b_j\) for each cohort \(j\), maximizing \(\exp\!\left(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}\log\!\left(\sum_j v_{tj}z_j\right)\right)\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of recipients described by complete valuation types \(t\) with masses \(\mu_t\), supplied by goods cohorts \(j\) at per-capita rates \(b_j\). Decision variables \(x_{t,z}\) split each type’s mass among integral bundles \(z\); the objective is the population geometric mean of additive utilities.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 3’s exact-optimization hardness for fixed \(p>q\ge3\); it leaves Theorems 1 and 2 and the paper’s other results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can pricing over integral bundle vectors be solved in polynomial time in the number of cohorts, type support, and input bit length?
  • Does the \(q\ge3\) hardness persist when each type’s mass can split among integral bundles, or does the mass relaxation yield an exact polynomial-time algorithm?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 3’s hardness result, and the mirror I would put to the authors is a high multiplicity version of that same allocation problem.

Consider a large population of recipients and a stock of goods arriving in repeated, interchangeable cohorts. A recipient’s type is their complete valuation vector over the cohorts: for each cohort \(j\), a type \(t\) values one unit at \(v_{tj}\in\{1,p/q\}\), for fixed coprime integers \(p>q\ge 3\). Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and cohort \(j\) has per capita supply \(b_j\), with \(\sum_j b_j\ge 1\). The type support \(\tau\) may be small compared with the population, while supplies scale with it. This fits, for example, a large allocation authority distributing standardized, indivisible service slots among recipient groups with a small number of valuation profiles.

Here is the continuous problem I would name Mass-\(p/q\) NSW Allocation. A bundle is a vector \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^r\): every recipient still receives integral quantities of goods, and type \(t\)’s utility from that bundle is \(u_t(z)=\sum_j v_{tj}z_j\). Choose masses \(x_{t,z}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,z}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(z\). They must satisfy \(\sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\) for every type and \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_j=b_j\) for every cohort. The question is to find a feasible allocation scheme maximizing \(\exp(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}\log u_t(z))\), the population’s geometric mean utility. Thus every individual bundle remains integral, although a type’s average bundle can be fractional because its mass can be split among bundle choices.

Theorem 3 states that, for every fixed coprime \(p>q\ge3\), computing an allocation with maximum NSW is NP-hard for discrete 2-value instances. The paper proves this theorem in Section 5, by reducing from Exact \(q\)-Dimensional Matching. In that reduction, there is a good for each vertex and \(p(m-n)\) dummy goods; each hyperedge gives one agent who values its \(q\) incident vertex goods at \(p/q\) and all other goods at \(1\). A perfect matching exists exactly when an allocation with NSW \(p\) exists; if there is no perfect matching, every allocation has NSW strictly below \(p\). That is a substantial hardness anchor, not just a hardness claim for a special encoding.

I expect the mass version to be Class A, though this is a conjecture, not a result of the paper. Its natural formulation is a linear optimization problem over bundle patterns, with potentially many columns. The candidate route is column generation: for each type, the pricing problem chooses an integral bundle to trade off log utility against cohort prices. The two-value structure gives that pricing problem a foothold: for a fixed number of heavy and light goods, one can choose the cheapest available cohorts of each kind. Whether the resulting count optimization admits a polynomial algorithm is the key question for a prover.

The connection to Theorem 3 is the reason this mirror is worth asking. Its reduction relies on selecting a discrete set of hyperedges; in the mass model, a valuation type can divide its mass among different bundles. The matching obstruction may therefore become a fractional flow problem. That does not prove tractability, but it makes the continuous question consequential: does the paper’s \(q\ge3\) boundary survive when a society is represented by masses?

The authors should recognize this as their problem’s population analogue: recipient valuations remain additive and two-valued, each person still receives indivisible goods, and the objective is still Nash welfare. Repeated goods cohorts and population-scaled supplies are the co-modification that makes the high multiplicity regime meaningful. This case covers Theorem 3 only; I would not claim it mirrors the paper’s \(q=1\) or \(q=2\) algorithms.

The weak point is also clear. The theorem’s reduction uses many distinct agent valuation patterns, so its hardness may be about the structure of the types rather than population multiplicity. And even after mass splitting, the continuous problem still has integral bundles and an exponential family of bundle patterns. The mirror’s central open question is whether pricing those patterns is polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of cohorts, and the input bit length; a related question is whether an exact algorithm is feasible for the logarithmic welfare objective or whether approximation is the right target.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the proposed connection between the continuous model and Theorem 3’s reduction. That reduction stores the Exact \(q\)-Dimensional Matching instance in the incidence pattern: one recipient type per hyperedge, with high values on its incident vertex goods. Replicating those recipients creates a large population, yet the type support still carries the reduction’s combinatorics. This weakens the claim that the theorem’s hardness reflects population size, and the proposed Class A route remains only a conjecture: column generation needs an efficient pricing algorithm for integral bundles, which the paper does not provide.

That objection does not defeat the mirror. The question remains well posed when masses of each complete valuation type can be divided among integral bundles, with per-capita supplies scaling with the population. It retains the paper’s agents, two-valued additive utilities, indivisible individual bundles, and Nash welfare objective. Large authorities allocating standardized goods to recurring recipient groups are a plausible setting. Prior work on high-multiplicity fair allocation also supports the regime’s computational relevance, though it studies other fairness and efficiency questions rather than this exact NSW problem. [Bredereck et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04907)

So I cannot make a strong case against this anchor. The reduction may fail to transfer, and the suggested algorithmic payoff is unproved; those are reasons to avoid promising a Class A result. They do not make the continuous population question unworthy. Under the programme’s stated standard, Theorem 3 survives as a credible mirror.
