The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying computational case from this paper: it contains no numbered result about computational complexity—no problem shown to be in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here, but it is an axiomatic equivalence. It says that for a lottery rule satisfying probabilistic monotonicity, when total object capacity exceeds the number of agents, ex-post pairwise efficiency together with ex-post non-wastefulness holds if and only if ex-post Pareto efficiency holds; when total capacity equals the number of agents, ex-post pairwise efficiency alone holds if and only if ex-post Pareto efficiency. Corollary 1 gives the deterministic counterpart for rules satisfying pairwise strategy-proofness. Neither result analyzes an algorithm or the complexity of a computational problem, so neither can honestly serve as the required anchor.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting behind the paper’s allocation examples. For instance, students in a large housing cohort could have strict rankings over dormitory types, with each type representing students indistinguishable in the preferences relevant to allocation. Mass would be each ranking type’s share of the cohort, and dormitory capacities would scale with cohort size. The support of types could be small compared with the population. A continuous allocation would assign mass of each type to each dormitory type, allowing fractional type-level totals even though each student receives one indivisible assignment. That is a credible population continuation of object allocation.

But the paper asks a different kind of question: which axioms on rules imply efficiency, and how those axioms characterize named rules. Turning that into a computational question—such as deciding whether a particular allocation is Pareto efficient—would introduce a problem the paper does not study, rather than mirror one of its computational results. The lottery formulation does not supply the missing anchor: lotteries are over matchings for a finite set of agents, so their probabilistic outcomes do not themselves make the population continuous. A continuum version would also need to redefine the paper’s probability assigned to a particular matching, which is central to Theorem 1.

So the positive case is limited to this: the underlying allocation domain has a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and a continuous axiomatic extension might be worth studying. It does not yield a prover-ready continuous computational problem per anchor, or a justified prediction of tractability or hardness. The weak point is decisive for this programme’s scope: the paper’s named contributions are axiomatic, not computational.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is decisive: this paper states no computational result. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 are equivalences between axioms on rules; Theorem 2 is another such equivalence on a restricted domain. Corollaries 2–5 use those equivalences to strengthen rule characterizations. None specifies an algorithmic problem, its input and output, or a complexity claim. There is therefore no named computational result here to continuize.

That is not because object allocation lacks a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A large housing cohort with students grouped by ranking and dormitory capacities scaled with the cohort is sensible. But the paper studies rules as functions over an entire domain of preference profiles, and asks which properties characterize those rules. Turning its results into questions about finding an efficient allocation, checking whether one is Pareto efficient, or computing RSD would supply computational tasks the paper does not pose.

The best continuous version of Theorem 1 could represent each ranking type by a mass and each type’s assignments by fractions. But the result would still be an axiomatic characterization. In a nonatomic model, individual deviations affect zero mass, and the lottery’s probability on one exact matching may be zero; preserving the paper’s incentive and lottery conditions would require new type-level or aggregate formulations. Those may be worthwhile questions in allocation theory, but they do not create a computational mirror of this paper.

So I would reject a continuous mirror for this paper under ChoCo’s scope. The negative case is weaker only if “mirror” is broadened to include new computational problems motivated by the allocation setting, rather than computational continuizations of the paper’s results.
