The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Propositions 1–9 concern economic core membership and convergence; none asserts a complexity result or analyzes an algorithm. The closest results are Proposition 5, which says that under its assumptions, strictly incentive-compatible allocations that remain in the core of unrestricted mechanisms across replicas must be ex-post Walrasian, and Proposition 8, which gives a probabilistic convergence result for signal-based replicas. Those are analytic results, not computational ones.

The strongest point for a continuous mirror is that the paper’s replica regimes already make a large population with repeated agent types a sensible object. But a continuum of replicas with measurable allocations and blocking mechanisms would pose an analytic core-convergence question—the very kind of question this programme excludes. The paper even notes that the ex-post continuum case is covered by existing results. So there is no honest per-anchor continuous computational problem to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against using this as a ChoCo mirror is that its results give no computational problem to continue. Proposition 5 is an asymptotic characterization: under stated assumptions, an allocation whose replicas remain in the unrestricted-mechanism core must be ex-post Walrasian. Proposition 8 is a probabilistic convergence theorem. Neither defines an algorithmic task, a finite input and output, or a complexity claim.

The best computational versions of those anchors would ask whether a given allocation is in the core at a finite replica size, find a blocking plan, or calculate how large a replica is needed for convergence. Those could be worthwhile questions, but they require choices the paper does not make: how to encode its broad utility and allocation data, represent unrestricted mechanisms, and turn asymptotic convergence into a finite decision or search task. They would be new computational-economics problems, not computational mirrors of Propositions 5 or 8.

The population limits also need care. In Proposition 5, replicas preserve correlations within each finite economy. An anonymous mass distribution over individual types loses that structure unless the model keeps replica blocks or adds a coupling between types. In Proposition 8, signal frequencies conditional on the common state become deterministic in the continuum limit; the theorem’s finite-sample probability bound gives way to an analytic aggregation result. A richer continuous model could preserve these features, but it would still need a separately chosen computational question.

This is not a case that high multiplicity is nonsensical: the replica regimes are good evidence that large populations with repeated types are natural here. Nor does existing continuum economics settle the computational questions. The negative case is narrower: this paper offers strong analytic material for a different research programme, but no anchor for the computational one. The limitation is that a new algorithmic project could still be built from its models; what cannot be claimed is that the paper itself supplies a worthwhile continuous computational mirror.
