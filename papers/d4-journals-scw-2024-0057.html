What the mirror covers

The numbered results characterize equilibria in the finite-voter market and establish platform convergence; none supplies a computational result for a continuous population.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is only a near-miss: this paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a mirror on. Its numbered results characterize equilibria. For example, Proposition 2 establishes an SPE with both platforms at \(1/2\), unique among equilibria with a full-trade equilibrium in every subgame with distinct platforms. It does not classify a computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. Proposition 1 likewise establishes the existence of a full-trade equilibrium; it is not a complexity result. So there are no eligible anchors here, and no per-anchor prover questions or expected complexity outcomes to give.

A plausible population-level analogue would take \(\mu\) to be a distribution over voters’ ideal policies \(y\in[0,1]\), with mass representing the share of society at each ideal policy, and ask how bid and offer strategies affect party vote shares and equilibrium platforms. That scenario is easy to motivate for a large electorate. But the paper’s market game gives individual voters strategic influence through their bids; in an atomless continuum, that influence may disappear, changing the game. The paper’s policy space and votes are already continuous or divisible in places, but its population is still finite. That makes the proposed analogue a possible new equilibrium model, not a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no eligible computational anchor. Proposition 1 and Proposition 2 are equilibrium claims, not results about the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 2 is the closest candidate: it characterizes platform convergence in a finite game. But the paper gives no input representation, algorithmic task, or complexity claim to continue. A computational question built from that proposition would be a new research problem, not a mirror of a named computational result.

There is a deeper obstacle to the proposed population model. The paper’s vote market is strategic: an individual buyer’s bid affects the common price, and the equilibrium described in Proposition 1 has the two extreme supporters as the only buyers. In a genuinely atomless society, an individual voter has zero effect on aggregate bids and offers. The natural continuation is therefore a price-taking market game, whose equilibrium concept and strategic mechanism differ from the paper’s. One could preserve strategic influence by treating whole ideal-policy groups as coordinated buyers, but that makes each type a bloc rather than a collection of independently acting voters. Keeping the two extreme supporters as finite strategic players yields a hybrid model in which the key market influence still comes from atoms.

The better version of the proposed mirror—give ideal policies a rational, finite-support distribution and ask for an algorithm to compute equilibrium platforms and market behavior—could be worth studying. But it first needs a specified continuum market model and an equilibrium-computation problem. Neither is supplied by this paper, and its propositions do not choose between the price-taking, bloc, or hybrid versions.

So the negative case is strongest against treating this paper as a ChoCo computational mirror: its results provide no computational anchor, and the most natural continuum changes the mechanism behind them. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists. A large electorate with repeated ideal-policy types is plausible, and a computational study of a carefully specified continuum vote market could be valuable in its own right. That would be a new problem motivated by the paper, rather than a continuization of one of its computational results.
