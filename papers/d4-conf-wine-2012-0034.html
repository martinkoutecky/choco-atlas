Why no mirror

The two anchors are a welfare impossibility bound and a structural mechanism characterization, neither of which supplies the required computational result. The proposed μ-weighted ensemble of separate two-player markets is a coherent LP, but it changes the theorem's quantifiers and averages independent copies rather than relaxing a population of agents. It therefore cannot rescue the missing computational anchor.

fails bit a — no named computational result to mirror

What the mirror covers

The candidate covers only average welfare over repeated demand profiles; it leaves the aligned characterization, pointwise price-of-truthfulness theorem, and original two-player mechanism model without a computational population result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a qualified one: the paper admits a coherent continuous-population mirror, but it contains no qualifying computational-complexity anchor. None of its numbered results states NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), parameterized hardness, or an algorithmic complexity bound. I would not pretend that its welfare theorems are complexity anchors.

The closest and strongest substantive anchor is Theorem 8, “Price of Truthfulness,” proved in this paper. It says that every deterministic or randomized incentive-compatible mechanism for two-player cake cutting, even allowing wasteful allocations, achieves at most \((8-4\sqrt{3})^{-1}\approx 0.93\) of optimal social welfare on some valuation profile. Theorem 1, repeated as Theorem 4, is useful supporting structure: every non-wasteful deterministic truthful mechanism in the aligned model is exactly one member \(f_\theta\) of the stated one-parameter family. Neither theorem is a computational result in the strict sense required by the programme.

My lead mirror of Theorem 8 would be the following.

Consider a continuum of repeated two-player cake-allocation markets. Each market has its own unit cake \([0,1]\); with \(N\) markets, total cake supply is \(N\). A unit of mass is one market, so \(\mu_{ij}\) is the fraction of markets in which player I desires \(A_i\) and player II desires \(B_j\). The demand sets \(A_i,B_j\subseteq[0,1]\) are finite unions of rational intervals. Equivalently, a finite realization contains \(N\mu_{ij}\) identical markets of pair-type \((A_i,B_j)\), with \(N\) much larger than the number of distinct demand types.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a platform may repeatedly resolve the same kind of two-party allocation problem, with many anonymous pairs sharing the same demand geometry. The cake remains the paper’s divisible outcome space, but the new continuous object is the population distribution \(\mu\) over demand types. Supplies scale with population, and the objective is an average over markets.

Define \(\operatorname{Population\mbox{-}PoT}_\infty\) as follows. An instance consists of the finite type lists \((A_i)_i,(B_j)_j\), a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_{ij})\), and the uniform valuations
\[ u_i(C)=\frac{|C\cap A_i|}{|A_i|},\qquad v_j(D)=\frac{|D\cap B_j|}{|B_j|}. \]
A mechanism specifies, for every reported pair \((A_r,B_s)\), disjoint measurable allocations \(C_{rs},D_{rs}\). It must satisfy, for every true type pair \((i,j)\),
\[ u_i(C_{ij})\ge u_i(C_{rj}) \quad\text{for every report }A_r, \]
and
\[ v_j(D_{ij})\ge v_j(D_{is}) \quad\text{for every report }B_s. \]
The mechanism may either be required to be non-wasteful, or may allow waste exactly as in Theorem 8.

The objective is to output an incentive-compatible mechanism maximizing
\[ \rho_\mu(F)= \frac{\sum_{i,j}\mu_{ij}\bigl(u_i(C_{ij})+v_j(D_{ij})\bigr)} {\sum_{i,j}\mu_{ij}\operatorname{SW}_{\max}(A_i,B_j)}. \]
A solution is the allocation rule \(F\), together with its exact value or an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: the private object is still a desired subset, the utility is still uniform and normalized, truthfulness is still dominant-strategy incentive compatibility, and the benchmark is still optimal welfare. The only change is that many identical two-player markets are represented by their type masses instead of being listed individually.

For this finite-interval version, I expect Class A tractability. Refine \([0,1]\) at all interval endpoints into elementary intervals \(I_k\). For every report pair and elementary interval, introduce variables for the length assigned to each player. All feasibility, incentive-compatibility, and welfare constraints are linear. Since the cake is atomless, any feasible assigned length can be realized by an actual measurable subset, so the LP is exact rather than merely a relaxation. Its size is polynomial in the number of demand types, elementary intervals, and input precision.

The paper’s \((8-4\sqrt{3})^{-1}\) bound does not automatically transfer to every fixed \(\mu\): an easy population may admit welfare ratio \(1\). The natural follow-up is whether the worst population distribution, or a universal mechanism required to work across all populations, recovers exactly the paper’s price of truthfulness. That is a genuine further question rather than a claimed consequence of Theorem 8.

The weakest point is that repeated two-player markets may look like a population wrapper around the original problem. The mass distribution changes the objective and gives a proper high-multiplicity representation, but it does not create the kind of new pricing or separation difficulty found in the ChoCo bribery examples. More seriously, because the paper has no named complexity theorem, this is not a strong anchor for the programme’s central hardness-versus-tractability question. I would therefore argue that the paper has a defensible continuous-population mirror, probably tractable, but only a modest positive case under the programme’s formal grading rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is stronger than the proponent’s qualification suggests: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorems 1/4, 2/6/7, 3/8, and 5 are characterizations, mechanism reductions, and welfare impossibility bounds. None states a complexity class, an algorithm, a hardness result, an approximation guarantee for an algorithm, or a parameterized problem. The reductions are reductions between families of mechanisms, not computational reductions. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, that is decisive: any computational population problem constructed here would be a new problem inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its computational results.

The proposed Population-PoT formulation does not repair this. Theorem 8 concerns a single bilateral mechanism and the quantifier “for every mechanism, there exists a valuation profile.” The proposed problem instead gives a distribution over profiles and asks for a mechanism maximizing average welfare. That changes the theorem’s object and quantifiers. Its “type” \((A_i,B_j)\) is a joint market instance, not the complete type of an individual agent. The population consists of repeated bilateral problems, while every underlying problem still has exactly two players.

If the markets have separate cakes and separate utilities, the copies do not interact. The distribution \(\mu\) merely weights independent instances of the original problem. In fact, writing \(W_F(x)\) and \(W^*(x)\) for achieved and optimal welfare on profile \(x\),

\[ \frac{\sum_x \mu_x W_F(x)}{\sum_x \mu_x W^*(x)} = \sum_x \frac{\mu_x W^*(x)}{\sum_y\mu_y W^*(y)} \frac{W_F(x)}{W^*(x)}. \]

Thus the population distribution is only an averaging device over pointwise performance; it does not create a population-level allocation, transfer, or strategic interaction. The proponent’s endpoint LP may be perfectly valid, but it is a finite-type mechanism-design LP for a newly introduced ensemble of markets. It is not a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s two-player theorem in the sense relevant to ChoCo.

Allowing cross-market coordination does not help. Then a player’s private type is no longer one set \(A\), but a vector of demands across markets, and the player’s utility must aggregate allocations across those markets. The two-player incentive constraints and Theorem 8 no longer describe the model. This is a new multi-unit mechanism-design problem, not a mirror of the paper.

The same dilemma defeats Theorem 1/4. In the aligned model, preserving the paper’s full report domain leaves every deterministic non-wasteful truthful mechanism in the already-characterized one-parameter family \(f_\theta\). A distribution over \((a,b)\) only changes how that family is evaluated or which value of \(\theta\) is preferred; it does not turn the characterization into a continuous-population computational question. Restricting to finitely many report types yields an ordinary finite mechanism-design LP, while retaining all measurable demands yields no finite computational input model. Neither is the missing ChoCo mirror.

A seemingly better construction would use a genuine continuum of players sharing one cake. That makes the population real, but it destroys the paper’s model. With one unit cake and disjoint allocations, an uncountable population cannot receive positive-measure pieces individually: at most countably many disjoint measurable pieces can have positive measure, so almost all agents receive zero utility under the paper’s valuation model. Giving each type an aggregate piece changes individual utilities and incentive compatibility. Scaling cake supply per capita avoids the degeneration only by returning to replicated markets or by introducing a different many-agent allocation problem.

So the honest conclusion is not that no conceivable extension can be formulated. The repeated-market construction is coherent, and its LP could be studied. The stronger conclusion is that neither named anchor survives as a worthwhile continuous-population mirror: Theorem 8 is a non-computational bilateral impossibility theorem, and Theorem 1/4 is an already finite-dimensional mechanism characterization. Every route that introduces a genuine population either decouples into weighted copies of the original problem or changes the agents, utilities, and strategic structure so substantially that it is no longer this paper’s problem.
