The case FOR (proponent)

There is a small positive foothold: let a type be a complete ranking \(t\in L(X)\), and let \(\mu_t\) be its share in a large population, with many people per type and relatively few supported types. The paper’s diversity object then maps directly to \(v(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\): it measures which preference attributes occur, not how often they occur. That is a recognizable population-level version of the paper’s object.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor the required case. The numbered Theorems 1–8 characterize diversity models through monotonicity conditions; Theorem 9 characterizes the logical relations among those models. None classifies an underlying problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or similar. Section 5 discusses maximally diverse preference sets, but gives no numbered computational-complexity result.

The proposed lift also leaves little computational question: since the diversity measure depends only on whether a ranking has positive mass, the mass values do no work. Maximizing diversity over a fixed number of supported rankings is essentially the paper’s finite-set committee-design problem relabeled. So the object has a plausible continuous interpretation, but I can’t honestly supply the required named anchor or a substantive continuous computational problem per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–8 characterize diversity models through monotonicity conditions, and Theorem 9 characterizes relations among those models. Section 5 studies maximally diverse finite preference sets, but supplies no complexity result. So the case for names no result-level anchor, and there is no continuous computational question attached to one.

The strongest salvage is Section 5: reinterpret committee design as choosing a population distribution \(\mu\) to maximize \(v(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\). But for these models, any two distributions with the same support have the same value, however differently their mass is distributed. With no constraints, the full support is optimal; with constraints, the relevant question is which supports are feasible and worth selecting. That may be a combinatorial optimization problem, but its difficulty comes from selecting a finite set of rankings, not from continuizing the population. Giving the types positive rational masses adds no computational content.

A frequency-sensitive extension is the best counterproposal: measure how much mass realizes each attribute rather than whether any positive mass realizes it. The authors themselves flag frequency and preference multiplicity as future work, so this could be worthwhile research. But it changes the diversity measure and has no computational theorem in this paper to mirror. The negative case is therefore strong against a continuous mirror of this paper’s results, though weaker as the universal claim that no related continuous computational project could be worthwhile.
