What the mirror covers

No named result is mirrored: Theorems 1–5 are axiomatic characterizations of allocation rules.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population mirror treats viewers as the society, not the teams. Fix a league with \(n\) teams and its scheduled home fixtures. A viewer type \(t\in\{0,1\}^{n(n-1)}\) records which fixtures that viewer would watch; include any stable fan category the model needs in the type as well. If \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of viewers of type \(t\), then the audience for fixture \(ij\) is the corresponding marginal, \(a_{ij}=M\sum_{t:t_{ij}=1}\mu_t\), where \(M\) is the viewer population. A cancellation mask marks which fixtures were played, and \(E\) is the revenue to allocate. The output remains an allocation \(x\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^n\) summing to \(E\), using the paper’s zero or leg imputation. A plausible regime would be a large broadcast audience with many viewers but relatively few stable viewing-pattern types.

That makes the aggregate-audience model interpretable as a population model, but it supplies no eligible computational anchor. Theorems 1–4, proved in this paper, characterize equal-split and concede-and-divide extensions by combinations of axioms; Theorem 5 gives benchmark-domain characterizations. For example, Theorem 4 says that a rule satisfies weak reallocation proofness, essential team on non-cancelled games, leg-multi-conference, and leg-baseline monotonicity if and only if it is the leg-extended concede-and-divide rule. These are normative characterization results, not claims that an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, or has another named complexity status.

One could pose a new computational task—given rational viewer-type masses, cancellations, and \(E\), compute an extended rule’s allocation—but that task is not a named result of this paper. The paper already takes aggregate audiences as input, and its contribution is axiomatic rather than computational. So the viewer-population interpretation is a possible modeling bridge, not a grounded continuous mirror of a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best viewer-population model is coherent: let a type record which fixtures a viewer would watch, and let type masses generate the fixture audiences. But it does not supply a computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–4 characterize allocation rules by axioms; Theorem 5 gives characterizations on the completed-season domain. None states a complexity claim about a computational problem.

More fundamentally, the viewer types disappear as soon as their masses are converted to the audience matrix. The paper’s rules and axioms use that matrix and the endowment, not correlations among viewers’ viewing patterns or operations on viewer mass. Any two viewer distributions with the same fixture-audience marginals therefore give the same inputs to every result. Computing an allocation from viewer masses would be a newly posed task, not the computational question addressed by these theorems.

Trying to make the teams the continuous population runs into the other side of the model: they are the fixed, distinct recipients, with pair-specific home fixtures. Treating them as interchangeable team types would change what is being allocated. The viewer interpretation avoids that change, but only recasts the audience data as a population model; it does not create a computational mirror of this paper’s contribution.

So the negative case is strong at the gate the programme set: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. I cannot honestly prove the broader claim that no worthwhile computational question could ever be built from its setting. Choosing among imputation operators or studying allocation under uncertain audiences might be worthwhile new work, but it would need its own computational question rather than being a mirror of these characterization theorems.
