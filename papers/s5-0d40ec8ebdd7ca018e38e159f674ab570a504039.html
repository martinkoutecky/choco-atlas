The continuous mirror question

Given a support of operational roles \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and an undirected graph \(H\) on \(T\), define \(v_H(S)=1\) when the induced graph \(H[S]\) is connected and \(0\) otherwise, with \(v_H(\varnothing)=0\). Compute exactly, for each \(t\in T\), the cohort total \(\Phi_t=\mu_t q_t\), where \(q_t=\sum_{S\subseteq T\setminus\{t\}}\frac{|S|!(|T|-|S|-1)!}{|T|!}(v_H(S\cup\{t\})-v_H(S))\).

The model it lives in

A society with role types \(T\) and masses \(\mu\), whose role-level connectivity is represented by \(H\). Each role is treated as one actor in the Shapley connectivity game; its per-role score \(q_t\) is multiplied by its population mass to obtain its cohort total \(\Phi_t\).

The objection that survived

The role graph does not specify how repeated individual agents and their links induce its connectivity structure, so it is not established as a high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s individual-node game.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case I see is narrow: Chapter 7’s connectivity game, in a setting where network interventions are made by role or facility class. That is plausible for a large infrastructure system with many interchangeable units and relatively few distinct connectivity roles—for example, a network operator that hardens every instance of a given equipment role together. A type \(t\) is a complete operational role, including its link pattern and intervention attributes; \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the population. The regime has \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of occupied roles.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this dissertation: #CG-SHAPLEY is #P-hard. Its problem is to take a graph \(G=(V,E)\) with coalition value \(\nu(C)=1\) when the induced coalition is connected and \(0\) otherwise, and compute every node’s Shapley value. The theorem is a hardness result for that computation, not merely for finding a representation.

A continuous role-level mirror would be Mass-Connectivity Shapley. Its instance is a support list of roles \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and an undirected graph \(H\) on the roles, where an edge records a communication link between the two role classes. A coalition \(S\subseteq T\) means that the whole cohorts in \(S\) are included. Set \(v_H(\varnothing)=0\), and \(v_H(S)=1\) exactly when \(H[S]\) is connected. For each role \(t\), compute its per-unit centrality
\[ q_t=\sum_{S\subseteq T\setminus\{t\}} \frac{|S|!(\tau-|S|-1)!}{\tau!} \bigl(v_H(S\cup\{t\})-v_H(S)\bigr), \]
and its cohort total \(\Phi_t=\mu_t q_t\). The solution is the exact rational vector \((\Phi_t)_{t\in T}\). Equivalently, \(q_t\) is the expected change in connectivity value when role \(t\) joins the preceding roles in a uniformly random ordering. This preserves the paper’s central question—how much a node contributes to connected coalitions—while reporting the result for a population distribution over roles.

I expect hardness transfers when \(\tau\) grows: the combinatorics live in the role graph. In fact, the per-unit scores \(q_t\) are exactly the scores in Theorem 5 on \(H\), so the hard instance can be encoded directly as a type graph; population size can grow while that graph remains fixed. For fixed \(\tau\), direct enumeration is polynomial in the mass bit length and independent of the number of represented agents. This gives the mirror a useful parameterized question: how much can algorithms exploit small support, treewidth, or other structure in \(H\)? The dissertation’s Chapter 6, which studies centrality with a given community structure, also makes group-level network analysis a recognizable extension for these authors.

The weak point is the change in the unit of intervention. The paper’s players are individual vertices; this mirror treats an entire role cohort as one actor. It is credible when interventions really apply class-wide, but it does not establish that individual-node Shapley values on a large network converge to these cohort scores. Nor does the stated binary connectivity payoff make \(\mu\) affect \(q_t\); mass scales the cohort total. Allowing partial inclusion of each type would be another natural question, but connectivity can become discontinuous as mass approaches zero, so a useful continuous definition would need care. I would present this as a serious, bounded mirror of Chapter 7’s hardness result—not as a continuum version of the whole dissertation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is a genuine computational anchor: the dissertation proves #CG-SHAPLEY is #P-hard. The strongest objection is about whether the proposed mirror continuizes that problem.

In the paper, each vertex is an independently selectable player, and the Shapley value averages over coalitions of individual vertices. In the proposed mirror, a type is a single bloc: a coalition either includes the whole role class or none of it. A high-multiplicity version of the original game would allow coalitions containing some members of a type. Those are different games, and \(\Phi_t=\mu_t q_t\) is not shown to equal the aggregate Shapley centrality of a population of that type. Moreover, \(\mu\) does not affect the connectivity game or \(q_t\); it only scales the reported score.

The natural repair is to let a coalition contain fractions \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each role. But binary connectivity then depends on which roles have positive mass, not how much mass they contribute. Within a positive-support region, adding an infinitesimal amount changes nothing; at the boundary \(x_t=0\), the value can jump. An atomless population therefore gives no straightforward, nondegenerate per-agent Shapley contribution. Preserving the contribution requires treating whole roles as indivisible atoms, which returns to the finite role graph, or choosing a different connectivity payoff and thereby changing the problem.

That is a real limitation on the claimed population mirror, but it does not defeat the best version of the case. Class-wide network interventions are plausible, the masses affect cohort rankings, and Chapter 6 shows that bloc-based game-theoretic centrality is recognizable territory for these authors. So the honest negative case is weak: it challenges whether this is a continuous-population mirror of Theorem 5, but it cannot rule out a worthwhile role-level computational question that the authors would accept.
