The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of location types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a source \(0\), and a nonnegative symmetric link-cost matrix \(C\) on \(\{0\}\cup T\), with zero cost between residents of the same type, compute a minimum-cost spanning tree on the support and the mass-weighted Borůvka allocation \(a_t\), where \(\mu_t a_t\) is type \(t\)’s total contribution. Determine whether the allocation is independent of arc order and equals the limit of the discrete folk allocations when each type is replicated in proportion to \(\mu_t\).

The model it lives in

Types are locations with identical link-cost profiles; \(\mu_t\) is each type’s population mass. The tree is chosen over the source and support types, and the allocation outputs are type-level contribution densities \(a_t\), with total type payment \(\mu_t a_t\) and aggregate payments summing to the tree cost.

The objection that survived

The manuscript does not establish that the proposed mass allocation is the limit of its folk allocation on replicated agents; the proponent identifies this as a further question but does not answer it.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s computation of the folk allocation and its independence from arc order. It leaves the paper’s other allocation properties and comparisons with the Shapley value outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For rational masses, does the mass-weighted Borůvka allocation equal the limit of the folk allocations in finite replications of the types?
  • Is the allocation independent of arc order and computable exactly in time polynomial in support size and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow algorithmic mirror, not a hardness story. Theorem 1 in the supplied manuscript is proved there: for every ordering \(\pi\) of the arcs, the cost allocation \(\beta^\pi\) produced by the authors’ Borůvka-based rule equals the folk solution \(\phi\). Thus tie-breaking in Borůvka’s construction does not change the allocation. The theorem is not itself a formal “in P” statement; its computational force is that it gives a constructive way to compute the folk allocation using the spanning-tree algorithm. [The supplied MPRA manuscript](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/17839/1/MPRA_paper_17839.pdf)

Lead mirror — Continuous Folk–Borůvka Allocation. Consider a large population living at \(\tau\) service locations. A type is a location and its complete link-cost profile: residents of type \(t\) have the same costs to connect to every other type and to the source. Their masses \(\mu_t>0\) are rational, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Assume connections within a type cost zero, and linking two locations has a fixed infrastructure cost \(c_{tu}\), independent of how many residents live there. The instance is \(T,\mu\), and a nonnegative symmetric cost matrix \(C\) on \(\{0\}\cup T\), where \(0\) is the source.

The network decision is a spanning tree \(Q\) minimizing \(\sum_{e\in Q}c_e\). The allocation output is a rational density \(a_t\) for each type; \(\mu_t a_t\) is that type’s total assigned contribution. A valid output returns a minimum-cost tree and a vector satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t=\sum_{e\in Q}c_e\), with the shares produced by the continuous version of the paper’s Borůvka rule.

Precisely, run its component and arc-selection procedure on the support types. In each round, let \(q_e\) be the mass assigned to selected arc \(e\), and let \(\rho_e\) be the fraction of that arc’s cost already paid. Set
\[ p=\min_{e:q_e>0}\frac{1-\rho_e}{q_e}. \]
Each type assigned to \(e\) gains \(p c_e\) in contribution density, and \(\rho_e\) increases by \(q_e p\). Continue the paper’s procedure for reusing unpaid selected arcs until the tree’s costs have been allocated. The question is to compute the exact tree and type-level allocation and establish that the allocation is independent of the tie order. A further question is whether it equals the high-multiplicity limit of the discrete folk allocation: replicate each type \(n\mu_t\) times, give same-type residents zero mutual link costs, and take the limit of each type’s aggregate allocation divided by \(\mu_t\).

This is a plausible mirror because the paper’s agents are already defined by locations and link costs. In the proposed regime, many residents share exactly the same relevant information, so mass replaces headcount in the cost-sharing step. The large population might be residents of a few service districts, while the distinct types are the districts, not the individual households. Fixed infrastructure costs and fractional type-level contributions are natural co-modifications: a district needs a link regardless of how many residents it contains, and its aggregate burden can be divided across its mass.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, with an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\): compute on the support graph, aggregate mass by type, and use exact rational arithmetic. The paper offers no named hardness result to anchor a hardness-dissolution question, and the discrete spanning-tree problem is already tractable. This is therefore a small tractable mirror, not evidence that continuization rescues a hard problem.

The weakest point is that the continuum changes the cost allocation’s mass denominator more than it changes the network optimization: the tree is still chosen on the finite support. A skeptic could reasonably call the proposed problem a weighted aggregation of the authors’ finite procedure. The positive case rests on the resulting population being the actual modeled society, with cost shares defined over its masses; it does not claim to mirror every minimum-cost spanning-tree instance.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole strong anchor is Theorem 1: for every arc ordering \(\pi\), the Borůvka allocation \(\beta^\pi\) equals the folk solution \(\phi\). It is constructive, so I would not say the paper has no algorithmic result. But it establishes an identity between two allocations on a finite set of agents; it does not analyze the complexity of a computational problem. Its distinctive claim is that the allocation is independent of the arc order.

The proposed district model is the best case for a mirror: many residents share a location and its costs, and links within a location are free. But that assumption contracts all residents at each location into one component. The replicated finite MCST then has the same operative tree and total cost as the support graph; the masses enter when that finite cost is apportioned among residents. This is a coherent weighted cost-sharing extension, but its new population-level step is the apportionment rule, not a continuous version of the tree computation or of Theorem 1’s tie-order result.

Giving same-type links positive costs does not preserve the mirror cleanly. With fixed per-link costs, adding \(n_t\) copies can require \(\Theta(n_t)\) links and make total cost grow with population; a literal atomless network may require infinitely many links. Dividing costs by population to keep totals finite makes the finite number of links between types vanish on a per-capita scale. One can choose size-dependent within-type costs to retain both contributions, but that imposes a new cost-scaling model rather than following from the paper’s cost matrix.

A further version could treat locations as the network nodes and residents as the people who share their costs. That may be a sensible application, but then the graph already has one node per location: the population is outside the network problem, and the result is a new weighted cost-sharing question. So the negative case is strongest against this being a worthwhile continuization of the paper’s named result, not against the possibility of a useful model. The weakness is real: the zero-cost replication regime is formally sound, and the authors might recognize its weighted allocation as valuable. What it does not clearly offer is a continuous complexity question of the kind this programme is meant to chart.
