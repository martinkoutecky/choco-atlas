What the mirror covers

Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Theorem 3, and Corollary 1 are all monotonicity or equilibrium claims; none provides a computational result to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous problem. Theorem 1, attributed to Saijo (1987), says a monotonic function from preference profiles to legislatures must be constant. Theorem 2, attributed to Müller and Satterthwaite (1977), says that with at least three legislatures, a monotonic, citizen-sovereign function on strict orders must be dictatorial. Theorem 3, proved in this paper, says that for every electoral rule, the Nash-equilibrium outcome correspondence is monotonic. Corollary 1, attributed to Müller and Satterthwaite, says that for a nonmonotonic rule, sincere voting fails to be a Nash equilibrium for some preference profile. These are social-choice results, not classifications of computational problems as tractable or hard.

A plausible population setting would be a large single-district electorate with relatively few shared preference types. But turning Theorem 3 into a continuous Nash-equilibrium question runs into a serious modeling problem: in a nonatomic electorate, one voter’s deviation has no effect on the outcome. A nondegenerate version would need to change the deviation model, for example to allow positive-mass groups to deviate. That might be an interesting new question, but it would not be a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that none of the paper’s results is a computational result. Theorem 1 says monotone rules on the stated domain must be constant; Theorem 2 gives a dictatorship characterization; Corollary 1 concerns when sincere voting fails to be a Nash equilibrium. Their continuous versions would ask whether those social-choice properties hold over a distribution of preferences. That continuizes the preference domain or an axiom, while the population-continuum programme asks computational questions. This objection does not depend on whether the results are old or new.

Theorem 3 is the best possible anchor: it concerns the Nash-equilibrium outcome correspondence for every electoral rule. One could ask how to compute that correspondence for a large electorate with a small set of preference types. But in a nonatomic electorate, one voter’s ballot change leaves the aggregate ballot distribution unchanged. With payoffs determined by the resulting legislature, every individual is indifferent among their own ballot choices; ordinary Nash equilibrium then loses the strategic content that makes Theorem 3 interesting. Its monotonicity statement becomes vacuous or reduces to a property of which outcomes the rule can generate.

A stronger proposed mirror might let positive-mass groups deviate, or treat each type as a coordinated bloc, then study equilibrium computation for a concrete rule such as STV. That could be a worthwhile project, but it changes the solution concept or the agency of the voters. Its computational question is newly supplied by the modeller; it is not a continuous version of a computational question in this paper. Likewise, searching for a minimum mass shift that produces a nonmonotonicity witness adds an optimization problem absent from the paper.

So the case against a mirror is strong under ChoCo’s stated scope: the paper’s anchors are axiomatic and strategic claims, not complexity results. The universal claim is less airtight: a computational study of a chosen continuum voting game might be valuable in its own right. But that would be adjacent to this paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.
