The continuous mirror question

Input a rational mass vector μ over a finite type set \(T\), with \(\sum_t μ_t=1\), positive per-member singleton worths \(a_t\), a rational polynomial coalition-worth density \(W:[0,1]^T\to\mathbb{Q}\) with \(W(0)=0\) and ∂_t W(0)=a_t\), and a rational polynomial kernel \(h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{Q}\). Set \(K=\sum_t μ_t a_t\) and \(A_h=\int_0^1 h(q)W(qμ)\,dq\). Compute the exact per-member allocation Φ_t=\frac{a_t}{K}A_h+W(μ)-A_h\) for each type, or equivalently its total mass allocation \(y_t=μ_tΦ_t\).

The model it lives in

A continuum cooperative game with society distribution μ over interchangeable producer types. A coalition of type masses \(x\) has worth density \(W(x)\); the kernel \(h\) encodes the limit of the finite coalition-size coefficients. The task is to compute each type's per-member payoff Φ_t, and hence its total mass allocation \(y_t\), from the rational polynomial input.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5's procedural implementation for the α-mollified values. The axiomatic characterizations in Theorems 1–2 and the consistency results in Theorems 3–4 are not computational anchors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove convergence from finite type-count games under explicit regularity assumptions on \(W\) and the finite worth functions.
  • Determine how the computational question changes when \(W\) is represented by an arithmetic circuit or an evaluation oracle rather than an explicit polynomial.

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, the paper has no theorem classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized tractable. The best positive case is narrower: Theorem 5, proved in the paper, gives a procedural implementation of the values. It is an algorithmic anchor only in that broad sense; it makes no complexity claim, and the paper has no hardness result to prefer.

Theorem 5 says that for any finite TU-game and any linear function \(\alpha(N,v)=\sum_{S\subseteq N}\alpha^n_{|S|}v(S)\), the \(\alpha\)-mollified payoff to each player equals the average of that player’s payoff over all \(n!\) arrival orders. In each order, the first entrant receives their stand-alone worth. Later entrants receive their stand-alone-worth-proportional share of \(\alpha(N,v)\); the entrant’s remaining marginal contribution is divided equally among those already present. Averaging those procedural allocations gives
\[ \psi_i^\alpha(N,v)= \frac{v(\{i\})}{\sum_{k\in N}v(\{k\})}\alpha(N,v) +\frac{1}{n}\bigl(v(N)-\alpha(N,v)\bigr). \]
This anchors an allocation computation and implementation, not a claim about the running time of computing it.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large cooperative of small producers, grouped into a fixed number \(\tau\) of technology or role types. Members of a type have the same stand-alone worth and are interchangeable in every coalition: worth depends on the coalition’s type composition, not on its members’ names. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s fraction of the population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(W(x)\) be the coalition’s worth per population member when its type-mass vector is \(x\in[0,\mu]\), and let \(a_t>0\) be type \(t\)’s limiting stand-alone worth per member. This is a society of \(n\) producers across \(\tau\) types, with \(n\) large and \(\tau\) comparatively small.

My lead mirror is Continuum \(\alpha\)-Implementation. Its instance is rational \(\mu\), positive \(a_t\), a rational polynomial worth function \(W\) with \(W(0)=0\) and \(\partial_t W(0)=a_t\), and a rational polynomial kernel \(h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{Q}\). Define
\[ K=\sum_t\mu_t a_t,\qquad A_h(W,\mu)=\int_0^1 h(q)W(q\mu)\,dq. \]
The problem is to output the per-member payoff \(\Phi_t\) for each type, or equivalently the type’s total mass allocation \(y_t=\mu_t\Phi_t\), where
\[ \Phi_t=\frac{a_t}{K}A_h(W,\mu)+W(\mu)-A_h(W,\mu). \]
This conserves the grand-coalition worth: \(\sum_t y_t=W(\mu)\).

The link to Theorem 5 is a high-multiplicity limit, not just an analogy. For a finite population of \(n\) producers with proportions tending to \(\mu\), choose the paper’s size-based coefficients as \(\alpha^n_s=h(s/n)/(n\binom ns)\). Uniformly sampling a coalition of size about \(qn\) gives type-mass composition tending to \(q\mu\). Thus the normalized finite \(\alpha(N,v)\) tends to \(A_h(W,\mu)\); Theorem 5’s average allocation then tends to \(\Phi_t\) for each type. The question is whether to compute this limiting type allocation from a compact description of the society and its worth function. With the stated polynomial input, it is tractable by exact one-variable polynomial integration. That tractability comes from this chosen representation; it is not a complexity result proved by the paper.

The mirror covers Theorem 5’s procedural implementation only. Theorems 1–2 axiomatize values, and Theorems 3–4 use projection consistency to select subclasses; those are not computational anchors for this programme. The proposed question is plausible as a continuous limit of the authors’ own random-arrival allocation procedure, and high multiplicity is sensible for large populations of interchangeable producers.

The weak point is substantial: the paper neither asks how to compute these values nor studies complexity, and the continuous kernel and polynomial representation are choices needed to make the limit precise. This is therefore a modest case for a computational follow-up grounded in a named implementation theorem—not evidence that the paper already contains a computational result. Further questions include how much the conclusion survives for oracle or piecewise-defined worth functions, and whether the random-arrival process itself has a useful continuum formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative starts with the paper’s results: none states the complexity of a computational problem. Theorems 1–4 are axiomatic characterizations; Theorems 3–4 classify which values satisfy consistency. Theorem 5, the proponent’s anchor, proves an identity between a value and the average of allocations over arrival orders. It gives no computational input model or complexity question. The value is already defined by the paper’s formula. An average over \(n!\) orders may look like an algorithm, but the theorem does not ask whether that computation is tractable. By ChoCo’s stated standard, there is no computational result here whose complexity landscape the programme can chart.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large cooperative with players grouped by role or technology, where coalition worth depends on the mass of each role. The proponent’s proposed limit is mathematically coherent. Its computational content, though, comes from adding a representation the paper does not supply: a polynomial \(W\), a kernel \(h\), and a rule connecting them to the finite coefficients. With that representation, one asks how to compute an integral along \(q\mu\). With a different representation, such as an oracle or an arithmetic circuit for \(W\), the computational question changes. The finite theorem motivates the limiting identity; it does not select the function language or make integration the paper’s computational problem.

A stronger mirror could start with finite games whose worths depend only on type counts, then ask for the continuum limit of their \(\alpha\)-mollified allocations. That avoids the specific polynomial choice, and such clone populations are sensible. But the finite input still needs a description of worth for every type-composition profile. Passing to a continuum replaces that table with a function whose representation must be chosen. If it is given explicitly, computation follows the table; if it is given compactly, the complexity depends on that compact language. Either way, the new work would be about a chosen encoding of symmetric cooperative games, rather than a complexity question the paper has already posed.

So the case against this anchor is strong at the programme’s entry gate, though weak as a universal claim. There is no population-based objection that rules out a useful model: homogeneous producers could provide one, and a continuous allocation problem could be worth studying. The honest negative is narrower: this paper offers no computational result to mirror, and any computational follow-up must supply its own input model and question. That makes it a possible new direction, rather than a compelling case that continuization is needed for this paper.
