What the mirror covers

Theorem 2 is the all-rules discrete OSP implementation result; Theorem 1 covers its individually rational subclass, and Proposition 1 gives a characterization. None is covered by a surviving population-continuous question; the paper's continuous-resource model retains finitely many agents.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror. The closest is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for a discrete division problem with single-peaked preferences, every sequential allotment rule has a finite perfect-information game form that OSP-implements it. Theorem 1 proves the same for the individually rational subclass. Proposition 1, attributed to Barberà, Jackson, and Neme, characterizes sequential allotment rules as the strategy-proof, efficient, replacement-monotonic rules. These are mechanism-design results, but none asserts a complexity classification, and the paper gives no running-time analysis for constructing the game form.

There is a plausible setting behind a follow-up: residents with shared peak demands divide a resource whose supply scales with population. A type could include a peak and any priority attribute the rule uses; its mass would be its population share. The paper’s own discussion of Sprumont’s continuous division model makes that direction recognizable to the authors. But the cited continuous model still has finitely many agents, and with an atomless population an individual’s unilateral report may have no effect. Making cohorts able to deviate together would change the incentive notion. That leaves an interesting extension, but not a continuous mirror anchored to a qualifying computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor is Theorem 2: every sequential allotment rule in the discrete division problem has an extensive game form in which truth-telling is obviously dominant. It is a constructive implementation theorem, but it gives no complexity classification for a computational problem. The construction calls the rule on selected profiles; the paper specifies neither an encoding of a rule nor a running-time measure. Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 do not supply such a target either. So the paper offers no named complexity result for ChoCo to mirror.

The strongest plausible setting is a large population dividing a resource whose supply scales with population, with a finite set of demand peaks and perhaps priority attributes. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper itself discusses continuous division. But it does not rescue this anchor. In an atomless population, one person’s report does not change the population distribution. Their individual allocation is then unaffected, making the unilateral incentive condition vacuous. Treating each positive-mass type as a player would restore consequential reports only by making the player a whole cohort: the question becomes whether groups can coordinate their reports, not whether an individual has an obviously dominant strategy. That is a different incentive problem.

One could ask for the complexity of constructing an OSP game form from a succinctly represented rule, or study a new cohort-level notion of obvious strategy-proofness. But neither question is specified or answered here, and the paper supplies no natural encoding or complexity measure for them. They would be new computational projects attached to this paper’s subject, rather than continuous mirrors of its result. The negative case is strongest on that boundary: large-population division is plausible, but this paper’s central theorem concerns implementation and incentives, not computational complexity.
