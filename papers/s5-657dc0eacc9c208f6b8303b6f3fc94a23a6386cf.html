The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Proposition 1 characterizes the scholarship choice rule; Proposition 2 gives it a path-independent, size-monotonic completion; Proposition 3 characterizes the student-proposing deferred-acceptance mechanism; and Theorems 1 and 2 give welfare results. They are proved here, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or has another complexity classification. The paper describes a DA algorithm, but does not give it a named complexity result. So I can’t honestly make the anchor-based case requested.

The closest positive lead is Theorem 2, proved here, though it is not a qualifying computational anchor. It says: with universal scholarship eligibility and all schools using the scholarship choice rule, a common school priority ranking is necessary and sufficient for this guarantee: for every within-school-monotone preference profile and every market of the specified kind, adding a scholarship weakly Gale-improves the scholarship-providing school’s student pool. That is a crisp comparative-statics result, and its population-level version seems worth asking.

One candidate follow-on is Continuum Scholarship-Gain Recognition. Its input is a finite set of agent types with rational masses summing to one; schools and their priority orders over types; each type’s ranking of school-scholarship contracts and outside option; and per-capita total and scholarship capacities. Every type is eligible for every offered scholarship. A type records the features relevant to the market, including preferences and merit category. Run a fractional version of student-proposing DA: mass proposes by type preference, schools apply the scholarship choice rule, and mass at a priority cutoff may be split. The instance asks whether adding a specified scholarship weakly Gale-improves the target school’s assigned mass. Here weighted Gale improvement means that the post-scholarship mass is at least as large and, at every rank up to the baseline mass, its merit score is at least the baseline score.

A stronger, universal version asks whether a given profile of school priorities guarantees that improvement for every rational mass distribution and every allowed preference profile. The expected answer is tractable: common priority order should be necessary and sufficient, as in Theorem 2, with a reversed pair of school rankings furnishing a small counterexample when orders differ. That is a plausible Class A question about aggregate admissions outcomes. The paper’s Theorem 1 also suggests the general negative effect should survive continuization: it constructs a scholarship-induced worsening market even with only two students.

The regime I find plausible is a large centralized admissions system where many applicants share application rankings, eligibility, and merit categories, so the number of applicants greatly exceeds the number of types. The assigned mass \(x_{t,s,m}\) says how much of type \(t\) receives contract \((s,m)\); the aim is to compute the DA outcome and assess the school’s merit pool, not to optimize a separate objective.

The weak point is substantial: the paper assumes distinct individual merit scores, while repeated complete types would share a score. The continuum model therefore needs a co-modification—priority tiers with fractional allocation at cutoffs. That may be a natural fluid version of its merit rule, but it is not the paper’s exact model. This candidate covers only the comparative-statics question in Theorem 2; it does not establish a continuous mirror for the paper’s axiomatic and mechanism characterizations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is a mismatch with the programme’s computational gate. Proposition 1 characterizes a choice rule, Proposition 3 characterizes a mechanism, and Theorems 1 and 2 establish welfare claims. The paper gives a deferred-acceptance algorithm, but no named result classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Calling the mechanism’s output a computational task does not make its axiomatic or comparative-statics results complexity results.

The best version of the proposed mirror is coherent: group applicants by preferences and merit tiers, use rational masses and per-capita capacities, allow a cutoff to split a type, run mass deferred acceptance before and after a scholarship, and compare the resulting merit distributions. But that asks a new, instance-level question: whether this particular market’s outcome improves. Theorem 2 instead asks whether a priority profile guarantees improvement across every allowed market and preference profile. Preserve that universal claim, and the input is the priority profile, not a continuous society. Make the society the input, and the question is no longer the theorem’s claim. A search for a market witnessing failure under non-common priorities is another possible problem, but it too is newly posed rather than a computational result of the paper.

There is also a real, though not decisive, fit problem. A complete type must include the student’s merit scores at every school. Because the paper assumes distinct scores at each school, its literal model has no repeated complete types. A plausible admissions mirror could replace exact scores with merit tiers and split mass at cutoffs. That is a sensible co-modification, especially for exam systems, but it changes the strict-priority model on which the theorem is stated.

So I would argue against grading this paper as a strong continuous-computational mirror: the proposed mass model may be worth studying, but the paper does not supply the kind of computational result the programme is organized to continuize. This is not an airtight claim that no scenario could make a mirror worthwhile. Repeated score and preference categories are plausible, and a fluid version of deferred acceptance is well-defined enough to investigate. The negative case is strongest on the missing computational anchor, and weaker on ruling out a useful adjacent project.
