The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), seat count \(k\), a rational-mass distribution \(\mu\) over submitted ballot types, fixed tie-breaking and weighted-inclusive Gregory rules, and a target RCV structure \(\sigma\), find the minimum added ballot mass \(\sum_b x_b\), where \(x_b\geq 0\), such that running RCV on \(\mu+x\) yields \(\sigma\). The quota is the total mass divided by \(k+1\). The instance may restrict added ballots by a ranking-length cap or require that specified ballot prefixes still yield \(\sigma\) under every legal suffix.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity RCV electorate: ballot rankings are the types, \(\mu\) gives each type’s population mass, and the decision variables \(x_b\) assign new mass to ballot types or permitted prefixes. Minimize total added mass subject to the target structure under the mass-scaled quota and Gregory transfers.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers fixed-structure ballot addition and, in a related variant, completion of exhausted ballots. It leaves the paper’s empirical comparisons, completion probability models, and conclusions about observed elections untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does fixed-structure mass allocation admit an exact algorithm polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length?
  • Can the discrete optimality and robustness guarantees be extended when the quota and Gregory transfers vary with added mass?
  • For multi-winner RCV, can exhausted-ballot completion be characterized when completion changes the win/elimination sequence?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a targeted mirror of the paper’s ballot-addition and ballot-completion computations. In a plausible large-city or statewide race, take each voter’s complete submitted ranking—including any truncation—as their type. The society is a rational-mass distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with total mass \(1\) and support size \(\tau\). The regime is one where \(N\) is in the hundreds of thousands or more, while recurring party and endorsement ballot patterns leave \(\tau\) far smaller than \(N\). The paper’s own use of ballot additions to model voter mobilization makes the action especially natural: recruited voters contribute mass with specified rankings.

Keep candidates, rankings, elimination, and weighted-inclusive Gregory transfers discrete and unchanged. For a society of total mass \(M\), use the proportional quota \(M/(k+1)\), the continuum limit of the normalized Droop quota. Thus adding ballot mass \(a\) raises the total to \(1+a\) and changes the quota along with it. This is a co-modification forced by continuizing the population, not a change in the continuity axis. Ballot rankings remain discrete; the population becomes continuous.

The lead anchor is Proposition 1 in Section 2.2.1, proved here as extensions of the earlier algorithm of Deshpande et al. [23]. It says that the ballot-allocation algorithm can produce optimal strategies that remain effective when arbitrary subsequent preferences are appended to each added ballot; remain effective when irrelevant candidates are prefixed to those ballots; and obey ballot-length restrictions, including single-choice ballots. Each extension preserves polynomial-time complexity and optimality within its stated constraint.

Its continuous mirror is \(\textsc{Structure-Add}_{\infty}\). An instance gives \(C\), seat count \(k\), a rational-mass society \(\mu\), a fixed tie-breaking hierarchy and RCV transfer rule, and a desired structure \(\sigma=(f,s)\), specifying the social-choice order and win/elimination sequence. It may also specify a ballot-length cap and whether the strategy must tolerate arbitrary legal suffixes or prefixes from candidates known to be irrelevant. For a rational budget \(a\), ask whether there is an allocation of new ballot mass \(x\), supported on allowed ballot types and satisfying \(\sum_b x_b\leq a\), such that RCV on \(\mu+x\) produces \(\sigma\). In the suffix-robust variant, the allocation specifies ballot prefixes and must produce \(\sigma\) for every allowed completion of those prefixes. A solution is the mass assigned to each ballot type or prefix, together with the resulting RCV outcome; the optimization version asks for the infimum added mass.

This is my lead because the paper’s unit of intervention is already an amount of new ballot support, and its question is whether that intervention realizes a specified RCV outcome. Replacing ballot counts by shares preserves the question. For a *fixed* structure, I expect the mass problem to be Class A: the paper’s round-by-round allocation logic may admit an exact rational-mass counterpart. That is a research expectation, not a result established by Proposition 1. Gregory surplus transfers and the endogenous quota couple rounds, so a proof must show that the mass version retains the needed structure. Minimizing over every structure to obtain a target candidate’s victory gap is an additional outer optimization; the paper itself says that enumerating structures is intractable in the worst case. Tractability of the fixed-structure mirror would not settle that larger problem.

A second, credible anchor is Proposition 2 in Section 2.2.1, proved here. It states that Algorithm A determines which candidates can win through completion of exhausted ballots. Its proof gives the key single-winner condition: if \(g_c\) is the number of ballots in candidate \(c\)’s optimal winning strategy, \(r_c\) the earliest round when that strategy affects the election, and \(E_{r_c-1}\) the exhausted ballots available before then, \(c\) can win by completion exactly when \(g_c\leq E_{r_c-1}\). For multi-winner STV, the characterization is restricted to strategies that preserve the win/elimination sequence, because ballot additions can change the Droop quota and therefore the sequence.

The corresponding question is \(\textsc{Exhausted-Mass-Completion}_{\infty}\). Given \(C,k,\mu\), and the fixed RCV rules, keep total mass at \(1\). For each exhausted ballot type, a completion plan may split its mass among rankings extending its original submitted ranking; uncompleted mass retains its original ballot. Ask, for a target candidate \(c\), whether some such mass-preserving completion makes \(c\) a winner. A solution lists the mass assigned to each legal extension and demonstrates the resulting winners. For multi-winner instances, the sequence-preserving version matches Proposition 2’s proved scope.

I expect the single-winner problem, and the sequence-preserving multi-winner version, to be Class A: the paper’s exhausted-ballot threshold becomes a comparison between required added mass and available exhausted mass. A useful next question is whether arbitrary multi-winner completions can be handled without the sequence-preservation restriction. The paper’s beta and bootstrap completion models are not themselves this mirror: they put probability over possible completions. Here the population is the continuous object, and completion reachability is the computational question.

These models should be recognizable to the authors. They retain the same ballot types, RCV rule, target outcomes, and practical interventions—new turnout and completion of exhausted ballots. Only the electorate’s scale changes from ballot counts to mass. Neither model merely makes outcomes fractional or adds noise to a finite profile. The high-multiplicity regime is plausible where many voters share the same ranking or ballot template; it is not a claim that every RCV election has small support.

The paper’s related-work section cites NP-hardness of STV manipulation, including Bartholdi and Orlin [7], but it does not state a numbered hardness result of its own for the ballot-addition problem studied here. I would not use that citation as an anchor: manipulation and ballot addition are different interventions, and the cited theorem is not a result of this paper. The named anchors available here are algorithmic.

The weakest point is the change from integer elections to fractional mass. Discrete one-vote margins and the rounded Droop quota do not carry over verbatim, and the paper’s polynomial guarantee for a specified structure does not establish a polynomial algorithm for the continuous problem in \(m,\tau\), and mass bit length. Nor does the empirical paper establish that the proposed low-support regime occurs in its datasets. Those gaps matter, but they do not undo the positive case: these are precisely well-posed computational questions about the same RCV interventions, in a plausible large-population regime. The paper supplies useful algorithmic anchors; the continuous complexity and the finite-to-continuous dictionary are open work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proponent’s tractability predictions go beyond what the paper proves. Proposition 1 gives allocation procedures for discrete added ballots and particular target structures. It does not give a polynomial algorithm for finding the cheapest mass allocation over all structures. In the continuous STV version, the quota changes with added mass, and Gregory surplus transfers depend on vote totals through ratios. The paper’s round-by-round integer allocation argument therefore does not automatically become an LP or preserve its guarantees. That weakens the claim that \(\textsc{Structure-Add}_{\infty}\) is an easy continuation of the result. It does not, however, make the question unworthy: establishing the continuous algorithm, or showing where it fails, would itself be useful.

Proposition 2 offers a narrower objection. For single-winner completion, its condition \(g_c\leq E_{r_c-1}\) becomes a mass comparison. A continuous version may therefore amount to rescaling an existing characterization rather than opening a substantial new problem. The paper’s multi-winner result is more limited: it covers completions that preserve the win/elimination sequence. Asking about arbitrary mass-preserving completions, including those that change that sequence as the quota shifts, is a stronger mirror. The proposition does not answer it, and it remains a plausible computational question.

The paper also gives no evidence that its election data have support size \(\tau\) much smaller than the number of ballots. Its algorithms work with ballot counts, but the reported results do not establish that a low-support regime applies to those elections. That makes the paper a less direct empirical case for a high-multiplicity study. Still, common ranking patterns or endorsement slates offer plausible regimes, so this does not rule out a mirror.

I can make a case against treating either proposition as an already-tractable continuous result, and against prioritizing the simplest single-winner completion variant. I cannot honestly sustain the broader claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The more general completion question survives my objections, and Proposition 1 still points to a recognizable computational problem whose continuous complexity is open.
