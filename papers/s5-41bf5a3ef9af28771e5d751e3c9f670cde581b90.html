The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent gives no continuous question and names no result as an anchor, so there is no anchor to defeat. Their narrow point is sound: the paper states no numbered theorem classifying a problem’s complexity.

That point does not establish that the paper has no computational result at all. Its listed main results include a procedure for computing generalized Banzhaf power from voting structures, and the summary says those values can be computed with polynomial arithmetic. A possible mirror would ask how to compute type-level or team-level power when many members share vote weights and cohesion parameters. The paper’s own \(n=50\) team example and Senate-party model make that a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

The strongest objection is that this mirror may change what is being measured. Banzhaf power is based on a player’s ability to flip a coalition; in a continuum of independent members, an individual has zero mass, and their vote fluctuations vanish in the limit. Preserving nonzero power would require aggregating members into types or blocs, or retaining finite-size fluctuations. Those choices may leave a finite bloc game or an asymptotic probability model, rather than deliver a complexity result about a continuous society.

That is the best negative case, but it is not airtight. Team-level power is already part of the paper, so aggregating members into blocs is a natural extension the authors might recognize. I would not claim the universal “no worthwhile mirror” case is strong; it depends on treating the absence of a named complexity theorem as decisive, despite the paper’s algorithmic computation claims.
