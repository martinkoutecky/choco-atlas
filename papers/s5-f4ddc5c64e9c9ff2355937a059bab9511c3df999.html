The continuous mirror question

Given request types \(r\) with rational masses \(q_r\), vehicle types \(v\) with rational masses \(p_v\), feasible pairs \(E\), scores \(s(v,r)\), and historical group rates \(z_g\), compute a maximum-score mass assignment for a specified \(\beta\), including null actions and the passenger incentive \(\beta(\bar z-z_{g(r)})\). With post-batch rates \(z'_g\) computed from historical service mass and current request mass, determine whether a mass-level analogue of Theorem 1 holds: under a suitable positive-mass version of passenger-min-unfairness, does some \(\beta>0\) yield \(z'_{g_{\min}}(\beta)>z'_{g_{\min}}(0)\)?

The model it lives in

A batch dispatch model over request and vehicle types with rational masses: each vehicle unit takes one integral action, while the mass of a type may split across actions. The decision variables \(x_{vr}\) assign vehicle mass to compatible request mass, with \(x_{v,\bot}\) for null actions; maximize total score plus the passenger fairness incentive, subject to vehicle-mass equalities and request-mass capacities.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the passenger side, anchored on Theorem 1. It is proved in this paper: under the assumption that each vehicle can accept at most one request in a time step, if the baseline assignment \(A(0)\) is passenger-min-unfair, then some \(\beta>0\) makes the updated service rate of the least-served group strictly higher: \(z'_{g_{\min}}(\beta)>z'_{g_{\min}}(0)\). Here passenger-min-unfair means that a request \(r_f\) from the least-served group is left unserved, yet some vehicle that could serve \(r_f\) is assigned a request from another group. The paper gives a proof sketch and supplies the full proof separately. It does not give a named P-versus-hardness result, so I would use Theorem 1 as an algorithmic guarantee for its matching problem, not misstate it as a complexity classification.

The matching question has a natural high-multiplicity regime: a large dispatch operation serving repeated flows between hubs or corridors. Passenger types can be origin–destination area, remaining wait time, and any other attributes that affect feasibility or score. Vehicle types must include the full dispatch-relevant state—such as location, route, capacity, and value profile. Agents within a type are then indistinguishable for this batch. A city-scale batch might have thousands of requests and a large fleet, but only hundreds of recurring request and vehicle types if service is organized around standardized hubs and routes. The exact-type requirement matters: two requests in the same area pair are not the same type if their different locations or deadlines change which vehicles can serve them.

I would ask a prover to study Continuous Passenger Incentive Dispatch (CPID), the lead mirror. Normalize the batch’s request mass to one, and let vehicle mass sum to the rational fleet-to-request ratio \(\kappa\). The instance gives rational masses \(q_r\) for request types \(r\), rational supplies \(p_v\) for vehicle types \(v\), historical service rates \(z_g\) for geographic groups \(g\), rational scores \(s(v,r)=V(v,r)+R(v,r)\), and an explicit list \(E\) of feasible vehicle–request type pairs. Each vehicle unit must receive one integral action: serve one request or take the null action. For a specified rational fairness weight \(\beta\), find a maximum-score mass assignment \(x\), where \(x_{vr}\) is the mass of type \(v\) serving type \(r\), and \(x_{v,\bot}\) is the mass taking the null action:

\[ \max_x\ \sum_{(v,r)\in E}x_{vr}\bigl[s(v,r)+\beta(\bar z-z_{g(r)})\bigr] +\sum_v x_{v,\bot}s(v,\bot), \qquad \bar z=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}z_g, \]

subject to

\[ \sum_{r:(v,r)\in E}x_{vr}+x_{v,\bot}=p_v,\qquad \sum_{v:(v,r)\in E}x_{vr}\le q_r,\qquad x\ge0. \]

The output is an optimal assignment and its updated group service rates. Those rates can be computed from historical request mass \(h_g\) as
\[ z'_g(x)=\frac{h_gz_g+\sum_{r:g(r)=g}\sum_vx_{vr}} {h_g+\sum_{r:g(r)=g}q_r}. \]
Fractional \(x\) means that a fraction of a type’s population takes each action; each unit of mass still receives one discrete ride assignment.

I expect CPID to be tractable when the compatibility pairs are explicit: it is a rational maximum-weight flow problem, polynomial in the type supports, the number of feasible pairs, and the input bit length. This keeps the paper’s core object—the score-maximizing dispatch with its passenger fairness incentive—and gives a direct continuous counterpart to Theorem 1’s group-level service-rate guarantee. A further question is whether the theorem’s strict-improvement guarantee carries over to mass assignments, especially with tied worst-off groups and multiple optimal assignments.

This mirror covers the one-request-per-vehicle batch matching and passenger-side incentive result. It does not claim to continuize the value-function training, the full online dynamics, or the paper’s general multi-request trip bundles; those bundles would bring a separate pricing or trip-generation question. The weakest point is the regime: in the paper’s detailed street-network model, exact locations, routes, and deadlines can make nearly every agent a distinct type. Standardized hubs and recurring flows make high multiplicity credible, but narrow the empirical setting. The model remains recognizable to the authors because it preserves their dispatch objective, fairness term, and one-request theorem assumptions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is that Theorem 1 is a weak anchor for ChoCo: it proves a fairness improvement under a particular assignment condition, not a complexity or algorithmic result. The paper gives no named classification of the dispatch problem’s complexity. Its main computational object is a one-step matching ILP; its main contribution is the incentive scheme and its empirical effect. A mirror of Theorem 1 would therefore turn primarily on how to extend that fairness guarantee, rather than on the complexity landscape the programme is meant to chart.

The support problem is real in the paper’s operational setting. A request’s origin, destination, arrival time, and remaining wait can change which vehicles may serve it and at what score. A vehicle’s location, path, capacity, and value state can do the same. Since a type must include every dispatch-relevant attribute, merging riders by broad geographic group or vehicles by rough location would change feasibility. Retaining exact attributes may leave few repeated types. The proposed corridor or hub setting can create genuine repeated types, but it narrows the paper’s dynamic, spatial matching problem substantially.

That is the strongest objection to CPID: it fixes one-request-per-vehicle batches and gives the solver an explicit compatibility graph. This makes the population flow model well-defined, but it isolates a static assignment slice. Restoring the paper’s pooling and changing vehicle states brings in multi-request trips, route constraints, and future values; then actions are bundles of requests, and a type-level formulation has to represent those bundle choices without losing their interactions. If one coarsens types to keep the support small, the model may allow matches that the original system forbids. If one preserves those distinctions, the support can grow with the batch. This is a tension in finding a representative high-multiplicity regime, not a claim that the richer problem’s eventual answer would be hard or uninteresting.

The strict-improvement guarantee also needs a substantive restatement. A discrete instance can be passenger-min-unfair because of one unmatched request; its share of a growing population can tend to zero. The mass analogue needs positive mass of compatible underserved requests and enough compatible vehicle mass to serve it. That is a sensible condition, but it describes a stronger population-level phenomenon than the paper’s stated premise. It leaves the mirror dependent on whether recurring cohorts with that much interchangeable mass occur in a convincing dispatch regime.

This case does not defeat CPID. A large operation with repeated request and vehicle types is plausible, and the one-request assumption is the assumption of Theorem 1 itself. I cannot honestly claim that every such scenario is artificial or that the continuous question is ill-posed. The negative case is that the strongest anchor does not, by itself, carry the paper’s broader ridesharing setting into a compelling population-continuous model; the proposed mirror survives precisely in its narrower regime.
