The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest positive case under the programme’s anchor requirement. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting a computational result. Its named *Pre-negotiation Coalition Support Hypothesis* and the regression findings—support predicts proposal inclusion, while party size does not—are empirical claims, not complexity or algorithmic results.

The closest imaginable mirror would treat coalition parties with identical preferences, costs, and other relevant attributes as a type, and their share of a large coalition as mass. One could then ask whether a proposal’s inclusion probability rises with the mass of other parties that endorsed it before negotiations. But the paper studies coalitions of only two to four parties, and gives no computational decision problem for that question. A large population of interchangeable negotiating parties would need a different scenario and likely change the authors’ problem.

So there are no eligible anchors, and therefore no continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover. The weakness is decisive: proposing one anyway would attach a new computational problem to an empirical finding the paper does not make.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s first point is decisive for a mirror of this paper: it names no computational problem and proves no complexity or algorithmic result. The Pre-negotiation Coalition Support Hypothesis is an empirical hypothesis; the paper tests it with regressions on policy proposals. Its findings—even their robustness across 150 models—are not computational claims for a continuous society to mirror.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror would assign mass to party types and ask whether proposal inclusion rises with the mass of other coalition parties that supported the proposal beforehand. That can be a coherent question in a hypothetical large-party system. But it would turn the paper’s empirical association into a new model of coalition bargaining. The article supplies no computational task for that model: no formal choice problem, feasible set of agreements, or objective to optimize. Computing support mass or a regression prediction would not supply the complexity question the programme is looking for. Adding agreement selection or coalition formation would require specifying those missing ingredients, so the resulting problem would be an extension rather than a mirror of a result in the paper.

Changing the population to voters does not repair the anchor. Voters are affected by policy in the paper’s theory, but the measured outcome is whether a party’s proposal enters an agreement; voter preferences are not the bargaining input. Aggregating cabinets or jurisdictions instead would likewise create a new setting, not a population underlying the paper’s proposal-level analysis. At the scale actually studied, coalitions have two to four parties, and parties are the negotiating principals.

So the strongest case against a continuous mirror is that there is no eligible computational anchor, and the apparent support-mass mirror has no computational problem in the paper to inherit. I would not claim that no useful high-multiplicity study of coalition bargaining could ever be designed; a large-party version might be worth studying on its own. The negative case is strongest for this paper specifically: such a study would be new model-building, not continuization of one of its results.
