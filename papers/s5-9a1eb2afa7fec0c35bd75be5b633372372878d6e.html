The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a valid *anchored computational* case for this paper: it contains no named result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

Its numbered theorems concern axiomatic properties of apportionment methods. For example, Theorem 4—proved in the paper—says Jefferson’s method is the unique consistent, monotone, balanced method that encourages coalitions. Theorem 7, cited from elsewhere, says the quota method is the unique house-monotone, quota-consistent method satisfying quota. Neither is a computational result. The paper’s recursive definitions of allocation methods also do not establish complexity bounds.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large electorate has many voters but relatively few party-affiliation types. Let type \(i\) mean support for party \(i\), with mass \(\mu_i\) equal to that party’s vote share. One could ask for an integer seat allocation under Jefferson’s or the quota method. But that mainly rewrites vote totals as proportions; the seat vector and the paper’s fairness questions remain discrete and axiomatic. Letting seats become continuous would remove the rounding issue at the heart of the paper.

So the honest positive case is limited: proportional representation naturally has large populations and small support, making vote shares a sensible description. But this paper offers no qualifying computational anchor, and its central results ask which method satisfies which fairness properties—a scope the programme explicitly excludes. The strongest weakness is therefore also decisive: there is no paper result here whose computational complexity a continuous mirror could investigate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper proves no computational result. Theorem 4 characterizes Jefferson’s method by fairness properties; Theorems 6 and 7 concern quota and consistency; the other numbered results are likewise axiomatic. None classifies the complexity of computing an allocation or solving an optimization problem. That is a decisive mismatch for ChoCo’s programme.

There is a sensible high-multiplicity setting: take party support as voter types with masses \(\mu_i\), and keep the house size and seat allocations integral. That preserves the apportionment problem and its rounding issue. But it does not turn the paper’s characterizations into computational questions. Replacing vote totals by normalized shares simply supplies different notation for the input the paper already uses. Making seats fractional would change the outcome space and remove the problem’s central concern.

The best rescue would be to ask how efficiently one can compute a Jefferson or quota allocation from those masses. That is a well-posed algorithmic question, but it is not a computational result established or classified by this paper. Questions about the minimum vote mass needed to change an allocation would go further still: they add a costed intervention problem absent from the paper. Those could be worthwhile research questions in their own right, but they do not provide a continuous mirror of this paper’s results. This case is strongest as a judgment about the paper’s fit for the programme; it cannot rule out every future computational study inspired by apportionment.
