The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(A,B\), rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\) summing to \(1\) on each side, allowed pairs \(E\subseteq A\times B\), positive rational shared benefits \(w_{ab}\), and rational initial and target mass matchings \(x^0,x^\star\), decide whether a finite sequence of legal blocking steps takes \(x^0\) exactly to \(x^\star\). A mass matching satisfies \(\sum_b x_{ab}\leq\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\leq\nu_b\). A step selects an allowed pair \((a,b)\) with positive mass on both sides of agents who are unmatched or strictly prefer that pair to their current partner, then matches the same positive rational mass \(\delta\) from those pools, dissolving their prior matches. The target must be stable, meaning no allowed pair has positive willing mass on both sides.

The model it lives in

A two-sided market with finitely many preference-and-benefit types carrying rational masses; states are mass matchings \(x\), and a legal step transfers a positive rational mass between willing pools along a blocking type pair. The objective is exact reachability of a specified stable target from a specified initial state.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3, item 4: reachability in ordinary bipartite stable matching with correlated preferences and ties. It leaves the paper’s constrained-matching results, convergence theorems, and other preference settings aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of exact mass-target reachability in \(|A|+|B|\), \(|E|\), and the input bit length \(L\), and do reachable targets always have polynomial-length rational path certificates?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Mass Reachability in Correlated Stable Matching. Consider a large two-sided market—say, recurring cohorts of researchers and potential collaborators—where agents fall into a modest number of role and preference types. Every agent in a type has the same preferences over counterpart types, and the value of a match is shared by both sides. That common compatibility score makes the paper’s correlated-preference model plausible. The number of agents can be very large while the number of types, \(\tau\), stays modest.

Here is the continuous problem. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be the types on the two sides, with rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\) summing to \(1\). Let \(E\subseteq A\times B\) be the allowed matches, and let each \((a,b)\in E\) have a positive rational common benefit \(w_{ab}\). Agents rank counterpart types by this benefit, with ties when benefits are equal. A mass matching is a matrix \(x\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_b x_{ab}\leq\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\leq\nu_b\). Each individual still has at most one partner; \(x_{ab}\) is the mass of agents paired across those two types.

The instance also gives an initial mass matching \(x^0\) and a stable target \(x^\star\). At a state \(x\), the mass of type \(a\) willing to match with \(b\) is
\[ W_a(b;x)=\mu_a-\sum_{b':\,w_{ab'}\geq w_{ab}}x_{ab'}. \]
This counts agents who are unmatched or currently matched to a strictly less preferred type. Define \(W_b(a;x)\) symmetrically. The target is stable when no allowed pair has positive willing mass on both sides.

A legal step chooses \((a,b)\) with \(W_a(b;x)>0\) and \(W_b(a;x)>0\), selects the same positive rational mass \(\delta\) from each willing pool, breaks their old matches, and matches that mass across \((a,b)\). The question is whether a finite sequence of such steps takes \(x^0\) exactly to \(x^\star\). Its computational complexity is measured in \(|A|+|B|\), \(|E|\), and the input bit length \(L\). This keeps the paper’s discrete blocking-pair dynamics and specified-target question. Only the population becomes continuous; fractional edge mass records many individually integral matches.

My anchor is Theorem 3, item 4, proved in this paper. It says that, for ordinary bipartite stable matching with correlated preferences and ties, it is NP-complete to decide whether a sequence of improvement steps leads from a given initial matching \(M_0\) to a given stable matching \(M^\star\). The authors prove this via their reduction in Appendix A.5; Theorem 2 supplies the polynomial-size reachability certificate used for membership in NP. The correlated scores and ties make this a particularly apt anchor: they let a cohort share one preference description without pretending its members have distinct identities.

I would expect the unrestricted mass-reachability problem to be hard for continuum-specific reasons, though that is a conjecture, not a hardness transfer from Theorem 3. The target still imposes an exact endpoint, and the order in which type-pair masses are released and matched may preserve difficult sequencing choices. The paper’s discrete reduction does not establish this: fractional moves could split the mass in its gadgets and create paths absent from the finite instance. Fixed or modest \(\tau\) may well be tractable, which makes the dependence on support size a natural follow-up.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors the paper’s ordinary bipartite stable-matching reachability result, not its convergence theorems for constrained games or all its preference settings. The weakest point is the gap between the reduction’s individually specified agents and a society of interchangeable cohorts. The scenario makes the high-multiplicity regime credible, but it remains to show whether any meaningful complexity survives aggregation and mass splitting. That gap is the research question, rather than a reason to dismiss the mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3(4) is a genuine computational anchor: it proves NP-completeness for reachability in ordinary bipartite stable matching with correlated preferences and ties. So the case against cannot rest on a lack of computational results. The issue is whether the hardness belongs to a population that can sensibly be treated as a few large types.

In the reduction, agents are vertices with specific partners and edges; reaching the target depends on which particular vertices get matched and when. A faithful type must preserve the full preference and availability information the problem uses. Agents with different gadget roles therefore belong to different types, often leaving essentially one agent per type. The stated input model does allow repeated types, but the paper’s hardness result does not show that its difficult instances have them.

The proponent’s best repair is a genuinely type-homogeneous market, with agents interchangeable within each role and a target mass matrix \(x^\star\). That is coherent, but it changes the reachability question: it identifies individual matchings that have the same type-pair masses, and permits a mass move to represent many individual moves. Those changes erase the clause- and variable-specific identities used by the reduction. Restoring those distinctions with more types brings back the vanishing-mass problem. Thus Theorem 3(4) gives no evidence that its hardness survives this repair; a finite-to-continuum dictionary would need to establish what the new paths mean and how they relate to discrete reachability.

That is a serious weakness in the proposed *mirror*, but it does not defeat the repaired question. Recurring cohorts with common preferences and compatibility scores are plausible, and type-level reachability is a well-posed computational problem. The paper’s authors might recognize it as a natural extension of their dynamics. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile scenario exists. The strongest negative case is that the paper’s NP-completeness result is identity-sensitive, and the credible anonymous version is a new problem whose connection to that result remains unshown. The case against the mirror is therefore weak on the user’s universal claim.
