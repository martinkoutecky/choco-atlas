The continuous mirror question

Given \(p\) chore kinds, rational type masses \(\mu_A,\mu_B>0\) with \(\mu_A+\mu_B=1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma_h\ge 0\), compute a rational assignment \(x_{t,b}\ge 0\) over types \(t\in\{A,B\}\) and integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^p\). Require \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_h=\sigma_h\) for each chore kind \(h\). Type \(A\) has a shared general monotone cost \(C_A(b)\); type \(B\) has a shared additive cost \(C_B(b)=\sum_h w_h b_h\), with \(\max_h w_h/\min_h w_h\le 2\). Require tEFX across the positive-mass support: whenever \(x_{t,b}>0\), \(x_{u,d}>0\), and \(b_h>0\), \(C_t(b-e_h)\le C_t(d+e_h)\). Determine whether such an assignment can be computed in time polynomial in \(p\) and the input bit length, using cost oracles and without expanding the denominator-cleared population.

Two agent types carry masses \(\mu_A,\mu_B\); each person receives an integral bundle of recurring chore kinds, while \(x_{t,b}\) records the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(b\). Find a mass assignment matching per-capita supplies and satisfying tEFX across its support; there is no allocation-cost objective.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Corollary 4.6, the strongest anchor for a population mirror in this paper. It says that when agents split into two groups, with identical general monotone cost functions within the first group and identical additive \(2\)-ratio-bounded cost functions within the second, a tEFX allocation exists. The corollary is derived here from Lemma 4.3, whose proof gives a constructive algorithm using value oracles; it is not a result merely cited from elsewhere. I take it under the non-degeneracy convention used in Section 4, or with the paper’s tie-breaking reduction.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large network of sites assigning recurring duties to many teams. Teams fall into two standardized contract or operating types: everyone of type \(A\) has the same complete cost function over duty bundles, and everyone of type \(B\) has the same additive costs, with the costs of individual duties within a factor of \(2\). The number of teams \(N\) can be large while the number of types is \(\tau=2\). Duty inventory grows with the workforce, so its natural input is supply per capita. This is a direct extension of the paper’s identical-cost groups, and the types preserve all the distinctions the model uses.

Two-type tEFX mass allocation is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. There are \(p\) duty kinds, type masses \(\mu_A,\mu_B\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\) with \(\mu_A+\mu_B=1\), and per-capita supplies \(\sigma_h\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) for each kind \(h\in[p]\). An individual receives an integral multiset \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^p\) of duties. Type \(A\) has a shared general monotone cost function \(C_A(b)\); type \(B\) has a shared additive cost function \(C_B(b)=\sum_h w_h b_h\), with \(\max_h w_h/\min_h w_h\le2\).

The decision variable \(x_{t,b}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents assigned bundle \(b\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_h=\sigma_h\ \text{ for every }h. \]
The task is to find such an \(x\) satisfying tEFX across its support: whenever \(x_{t,b}>0\), \(x_{u,d}>0\), and \(b_h>0\),
\[ C_t(b-e_h)\le C_t(d+e_h). \]
Thus each individual receives an integral bundle; only the measure of people receiving each bundle is fractional. The output is the mass assignment \(x\), not a lottery over fractional chores.

For rational inputs, the existence half is already strongly grounded in the anchor. Clear the mass denominators to obtain a finite instance with integer group sizes and chore copies, apply Corollary 4.6, then divide the resulting bundle counts by the scale factor. The continuous computational question is whether one can produce the mass assignment in time polynomial in \(p\), the bit length \(L\), and the number of cost-oracle calls, without expanding to that possibly enormous finite population. A solution can be sparse: the mass and supply equations give \(p+2\) linear constraints, and removing a bundle from the support cannot break tEFX. So any feasible solution can be reduced to at most \(p+2\) positive type-bundle masses.

I would tentatively expect the compressed problem to be Class A. The paper already proves the corresponding two-group result constructively, and the continuous output has a short sparse representation. The open algorithmic step is finding a fair support efficiently among the possible integral bundles; tEFX depends on which bundles have positive mass, so the mass equations alone are not an LP solution. Useful follow-up questions are whether the two-group transfer argument can find that support in polynomial bit complexity, and how far the result extends beyond two shared-cost types.

This mirror covers the paper’s two-group tEFX result. It does not claim to continuize the three-agent \(2\)-EFX theorem, the IDO result, or the ratio-bounded EFX bounds. The paper’s treatment of identical-cost groups supports the high-multiplicity regime; the proposed question asks whether that regime admits a denominator-independent algorithm.

My weakest point is the compression step. Lemma 4.3’s finite algorithm does not itself establish a polynomial-time algorithm in the binary encoding of masses and supplies, and pairwise tEFX constraints make support discovery the hard part. The proposed duty cohorts also require genuinely shared cost functions and recurring task kinds. Those are substantial conditions, but they describe a recognizable regime and preserve the paper’s individual-level bundles and fairness test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 4.6 is the right anchor, and it is a strong one: the paper constructively handles groups of agents with identical cost functions. I would not argue that agents are too individually named, that the result is noncomputational, or that high multiplicity is implausible. The best objection is that the paper’s chores are a fixed set of distinct indivisible items, while the proposed mirror needs the chore supply to grow with the population.

If the set \(M\) stays fixed as the population grows to \(N\), at most \(|M|\) people receive nonempty bundles. Their total population mass is at most \(|M|/N\), which tends to zero. The limit therefore loses the agents holding the chores: almost all mass has the empty bundle, while the finite stock of chores still has to be allocated. Since tEFX is tested on bundles in the support, those disappearing nonempty bundles can also make the fairness test disappear in the limit. With positive masses for both types, group sizes eventually exceed \(|M|\), so increasing them further does not create a meaningful per-capita allocation problem.

The proposed fix—scaling the chore supply and representing repeated tasks by \(p\) kinds—avoids that degeneration, but adds a substantive model choice. The paper’s cost functions are defined on subsets of distinct chores; they do not specify how costs behave over repeated copies of a chore kind. A symmetric cost oracle \(C_t(b)\) over integral count vectors is plausible for recurring duties, and denominator clearing then gives a finite instance to which the corollary applies. But that is a new clone-based chore model. The sparse-support bound does not settle its computational question: it applies after a fair support has been found, while tEFX itself depends on which bundles are in that support.

That is the strongest case against treating this as an established mirror: the faithful fixed-stock limit degenerates, and the nondegenerate version relies on added recurring-duty structure and leaves a distinct support-search problem. I can’t make the universal negative case airtight, though. A recurring-duty model with shared type costs is plausible, and the binary-compressed search for a tEFX allocation could be a worthwhile high-multiplicity problem. If the authors recognize that setting as theirs, Corollary 4.6 survives the objection.
