The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a valid anchor-based case for a continuous mirror: the paper has no named computational result. Its named results concern axiomatic characterizations and impossibilities. For example, Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes menu-contingent utilitarianism under its stated axioms; Theorem 2, also proved here, characterizes setting-contingent utilitarianism; Proposition 5, proved here, shows that no rational aggregation rule is consistent with either Nash or Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining. None asserts a complexity classification or algorithmic bound. The paper’s MCU maximization formula is a defined bargaining solution, not a named result about computing it.

A possible direction to study independently would represent a large bargaining or consumer population by masses over normalized utility types, then ask how to find a welfare-maximizing alternative. But the paper gives no general input model for that computation, and its contribution is axiomatic rather than computational. That would be a new problem motivated by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the decisive problem: none of the paper’s named results is computational. Theorem 1 characterizes menu-contingent utilitarianism; Theorem 2 characterizes setting-contingent utilitarianism; Proposition 5 proves incompatibility between vNM rationality and consistency with Nash or Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining. They give no algorithm, runtime, or complexity classification. The MCU formula defines a bargaining solution; it does not analyze the cost of computing it.

The strongest population version would take a distribution \(\mu\) over complete preference types and replace the finite sum of normalized utilities with an integral, using menu normalization for Theorem 1 or setting normalization for Theorem 2. That is a sensible model: the consumer-welfare example already uses group shares \(\gamma_J\), and anonymity makes repeated types natural. But with finite type support and an explicit menu, evaluating the resulting ranking is just a weighted sum for each pair of alternatives. To get a harder optimization problem, one must add an implicitly represented menu or bargaining set and specify how utilities and feasibility are encoded. Any resulting complexity then belongs to that newly specified problem; it is not a computational result being mirrored from these theorems.

The same applies to Proposition 5. One could ask how hard it is to compute the MCU maximizer for a distribution of bargaining types. That might be worth studying, but the proposition’s result is about axiomatic consistency, and the paper supplies no computational input model to preserve. Its alternative-space representation would drive the new problem’s complexity.

So I find no surviving anchor, and the proposed welfare-optimization direction does not rescue one. The honest limit on the negative case is that a new computational project built around population-level utilitarian welfare could still be worthwhile. I would call it an adjacent research question, not a continuous mirror of this paper.
