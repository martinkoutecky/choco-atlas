The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. It therefore provides no qualifying anchor for the required continuous problem. The paper does give a selection objective in Equation (5), a greedy rule in Equation (7), and simulation results, but those are not named complexity or algorithmic results of the kind this case must rest on.

There is a plausible unanchored mirror: in a large organization, let each type describe voters with the same preference and delegation pattern, and let its mass be their fraction of the population. The decision would be how much mass of each type to recruit to vote; the objective would be to maximize the delegated vote captured, or improve the resulting estimate of the population’s preference distribution, subject to an outreach budget. But that would be my proposed formalization of the paper’s objective, not a continuous version of a qualifying named result. The paper’s simulations do not establish its complexity or guarantee an algorithmic property that could serve as an anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper proves no computational result to mirror. Equation (5) defines a selection objective and Equation (7) gives a greedy rule, but the paper establishes no complexity bound, optimality result, or approximation guarantee for that rule. Its evidence is simulation. A continuous optimization built from those equations could be an interesting new question, but it would not be a continuous version of a proved computational result in this paper.

The more serious version of the proposed mirror would model a large organization with a fixed set of roles or blocks. Members of each type share preferences and the same delegation pattern across types; recruiting a mass of a type switches that mass from delegation to direct voting. One could then optimize propagated vote mass or preference-distribution accuracy under an outreach budget. That model can preserve the paper’s central mechanism, so I cannot dismiss it as incoherent or as losing individual identity by definition.

The difficulty is that this useful model adds assumptions the paper does not establish. In its random directed networks, a voter’s influence depends on their position and paths through the network. Preference alone is not a complete type; aggregating by preference would generally lose the information that drives the result. Encoding each voter’s network role may leave nearly every voter with a distinct type, so the support grows with the population. A block model avoids that problem, but makes network structure part of a new high-multiplicity regime. Optimizing distribution-estimation error would also promote a simulation metric to an objective the paper does not optimize; Equation (5) instead maximizes captured vote mass.

So the case against a *paper-anchored* mirror is strong: there is no named computational result here, and the natural extension needs substantial modeling choices. But the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is not honestly defensible. A block-structured delegation society makes a plausible continuous computational problem. The positive case has not identified a qualifying result to mirror, but its unanchored proposal remains a reasonable research question.
