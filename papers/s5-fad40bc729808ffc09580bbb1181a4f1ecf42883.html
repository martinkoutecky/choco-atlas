The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is qualified: this paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule asks for. Its numbered result is Theorem 1, proved here: there is an instance with four agents and 52 goods, strictly positive additive valuations, and no complete PMMS allocation. The instance has two valuation types and four good classes: the two types value classes \(A,B,C,D\) at \((81,100,94,101)\) and \((81,94,100,101)\), respectively; the class sizes are \(3,5,5,39\). That is a significant existence theorem, but it asserts no complexity classification or algorithmic result, so it cannot honestly serve as a qualifying computational anchor.

The nearest continuous question I would put forward is Continuous PMMS Existence for Type-and-Good Populations. An instance has a finite set of agent types \(T\), a finite set of good classes \(G\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and strictly positive additive values \(v_t(g)\). A solution is a finite list of bundles \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}\) and masses \(x_{t,b}\ge0\), satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_g=q_g\) for each good class. Every individual receives an integral bundle; only the aggregate mass assigned to each bundle can be fractional.

The question is whether such an allocation exists in which every ordered pair of realized agent outcomes \((t,b),(u,c)\) satisfies PMMS: \(v_t(b)\ge \max_{d\subseteq b+c}\min\{v_t(d),v_t((b+c)\setminus d)\}\), where \(d\) ranges over submultisets of the pooled integral bundles. A solution is the supported bundle-and-mass list. This preserves the paper’s pairwise benchmark and indivisible individual bundles. The proposed regime is a large allocation programme with many households but only a few complete valuation profiles and good classes. For the paper’s example, the candidate type masses are \((1/2,1/2)\), with per-capita supplies \((3/4,5/4,5/4,39/4)\). That is a proposed continuous instance, not a claim that Theorem 1’s nonexistence proof carries over.

I think the authors would recognize this as a high-multiplicity version of their PMMS allocation-existence problem, provided the goods are repeated copies of a few classes and the population is large relative to the number of types. My weak guess is Class C: choosing a mutually PMMS-compatible support of integral bundles may retain substantial combinatorics even when type masses are continuous. But that is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of their theorem; the paper supplies no hardness result from which to infer it. Further questions include whether the proposed instance remains infeasible after allowing type-level fractional masses over bundles, and how the continuous existence problem relates formally to finite instances.

The weakest point is also decisive for the anchor requirement: Theorem 1 concerns only four agents, and the added population model changes the feasible allocation space. Its nonexistence result may disappear under that relaxation. So this is a plausible, precisely stated existence mirror, but the paper does not provide a named computational result that anchors a complexity-mirroring case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against using this paper as a ChoCo anchor is that it supplies no computational result to continue. Its Theorem 1 proves an existence failure for one finite instance: four agents, 52 goods, and no complete PMMS allocation. It gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or computational problem. The proposed continuous question therefore starts a new line of work; it does not mirror a computational result in the paper.

That remains true of the stronger formulation. One could ask for an algorithm that, given rational type masses, per-capita supplies, and valuations, finds or rules out a distribution over integral bundles satisfying PMMS for every pair of supported outcomes. That is a coherent computational problem, and it may be interesting. But the paper neither states that problem nor gives a result that anchors its continuous version. The paper’s particular counterexample could supply a test instance, with masses \((1/2,1/2)\) and per-capita supplies \((3/4,5/4,5/4,39/4)\). Studying that one point would not yield a complexity landscape; scaling it into a family would require choosing and analyzing a new problem.

There is a modest methodological concern as well. Continuous masses make resource-balance constraints linear, while PMMS still imposes pairwise compatibility between integral bundles, each checked against a discrete repartitioning benchmark. The paper offers no pricing method or structural insight for handling that compatibility condition. A continuous formulation may expose a difficult support-selection problem rather than bring the paper’s result within reach of continuous optimization. That is a reason to expect a substantial new project, not evidence that the project would be unimportant.

I cannot honestly turn those points into the requested universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proposed regime is sensible: many agents can share complete valuation types, and good supplies can scale with population while each individual still receives an integral bundle. PMMS does not depend on named identities, and requiring the condition across supported outcomes gives a clear way to state its pairwise benchmark. The paper’s counterexample could motivate a serious high-multiplicity existence or search problem. Those are genuine strengths of the mirror.

So the negative case is narrow but firm on the anchor: this is not a continuous mirror of a computational theorem in the paper, because there is no such theorem. The broader assertion that no scenario would make the resulting computational problem worthwhile is weak; the model itself appears viable, and judging its value would require engaging with the new problem it defines.
