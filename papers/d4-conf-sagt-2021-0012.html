The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), ranking types \(T\subseteq S_m\), masses \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), and adaptive access to pairwise-majority queries determined by \(p_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t\), determine the smallest worst-case metric distortion achievable by a mechanism using at most \(m-1\) queries over all unit-mass metric realizations consistent with \(\mu\).

The model it lives in

The society is a finite-support mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types; each query chooses a candidate pair and receives the sign of \(p_\mu(a,b)-1/2\), while the decision variable is the adaptive query-and-output policy and the objective is worst-case metric distortion.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3.5 uses \(\mu\) only to generate pairwise-majority signs, so once those signs are available the computation is a candidate-only tournament algorithm; the proponent does not show a mass-sensitive optimization or a new population-dependent bound.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers the \(\mathrm{DominationRoot}\) query guarantee in Theorem 3.5 and also expresses Proposition 3.1 and Theorem 5.9; it leaves the incomplete-order bounds, metric-LP comparisons, conditional conjecture, and empirical findings without a continuous computational theorem.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the \(2\lceil\log m\rceil+1\) guarantee tight with only adaptive majority-query access to \(\mu\)?
  • Can the optimal query policy or metric-minimax winner be computed from rational \(\mu\) in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length?
  • Can Theorem 5.9 be lifted rigorously to a capacitated type-level matching formulation with masses \(\mu_t\), and can its dependence on \(m\) be improved?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a qualified one. This paper contains no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or parameterized-complexity theorem in the usual sense. Its named results are approximation, query, and sample-complexity guarantees. Nevertheless, the pairwise-comparison and sampling results admit unusually natural continuous-population mirrors.

Take a finite set \(T\subseteq S_m\) of complete ranking types and a rational mass vector \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). A type-\(t\) block has population mass \(\mu_t\). For candidates \(a,b\), write
\[ p_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t. \]
The pairwise oracle returns the majority winner according to these masses. The latent metric is still adversarial, exactly as in the paper: a consistent metric realization consists of a unit-mass population measure whose ranking-type masses are \(\mu\), together with candidate locations, such that every voter’s ranking agrees with her distances. Its social cost is
\[ \mathrm{SC}_\nu(a)=\int d(v,c_a)\,d\nu(v), \]
and the distortion of an output \(w\) is
\[ \sup_{\nu\text{ consistent with }\mu} \frac{\mathrm{SC}_\nu(w)}{\min_{a\in C}\mathrm{SC}_\nu(a)}. \]

This is a genuine high-multiplicity regime: a national election, large survey, or recommendation population may contain millions of voters but only a small number \(\tau=|T|\) of repeated ballot types. Rational masses reproduce finite elections exactly after scaling, while the continuous formulation makes the population size disappear from the query and sample bounds.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper. It states that the paper’s \(\DR\) mechanism elicits only \(m-1\) pairwise comparisons and guarantees distortion at most
\[ 2\lceil\log m\rceil+1. \]
The mechanism repeatedly pairs the surviving candidates, queries each pair, removes the loser, and continues until one candidate remains. Claim 3.3 proves that exactly \(m-1\) edges are elicited; Claim 3.4 proves that the surviving candidate reaches every other tournament vertex by a path of length at most \(\lceil\log m\rceil\). The distortion bound then follows from Lemma 3.2, which is cited from Kempe: if every consecutive pair in a candidate path is won by the preceding candidate by at least half the population, then the first candidate’s social cost is at most \((2\ell-1)\) times the last candidate’s.

The corresponding problem is:

Continuous-DominationRoot\(_\infty\). Given \(C,T,\mu\), access only to the pairwise-majority oracle induced by \(\mu\), and no cardinal metric information, output a candidate after at most \(m-1\) adaptive queries so as to minimize worst-case metric distortion over all metric realizations consistent with \(\mu\).

The paper’s \(\DR\) algorithm is a valid solution: it returns a candidate with distortion at most \(2\lceil\log m\rceil+1\). The proof transfers directly. Every occurrence of “at least half of the voters” becomes \(p_\mu(a,b)\ge 1/2\), and the finite social-cost sums in Lemma 3.2 become integrals. No outcome is fractionalized and no time variable is introduced; only the population is continuized.

I would classify this as Class A for bounded-distortion winner determination under a strict pairwise-information budget. The natural follow-up questions are whether the logarithmic factor can be improved in the continuous model, whether the exact metric-minimax winner is computable in time polynomial in \((m,\tau,L)\), and how the answer changes with partial ranking types or weighted query costs.

A useful second anchor is Proposition 3.1, also proved here. It states that there are instances for which every deterministic mechanism eliciting strictly fewer than \(m-1\) tournament edges has unbounded distortion. The continuous version is:

Continuous-Pairwise-Query Threshold\(_\infty\). Determine the least number \(q\) of adaptive pairwise-majority queries needed to guarantee finite worst-case metric distortion for every finite-support mass distribution \(\mu\).

The paper’s lower-bound construction survives unchanged after replacing voter counts by masses. One can take a large population divided into repeated ranking cohorts, with the same tournament ambiguity as in the finite construction. If fewer than \(m-1\) comparisons are made, at least two candidates remain compatible with the transcript as the unique socially good candidate. The adversary can place almost all population mass close to whichever of those candidates is selected in the corresponding metric realization, while placing the other candidates arbitrarily far away. Hence the distortion is unbounded.

This lower bound is not continuum-specific hardness: the obstruction lives in the number of alternatives and the information revealed by pairwise queries, not in population multiplicity. It therefore gives a useful boundary result for the mirror. The continuous relaxation helps with population representation, but it does not magically remove winner uncertainty caused by an insufficient query budget.

A third, weaker but still natural anchor is Theorem 5.9, proved here. It states that for every \(\epsilon\in(0,4]\) and \(\delta\in(0,1)\), there is a mechanism using
\[ \Theta\!\left(\frac{m+\log(m/\delta)}{\epsilon^2}\right) \]
sampled voters which achieves distortion at most \(3+\epsilon\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\). The mechanism is a sampling approximation of the paper’s \(\pluralitymatching\) rule: from sampled ranking types it constructs empirical domination graphs, computes maximum matchings, and returns the candidate with the largest normalized matching value.

The continuous problem is:

Continuous-Sample-PluralityMatching\(_\infty\). Given \(C,T,\mu,\epsilon,\delta\), draw i.i.d. ranking types from \(\mu\), observe only the sampled rankings, and output a candidate \(w\) such that
\[ \Pr_{S\sim\mu^c}\!\left[ \sup_{\nu\text{ consistent with }\mu} \frac{\mathrm{SC}_\nu(w)}{\min_a\mathrm{SC}_\nu(a)} \le 3+\epsilon \right]\ge 1-\delta. \]
The theorem says that
\[ c=\Theta\!\left(\frac{m+\log(m/\delta)}{\epsilon^2}\right) \]
samples suffice, independently of the total population size.

This is especially plausible as a continuous-population formulation because the authors themselves motivate their result by the regime \(n\gg m\), polls, surveys, and sublinear algorithms whose running time should not depend on \(n\). In the mirror, the sample is drawn directly from the population distribution \(\mu\). The expected classification is again Class A, now in a statistical-query sense. Natural extensions ask whether the dependence on \(m\) can be improved, whether exact mass information permits a polynomial-time fractional domination formulation, and how the result behaves for partial rankings.

The weakest point is formal rather than mathematical: under ChoCo’s strict anchor rule, these are not conventional complexity-theoretic results. The paper does not prove NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, or a comparable classification. Moreover, for the pairwise results, the continuous theorem is structurally close to replacing voter counts by population masses; an opposing reader may call that a straightforward measure-theoretic lift rather than a new computational problem.

I think that objection can be partly answered by insisting on the actual oracle and mass-input problems above. Their resource bounds are now stated in terms of \(m\), \(\tau\), precision, queries, and samples rather than named voters. The continuous formulation also exposes new questions—exact computation from rational \(\mu\), mass perturbation robustness, optimal query allocation, and the complexity of metric-minimax selection—that the finite paper does not formulate. But I would not claim that this paper alone supplies a flagship ChoCo complexity result. It supplies a credible, author-recognisable Class A mirror, with a sharp information-theoretic boundary, rather than a hardness-transfer or continuum-specific-hardness case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper is almost entirely on the wrong side of ChoCo’s active-variable test. Its agents can certainly be repeated ranking types; I would not object that a high-multiplicity election is implausible. The problem is that the paper has already aggregated the population before its computational object begins.

Theorem 3.5 is the clearest example. For a mass vector \(\mu\), define
\[ p_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:a\succ_t b}\mu_t. \]
The \(\DR\) mechanism uses only the signs of \(p_\mu(a,b)-1/2\). Thus the entire distribution \(\mu\) is immediately collapsed to a tournament \(T_\mu\). Two societies with different type masses but the same tournament produce exactly the same computation, queries, transcript, and guarantee. The mass is a passive coefficient, never a decision variable, constraint capacity, or source of a pricing problem.

This is not merely a complaint that the theorem has the same answer after normalization. The proposed continuous input is internally inconsistent. If \(\mu\) is supplied explicitly, each pairwise comparison is just a finite sum over \(T\), after which \(\DR\) performs a candidate-only tournament algorithm. If the majority relation is supplied through an oracle, then \(\mu\) is not computational input at all. In neither formulation does the continuous society create a new algorithmic object. Clearing denominators also gives an exact finite clone instance, and every rational finite profile gives such a \(\mu\).

Proposition 3.1 has the same defect. Its lower bound is really a statement about identifying a candidate from fewer than \(m-1\) edges of a tournament. The continuous society contributes no parameter: the threshold is determined entirely by \(m\), and the lower-bound construction can be realized by finitely many repeated ranking cohorts. A stronger version retaining pairwise margins, query prices, or an optimal adaptive query policy might be worthwhile, but that would be a new active-elicitation problem, not the continuous mirror of Proposition 3.1. The proposition itself gives no result about computing over population masses.

Theorem 5.9 is the proponent’s best anchor, but it still does not establish a ChoCo-style computational mirror. In the paper, the population is already used only through its distribution of rankings, and the sample bound is explicitly independent of \(n\). Replacing a large finite population by a distribution therefore removes no computational dependence that the theorem had. If \(\mu\) is known, sampling is unnecessary; if only samples are available, then \(\mu\) is a hidden sampling source rather than an explicit continuous society. The two access models should not be presented simultaneously.

Moreover, the theorem’s matching argument is indexed by individual voters and by a particular finite matching decomposition \(V_j^0,\ldots,V_j^m\). A type-level version would need to prove that a capacitated or fractional matching over type masses preserves the relevant integral domination structure. That may well be true, but it is a new flow formulation and a new proof obligation, not a direct consequence of Theorem 5.9. If one merely replaces cardinalities by masses, one has not shown that the paper’s matching object survives.

The same objection defeats a stronger attempt based on Theorem 2.2 and the \(\minimax\) mechanism. The finite metric LP contains variables and triangle inequalities for named voters. Two voters with the same ranking can occupy different positions and have different distances to candidates. Collapsing them to one representative per ranking type may change the set of admissible metric witnesses. Retaining arbitrary within-type metric distributions instead produces an infinite-dimensional adversarial problem. Hence neither obvious quotient is a finite \((m,\tau,L)\)-algorithmic mirror without a substantive symmetrization theorem.

So the paper does not offer a convincing Class-A, B, or C continuous-computational problem. It offers valid distributional reinterpretations of query and sampling statements whose computational content is already entirely candidate-side or sample-side. The honest weakness in this negative case is Theorem 5.9: a judge who accepts statistical sample complexity as a computational anchor can reasonably regard its distributional formulation as a worthwhile, author-recognisable extension. But under the stricter ChoCo standard—where mass must carry the computational object rather than merely normalize or generate observations—all three proposed anchors are scientifically thin, and none supports the claim that this paper opens a meaningful continuous-computational landscape.
