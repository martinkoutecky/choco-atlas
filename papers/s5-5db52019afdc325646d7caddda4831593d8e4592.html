The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population-level continuation here, but I cannot give it a qualifying anchor under your rules. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, bounds the expected core size by \(O^*(n^{-1/\max(K,Q)})\) under its imbalance and type-size assumptions, and gives a market sequence attaining the matching lower bound. Theorem 2, also proved here, gives a tighter expected bound when \(K\ge2\), \(Q=1\), and employers outnumber workers. These are asymptotic results about random markets, not complexity classifications or algorithms. Propositions 1 and 2 characterize core payoffs and stability conditions; they do not compute with a formulation. So none is an eligible computational anchor.

The best positive intuition is that the paper already studies the right kind of regime: \(n\) grows while the number of coarse skill and job categories stays fixed. A natural continuum model would treat a worker’s full type as their category plus productivity vector \(\eta\in[0,1]^Q\), and an employer’s as their category plus vector \(\epsilon\in[0,1]^K\). Mass would be distributed over these vectors, matches would have value \(u(k,q)+\eta_q+\epsilon_k\), and the object of interest would be the mass-normalized spread of utilities across core outcomes. That is recognizable as a continuous-population version of this assignment market. The continuum assignment literature cited in the paper supports the plausibility of that model, rather than colliding with it.

But turning that object into an exact or approximation problem would require specifying how the productivity distributions are represented and how the core-width functional is computed. The paper does not pose or solve that computational question. I would therefore treat the continuum model as a promising follow-up, not claim that this paper supplies a named anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither Theorem 1 nor Theorem 2 is a computational result. They bound the expected core size in finite random markets; they give no computational problem, input encoding, algorithm, or complexity classification. Theorem 2 sharpens the same kind of asymptotic bound in a special case. Propositions 1 and 2 characterize core payoffs but likewise do not analyze the complexity of computing them. So the paper supplies no named computational anchor for ChoCo.

The proposed model—worker and employer types augmented with their productivity vectors, distributed over the corresponding trait spaces—is a natural continuum assignment market. But asking for the mass-averaged range of core payoffs in that market is still an analytic question until its input and computational task are specified. For the smooth distributions matching the paper’s assumptions, the paper’s \(C_n\) is a finite-sample statistic driven by random order-statistic gaps, and its stated bounds make the unscaled quantity vanish as \(n\) grows. Taking the continuum limit loses those gaps and the rates that are the paper’s main contribution. Rescaling to retain the rates keeps the sample size and finite-market randomness central; that is a new stochastic-computation question, not a direct computational mirror of the continuum core.

A stronger computational candidate would encode rational finite-support distributions over productivity vectors and ask for the core-payoff width. That gives a legitimate mass-market problem, but it replaces the paper’s atomless random draws with an atomic deterministic model. The resulting question concerns the core of a finite-support assignment market, rather than the expected finite-sample width established by the paper. The cited continuum assignment literature is evidence that continuum models are sensible, not a computational collision or a computational anchor here.

So the case against the paper as a ChoCo computational anchor is strong: its results do not formulate the kind of question the programme charts. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario is weaker. A reader could reasonably see the finite-support core-width problem, or an algorithmic question about expected width from a productivity distribution, as a worthwhile follow-up. I cannot rule those out without manufacturing an objection; they would be new questions built from the paper’s model, rather than computational results the paper itself anchors.
