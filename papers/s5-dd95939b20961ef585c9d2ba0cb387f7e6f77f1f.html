What the mirror covers

The proposed continuum idea concerns the ESS and PS characterizations in Theorem 2; Theorems 3–6 provide related axiomatic characterizations for communication games and coalition structures. None states a computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered theorems are axiomatic characterizations, not results about an algorithm, a decision or optimization problem’s complexity, or a computational formulation used to solve one.

The closest result is Theorem 2, proved here: an efficient extension operator on \(\mathcal F\) is the ESS operator exactly when it satisfies operator-level equal treatment and equality for equal surplus, and every output satisfies \(f\)-IES; on \(\mathcal F^+\), the corresponding characterization uses \(f\)-IER and gives the PS operator. Theorems 3–6 similarly characterize solutions or operators in communication and coalition-structure games. None supplies the kind of computational anchor this task requires.

The most charitable continuous construction would take a nonatomic TU-game with a distribution \(\mu\) over player roles, where a role fully captures how a player affects coalition worths. A benchmark payoff density \(f(t)\) would be extended to an efficient density \(x(t)\), with \(\int x\,d\mu=v(N)\); an ESS-style extension would distribute the residual equally per unit mass. That is a plausible new axiomatic question about continuum-player games. But it needs additional choices about the representation of coalition worths and the meaning of a role, and it does not mirror a computational problem or complexity result in this paper.

So I cannot honestly name a continuous problem per anchor or predict whether one falls in the ChoCo trichotomy. The strongest case for a mirror is a possible axiomatic extension of the paper’s framework; its weakest point is decisive for this programme: the paper’s results do not ask computational questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is decisive: this paper states no computational problem and proves no complexity or algorithmic result. Theorem 2—the proponent’s closest anchor—characterizes when an operator is the ESS or PS operator. Theorems 3–6 extend that axiomatic programme to communication games and coalition structures; they do not add a computational anchor. Their inputs are rules over whole domains of games, and their conclusions are equivalences between axioms and formulas.

The most charitable continuous-population version is coherent: take a distribution over player roles, give coalitions a worth functional on groups of that population, and represent a benchmark allocation as a payoff density. Then ask for an axiomatic characterization of extensions whose integral equals the grand coalition’s worth. But that is the proponent’s own proposed *axiomatic* question, which falls outside ChoCo’s computational scope. To turn it into a computational problem, one would have to choose finite encodings for the worth functional and benchmark rule—choices the paper neither makes nor studies. With evaluable inputs, computing the stated ESS or PS extension uses the benchmark payoffs and their total; any added complexity would come from the chosen encodings, not from a computational result in this paper.

The communication and coalition-structure results do not change that assessment. A continuum of network or coalition roles could motivate further modelling, but the paper gives no computational question about those structures to carry into that model. The negative case is therefore strong within the programme’s remit: a worthwhile axiomatic continuation may exist, but no continuous mirror of this paper’s computational results does. The broader claim that no scenario could motivate any computational question is harder to establish; it is not needed to reject these proposed anchors.
