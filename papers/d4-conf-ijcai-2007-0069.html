The continuous mirror question

Given item categories with per-capita supplies \(b_i\) and bidder types \(t\) with masses \(\mu_t\), bundles \(S_t\), values \(v_t\), and cohort-withdrawal probabilities \(p_t\), compute an initial allocation and contingent repairs maximizing expected social welfare when at most one type cohort withdraws.

The model it lives in

Use type \(t\) for interchangeable single-minded bidders with bundle \(S_t\), value \(v_t\), and withdrawal probability \(p_t\). Let \(x_t\) be initially served mass and \(r_{kt}\) additional mass served if cohort \(k\) withdraws. Maximize \(\sum_t(1-p_t)v_tx_t+\sum_k p_k\sum_{t\ne k}v_tr_{kt}\), subject to \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), \(0\le r_{kt}\le\mu_t-x_t\) for \(t\ne k\), \(\sum_{t:i\in S_t}x_t\le b_i\), and \(\sum_{t\ne k:i\in S_t}(x_t+r_{kt})\le b_i\) for each event \(k\) and item \(i\), with \(\sum_t p_t\le1\).

The objection that survived

Theorem 4's (m+1)-approximation guarantee does not establish a guarantee for correlated type-cohort withdrawals; the proposed mirror changes the failure event from one winning bid to a whole cohort.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4's expected-social-welfare allocation with a single type-cohort withdrawal event. It leaves aside Theorems 1–3 and the corollaries on impossibility and truthfulness.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can solutions for finite high-multiplicity instances be rounded from the mass LP with a bounded welfare loss?
  • Can a truthful mechanism be designed for this cohort-risk model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for a mirror of Theorem 4, which is proved in this paper (with a proof sketch). It says that Algorithm 2 gives an \((m+1)\)-approximation to expected-social-welfare maximization in a combinatorial auction with \(m\) items, single-minded bidders, exogenous withdrawal probabilities, and the possibility of a single winning-bid withdrawal. The underlying optimization chooses an initial allocation \(x_j\) and contingent repair allocations \(r_{kj}\), maximizing
\[ \sum_j (1-p_j)v_jx_j+\sum_k\sum_j p_kv_jr_{kj}, \]
subject to item feasibility in the initial allocation and in each repair scenario. That is an algorithmic anchor about the risk-managed allocation problem itself.

I would name the mirror Mass Risk-Managed CA. An instance has \(m\) item categories, per-capita supplies \(b_i\), and a finite support \(T\) of bidder types. Type \(t\) specifies a desired bundle \(S_t\subseteq [m]\), value \(v_t\) per served bidder, withdrawal probability \(p_t\), and population mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Think of standardized procurement lots: many interchangeable suppliers seek the same bundle, make the same offer, and face the same risk; supply scales with the market. Every person receiving an allocation still gets their whole bundle. A mass such as \(x_t\) means that fraction of type \(t\) is served.

The question is: choose an initial allocation and repair plans that maximize expected social welfare when a type-cohort withdrawal can occur. Let \(x_t\) be the initially served mass of type \(t\), and \(r_{kt}\) the additional mass of type \(t\) served if cohort \(k\) withdraws. With at most one cohort event, \(\sum_k p_k\leq 1\), the objective is
\[ \max\quad \sum_t(1-p_t)v_tx_t+ \sum_k p_k\sum_{t\ne k}v_tr_{kt}. \]
The solution must satisfy \(0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t\), and for each item \(i\),
\[ \sum_{t:\,i\in S_t}x_t\leq b_i. \]
After event \(k\), the failed cohort is unavailable, so the repair must also satisfy
\[ 0\leq r_{kt}\leq\mu_t-x_t\quad(t\ne k),\qquad \sum_{\substack{t\ne k:\\i\in S_t}}(x_t+r_{kt})\leq b_i \quad\text{for every }i. \]
A solution is the maximizing pair \((x,r)\). This is a linear program with \(O(\tau^2)\) variables and polynomially many constraints in \(m\) and support size \(\tau\), so I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable.

The procurement regime makes the high-multiplicity premise concrete: there may be millions of lots or suppliers but only dozens of combinations of bundle, value, and risk. I would include a common-cause reason for the cohort event, such as a shared certification failure or a disruption affecting a supplier class. That makes it a plausible scenario, though it is a co-modification of the paper’s risk model: Theorem 4 concerns one winning bid withdrawing, while this mirror treats a type cohort as the withdrawal unit. The authors could recognize the underlying question—expected-welfare allocation with contingent repair—as theirs, but whether they accept that withdrawal granularity is the key test.

This mirrors the allocation result in Theorem 4, not the paper’s truthfulness results. In particular, it does not claim that critical-value payments or truthfulness-in-expectation carry over to a continuum of agents. The paper’s WDP NP-completeness claim is useful context, but it is an unnumbered, cited background result, so I would not use it as an anchor under the stated rule.

My weakest point is the cohort-shock assumption. If the intended scenario requires literal withdrawal by one bidder while population mass is normalized, that bidder’s mass vanishes in the continuum limit, and the risk-and-repair part of the question may vanish with it. The mirror survives that objection only for settings where a cohort-level withdrawal is meaningful. It also raises useful next questions: can the continuous optimum round to a finite high-multiplicity allocation with a controlled welfare loss, and is there a truthful mechanism for this cohort-risk model?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The weak point in the proposed mirror is the event it makes macroscopic. Theorem 4 concerns the withdrawal of one winning bidder, with conditional probability of multiple withdrawals set to zero. In a high-multiplicity society of \(N\) bidders, one bidder has mass \(1/N\). Under the natural per-capita scaling of supplies and welfare, that withdrawal changes the allocation by \(O(1/N)\). If bidders of the same type are interchangeable, the remaining mass of that type is still available; if a type has only one bidder, its mass disappears in the limit. The theorem’s individual withdrawal and repair therefore lose their substance as population mass grows.

The proposed common-cause scenario keeps the event substantial by making an entire type cohort withdraw. That is a coherent risk-management question, but it changes the failure model: a withdrawal of \(N_t\) bids is not the single-bid withdrawal in Theorem 4. The paper’s broader robust-solution definitions allow sets of bids to break, which makes this extension plausible, but the theorem and approximation guarantee the proponent chose do not cover it. Calling the cohort one bidder would preserve the event only by treating a mass of suppliers as one indivisible bidder, which gives up the high-multiplicity population interpretation.

Independent individual withdrawals offer another route: with many bidders, a positive fraction can fail, leaving a nontrivial fluid-scale repair problem. But that replaces the paper’s single-withdrawal assumption with aggregate attrition. In the continuum limit, the realized failed mass becomes deterministic under the usual independent-risk model, so the question becomes allocation under predictable type-specific supply reductions, rather than repair after a particular winning bid withdraws.

That is the strongest negative case: the paper’s computational anchor attaches risk to an individual contract, while a population continuum either makes that event negligible or requires a different, correlated or aggregate failure model. It does not rule out a worthwhile extension. The cohort-shock formulation is plausible, and the paper’s general discussion of multiple breaks gives its authors a reason to recognize it. So the objection is meaningful but not decisive: it challenges whether that formulation is a mirror of Theorem 4, rather than showing that no valuable continuous mirror of the paper exists.
