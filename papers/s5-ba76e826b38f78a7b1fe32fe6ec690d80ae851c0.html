The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow. The paper has no numbered result classifying a problem as P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. My lead anchor is 命題1 (Proposition 1), proved in the paper using the assignment formulation and results such as Leonard’s cited work. It says that the permit market produces an efficient allocation; that the minimum competitive-equilibrium prices equal the Vickrey payments; and therefore that the market mechanism is strategy-proof. This is a mechanism-design result rather than a complexity theorem. Its computational foothold is the allocation problem \([A\text{-}P]\), which the paper formulates and computes as a network-flow linear program.

A plausible mirror of that allocation is High-Multiplicity Bottleneck Permit Allocation. Take commuters into a CBD, grouped by complete type: origin and their reported value for each arrival window, including any preference or cost parameter the allocation uses. Let \(\mu_t\) be the rational fraction of the commuter population of upstream type \(t\), and let \(\kappa_i\) be the rational permit capacity for arrival window \(i\), both normalized by population. For the current observed traffic, let \(b_{ti}\) be type \(t\)’s reported value for window \(i\), net of the current generalized travel cost. The instance is \((T_U,I,\mu,\kappa,b)\). Choose mass assignments \(z_{ti}\geq 0\) to maximize
\[ \sum_{t\in T_U}\sum_{i\in I} b_{ti}z_{ti} \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{i\in I}z_{ti}=\mu_t\quad(t\in T_U),\qquad \sum_{t\in T_U}z_{ti}\leq\kappa_i\quad(i\in I). \]
A solution is a welfare-maximizing assignment of each type’s mass across time windows; \(z_{ti}/\mu_t\) is the share of type \(t\) assigned to window \(i\). Each commuter still gets one window—the fractional quantities describe how a large cohort is divided.

The regime is a large commuting population with many people in a relatively small number of exact classes: for example, workers sharing an employer’s shift pattern, origin zone, and reimbursement or time-value category. Then the number of commuters is large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct supported types is comparatively small. The interpretation is close to the paper’s own single-bottleneck permit market: its auction assigns time-specific rights to commuters, subject to per-window capacities, using their net values.

This problem is a rational transportation LP with \(O(\tau |I|)\) variables, so it is solvable in polynomial time in \(\tau\), \(|I|\), and the input bit length \(L\). It is a Class A mirror of Proposition 1’s efficient-allocation component. It does not claim that the continuous version preserves the proposition’s strategy-proofness result. That is a separate question: do minimum supporting prices retain an appropriate incentive property when reports and allocations are organized by positive-mass types, rather than individual bidders?

The scope is just the single-round permit allocation in Proposition 1, conditional on the current aggregate traffic costs. The paper’s aggregate day-to-day analysis already uses population shares and continuous utility distributions, so that dynamic convergence story is not a new mirror to claim. The further computational questions are whether the minimum equilibrium prices can be computed for the finite-support mass model, and whether the paper’s coupled traffic dynamics still converge with exact rational type support.

The weakest point is that the paper offers no named complexity result to anchor the mirror, and its allocation problem is already tractable in the finite-bidder model. Also, exact repeated valuation vectors may be uncommon, making the small-\(\tau\) regime restrictive. So this is a plausible and precise continuous version of one computed subproblem, but it does not expose a discrete hardness result whose fate under continuization would be informative.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Proposition 1 is a mechanism-design result, not a complexity result: it establishes efficient allocation and strategy-proof prices. The paper does say how to compute the allocation, but its problem \([A\text{-}P]\) is already a network-flow LP, and the paper notes that total unimodularity gives integral solutions. The proposed mass-allocation LP therefore does not uncover a new computational problem.

The high-multiplicity version is a faithful model of a large commuting population: group commuters by origin and complete value vector, with many commuters per class. But for that model, the type-mass formulation is exactly the aggregation already present in \([A/\mathrm{macro}\text{-}P]\). With a finite-support distribution, its aggregate variables describe the same allocation of each type’s mass across arrival times. Equivalently, multiply rational masses and capacities by a common denominator and recover the original transportation problem; identical commuters can be grouped into type nodes without changing it. This is a collision in the problem formulation, not merely a complaint that its answer is easy.

The proposed follow-up questions do not rescue the anchor. The paper’s dual allocation problem and minimum-price formulation already address price computation, while its aggregate optimization and day-to-day dynamics already study the population-level state. A sharper runtime bound in \(\tau\) and \(L\) would describe a compact encoding of the same flow problem. If one instead tries to preserve Proposition 1’s incentive result, aggregating identical bidders leaves the finite mechanism unchanged; in a nonatomic model, an individual’s effect on prices disappears, while treating a whole positive-mass type as a strategic bidder changes the incentive question.

I would reject this particular mirror as a worthwhile new computational target. But the universal negative is weak: commuter populations with many people per type are entirely plausible, and the paper itself uses aggregate population distributions. Those facts support the high-multiplicity regime. The case against rests on this anchor’s lack of a distinct computational question, not on a general claim that the paper has no sensible continuous-population mirror.
