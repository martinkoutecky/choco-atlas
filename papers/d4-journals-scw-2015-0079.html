The continuous mirror question

Given a fixed set of alternatives, a finite support \(T\) of complete respondent-report types, and binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), let each type specify its paired-comparison outcomes and which pairs were compared, represented by \(A^t\) and \(M^t\). Form \(A_\mu=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t A^t\), \(M_\mu=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t M^t\), the Laplacian \(L_\mu\), and \(s_\mu=A_\mu e\). Assuming the comparison graph of \(M_\mu\) is connected, compute the unique rating \(q\) satisfying \(L_\mu q=s_\mu\) and \(e^\top q=0\), and return its induced ranking.

The model it lives in

Types are complete paired-comparison reports, \(\mu_t\) is the mass of each type, and \(q\) is the mean-zero rating vector. Compute \(q\) by minimizing \(\frac12 q^\top L_\mu q-s_\mu^\top q\) subject to \(e^\top q=0\), then rank alternatives by \(q\).

What the mirror covers

Covers the least-squares rating computation in Theorem 3.1; it leaves the iterative convergence result in Theorem 4.1, other rating methods, and the axiomatic discussion aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a continuous population of people who submit paired comparisons of a fixed set of alternatives. For example, a large survey might ask many people to compare four policies. A type is one person’s complete response pattern across the six possible pairs, including which pairs they were asked and which option they preferred. With strict binary responses there are at most \(3^6=729\) such types. A survey with hundreds of thousands of respondents can therefore have many people but relatively few distinct types. This is a high-multiplicity regime: people with the same response pattern are interchangeable for the calculation the paper performs.

Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of respondents of type \(t\). Each type contributes a results matrix \(A^t\) and a matches matrix \(M^t\); aggregate them as \(A_\mu=\sum_t\mu_t A^t\) and \(M_\mu=\sum_t\mu_t M^t\). The entries of \(M_\mu\) can be fractional average comparison counts per respondent. That extension is compatible with the paper, which explicitly allows real-valued comparison weights. Form the Laplacian \(L_\mu\) from \(M_\mu\), and let \(s_\mu=A_\mu e\). The decision variable is the mean-zero rating vector \(q\), minimizing the least-squares discrepancy between observed pairwise margins and rating differences. Equivalently, minimize
\[ \frac12 q^\top L_\mu q-s_\mu^\top q \quad\text{subject to}\quad e^\top q=0. \]
For a connected comparison graph, the output is the unique exact rating vector, and its induced ordering of the alternatives.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It states that, when the comparison multigraph is connected, the least-squares rating has a unique solution and that solution is
\[ q=L^+s=[L+(1/n)J]^{-1}s, \]
where \(L^+\) is the Moore–Penrose inverse, \(J\) is the all-ones matrix, and \(s\) is the scores vector. This is a named result that gives the computation, though it is not itself phrased as a complexity-class theorem. The paper also says the rating can be calculated with \(O(n^3)\) flops. For the continuous mirror, constructing the aggregate matrices from \(\tau\) types and solving the rational linear system gives exact computation in time polynomial in the number \(n\) of alternatives, the support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). I expect this mirror to be tractable. It compresses repeated respondent data; it does not make the already tractable rating problem newly tractable.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: their model already includes questionnaire rounds, partial comparisons, and multiple comparisons, and the rating depends on the aggregated outcomes and comparison weights. The mirror covers the least-squares rating computation in Theorem 3.1. It does not claim to continuize the alternatives or the ranking outcome, and it does not mirror the paper’s other methods or axiomatic discussion. It also supplies no hardness anchor; the paper’s relevant named results are about calculating the rating, not proving computational hardness.

The weakest point is that the paper often describes rounds, matches, or experiments rather than a population of evaluators. The evaluator scenario is still a credible fit: questionnaire responses are among the paper’s own examples, and a person’s full comparison report is a complete type for this calculation. The continuous model is a faithful aggregate of those reports, but the gain is mainly a high-multiplicity representation of an easy computation.

This mirror also raises follow-up questions: how sensitive is the ranking to shifting a small fraction of mass between report types, and how many respondents of particular types would need to change their reports to reverse a given pairwise order? Those extend the population model toward robustness and control, but they are not results anchored in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor is real: Theorem 3.1 is a named computational result, and the paper says the least-squares rating can be computed in \(O(n^3)\) operations. I would not reject the mirror on the ground that the paper lacks a computational result.

The problem is that the proposed population mirror feeds the paper exactly the aggregate data it already accepts. In a survey with a fixed set of alternatives, let each respondent type \(t\) contribute comparison matrices \(A^t\) and \(M^t\). A society with masses \(\mu_t\) produces
\[ A_\mu=\sum_t \mu_t A^t,\qquad M_\mu=\sum_t \mu_t M^t. \]
The rating then uses \(L_\mu\), the Laplacian of \(M_\mu\), and \(s_\mu=A_\mu e\). The paper allows real-valued comparison weights and treats pairwise results as likelihoods or intensities. For connected \(M_\mu\), Theorem 3.1 already gives
\[ q=[L_\mu+(1/n)J]^{-1}s_\mu. \]
So a population distribution over reports is a valid interpretation of its inputs, but computing the rating from that distribution is already covered by the paper’s method.

This is not a claim that high multiplicity is implausible. A large survey with a bounded set of report types is a sensible regime, and full reports are natural types. The collision is computational: for this rating method, the distribution matters only through the aggregate matrices. From a list of \(\tau\) types, one can form those matrices in \(O(\tau n^2)\) work and apply the paper’s solver. Type-specific schedules or weights can be included in the type and folded into the same sums. No new pricing problem, optimization structure, or complexity question appears in the anchored result.

The suggested robustness and control questions are the strongest possible rescue, but they add a different objective. “How much mass must change to reverse a ranking?” needs a cost for changing reports and a precise intervention model; the paper supplies neither. A Hamming cost on complete reports could make a coherent new problem, and it might be worth studying. It would be a new mass-control problem built on the paper’s rating map, though, not a continuous mirror of Theorem 3.1.

That is the strongest negative case I can make, and its limit is clear: the paper does not explicitly call its fractional comparison weights a voter population. The case for collision rests on the fact that those weights are already the sufficient statistics generated by such a population, and the theorem already computes the resulting rating. I cannot rule out a worthwhile, separately specified control problem.
