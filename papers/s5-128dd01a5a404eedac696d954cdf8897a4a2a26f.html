The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of hub types \(H\), positive rational masses \(\mu_h\) summing to \(1\), and a symmetric metric \(d\) on the depot and hubs, treat each type cohort as one player. For each \(A\subseteq H\), let \(c(A)\) be the minimum depot-tour cost visiting the hubs in \(A\). Decide whether nonnegative per-capita allocation rates \(x_h\) exist such that \(\sum_{h\in H}\mu_hx_h=c(H)\), \(\mu_hx_h\le c(\{h\})\) for every \(h\), and \(\sum_{g\ne h}\mu_gx_g\le c(H\setminus\{h\})\) for every \(h\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over hub types; each positive-mass type acts as one contracting cohort. Costs \(c(A)\) are TSP costs for subsets of hubs, and the decision variables are per-capita rates \(x_h\), equivalently total cohort allocations \(y_h=\mu_hx_h\). The task is feasibility of the semicore constraints.

The objection that survived

The mirror’s feasibility answer is invariant under changes to positive hub masses, so it captures support-level hub games rather than effects of farmer multiplicity.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the semicore feasibility criterion and empty-semicsore examples in Propositions 1 and 5. It leaves Theorem 2’s characterization of symmetric TSG cost functions and the DSS feedback and other allocation methods outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For symmetric metrics, is \(\tau=6\) the minimum support size for an empty-semicsore instance? The paper reports solver unsatisfiability at \(\tau=4,5\) without a formal proof.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I can defend is narrow: a continuous version of the paper’s semicore feasibility problem for pickup hubs. The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity theorem, so I cannot claim it supplies a hardness result that continuation might erase. Its useful anchors are structural and computational instead.

My lead is Proposition 5, proved here. It states that some TSGs with at least four players have empty semicore, and some *symmetric* TSGs with at least six players do too. The asymmetric example uses a distance matrix from Curiel (2013). For the symmetric six-player example, the paper gives \(c(N)=242\); the costs after removing each player are \(216,182,210,196,196,208\). Their marginal contributions are therefore \(26,62,32,46,46,34\), summing to \(244>242\), which certifies that the semicore is empty. The symmetric witness was found with an SMT solver. The paper also reports that its solver finds no symmetric examples with four or five players, while noting this is not a formal proof.

The scenario is a large cooperative network in which many farmers use a small number of shared collection hubs. A type is a hub together with its location and standardized service terms; its mass \(\mu_h\) is the fraction of participating farmers using that hub. It is plausible to have thousands of farmers but only a few dozen hub types. This fits the paper’s pickup setting: the route visits collection locations, and the cost allocation is attached to those stops. For the mirror below, the hubs must be the contracting units: farmers at a hub share a cost allocation and consider leaving together.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Mass Semicore Feasibility for Hub TSGs: the input is a finite set \(H\) of hub types, positive rational masses \(\mu_h\) summing to \(1\), and a symmetric distance matrix satisfying the triangle inequality on \(H\) and a depot \(0\). For any set of hub types \(A\), let \(c(A)\) be the minimum TSP tour cost visiting \(A\) and the depot. Ask whether there are nonnegative per-unit allocation rates \(x_h\) such that

\[ \sum_{h\in H}\mu_hx_h=c(H), \]

\[ \mu_hx_h\le c(\{h\})\quad\text{for each }h, \]

and

\[ \sum_{g\ne h}\mu_gx_g\le c(H\setminus\{h\})\quad\text{for each }h. \]

A solution is a rate vector \(x\); a “no” answer certifies that no such allocation exists. The constraints are the semicore conditions applied to hub cohorts: efficiency, each cohort’s stand-alone cost bound, and the stability condition when that cohort leaves.

Proposition 1, proved here, gives the relevant criterion: for a subadditive cost game, the semicore is nonempty exactly when the sum of the players’ marginal contributions is at most the grand-coalition cost, that is, \(\sum_i[c(N)-c(N\setminus\{i\})]\le c(N)\). It is an exact feasibility criterion, though not a complexity-class result. In the hub model, it tests the analogous type-cohort marginal contributions. Once the needed TSP costs are known, feasibility is straightforward; computing them gives an algorithm fixed-parameter tractable in the number of hub types \(\tau=|H|\), using exact TSP dynamic programming. Thus this mirror removes dependence on the number of farmers when \(\tau\) is moderate, while leaving route optimization over the distinct hubs.

The corresponding Mass Empty-Semicore Witness problem mirrors Proposition 5: for a given \(\tau\), does there exist a symmetric metric on \(\tau\) hub types and a positive rational mass distribution for which the displayed system has no solution? A solution is the metric and masses, together with a certificate such as a marginal-contribution sum exceeding the grand cost. In this model, the paper’s six-hub witness supplies a “yes” instance: any positive masses on those six hub types give the same cohort cost game, and \(244>242\) rules out an allocation. I expect this witness-search problem to be tractable when parameterized by \(\tau\), by enumerating candidate optimal tour orders and checking the resulting finite systems of linear inequalities. That is an inference about the mirror, not a result claimed by the paper. Proposition 5 supplies the six-type witness; it does not establish a complexity classification.

The scope is the semicore feasibility and emptiness results, especially the symmetric TSG result. Theorem 2’s union-of-polyhedra characterization helps explain how the authors could search for symmetric examples with an SMT solver, but it is not itself a population-continuity result. The mirror does not cover the paper’s wider discussion of allocation methods or its DSS proof of concept.

The weak point is the hub-level deviation assumption. In the paper, a player is a location; in the mirror, a type cohort at that location leaves as a block. If individual farmers can leave separately, duplicating a location changes the relevant marginal contributions, and this model no longer mirrors their semicore. There is a further limitation: with costs based on visiting active hubs, changing a positive mass does not change the route cost—only the support does. Mass changes per-unit allocations, but the emptiness condition depends on which hub types are present. So the case is strongest where hubs really are the operational and contractual units; it becomes much weaker if the authors require mass to affect routing or individual farmers’ deviation rights.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The first difficulty is that the paper does not state a computational-complexity result. Proposition 1 characterizes when a finite game’s semicore is nonempty; Proposition 5 gives finite TSG examples with empty semicore; Theorem 2 characterizes symmetric TSG cost functions. The SMT solver is used to find examples, but the paper gives no complexity classification for allocation or witness-finding. One can formulate algorithmic questions around these results, but those would be new questions rather than continuizations of a complexity result in the paper.

The proposed Mass Semicore Feasibility problem also loses its population variable under a direct change of notation. Set \(y_h=\mu_hx_h\). Since every \(\mu_h>0\), this is a bijection, and the displayed constraints become
\[ \sum_h y_h=c(H),\qquad y_h\le c(\{h\}),\qquad \sum_{g\ne h}y_g\le c(H\setminus\{h\}). \]
These are exactly the finite semicore constraints for the TSG whose players are the hubs. The masses can be changed arbitrarily without changing feasibility; they only convert total allocations into rates. The proposed algorithm therefore solves a finite TSP game on \(\tau\) hubs. Its dependence on the number of farmers disappears because the model has made each hub, rather than each farmer, a player.

That distinction breaks the Proposition 5 mirror. The six-hub witness remains a witness for every positive mass assignment, but it is the same finite witness, with no mass-dependent content. If the players are instead the individual farmers, removing one farmer from a hub with other farmers leaves the route unchanged: \(c(N\setminus\{i\})=c(N)\). Their marginal contribution is zero. In an atomless limit, deleting one agent likewise leaves every positive-mass hub occupied. The paper’s empty-semicore certificate, which sums the costs of removing individual players, then loses the contribution that made the six-player inequality strict. Treat departures as whole hub cohorts and the certificate returns, but so does the finite hub game, with masses cancelling as above.

A more population-faithful definition could test deviations by arbitrary positive-mass groups. But that replaces the paper’s singleton and all-but-one stability conditions with a different stability concept; Proposition 1 does not establish its criterion. Alternatively, route costs could depend on quantities picked up, so mass affects capacity, vehicle use, or trip frequency. That may be a worthwhile logistics problem, but it changes the distance-only TSG into a quantity-dependent routing model. The paper’s results about its semicore would no longer be the anchors. Theorem 2 does not repair this gap: its continuous space is the space of cost functions induced by finite distance matrices, not a continuous society.

The strongest exception is the authors’ accepting hub collectives as the actual decision-makers. That gives a plausible finite TSG with useful cost-allocation questions, but the farmers’ multiplicity is outside the game. I cannot rule out a valuable population-aware transportation model that the authors would recognize; it would need additional operational assumptions and new results. On the anchors offered here, though, the mass model either collapses to the finite hub game or changes the problem enough that Proposition 1 and Proposition 5 no longer support it.
