Why no mirror

The participatory-budgeting type distribution is a sensible high-multiplicity population, and the proposed hidden-μ questions preserve the paper's panel-versus-population setting. However, Theorems 3.2–3.4 provide panel-size and information-theoretic guarantees, not a named algorithm, runtime result, computational hardness result, or solver-backed formulation over a supplied continuous society. Thus bit (a) fails.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the participatory-budgeting panel-complexity results in Theorems 3.2–3.4. It leaves the core-fairness theorem, the representativeness lemmas, and all facility-location results untreated.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a continuous high-multiplicity version of the paper’s participatory-budgeting sortition problem. It is substantively a good mirror, although there is an important eligibility caveat at the end: the paper’s named results are panel/sample-complexity theorems, not runtime-complexity theorems such as \(P\), NP-hardness, or W[1]-hardness.

Take a finite set \(\mathcal T\) of complete cost-function types. A type \(t\) is a monotone, \(1\)-Lipschitz cost function \(C_t:X_B\to[0,1]\), where \(X_B=\{x\in[0,1]^m:\sum_jx_j\le B\}\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal T)\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of citizens with type \(t\). Thus a finite population with \(N\mu_t\) identical citizens is an exact high-multiplicity realization.

A panel consists of \(k\) independent draws \(t_1,\ldots,t_k\sim\mu\), the natural limit of uniform sampling without replacement as the population grows. A panel rule \(D_k\) sees only these sampled types and returns \(x=D_k(t_1,\ldots,t_k)\in X_B\). Define \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_tC_t(x)\), \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu=\min_{x\in X_B}\mathrm{SC}_\mu(x)\), and \(\mathrm{PC}_{\mathbf t}(x)=k^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^kC_{t_r}(x)\).

The scenario is plausible: a large municipality may contain millions of residents but only a modest number of recurring preference profiles over projects, perhaps associated with neighbourhoods, demographic cohorts, or standardized benefit-weight vectors. The type is a full preference description, so treating cost functions as type-level data is exactly the high-multiplicity assumption, not an objection to it. The continuous object is \(\mu\), not merely the already-divisible budget allocation.

My lead mirror is the following.

Continuous Multiplicative Panel Impossibility, \(\mathrm{CPB\text{-}Mult}_\infty\). Given \(m,B,k\), decide whether there exists a panel rule \(D_k\), using only the sampled types and not the unknown population distribution, such that for every finite-support rational \(\mu\),

\[ \mathbb E_{\mathbf t\sim\mu^k} [\mathrm{SC}_\mu(D_k(\mathbf t))] \le \rho\,\mathrm{OPT}_\mu \]

for every \(\rho>1\). A solution is either such a universal panel rule or a pair of population distributions witnessing impossibility.

This mirrors Theorem 3.4, proved in the paper. The theorem says that for every \(m\ge2\), every \(k\ge1\), every \(0<B<m\), and every panel decision function \(\widehat x:\mathcal L^k\to X_B\), there is a participatory-budgeting instance such that, for every \(\rho>1\),

\[ \mathbb E[\mathrm{Social\text{-}Cost}(\widehat x(S))] > \rho\cdot\mathrm{Social\text{-}Opt}. \]

The continuous version is not merely suggestive; the proof’s indistinguishability argument survives with finitely many positive-mass types. For example, take \(m=2\), \(B=3/2\), and the three cost types

\[ C_0(x)=\max(1/2-x_1,0)+\max(1/2-x_2,0), \quad C_1(x)=1-x_1, \quad C_2(x)=1-x_2. \]

For any \(0<\alpha<1\), consider

\[ \mu^{(1)}=(1-\alpha)\delta_{C_0}+\alpha\delta_{C_1} \]

and

\[ \mu^{(2)}=(1-\alpha)\delta_{C_0}+\alpha\delta_{C_2}. \]

Both populations have optimum social cost \(0\): use \((1,1/2)\) for \(\mu^{(1)}\) and \((1/2,1)\) for \(\mu^{(2)}\). But with probability \((1-\alpha)^k>0\), the panel consists entirely of \(C_0\)-types in both worlds and therefore produces the same allocation \(y\). Since \(y_1+y_2\le3/2\), at least one of \(y_1<1\) or \(y_2<1\) holds, so one of the two populations incurs strictly positive social cost. Since its optimum is zero, no finite \(k\) can provide a universal multiplicative guarantee.

I would classify this as hardness or impossibility transferred through the high-multiplicity mirror, although not as NP-hardness. Its combinatorics live in the population-information restriction: the panel must infer which positive-mass type is present from samples.

The second mirror uses the paper’s quantitative lower bound.

Continuous Additive Panel-Size, \(\mathrm{CPB\text{-}AdditiveSize}_\infty\). Given \(m\), \(B\), \(\epsilon\), a finite type family, and \(\mu\), determine the least \(k\) for which the panel rule—exactly or approximately minimizing panel cost—guarantees

\[ \mathbb E_{\mathbf t\sim\mu^k} [\mathrm{SC}_\mu(D_k(\mathbf t))] \le \mathrm{OPT}_\mu+\epsilon. \]

The worst-case version asks for the least \(k\) working for every finite-support rational \(\mu\). A solution is a panel-size bound, together with the panel decision rule and its guarantee.

Theorem 3.3, proved here, says that for every \(m\ge2\), \(0<\epsilon<1/64\), and \(B=\lfloor m/2\rfloor\), any panel decision function achieving expected additive error at most \(\epsilon\) on every participatory-budgeting instance must have

\[ k=\Omega\!\left(m(1/\epsilon)^2\right). \]

The lower-bound family is already a continuous type family in the relevant sense. Let \(h=\lfloor m/2\rfloor\), \(w=\lfloor 1/(64\epsilon)\rfloor\), and use the \(2h\) linear types

\[ C_{j,+}(x)=1-x_{2j}, \qquad C_{j,-}(x)=1-x_{2j-1}. \]

For a hidden vector \(z\in\{-1,+1\}^h\), assign masses

\[ \mu_{j,+}=\frac{1+z_j/w}{2h}, \qquad \mu_{j,-}=\frac{1-z_j/w}{2h}. \]

The population optimum differs according to the signs \(z_j\). Any panel rule achieving additive error \(\epsilon\) must recover at least \(3h/4\) of those signs with constant probability. The paper’s Lemma 2.8 then implies \(k=\Omega(hw^2)=\Omega(m/\epsilon^2)\). Thus the lower bound remains even with only \(O(m)\) distinct types and rational masses; it is not an artefact of individually named voters.

The expected outcome is a genuine population-sampling lower bound. It is not a discrete NP-hardness result, but it shows that continuization does not erase the paper’s central quantitative obstruction.

The matching upper-side mirror is:

Continuous Approximate Panel Welfare, \(\mathrm{CPB\text{-}Welfare}_\infty\). Given \((m,B,\mathcal T,\mu,\epsilon,\rho,\tau)\), and a panel rule satisfying

\[ \mathrm{PC}_{\mathbf t}(D_k(\mathbf t)) \le \rho\min_{x\in X_B}\mathrm{PC}_{\mathbf t}(x)+\tau, \]

find a sufficient \(k\) guaranteeing

\[ \mathbb E_{\mathbf t\sim\mu^k} [\mathrm{SC}_\mu(D_k(\mathbf t))] \le \rho\,\mathrm{OPT}_\mu+\tau+\epsilon. \]

Theorem 3.2, proved here, gives

\[ k=O\!\left(\frac{m}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac1\epsilon\right) \]

for constant \(\rho\). It applies to every participatory-budgeting instance with monotone Lipschitz cost functions. The continuous proof is the same uniform-convergence argument over a covering of \(X_B\); rational finite-support societies are exactly the high-multiplicity objects to which the theorem applies.

For rational linear cost types, the realized panel allocation itself is obtained by a linear program, so this restricted mirror has a natural Class A interpretation: polynomial-time action selection together with an explicit sample-size guarantee. The exact computation of the smallest \(k\), however, may require summing over multinomial type-count vectors and is not established by the paper.

The paper’s authors should recognize these questions as theirs: the same panel, the same random-selection model, the same panel-versus-population welfare objective, and the same Lipschitz cost functions. The only substantive change is replacing a list of named citizens by a distribution of indistinguishable citizen types. The paper itself distinguishes its finite without-replacement model from earlier continuum-panel work, including Goel, Goyal, and Munagala; that prior art supports the plausibility of the regime, though it makes the novelty of this mirror weaker.

The weakest point is decisive under a strict ChoCo reading. None of Theorems 3.2–3.4 is a named computational-complexity result in the required sense. They give panel-size upper bounds and information-theoretic impossibility results, not \(P\), NP-hardness, parameterized complexity, or an algorithmic running-time classification. Moreover, the panel must be denied access to \(\mu\); if the continuous society is fully revealed to the decision procedure, the sortition problem largely collapses into direct population optimization.

So my honest verdict is: this is a strong substantive continuous-population mirror, led by Theorem 3.4 and sharpened by Theorems 3.3 and 3.2. It is worth studying as continuous computational social choice if sample/information complexity is admitted. Under the programme’s strict named-computational-anchor gate, however, I would not claim that this paper supplies a fully compliant positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s gate, not at the modelling level. The proponent has identified a legitimate high-multiplicity regime: repeated participatory-budgeting types in a large municipality are perfectly sensible. I therefore cannot honestly argue that no continuous population model exists. I can argue, however, that none of the three anchors yields a worthwhile ChoCo mirror, because all three are sample-complexity statements rather than computational-complexity results.

The basic obstruction is information access. In the paper, the panel rule sees sampled agents, not the population distribution. In the proposed continuum, this means that \(\mu\) is hidden. It is therefore not an input to a computational problem; it is an unknown statistical environment. The question is how many observations are needed to infer enough about \(\mu\), not how to optimize over a society represented by \(\mu\). If \(\mu\) is revealed, the panel restriction disappears: one can directly solve the population welfare problem, for example by an LP for linear cost types. These are two different problems, and neither is the paper’s continuous computational analogue.

Theorem 3.4 does not escape this dilemma. The proponent’s two distributions,
\[ \mu^{(1)}=(1-\alpha)\delta_{C_0}+\alpha\delta_{C_1} \quad\text{and}\quad \mu^{(2)}=(1-\alpha)\delta_{C_0}+\alpha\delta_{C_2}, \]
are a valid high-multiplicity construction. But the impossibility comes entirely from the event that a finite sample contains no rare type, with probability \((1-\alpha)^k\). Nothing computational happens to the continuous society: the rule is simply denied information. The same witness uses only three types and can be realized exactly by finite populations with appropriate multiplicities. Thus the proposed “continuous problem” is an information-theoretic indistinguishability theorem, not an algorithmic problem over a distribution.

A stronger formulation—compute the best distribution-oblivious policy over a finite type alphabet—would be a new statistical decision problem. A formulation in which \(\mu\) is supplied would no longer be a sortition problem. Neither is a continuous-population version of Theorem 3.4 in the ChoCo sense.

Theorem 3.3 has the same defect in quantitative form. Its \(O(m)\) linear types and rational masses are indeed an excellent high-multiplicity realization, so the modelling objection cannot be that the types are implausible. But the lower bound
\[ k=\Omega\!\left(\frac{m}{\epsilon^2}\right) \]
counts observations required to recover hidden signs in \(\mu\); it does not count computational steps required to solve a supplied instance. Once \(\mu\) is known, the hidden vector is already encoded in the input and no sampling lower bound remains. Once \(\mu\) is hidden, there is no continuous society available to an algorithm. The theorem therefore gives a sample-complexity result in a finite statistical experiment, not a complexity landscape for continuous social choice.

Theorem 3.2 is no better as a computational anchor. Its covering argument proves that sufficiently many samples make panel and population costs close uniformly. It neither computes a panel rule nor solves a continuous optimization problem. Indeed, the paper permits an arbitrary decision function over \(\mathcal L^k\), where \(\mathcal L\) is an infinite space of Lipschitz functions with no finite representation. Restricting to linear cost types makes the panel’s allocation computable by an LP, but that LP receives the sampled panel—not the continuous society—and the theorem’s substantive guarantee is still ordinary uniform convergence. Calling this a Class A continuous optimization result confuses “the panel can optimize after observing its sample” with “the population-distribution problem is algorithmically tractable.”

The proponent’s own concession is therefore decisive: none of Theorems 3.2–3.4 asserts a running-time classification, an exact or approximation algorithm for a supplied continuous society, or hardness of such an algorithm. The paper’s central resource is panel size, not computation. Under a broad programme that admits information and sample complexity, the positive case is strong. Under ChoCo’s stated computational-population remit, all three anchors fail the admissibility test.

The honest limitation is important: this is not a proof that no worthwhile continuous treatment could ever be designed. One could build a new problem about computing distributionally robust panel policies, or about optimizing over an explicitly represented type distribution. But those would be new research questions, not mirrors of the paper’s named results. Thus the universal modelling claim is too strong, while the narrower negative verdict—this paper supplies no worthwhile ChoCo computational mirror—is well supported.
