The continuous mirror question

Given jobs \(J\), vertical categories, HR traits, and a finite support \(T\) of complete applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rankings, eligibility, traits, and merit tiers, let each job have rational per-capita capacities and reservation quotas. Find a type-mass assignment \(x\) that is stable under fractional 2SMH choices: HR-position mass is matched to eligible trait mass, selected in merit order with mass split at tied score tiers, remaining capacity is filled by merit, and open-category choices precede VR-category choices. Require \(x\) to Pareto-dominate every other stable assignment, comparing each type’s assigned mass across preference prefixes; determine whether such an assignment can be computed in time polynomial in the input dimensions and bit length.

The model it lives in

Use rational mass \(\mu_t\) for each complete type \(t\), with each type’s mass split among jobs, categories, and unemployment through variables \(x_{t,j,v}\) and \(x_{t,\varnothing}\). Scale job capacities and HR quotas per capita; each infinitesimal applicant receives an integral job-category outcome, while a type’s aggregate mass may split across outcomes. Extend 2SMH to mass choices using fractional trait matching and merit tiers, then run a type-level DA procedure.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifying its complexity. So it has no anchor in the strict sense you asked for. Its strongest computationally adjacent result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: the 2SMH-DA mechanism Pareto dominates every other mechanism satisfying individual rationality, non-wastefulness, maximal accommodation of horizontal-reservation protections, no justified envy, and compliance with vertical-reservation protections. That is a mechanism-selection result, not a complexity theorem. It is nevertheless a good starting point for a continuous mirror because the result is built around computing an allocation with deferred acceptance.

My lead candidate is Individual-Optimal 2SMH Mass Assignment. Its instance has a finite set of jobs \(J\), vertical categories, HR traits, and a finite support \(T\) of complete applicant types. Each type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), a strict ranking of jobs and unemployment, a VR category, an HR-trait set, and a merit score for each job. Each job has per-capita capacities and HR quotas, all given as rationals. The type description includes every attribute the rule uses; applicants sharing a type are indistinguishable to the model.

The decision variable is \(x_{t,j,v}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to job \(j\) through category \(v\), together with \(x_{t,\varnothing}\), the unmatched mass. Each type’s assigned and unmatched mass must sum to \(\mu_t\); assignments must respect eligibility and the scaled capacities. The job’s choice rule is the fractional extension of 2SMH: match mass to HR positions using the trait-matching structure, choose that mass in merit order (splitting mass at a tied score tier when needed), fill remaining capacity by merit, and apply the open-category choice before choices for VR-protected categories.

A solution is an assignment \(x\) that is stable under these fractional 2SMH choices and Pareto dominates every other stable mass assignment. A block exists if positive mass of a type prefers a job to its assigned outcome and, when offered alongside the job’s current assignees, some positive amount of that mass is selected by the job’s fractional 2SMH choice. Since types can receive fractional assignments, Pareto dominance means that for every type and every prefix of its preference ranking, \(x\) assigns at least as much mass within that prefix as any competing stable assignment; some prefix must improve strictly. There is no scalar welfare objective here: the target is the stable assignment that is best for applicants in this type-wise Pareto sense.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable using a type-level deferred-acceptance procedure, with fractional trait matching reducible to flow or the corresponding matroid structure. The actual research question would be whether that procedure has polynomial running time in \(|J|\), \(|T|\), the number of categories and traits, and the input bit length. This is not a theorem of the paper. It is the continuous question suggested by Theorem 2. Restricting masses and capacities to multiples of \(1/N\) gives the discrete high-multiplicity version; arbitrary rational masses allow the fractional aggregate relaxation.

A credible regime would be a large centralized public-sector hiring pool: many applicants, but comparatively few combinations of service rankings, merit bands, VR eligibility, and HR traits. Then mass is the share of applicants in each complete type, and per-capita quotas express the reservation policy at population scale. The paper’s matching-with-reserves setting makes this a natural instance regime; making quotas scale with the population is an in-scope co-modification. The authors would likely recognize the proposed problem as a continuous version of their stable-assignment question, though the type-level averaging and fractional capacities are new.

The weak point is substantial: the paper does not establish that such a small support is plausible for its own applications. If merit scores are individually distinct, or preference rankings vary nearly person by person, encoding those attributes in complete types can make \(|T|\) approach the population size. The mirror is strongest in a setting with shared merit tiers and preference patterns. Also, individual strategy-proofness—the focus of Theorem 3—does not transfer directly to an atomless population, where one person has zero mass. So this case covers the Pareto-dominant stable allocation result of Theorem 2, not the paper’s full strategy-proofness claim. The further questions are whether the fractional DA procedure is polynomial in the stated parameters, how its output relates to finite \(1/N\)-grid markets, and whether a meaningful non-vacuous incentive notion survives that limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central weakness is that the paper gives no numbered result about computational complexity. Theorem 2 selects 2SMH-DA by showing it Pareto dominates other mechanisms that satisfy the axioms; Theorem 3 characterizes it using strategy-proofness. These are mechanism-design results, not complexity classifications. The proposed mirror turns Theorem 2 into a question about computing a continuum version of stability and dominance. That may be interesting, but it is not a computational problem the paper itself studies.

There is also a serious fit problem with the proposed types. The paper gives each person a distinct merit score for each job, and 2SMH uses those exact merit orders: they determine who is chosen to fill horizontal-reservation positions, who fills the remaining positions, and who is rejected to apply elsewhere. A complete type must therefore include preferences, protected-group status, traits, and the person’s merit scores. With distinct scores, a type is effectively one person, so the support size \(\tau\) grows with the population. The proposed mass model loses its hoped-for high-multiplicity compression.

Leaving scores out of the types does not fix this. Two people with the same preferences and traits but different merit ranks can be treated differently by a job’s choice rule, and that can change later applications and rejections. To preserve that information, one must retain a score coordinate or the individual order. An empirical distribution over score vectors then has essentially one atom per person; an atomless score distribution instead requires a new input model and a finite encoding for that distribution. A model with shared merit bands could be formulated, but it replaces the paper’s distinct-score assumption with a different priority model.

That banded model is the strongest version of the proponent’s case: a large hiring pool with few preference, trait, and merit-band combinations could support a meaningful fractional DA question. But its promise depends on changing the paper’s priority structure, and the paper’s own theorems do not establish that such a continuum problem has new computational content. The result might be an elegant extension of its mechanism theory; it might also reduce to running a finite-type DA procedure with flow-based choice rules. Neither possibility is a complexity landscape already anchored in this paper.

So the negative case is strongest against the stated mirror, not airtight against every imaginable reformulation. It rests on a mismatch between the paper’s identity-sensitive merit orders and finite-support mass types, compounded by the lack of a computational result to mirror. A shared-priority-bands version could still be worthwhile if the authors would recognize it as their problem, but it is an additional modeling proposal rather than a direct continuization of Theorem 2.
