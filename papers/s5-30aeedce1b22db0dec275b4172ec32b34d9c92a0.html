The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. The paper does not give a named \(P\)-versus-hardness classification; its strongest computational anchor is an exact formula for the length of the re-stabilization algorithm. Under the assumption that every acceptable pair is stable, take a stable matching \(\mu\), a worker \(w_0\) who is not at her worker-optimal assignment, and the output \(\nu\) of the paper’s algorithm on the reduced profile \(P_\mu\). If \(\{\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_k\}\) is any sequence of preference cycles from \(\mu\) to \(\nu\), then the number of algorithm steps is
\[ |A(P_\mu,w_0)|=|\sigma_k|+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(|\sigma_i|-1). \]
So the result computes the vacancy-chain length from the cycle sizes: it counts every pair in the last cycle and all but one pair in each earlier cycle.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large sectoral labor market with many workers and positions but relatively few complete preference and eligibility types. A worker type could encode qualifications, acceptable employers, and their ranking; a firm type could encode its hiring criteria and ranking of worker types. Members of a type share all the attributes relevant to the matching problem. The masses are population shares, so a cohort of, say, \(0.5\%\) resigning creates a positive vacancy mass. A seven-person committee or a boutique market where everyone’s preferences are unique is outside this regime; there, the number of types would approach the number of agents.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover. Call it Mass Vacancy-Chain Length. An instance gives finite worker-type and firm-type sets \(I,J\), rational masses \(p_i,q_j\) summing to one on each side, strict rankings of the opposite-side types, and a stable mass matching \(x^0\). The entry \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of type \(i\) matched to type \(j\), with row sums \(p_i\) and column sums \(q_j\). A mass matching is stable if there is no type pair \((i,j)\) for which positive worker mass of type \(i\) is assigned to less-preferred firm types and positive firm mass of type \(j\) is assigned to less-preferred worker types. Include the worker-optimal stable mass matching \(x^W\), a pair \((i_0,j_0)\) with \(x^0_{i_0j_0}\ge \rho>0\), and a rational shock mass \(\rho\) of type \(i_0\) that can be assigned to more-preferred firms in \(x^W\). Restrict to instances where every mutually acceptable type pair occurs with positive mass in some stable mass matching, matching Theorem 3’s assumption.

The shock unmatches mass \(\rho\) of type \(i_0\) from \(j_0\), creating the same mass of vacancies at \(j_0\). A legal macro-step moves a positive rational mass \(\delta\) of one worker type from its current firm type \(j'\) to a vacant firm type \(j\) that it prefers; it fills vacancy mass at \(j\) and creates vacancy mass at \(j'\). A step may instead place some of the shocked type-\(i_0\) mass into a vacancy, provided it prefers that firm type to \(j_0\). The decision question is: is there a sequence of at most \(k\) such steps that re-employs all shocked mass at more-preferred firms and ends at a stable matching? A yes-instance’s solution is the ordered list of type transfers, their masses, and the final matching. This asks for the length of a vacancy chain at cohort scale; it does not count individual people or treat the continuum as fractional outcomes.

I would initially expect the fixed-support version to be Class A: with \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) small, the main question is whether the cycle structure behind Theorem 3 descends to type-level flows, with rational masses handled as input data. The paper does not establish that extension, and I would not claim its formula carries over unchanged. The continuous question is to find an appropriate type-level characterization, or a counterexample to one.

The weak point is that the paper counts sequential offers to a named worker, while a mass cascade can split, merge, or move in batches. Its cycle cardinalities concern individual worker-firm pairs; a type-level cycle may erase distinctions that matter to the original process. The authors might accept this as a cohort version of their re-stabilization question, but could reasonably insist that individual waiting time is central. That is why this mirror covers Theorem 3’s chain-length result only. The key follow-up is whether mass transfers admit a canonical cycle decomposition that preserves the chain count.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a real computational anchor: it gives an exact count for the paper’s re-stabilization algorithm, and the paper also reports a polynomial-time bound for computing the relevant cycles. The strongest objection is that the count depends on a one-person, one-vacancy process. At each stage, one firm has one vacancy, one worker is selected, and exactly one worker–firm pair is added. The cycles being counted are cycles over those named agents.

In a normalized continuous society, a named worker has mass zero. Resigning one worker therefore creates no positive vacancy mass. Giving the shock mass \(\rho>0\) keeps it visible, but changes the process: many workers can move at once, and each move can leave partial vacancies and split a cohort. The resulting object is a fluid reallocation, not the sequential wait measured by Theorem 3.

The proposed Mass Vacancy-Chain Length does not yet fix that. Its steps can move any positive rational mass \(\delta\), so the same transfer can be divided into arbitrarily many steps. A rule requiring maximal moves could define a count, but then the count measures saturation events under that added rule. A synchronous process or a continuous-time process could define waiting times, but each needs further assumptions about offers, priorities, and rates. Those may support new computational questions; the paper does not supply a canonical choice that preserves its chain-length question.

There is a further structural warning in the paper itself. A type-level mass matching gives types capacities and allows many members to be matched at once. The paper’s Appendix B.2 shows that, in a many-to-one market, its adapted algorithm can reach stability while the cycle-count formula fails—even when all acceptable pairs are stable. That does not settle what a continuous model could prove. It does show that capacity and batch movement affect the very invariant the mirror would need to count.

One could instead study finite blowups of the distribution and run the original algorithm on individual workers. But a single-worker shock has mass \(1/n\), which vanishes in the limit. A positive-mass shock is a cohort problem, and its sequence of individual offers can depend on within-type priorities that the distribution does not record. Preserving those priorities can require distinguishing agents; suppressing them changes the strict preference structure on which the paper’s cycles rely.

So the negative case is strongest against treating Theorem 3’s vacancy-chain length as a natural continuous-population observable. It is not airtight: a specified fluid rule could yield a coherent cohort-level problem that the authors might recognize. Its weakness is that such a project would need to establish its dynamics and measure of “steps” afresh; the paper provides little reason to expect those choices to inherit its result.
