The continuous mirror question

Input candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a finite set of approval types \(\mathcal T\subseteq 2^C\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Decide whether there is a committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|=k\), such that for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\), every \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|=\ell\), and every submass vector \(\nu\) with \(0\le\nu_t\le\mu_t\), \(\nu_t=0\) whenever \(S\nsubseteq t\), and \(\sum_t\nu_t\ge\ell/k\), the weighted average satisfaction \(\frac{\sum_t\nu_t|W\cap t|}{\sum_t\nu_t}\) is at least \(\ell\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete approval types \(t\in\mathcal T\). A cohesive group is a submass \(\nu\) supported on types approving a common \(\ell\)-candidate set and having total mass at least \(\ell/k\). The decision variable remains a discrete size-\(k\) committee \(W\); its objective is feasibility under every such group's weighted average satisfaction.

The objection that survived

A fractional group may take partial mass from approval types, potentially simplifying the fixed-committee AJR check and leaving most difficulty in committee choice; the proponent acknowledges fractional groups but does not assess this structural effect.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 4.1, the paper's two main committee-existence results. It leaves the oracle-class result in Theorem 2.13 and the fixed-committee verification results in Theorems B.2 and B.3 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of this AJR existence problem for binary-rational masses, in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(k\), and mass bit length \(L\)?
  • Does the paper's Σ₂ᵖ-hardness reduction remain sound when cohesive groups can take fractional amounts of each approval type, or is a different reduction needed?
  • How do continuous answers relate to finite profiles with the same type proportions under replication?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the continuous AJR committee-existence problem. Let \(C\) be \(m\) candidates, let \(k\) be the committee size, and let \(\mathcal T\subseteq 2^C\) be the \(\tau\) approval types present in society. Type \(t\in\mathcal T\) is a complete approval ballot, and its mass \(\mu_t\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\) is its fraction of the population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For a proposed committee \(W\), type \(t\)’s satisfaction is \(|W\cap t|\).

A cohesive group is represented by a submass \(\nu_t\) of each type, where \(0\le\nu_t\le\mu_t\). For level \(\ell\), it is \(\ell\)-cohesive if \(\sum_t\nu_t\ge\ell/k\) and there is a set \(S\subseteq C\), \(|S|=\ell\), such that every type with \(\nu_t>0\) approves every candidate in \(S\). The continuous AJR question is: does there exist a committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|=k\), such that for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and every \(\ell\)-cohesive submass,
\[ \frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\nu_t\,|W\cap t|}{\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\nu_t}\ge \ell? \]
A solution is the committee \(W\); as in the paper, the objective is feasibility, not minimizing a cost. The \(\ell/k\) quota is the finite Hare quota \((\ell n/k)\) expressed as a population fraction. Allowing a type’s mass to be divided is what makes this a population continuum rather than a new outcome model: the committee remains a discrete set of candidates.

This is plausible in a large municipality, membership organization, or campus-wide election for a fixed-size representative body. The many agents are people whose ballots fall into a relatively small collection of recurring approval sets; roughly, \(n\gg\tau\). For this problem, approval is all that distinguishes one voter from another, so agents with the same ballot are genuinely the same type. The paper’s named-person voter list is a finite encoding of those ballots, not an essential part of the fairness question.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper: AJR-E is \(\Sigma_2^p\)-complete. In full, given a finite approval election \((N,C,\mathcal A,k)\), deciding whether there is a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) such that for every \(\ell\) and every voter group \(N'\) with \(|N'|\ge \ell n/k\) and at least \(\ell\) commonly approved candidates, the group’s average satisfaction under \(W\) is at least \(\ell\), is \(\Sigma_2^p\)-complete. This is not just prior hardness being cited: the paper proves membership and gives the hardness reduction. It is my strongest anchor because AJR’s average is already a sum over voters, so its population version becomes a weighted average over mass with a direct interpretation.

My working prediction is Class B: hardness transfers, though Theorem 3.1 does not establish that by itself. Its reduction encodes quantified assignments in committee choices and in approval intersections of large groups; that looks like hardness driven by the alternatives and their combinatorics, rather than by needing a particular number of individual voters. The concrete question for a prover is whether the reduction remains sound when a group may take a fractional amount of each approval type. The paper’s carefully calibrated group sizes and satisfaction deficits make that a credible target for a direct proof.

A second, independently useful anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved in this paper: SJR-E is \(\Theta_2^p\)-complete. Given an approval election, deciding whether there is a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) such that, for every \(\ell\)-cohesive group \(N'\), every voter \(i\in N'\) has satisfaction \(|W\cap A_i|\ge\ell\), is \(\Theta_2^p\)-complete. The paper strengthens prior NP-hardness by Brill et al.; the completeness result here is proved through membership and a reduction from Minimum Vertex Cover Inclusion.

Its continuous mirror, SJR-Exist\(_\infty\), uses the same distribution, quota, and submass definition as above, but requires \(|W\cap t|\ge\ell\) for every type \(t\) with \(\nu_t>0\) in every \(\ell\)-cohesive submass. The question is whether a size-\(k\) committee meeting all those pointwise requirements exists. I again expect Class B, because the anchor’s hardness couples vertex-cover choices with clique certificates through the candidate set. But the continuous version needs its own reduction or a careful robustness argument; the finite theorem cannot simply be relabeled as a result about distributions.

These questions cover the paper’s two main committee-existence results. They do not try to continuize the auxiliary oracle-class theorem or the appendix’s fixed-committee verification problems. Useful follow-up questions are whether the two continuous problems retain their respective completeness levels when parameterized by \(m,\tau,k\), and binary mass length \(L\); whether fractional submasses admit useful separation or optimization formulations; and how their answers relate to the discrete problems on profiles with the same proportions.

The weakest point is that the finite decision problem does not automatically embed with identical answers. A discrete coalition selects whole voters; a continuous coalition can take, for example, half the mass of a ballot type to meet a quota exactly. That can change which groups are cohesive and their average satisfaction. This is a real model change, not a technicality to wave away. It does not defeat the mirror: making the population continuous is the intended move, and the paper’s reductions already use large blocks of voters with repeated approval types. It does mean the complexity predictions above are honest research conjectures until the reductions are checked against fractional submasses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is weak because both anchors are named computational problems proved in the paper. The population regime is also credible: in a large approval election, many voters can share ballots, and a ballot fully captures every distinction these axioms use. Neither voter identity nor a vanishing individual axiom blocks the mirror.

For AJR, the strongest mirror lets a cohesive group take any submass of the approval types that contain some \(\ell\)-candidate set \(S\). Its average requirement then has a clear meaning: among those eligible types, can a submass of at least \(\ell/k\) have average committee satisfaction below \(\ell\)? For a fixed committee and \(S\), the worst such submass can be found by taking types in order of satisfaction. This makes the inner group check a weighted selection problem, while the search over committees and common approval sets retains the paper’s computational content. That may limit how much continuous-optimization machinery the mirror invites, but it does not make the question uninteresting.

The same approval-type model gives a sensible SJR mirror. A violation exists at level \(\ell\) if some positive-mass type \(t\) has satisfaction below \(\ell\), approves an \(\ell\)-candidate set \(S\), and the total mass of types approving \(S\) is at least \(\ell/k\). Requiring satisfaction for every type in the chosen submass preserves the axiom’s pointwise force. In an atomless population, an individual has measure zero; a type with positive mass does not. The type-level condition avoids making the requirement vacuous.

There is a useful high-multiplicity interpretation: a strict violation witnessed by rational masses can be scaled into a finite electorate containing repeated copies of each approval type, with the quota scaled accordingly. Conversely, every finite profile defines a distribution. That correspondence would be a result about behavior under replication, not evidence that the continuous problem is already solved. The paper’s repeated voter blocks support the plausibility of the regime; they do not create a novelty collision.

The best objection I can honestly offer is that fractional groups may make each fixed-committee check cleaner while leaving the main combinatorics in the discrete candidate choices. That could make the mirror’s eventual algorithmic payoff modest. It does not defeat either anchor, and the programme explicitly treats a well-posed mirror with a modest or unchanged answer as worthwhile. I cannot make a convincing universal case against a continuous mirror here; both main anchors survive.
