The continuous mirror question

Given finite household types \(A\), unit types \(J\), an acceptability relation \(E\subseteq A\times J\), rational household and unit masses \(p_a\) and \(q_j\), and feasible initial and target matrices \(x^0\) and \(x^1\), determine whether a finite sequence of positive rational mass swaps transforms \(x^0\) into \(x^1\). Each swap moves equal mass \(\delta\) from \((a,j)\) and \((b,k)\) to \((a,k)\) and \((b,j)\), where \(a\ne b\), \(j\ne k\), the source entries contain at least \(\delta\), and both cross assignments belong to \(E\).

The model it lives in

A complete-communication housing exchange pool with household types \(A\) and standardized unit types \(J\). Each type records the acceptability information used by the problem; rational masses describe how much of each type exists, and unit supplies scale with the population. A state is a feasible mass matrix \(x\), with every household receiving an indivisible unit; a permitted step is a positive-mass batch of mutually acceptable pairwise swaps. The question is reachability between two specified aggregate states.

The objection that survived

The aggregate endpoint forgets which labeled household receives which unit, so profile reachability cannot establish the paper’s exact-target reachability or its Markov-chain irreducibility; the proponent acknowledges this loss but gives no equivalence.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether a finite sequence of permitted mass swaps connects the two matrices, in terms of \(|A|\), \(|J|\), and the input bit length?
  • Under what conditions does aggregate reachability agree with reachability in finite instances whose type masses are integral multiples of \(1/n\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is Continuous Type-Exchange Reachability, anchored by Theorem 3. Theorem 3 is proved in this paper: given agents with dichotomous preferences, items, acceptable initial and target assignments, and exchanges in which two communicating agents swap items that both accept, deciding whether the target is reachable is PSPACE-complete even when every pair of agents can communicate. The proof reduces from the cited PSPACE-complete bipartite perfect-matching reconfiguration problem [7].

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large housing exchange pool: many households and standardized units, with households grouped by the same acceptable unit categories and units grouped by which household types accept them. A citywide exchange platform approximates the complete communication graph. The point is a regime where the number \(n\) of households and units is much larger than the number of types—say, \(n\gg |A|+|J|\)—not a claim that every housing market fits it. A type records everything the model uses: for a household, its acceptable unit types; for a unit, the household types that accept it.

Here is the continuous problem. An instance has finite type sets \(A\) and \(J\), an acceptability relation \(E\subseteq A\times J\), and rational masses \(p_a\) and \(q_j\), each summing to \(1\). It also gives rational initial and target matrices \(x^0,x^1\). A feasible assignment matrix \(x\) has row sums \(p_a\), column sums \(q_j\), and \(x_{aj}=0\) whenever \((a,j)\notin E\). Interpret \(x_{aj}\) as the mass of type-\(a\) households holding type-\(j\) units. Each household still holds one indivisible unit; fractional entries describe population shares, and unit supplies scale with the population.

Ask whether \(x^0\) can reach \(x^1\) through a finite sequence of mass swaps. A step chooses distinct household types \(a,b\), distinct unit types \(j,k\), and a positive rational \(\delta\), with \(x_{aj},x_{bk}\ge\delta\) and \((a,k),(b,j)\in E\). It subtracts \(\delta\) from \(x_{aj}\) and \(x_{bk}\), and adds \(\delta\) to \(x_{ak}\) and \(x_{bj}\), leaving all other entries unchanged. In an atomless population, the two cohorts of mass \(\delta\) can be paired for individual exchanges. A solution is such a finite sequence ending exactly at \(x^1\). The decision problem’s size is measured by \(|A|,|J|\), and the binary encoding length \(L\) of the masses and matrices.

This preserves the paper’s pairwise operation and mutual-acceptance rule, while making the society continuous. It is a genuine high-multiplicity question: large cohorts can be split into arbitrarily small masses, even though no individual receives a fraction of a unit. It also has a clear discrete bridge: any finite assignment induces such a matrix by grouping identical types. But the bridge need not preserve reachability, because the continuous version allows fractional cohort sizes and asks for a type-level target rather than a labeled-agent target.

My tentative prediction is Class A: fractional mass may make reachability describable by finite-dimensional linear or polyhedral structure. That is a hypothesis, not a result; nonnegativity can obstruct a sequence of otherwise valid switch directions, so a prover would need to characterize which feasible switches actually connect the endpoints. I would not claim that Theorem 3’s PSPACE-hardness transfers. Its reduction may rely on many distinct types and indivisible units. The natural further questions are whether reachability has a polynomial-time characterization in \((|A|,|J|,L)\), when the fractional model agrees with its finite high-multiplicity instances, and whether restricted communication graphs alter the answer.

The weakest point is the change in granularity: the paper allows one exchange at a time, while a nontrivial continuous step exchanges cohorts of positive mass; the target is also anonymous within types. That is the price of making a population continuum. I think authors working on assignment reconfiguration could recognize the resulting problem because the same state, acceptability rule, and exchange operation remain visible at type level. My case covers Theorem 3’s complete-communication hardness result only; it does not claim to mirror the tree algorithm or the paper’s other results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a genuine computational anchor: it classifies exact assignment reachability as PSPACE-complete, even with complete communication. The strongest objection is that exactness is doing essential work in the problem’s meaning. An assignment is a bijection \(a:N\to M\), and the target asks whether those particular agents can end with those particular items. The paper’s motivation in random assignment also concerns reachability among assignments, not just among population profiles.

Grouping households and units into types replaces an assignment by a matrix \(x_{aj}\). Different labeled assignments can have the same matrix, so reaching the target matrix need not reach the paper’s target assignment. Refining types to preserve which specific agent must receive which specific item restores that information, but can require a type for each individual; then the population does not have the small support that makes the high-multiplicity model useful.

The proposed cohort-swap model is the best repair. Rational mass trades can be scaled to finite populations and implemented as batches of pairwise exchanges. But this defines reachability between aggregate allocation profiles. In an atomless population, a single individual exchange has zero mass; a finite sequence of such exchanges cannot change the profile. Positive-mass steps therefore represent synchronized batches, and their endpoints still forget which households received which units. The resulting problem may be worth studying on its own, but its answer would not classify the exact reachability question of Theorem 3 or establish irreducibility of the paper’s assignment chain.

The tree algorithm does not supply an easier route around this issue. Its exchange options depend on agents’ locations in the communication tree, while its target is still an exact assignment. Preserving the topology and exact endpoints in types would again retain the identities that aggregation is meant to remove.

This is a substantial negative case, not a proof that no useful variant exists. A large exchange pool with interchangeable unit categories and an aggregate target could make the matrix question natural, and the authors might recognize it as a valuable extension. Its value would need a motivation independent of the paper’s exact assignment theorem. That gap is where this case is weakest.
