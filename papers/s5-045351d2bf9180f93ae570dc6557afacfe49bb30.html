The continuous mirror question

Given a finite preference DAG \(G=(V,A)\) and rational per-capita supplies \(q_v\ge0\) for each category \(v\in V\), find the minimum \(d\in\{0,\ldots,|V|\}\) for which there are masses \(x_B\ge0\) on integral bundles \(B\subseteq V\), with \(\sum_B x_B=1\), \(\sum_{B\ni v}x_B\le q_v\) for every \(v\), and \(x_B=0\) whenever \(\delta_G(B)>d\), where \(\delta_G(B)=|V\setminus\bigcup_{v\in B}\operatorname{succ}[v]|\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population sharing one preference DAG \(G\), with rational per-capita supply \(q_v\) of each item category. Each person receives an indivisible bundle \(B\subseteq V\); decision variables \(x_B\) describe the population mass receiving each bundle, subject to category-supply constraints, and the objective minimizes the maximum category-level dissatisfaction.

The objection that survived

With repeated copies, it is uncertain whether the authors would measure dissatisfaction by the number of categories left undominated rather than by the number of individual objects.

What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

  • For a fixed threshold \(d\) and category prices, can one efficiently find a bundle \(B\subseteq V\) with \(\delta_G(B)\le d\) that maximizes its total price?
  • Can fractional mixing across bundles lower the optimum relative to finite replicated-inventory instances?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Common-Preference Continuum Min-Max Dissatisfaction, anchored on Theorem 5, proved in this paper. Theorem 5 states that for every fixed number of agents \(k\ge3\), CG Min-Max Dissatisfaction is NP-complete, even when the preference DAG has no directed path of length two and no vertex with in-degree greater than two. The authors prove this by reducing from \(k\)-Colorability.

Here is a continuous version that keeps each person’s bundle indivisible. Let \(G=(V,A)\) be a finite preference DAG, and let \(q_v\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\) be the per-capita supply of item category \(v\in V\). Take a unit-mass population whose members all share \(G\). A bundle is a subset \(B\subseteq V\), and its dissatisfaction is
\[ \delta_G(B)=\left|V\setminus\bigcup_{v\in B}\operatorname{succ}[v]\right|. \]
For a threshold \(d\), ask whether there are masses \(x_B\ge0\), one for each bundle with \(\delta_G(B)\le d\), such that
\[ \sum_B x_B=1 \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{B\ni v}x_B\le q_v\ \text{for every }v\in V. \]
A solution is this distribution of population mass over integral bundles. The optimization problem minimizes \(d\), so every agent, apart from a measure-zero set, has dissatisfaction at most \(d\). This is a configuration LP with potentially exponentially many bundle variables.

The regime is a large cohort with one complete agent type: for example, members of a cooperative or public programme allocating repeated copies of item categories under a collectively adopted partial preference order. The category supplies scale with cohort size, while members receive actual indivisible bundles. The paper itself motivates common preference graphs as a consensus valuation, so this preserves a central feature of its model. In high-multiplicity terms, the population size \(N\) can be much larger than the number of distinct preference types, here \(\tau=1\); the rational supplies describe the repeated inventory per person. This co-modification of supply is necessary for a meaningful large-population allocation.

For this mirror, I would predict continuum-specific hardness (Class C), but that is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 5. The theorem’s reduction puts important combinatorics in the preference graph, yet its named agents play discrete roles in the coloring argument. A continuum allocation can mix different integral bundles across the population, so the coloring reduction does not transfer automatically. The mirror’s own difficulty seems likely to lie in pricing the configuration LP: for given item prices, find a bundle with dissatisfaction at most \(d\) and maximum total price. Whether that problem has an efficient algorithm, or is hard, is an immediate question. Others are whether the discrete hardness reduction can be adapted to rule out fractional mixing, and how much the continuum optimum can improve on finite high-multiplicity allocations.

The mirror covers Theorem 5’s general-graph hardness result, including its restricted shallow DAGs; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s other tractability results. Its weakest point is the inventory co-modification: the paper has distinct items with unit supply, whereas this mirror gives each item category repeated copies proportional to population size. That change is allowed by the programme’s scope, but it is substantial. The second vulnerability is that the mirror’s mass distribution may dissolve precisely the discrete coloring structure that makes Theorem 5 hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is the only anchor, and it is a genuine computational result, so the “no named result to mirror” objection does not apply. The direct population limit does degenerate: with \(n\) unique items and \(N\) agents, at most \(n\) agents can receive anything. As \(N\) grows, almost everyone has dissatisfaction \(n\). The paper’s hard instances also include \(k=3\), so they do not themselves describe a large-agent regime.

The strongest rescue is the proponent’s model with per-capita supplies of item categories and a mass distribution over integral bundles. It is coherent, but it changes what a vertex means. In the paper, a vertex is one exclusive object. Treating it as a replenishable category makes copies available to different population fractions; treating every copy as a vertex instead changes dissatisfaction to count copies. The paper’s agents already share one complete preference type, so the new model gains no compression of agent descriptions. Its substantive change is to the goods and their supplies.

That leaves a real gate question: would these authors regard allocation of repeated copies of item categories as their problem? Their discussion of consensus preferences makes the scenario plausible, but their formal model and motivation center on allocating a finite set of heterogeneous objects, each at most once. Theorem 5’s coloring reduction does not settle that question: the continuous configuration LP is a distinct computational problem, and any new pricing hardness or tractability would be a result about that replicated-supply model. The fact that the reduction might dissolve is not itself a reason to reject the mirror.

So the strongest case against is that the faithful limit is trivial, while the nontrivial version depends on a substantive change to the inventory model. I cannot make that case decisive. Because per-capita supply changes are permitted by the programme, and repeated categories are plausible in some allocation settings, the proponent’s mirror may well pass the author-recognition gate. The negative case is weak precisely there; I would flag the mirror as a close modeling judgment rather than argue that no worthwhile scenario exists.
