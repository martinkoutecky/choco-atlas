The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow, and it does not meet the anchor requirement as written: the paper has no named result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another computational complexity class. Its named results concern strategy-proofness. I shouldn’t present one of them as a complexity result.

The closest foothold is Theorem 3.2, stated in the paper but not given a formal proof; the paper supports it with four-team examples. It says that a monotonic group-based qualification system is manipulable if, in at least one group, some team enters the repechage group; the matches counted for each such team depend only on the group ranking; and some repechage team has a proper, nonempty subset of its matches against higher-ranked teams or against lower-ranked teams counted in the repechage score. Under those conditions, the system violates strategy-proofness. This is a result about whether manipulation exists, not the computational difficulty of finding one.

A plausible *extrapolation* from that theorem is Mass Manipulation in Repeated Group Qualifiers. Consider a large circuit of repeated group-stage qualifiers, such as youth or amateur competitions using a small number of recurring formats. A team’s type would include its group role and schedule, performance against each opponent type, effort-reduction costs, and preferences over the three qualification outcomes. The society is the distribution \(\mu\) of teams over these types; in a regime with \(N\) teams and \(\tau\ll N\) types, many teams share a type. Group templates preserve which types meet, and runner-up scores are compared across the circuit. If the rule advances a fraction \(\lambda\) of runners-up, ties at the cutoff can be split proportionally, yielding fractional aggregate outcomes.

The instance would give rational masses over baseline group templates, a finite set of permitted lower-effort changes to match results, their costs, a cost bound \(B\), and a target mass \(\eta\). A solution is a mass transfer \(x_{g,g'}\) from each baseline template \(g\) to an attainable altered template \(g'\), such that the total cost is at most \(B\) and at least \(\eta\) mass of teams strictly improves its allocation. Each allowed change must follow the paper’s manipulation direction: the team exerts lower effort in its matches, letting an opponent score at least as much. The question is whether such a transfer exists. The continuous quantity is the mass of teams and group instances subject to manipulation; outcomes and match rules are adapted to aggregate that mass.

For this fixed-template version, I would tentatively expect Class A: enumerate the finite match-result and repechage-cutoff patterns, then optimize the mass transfers with linear constraints. That is a conjecture about this proposed model, not a consequence of Theorem 3.2. A natural follow-up is whether tractability survives when group sizes, templates, or permitted score changes vary with the input, and whether discrete hardness then transfers.

The scenario gives the positive case its best support: there can be many teams but relatively few complete team types, and the paper’s central concern—whether lowering effort can improve qualification—remains visible. It covers the manipulation phenomenon behind Theorem 3.2, not the paper’s historical classifications or its proposed mechanisms. The weak point is substantial: the paper studies finite teams in a particular tournament, while this mirror aggregates many repeated groups and asks for a *mass and cost* of manipulation. The authors might recognize it as an extension of their question, but it is not a direct continuous version of a computational result they prove. Under the requested anchor test, the paper supplies no qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The main problem is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 3.2 is a structural claim about when a tournament rule admits manipulation; Theorem 3.1 gives sufficient conditions for strategy-proofness. Neither defines an algorithmic input-output problem or establishes a complexity bound. Theorem 3.2 is also supported by examples rather than a formal proof in the paper. It can inspire a computational question, but that question would need to be supplied from elsewhere.

Grant the proponent’s best scenario: many repeated youth or amateur qualifiers, with teams grouped into a small number of recurring types. The proposed team-level mass transfer still misses how the paper’s outcome is determined. A team’s result can change its group ranking, which changes which matches count toward the repechage score; its qualification then depends on the runners-up in every other group. A distribution over isolated group templates does not preserve those dependencies. To preserve them, the type must include the full group and its position in the whole tournament. Repeating such types gives a frequency distribution over tournament instances, rather than a continuous society of teams. The paper itself names Monte Carlo estimates of manipulation probability as future work; that is the closer analogue, and it varies the distribution over result profiles rather than continuizing the population.

There is a further gap between the paper’s model and the proposed optimization. The paper gives no effort-reduction costs, no technology mapping effort to possible score changes, and no distribution of teams’ preferences over qualification outcomes. Adding those data could make a useful aggregate intervention problem. With fixed templates and finite change menus, one could enumerate the states and write a linear program. But that is a new model whose central ingredients are those added costs and distributions; it does not compute the structural property established by Theorem 3.2. If the whole tournament state is compressed enough to create a harder problem, its difficulty comes from the within-tournament interactions the paper studies, not from high multiplicity among interchangeable teams.

I would reject this as a surviving mirror anchor. The honest weakness in the negative case is that a large circuit of repeated qualifiers is conceivable, and a mass-weighted study of manipulation could be worthwhile to tournament designers. I do not see a way to make that study both a continuous-population question and a computational mirror of this paper’s results without adding a different problem.
