What the mirror covers

The proposal covers Thiele’s optimization method, or PAV, but leaves the paper’s other methods, axiomatic theorems, examples, and equivalence results untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous version of Thiele’s optimization method, equivalently Proportional Approval Voting (PAV). It is also the case where the paper’s computational hook is clearest.

The paper’s relevant anchor is the sentence in §4.1 citing [12, Theorem 1]—Aziz et al.’s result, cited from elsewhere rather than proved by Janson—that finding an \(s\)-candidate set maximizing Thiele’s proportional satisfaction is NP-hard. In the paper’s notation, for approval types \(\sigma\subseteq C\), the objective is

\[ F(S)=\sum_{\sigma} v_\sigma f(|\sigma\cap S|), \qquad f(r)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac1j, \]

over all \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|=s\). Thus the anchor is the underlying winner-determination problem: given approval ballots and a committee size, find an \(s\)-candidate committee maximizing total harmonic satisfaction. The paper records this hardness but does not prove it; it points to [12, Theorem 1] and [70, Theorem 3].

My continuous mirror would be:

Continuous PAV/Thiele Winner Determination. An instance consists of a finite candidate set \(C\), an integer \(s\), and a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_\sigma)_{\sigma\subseteq C}\) over approval types, where \(\mu_\sigma\) is the fraction of society whose approval set is exactly \(\sigma\). Output a committee \(S\subseteq C\) with \(|S|=s\) maximizing

\[ F_\mu(S) = \sum_{\sigma\subseteq C} \mu_\sigma \sum_{j=1}^{|\sigma\cap S|}\frac1j. \]

The committee remains integral; only the population is continuous. There is no fractional committee, lottery, or continuized outcome.

This is a natural regime for the paper’s problem. Imagine a large membership organization, university system, or civic platform selecting \(s\) representatives from a common candidate slate. Millions of members may fall into a few hundred recurring approval archetypes—for example, “approves candidates associated with research and sustainability,” or “approves all candidates endorsed by this professional coalition.” The type is the complete approval ballot, and mass is the fraction of members of that type. This is exactly the paper’s own aggregation by ballot types. Its Remarks 2.1 and 2.2 explicitly observe homogeneity and allow ballot weights that are real rather than integral, which strongly supports the interpretation.

I would expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. Given any ordinary PAV instance with counts \(v_\sigma\), set

\[ \mu_\sigma=\frac{v_\sigma}{\sum_\rho v_\rho}. \]

Then \(F_\mu(S)\) is the original objective divided by the constant total electorate size, so the maximizing committees are identical. Consequently, an algorithm for the continuous problem would solve the discrete NP-hard problem. The hardness lives in the candidate-side combinatorics of choosing \(S\), not in the individuality of voters; replacing a large electorate by masses does not dissolve it.

The mirror also generates useful follow-up questions: whether approximation remains hard under rational masses; whether the problem becomes tractable for bounded \(s\), bounded approval-set size, or few types \(\tau\); and whether continuous mass-transfer variants of PAV control or bribery admit LP or convex formulations.

The weakest point is substantial: Janson’s paper already represents elections through ballot-type counts and explicitly permits real weights. A strict reader could therefore say that the model is mathematically already a weighted, high-multiplicity profile, making the mirror close to a restatement. Moreover, the paper contains no numbered theorem of its own asserting NP-hardness; its only hardness hook is the cited [12, Theorem 1]. Still, the computational question above is not merely a restatement of the paper’s axiomatic analysis: it isolates the distribution \(\mu\) as the input object and asks for the complexity of winner determination on that continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor fails before the modelling question is reached: [12, Theorem 1] is not a result of Janson’s paper. Janson merely cites an external NP-hardness theorem in §4.1; he neither states nor proves a numbered computational-complexity result. The paper’s own theorems concern axioms, proportionality, consistency, monotonicity, and equivalences between election rules. Thus this is not a valid computational anchor under the stated brief.

Even if that defect is waived, the proposed mirror is a direct collision with the paper’s own model. For a ballot profile \(v=(v_\sigma)_{\sigma\subseteq C}\), let \(V=\sum_\sigma v_\sigma\) and \(\mu_\sigma=v_\sigma/V\). Then, for every committee \(S\),

\[ F_v(S) = \sum_{\sigma}v_\sigma H_{|\sigma\cap S|} = V\sum_{\sigma}\mu_\sigma H_{|\sigma\cap S|} = V F_\mu(S), \]

where \(H_r=\sum_{j=1}^r 1/j\). Hence the maximizing committees are exactly the same. Conversely, every rational \(\mu\) becomes an ordinary finite election after multiplication by a common denominator. This is not merely hardness transferring between two regimes: it is the same winner-determination problem under normalization.

That conclusion does not misuse high-multiplicity work as prior-art objection. The stronger point is internal to this paper: Remark 2.1 says the methods depend only on proportions, and Remark 2.2 explicitly permits real voter weights. The \(v_\sigma\) already are the population masses. Renaming them \(\mu_\sigma\), and describing them as percentages of a large organization, adds no continuous-population layer.

The best attempted rescue—say, a continuum of demographic or ideological subtypes—also collapses. PAV observes only the approval set \(\sigma\). Any richer population measure is pushed forward to the finite distribution \((\mu_\sigma)_{\sigma\subseteq C}\); latent distinctions that do not alter the approval ballot are irrelevant. Making candidates, committees, or approval intensities continuous would change the election problem rather than continuize its population.

A continuous PAV control or bribery problem could certainly be a worthwhile new ChoCo problem, with mass transfers between approval types. But Janson’s paper does not contain a corresponding control or bribery result, so that would be a new problem inspired by the rule, not a mirror of this paper’s computational contribution. For the anchor actually offered, the negative case is therefore strong: there is no distinct continuous mirror to study, only the paper’s already-weighted profile written in different notation.
