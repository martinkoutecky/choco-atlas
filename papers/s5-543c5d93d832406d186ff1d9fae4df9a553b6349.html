The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of complete agent types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\), utilities \(u_{tj}\), and disagreement utilities \(c_t\), each type-\(t\) agent receives \(x_{tj}\ge 0\) units of good \(j\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le b_j\) and \(v_t=\sum_j u_{tj}x_{tj}\). Is there an exact algorithm polynomial in \(g\), \(|T|\), and the binary input length that decides whether \(v_t>c_t\) for every type, and, when feasible, computes an allocation maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\log(v_t-c_t)\) with supporting prices, or otherwise returns an infeasibility certificate?

The model it lives in

A population distributed over finitely many complete types \(t\), each of mass \(\mu_t\), utility vector \(u_{tj}\), and disagreement utility \(c_t\); each member of a type receives the same bundle \(x_{tj}\), goods have per-capita supplies \(b_j\), and the objective is the mass-weighted Nash product in log form, \(\sum_t\mu_t\log(v_t-c_t)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends Theorem 35's ADNB feasibility and solution computation to type masses; it leaves the paper's broader rational-convex-program questions and other market models aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the ADNB algorithm be generalized to rational type masses with running time polynomial in \(g\), \(|T|\), and the binary input length, without expanding masses into individually listed agents?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror I see is a continuous version of the paper’s ADNB bargaining problem. The paper’s named results give algorithms, not a comparable hardness result, so its best anchor is algorithmic.

The anchor is Theorem 35, proved in this paper. It says that Algorithms 1 and 2 solve both the decision and search versions of ADNB using \(O\!\left(n^4g(\log n+n\log U+\log C+g\log\kappa)\right)\) max-flow computations. Here \(n\) is the number of agents, \(g\) the number of goods, \(U\) the maximum utility coefficient, \(C\) the maximum disagreement utility, and \(1/\kappa\) a lower bound on the initial prices. Thus the paper gives a polynomial algorithm for deciding whether a finite ADNB instance is feasible and, when it is, finding its allocation and prices.

Continuous ADNB with typed agents is the mirror I would put to a prover. An instance has a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), \(g\) divisible goods with rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\), and, for each type \(t\), a utility vector \(u_{tj}\) and disagreement utility \(c_t\). A type completely specifies the parameters relevant to the bargaining problem. The variable \(x_{tj}\ge0\) is the amount of good \(j\) allocated to each member of type \(t\). Write \(v_t=\sum_j u_{tj}x_{tj}\). The instance is feasible if some allocation satisfies \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le b_j\) for every good and \(v_t>c_t\) for every type of positive mass.

The search question is to return an allocation maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\log(v_t-c_t)\), together with supporting prices; if the instance is infeasible, return an infeasibility certificate. A supporting price vector \(p\) satisfies \(p_j\ge0\), \(p_j>0\Rightarrow\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=b_j\), \(p_j\ge u_{tj}/(v_t-c_t)\), and \(x_{tj}>0\Rightarrow p_j=u_{tj}/(v_t-c_t)\). The expected target is an exact algorithm polynomial in \(g,\tau\), and the binary input length.

The regime could be a large population of subscribers or users in a repeated service market, with many people sharing a small set of complete rate-and-service-floor profiles: \(N\) agents with \(N\gg\tau\). Mass records each type’s share of the population. Goods represent service capacity, and supplies scale with population, so \(b_j\) is capacity per person. That scaling is part of the population mirror. The paper’s wireless throughput application makes the scenario recognizable: users have rates across channel states and may have minimum utility requirements. Within each type, equal treatment is natural; averaging allocations for identical agents preserves resource use and weakly improves their aggregate log utility.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. The paper’s Theorem 3, proved here, connects ADNB feasibility and optimality to equilibrium in its flexible-budget market; that gives a concrete route toward extending the paper’s flow-based algorithm to type masses. But Theorem 35 itself does not prove this extension. Its algorithm handles individually listed buyers, whereas the mirror needs rational weighted masses and a weighted version of the balanced-flow argument. The strongest claim is therefore a well-posed, author-recognizable algorithmic question—not that the paper already settles it.

The scope is the ADNB feasibility and allocation results, not every result in the paper. The main weakness is that the paper already has divisible goods and continuous convex programs: the proposed mirror must earn its place specifically through the continuous population and the \(\tau\)-type representation. If weighted masses force complexity to depend badly on their bit lengths or on the smallest mass, the expected tractability could fail. That is the boundary the mirror should test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against this mirror is that, at finite support, it may be a re-encoding of a bargaining problem the paper already knows how to formulate. Theorem 35 treats individually listed agents. If agents with the same utility vector and disagreement utility are grouped into types, the continuous objective \(\sum_t\mu_t\log(v_t-c_t)\) is the weighted Nash objective for representative players. The paper’s Section 12 already identifies nonsymmetric ADNB as a rational convex program and leaves its combinatorial algorithm open. So the proponent’s mirror may amount to asking for a support-sensitive algorithm for that known weighted variant, rather than opening a distinct continuous model.

That is a real limit on the novelty claim, but it does not dispose of the computational question. Clearing denominators turns rational masses into integer multiplicities, which can make the resulting discrete instance much larger than its binary input. Theorem 35’s polynomial dependence on the number of listed agents therefore does not establish a polynomial algorithm in \(g,\tau,L\). And the paper’s Theorem 3 reduction to flexible-budget markets supplies a route to investigate, not a proof that weighted masses preserve the balanced-flow algorithm or its running-time bounds.

The proposed wireless-user scenario also survives the stronger objections. A large population with a small number of complete rate-and-service-floor profiles is plausible; type masses and per-capita capacity are meaningful. Equal treatment within each type preserves the relevant bargaining objective and resource constraints. The divisible goods and convex program do not count against it: the mirror’s object is the population. Nor does the paper already answer the exact weighted algorithmic question. Theorem 35 is a genuine computational result, and the proposed mirror asks a well-posed question about its high-multiplicity regime.

So I cannot make the universal negative case stick. The mirror may be less novel than the proponent suggests because it sits close to the paper’s acknowledged nonsymmetric ADNB problem, but that is not enough to show it is unworthy of the programme. The honest case against is weak precisely where the strongest version of the mirror begins.
