The paper contains no eligible named computational result. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a complexity result. Propositions 1–3 establish implications between fairness and efficiency properties. Section 4 describes an algorithm, but the paper gives no named result classifying its running time or computational complexity.

There is a natural population extension: let types be strict rankings, \(\mu_t\) their population shares, and \(x_{t,a}\) the mass of type \(t\) assigned object \(a\), subject to per-capita supply constraints; compute the allocation by applying PR’s rank-by-rank consumption procedure. That is a plausible extension of the model, but it has no named computational finding here to mirror. And the paper’s fractional assignments are lotteries over outcomes for a finite set of agents; by themselves, they do not make the population continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed type model is sensible: rankings can be types, and object supplies can be scaled per capita. I would not reject it because agents receive fractional shares or because the original paper studies lotteries. But it still supplies no computational result to mirror.

The paper’s Theorem 1 characterizes the PR rule by two axioms. Propositions 1–3 relate efficiency and fairness properties. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. A continuous version of Theorem 1 would remain an axiomatic characterization, which is outside ChoCo’s scope.

The strongest computational rescue is to ask how quickly one can compute PR from rational masses on \(\tau\) rankings and per-capita supplies. That is a well-posed task, and the rank-by-rank procedure can be aggregated over types. But it is a new runtime question about evaluating the paper’s rule, not a continuous counterpart of one of its named findings. The paper gives no complexity result, hard algorithmic problem, or computational boundary for that question to extend.

So the case against a mirror is strong for this paper’s results, but not a proof that no useful project could be built around PR. The high-multiplicity allocation model is credible; a separate study of its computation might be worthwhile. The narrower point is that the paper offers no named computational result for that study to mirror.
