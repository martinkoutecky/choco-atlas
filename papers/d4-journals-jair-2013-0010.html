The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your rule. Its numbered results concern which Groves mechanisms are undominated, not whether a computational problem is in P, hard, or efficiently formulated and solved. So I can’t honestly give an anchor-based positive case.

The closest foothold is Theorem 5.3, proved in this paper: in multi-unit auctions with unit-demand bidders, an anonymous, linear, non-deficit Groves mechanism that is individually undominated must be an OEL redistribution mechanism. Corollary 5.5 completes the characterization within that class: a non-deficit, anonymous, linear Groves mechanism is individually or collectively undominated if and only if it is OEL. These are substantial structural results, but they are not computational-complexity results. Theorem 5.1 also shows that no non-deficit Groves mechanism collectively dominates an OEL mechanism; it does not supply the missing complexity anchor.

The most plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large market of unit-demand buyers whose values come from a small, shared alphabet \(V\). There are \(n\) buyers but only \(\tau=|V|\) valuation types, with \(n\gg\tau\); the supply \(m\) grows with \(n\) so that \(m/n\to\rho\). This is a natural regime for buyers in standardized categories. A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(V\), and the efficient allocation serves the highest-valued mass up to supply \(\rho\), splitting the mass at a tied cutoff if needed.

A concrete, exploratory mirror would be the following Mass-OEL Undominance problem. In addition to \(V\) and \(\rho\), give a rational partition \(I_1,\ldots,I_p\) of the unit interval and rational coefficients \(a=(a_0,\ldots,a_p)\). Let \(Q_\mu\) be the quantile function of \(\mu\), let \(q_\mu=Q_\mu(1-\rho)\) be the supply cutoff, and define a common per-person rebate
\[ b_a(\mu)=a_0+\sum_{j=1}^{p}a_j\int_{I_j}Q_\mu(s)\,ds. \]
Use efficient allocation with continuum VCG payments, then rebate \(b_a(\mu)\) to every agent. Since the population has mass one, non-deficit requires \(b_a(\mu)\leq \rho q_\mu\) for every \(\mu\). The problem is: given \(a\), is this rule non-deficit and collectively undominated among rules of this quantile-linear form? A yes-instance should include a certificate of non-deficit and maximality; if another rule dominates it, return rational coefficients \(a'\) and a distribution \(\mu\) where the improvement is strict. Here dominance compares total utility for every society distribution.

This is a credible *research question adjacent to* Theorem 5.3: finite rules linear in other bidders’ order statistics suggest limits based on integrals of the population quantile function. I would tentatively expect a tractable, perhaps fixed-parameter, classification in \(p+\tau\), since finite support gives a piecewise-linear description of the quantile expressions. That is a conjecture about the proposed problem, not a result of this paper. It covers the multi-unit-auction section only.

The weakest point is also the main modeling risk: in an atomless population, one person’s report does not change \(\mu\). Individual incentive constraints and the finite paper’s leave-one-bidder order statistics therefore lose force in the limit. Restricting the mirror to quantile-linear rebates keeps it close to OEL, but it adds a modeling choice and may make the resulting question a modest asymptotic design problem rather than one the authors would regard as their original problem. The strongest honest positive case is thus a plausible, testable extension of the paper’s auction characterization, not a paper-anchored computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor does not meet ChoCo’s computational criterion. Theorem 5.3 and Corollary 5.5 characterize which anonymous, linear Groves mechanisms are undominated; Theorem 5.1 establishes undominance for the OEL mechanisms. None asserts the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic result whose complexity a mirror could chart. The paper’s other results are also mechanism characterizations, not complexity results.

The auction setting itself is a credible high-multiplicity regime: a large population of buyers with values from a small shared set, and supply growing with the population, makes sense. The negative case should not rest on denying that. Its stronger concern is what happens to the paper’s undominance question under that scaling. With supply fraction \(\rho\) and cutoff value \(q_\mu=Q_\mu(1-\rho)\), continuum VCG collects \(\rho q_\mu\) per unit of population mass. The finite Bailey–Cavallo rebate to buyer \(i\) is \((m/n)[\theta_{-i}]_{m+1}\); as \(m/n\to\rho\), its natural mass limit is a common rebate of \(\rho q_\mu\). Thus the finite-population slack that redistribution rules compete over disappears at the scale used in the proposed mirror: the natural limit returns the full VCG revenue.

That exposes a problem with Mass-OEL Undominance. If its quantile-integral class includes this natural limit, its common rebate sits at the non-deficit boundary for every society. The proposed undominance question then loses the finite OEL characterization’s substantive competition among rebates. If the class excludes the cutoff rebate—quantile integrals need not represent a point evaluation at the supply cutoff—it is no longer a faithful limit of the paper’s canonical OEL mechanism. Either way, the proposed question is a poor computational mirror of Theorem 5.3. The integral-based class also adds a modeling choice not supplied by the theorem.

There is a further mismatch: the proposed decision problem takes a rule description as input and quantifies over all distributions \(\mu\). It does not take a society distribution as input and ask for an intervention or computation on that society, as ChoCo’s central model does. One could instead ask for a richer asymptotic theory of type-dependent redistribution rules. That could be worthwhile mechanism-design research, but it would be a new structural question, not a computational mirror of a named result here.

So the negative case is strongest on the programme’s stated remit: there is no computational result to anchor, and the closest auction candidate loses much of its finite-population content under the natural scaling. It is weaker as a universal claim: the auction regime is sensible, and the authors might recognize a broader continuum redistribution theory as their problem if ChoCo chose to study structural mechanism design alongside computational complexity.
