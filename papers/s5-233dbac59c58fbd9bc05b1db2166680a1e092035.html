What the mirror covers

The proposal covers only Theorem 4.5; it leaves Theorem 4.7's excludable-project approximation mechanism and the implementability characterizations without a mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I see is a mass-weighted public-project mechanism for stakeholder cohorts. It continuizes the population represented by the agents while keeping the paper’s central decision—choosing a public project—and its central constraint—truthful reporting under interdependent values.

Let \(T\) be a finite set of stakeholder roles, with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and let \(\tau=|T|\). A type \(t\) is a complete role description: its members share a signal and the same project-value function, including the same dependence on other types’ signals. For a reported signal profile \(\sigma\in[0,1]^\tau\), project \(j\) gives each member of type \(t\) value \(v_t(j;\sigma)\). The mechanism chooses one of \(m\) projects and charges each type a per-capita payment. Its welfare is \(\sum_t\mu_t v_t(j;\sigma)\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.5, proved in this paper: *There exist linear valuation functions for which no ex-post IC-IR mechanism can perform better than allocating a project at random, that is, no mechanism can guarantee better than a \(1/m\)-approximation to optimal social welfare.* I would mirror that with Mass-CPPP Truthful Approximation: given \(m,\tau\), rational masses \(\mu\), and explicitly represented, nonnegative, monotone linear value functions \(v_t(j;\sigma)\), determine the best worst-case welfare ratio achievable by a universally truthful, individually rational direct mechanism. Such a mechanism maps reported signals to a lottery over projects and per-capita payments. Truthfulness is required for every type \(t\), every true profile \(\sigma\), and every alternative report by \(t\); the ratio is the infimum over \(\sigma\) of expected welfare divided by \(\max_j\sum_t\mu_t v_t(j;\sigma)\). A solution is a mechanism attaining a ratio, or an upper-bound proof ruling one out.

There is an exact embedding of the paper’s lower-bound family. For its \(n\) agents, take \(\tau=n\), set \(\mu_i=1/n\), and set type \(i\)’s per-capita value to \(n\) times agent \(i\)’s value in the paper. The weighted welfare and each type’s incentive comparisons then reproduce the original ones, with payments scaled in the same way. Since the paper’s construction has \(m=n+1\), this mass version inherits the \(1/m\) impossibility on that family. So I expect a hardness-transfer style approximation barrier, rather than a continuum-specific obstacle. This is an approximation impossibility, not an NP-hardness claim.

A plausible regime is many residents represented by a smaller number of municipalities, professional associations, or service-user groups, where each group has a shared information signal and is weighted by the population it represents. Then the number of residents can greatly exceed \(\tau\). The mirror still concerns population mass: it weights welfare by represented population, while the mechanism chooses among the same indivisible projects. I would cover Theorem 4.5 alone, not stretch this into a mirror of the paper’s implementability characterizations or its excludable-project approximation theorem.

The mirror also raises useful follow-up questions: does the \(1/m\) barrier persist when \(\tau\) is fixed or much smaller than \(m\)? What is the best achievable ratio as a function of \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length? What changes when the mechanism may exclude a fraction of the population from a project?

The weak point is the strategic unit. In this model, a cohort is a single reporting actor, even though its welfare weight represents many people. That is plausible when a council or association really reports collectively, but it is not the same as independently strategic, atomless individuals: a single person’s report in a nonatomic population can have no effect on the outcome. Also, the paper’s hard family has \(\tau=m-1\), so it establishes the barrier when the number of roles grows with the agenda, not in the most compelling low-support regime. Those limits make this a credible, bounded mirror—not evidence that Theorem 4.5 already settles the high-multiplicity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.5 is a genuine mechanism-design result, so the objection cannot be that the paper has nothing computational to mirror. The strongest problem for this anchor is that its impossibility relies on a particular strategic unit: an individual whose signal can change while every other agent’s signal stays fixed. The proof’s incentive comparisons, including the \(f\)-single-crossing condition, are made one agent at a time.

In the proposed mass model, that leaves two choices for who reports. If residents report individually and the mechanism uses their aggregate signal distribution, one resident has measure zero. Their report cannot change the chosen project or the aggregate values, so with report-independent payments every allocation is truthful. The individual incentive constraints that drive Theorem 4.5 disappear in the atomless limit.

If a whole cohort reports as one actor, those constraints return, but the strategic players are now the \(\tau\) cohorts. When a cohort’s total utility and payment are both its mass \(\mu_t\) times the per-capita amounts, \(\mu_t\) cancels from its incentive comparisons. The masses affect welfare weights; they do not create high multiplicity in the strategic problem. This is a coherent weighted finite-player mechanism-design model, but the proposed embedding reproduces the paper’s finite game by scaling values and payments. It does not make population mass the object governing truthfulness.

One could instead study incentives against deviations by a positive fraction of the population. That could be a worthwhile question, but it changes the paper’s unilateral ex-post IC model into a form of coalition-proofness, with new assumptions about which groups can coordinate. A public-project setting with councils or associations as the actual reporting actors is also plausible; then the mirror is a weighted mechanism problem among those institutions, rather than among the residents they represent.

So I do not see an airtight universal case against every possible mirror. The negative case is strongest against Theorem 4.5 as a *population continuization*: its central incentive constraint has no natural nontrivial atomless version, and preserving it makes cohorts—not residents—the strategic agents. The institutional-cohort version remains the weak point in this case, because authors could recognize it as a useful model in its own right.
