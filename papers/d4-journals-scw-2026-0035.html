The continuous mirror question

Given \(M\) group types with rational masses \(\alpha_\lambda>0\), \(\sum_\lambda\alpha_\lambda=1\), and rational \(J_0,\bar J\) satisfying \(0\leq\bar J\leq J_0\) and \(J_0+(M-1)\bar J<1\), compute the ray of council weights minimizing \(\mathbb{E}[(S/\sqrt{N}-\sum_\lambda w_\lambda\chi_\lambda)^2]\) as group populations grow with \(N_\lambda/N\to\alpha_\lambda\), under the paper's balanced mean-field coupling.

The model it lives in

The society has \(M\) group types with masses \(\mu_\lambda=\alpha_\lambda\); members cast correlated binary votes under the balanced mean-field coupling. Choose council weights \(w_\lambda\) to minimize the large-population, \(\sqrt{N}\)-normalized expected squared democracy deficit.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 13's weak-coupling balanced friendly-world weights. It leaves aside the hostile and split weak-coupling cases in Theorems 15–16 and the strong-coupling and independent-cluster results in Sections 4–5.

Open questions for a prover

  • For rational positive-semidefinite coupling matrices \(J\) beyond the balanced weak-coupling subclass, can the asymptotically optimal weight set be computed efficiently, especially when the function \(F\) has multiple global minima?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that this paper gives a particularly clean high-multiplicity voting problem: many citizens fall into a small number of exchangeable groups, and the constitutional decision is how much weight to give each group. In an EU-like federation, for example, \(N\) may be in the millions while the number \(M\) of constituencies is only a few dozen. A type is a voter’s group affiliation \(\lambda\), together with the group’s shared voting protocol and coupling profile \(J_{\lambda\nu}\). Members of a type are indistinguishable in everything the model uses; their mass is \(\alpha_\lambda=\lim N_\lambda/N\). The votes remain binary and correlated. The decision variable is the vector of council weights, and the objective is to minimize the expected quadratic democracy deficit between the council vote and the population vote.

My lead anchor is Theorem 13, proved in this paper in Appendix A.6. It treats the balanced model with within-group coupling \(J_0\) and common between-group coupling \(\bar J\), under \(0\leq\bar J\leq J_0\) and the weak-coupling condition \(J_0+(M-1)\bar J<1\). It states that the asymptotically optimal weights have the form \(w_\lambda=D_1\sqrt{\alpha_\lambda}+D_2\eta\), where \(\eta=\sum_\lambda\sqrt{\alpha_\lambda}\), \(\rho=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\bar J}{1-J_0-(M-2)\bar J}\right)\), \(D_1=(1+(M-1)\rho)(1-J_0-(M-1)\bar J)\), and \(D_2=(1+(M-2)\rho)\bar J-\rho(1-J_0)\). The theorem also establishes \(D_1>0\) and \(D_2\geq0\), with \(D_2=0\) exactly when \(\bar J=0\). Thus, the independent-groups square-root rule is recovered when cross-group coupling vanishes; otherwise a shared positive offset appears.

The corresponding continuous computational problem is: given \(M\), positive rational masses \(\alpha_\lambda\) summing to one, rational \(J_0,\bar J\) satisfying those promises, and a requested output precision, compute the optimal council-weight ray for the large-population, \(\sqrt N\)-normalized expected squared democracy deficit. For a finite electorate, take \(S_\lambda=\sum_i X_{\lambda i}\), \(\chi_\lambda=\operatorname{sgn}(S_\lambda)\), and \(S=\sum_\lambda S_\lambda\); the limiting objective compares \(S/\sqrt N\) with \(\sum_\lambda w_\lambda\chi_\lambda\). The answer is the ray given by Theorem 13, using the paper’s convention that common positive rescaling does not change the weighted voting system. Evaluating its square roots and arcsine gives a direct route to an efficient algorithm for this promised subclass. That tractability is an inference from the theorem’s formula, not a complexity classification asserted by the authors.

This mirror preserves the authors’ actual problem: the groups still vote by majority, their votes remain correlated through the mean-field model, and the objective remains the democracy deficit. It does not make outcomes fractional; what becomes continuous is the population composition across groups. The finite-election bridge is also concrete: \(N_\lambda/N\) becomes \(\alpha_\lambda\), while Theorem 13 supplies the large-population answer. It does not, by itself, give a finite-\(N\) error bound or an algorithm for every finite instance.

The scope is deliberately narrow: weak coupling in the balanced friendly-world model. The paper contains no named theorem classifying a problem as in P, NP-hard, or otherwise. Theorem 13 is an analytic optimization result with a computational consequence, rather than a complexity result. Further questions include the complexity of computing weights for arbitrary rational positive-semidefinite coupling matrices, error guarantees for using the limiting weights at finite \(N\), and how nonnegative or integer-weight constraints affect the optimum.

The soft spot is substantial: this paper is already a mean-field, large-population study, and Theorem 13 already solves the particular optimization problem above. So I would not present this as a newly discovered continuous model or as an open problem about that theorem’s subclass. The positive case is that the paper establishes a well-posed population-continuous object and a useful tractable benchmark; a ChoCo contribution would need to chart the computational landscape beyond the cases the paper has already settled.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its numbered results characterize an asymptotic statistical model and solve an optimization problem analytically; they do not classify a computational problem by complexity. Theorem 13 is especially close to a collision: it already takes \(N_\lambda/N\to\alpha_\lambda\) and gives the optimal weights directly as functions of those continuous population shares. For the proposed input, the theorem has already supplied the answer. Computing it to a requested precision amounts to evaluating square roots, an arcsine, and a finite sum.

The best repair is to broaden the anchor beyond the balanced friendly-world case. That still leaves little new to ask in weak coupling: the appendix gives the covariance matrix and sign correlations for general \(J\), then obtains the weights from a finite linear system. Under rational inputs and the paper’s regime conditions, this is matrix inversion and standard-function evaluation, not a new complexity landscape produced by continuization. The proposed finite-election bridge also comes from the paper itself: its analysis already studies the \(N\to\infty\) limit and expresses the results using the limiting shares \(\alpha_\lambda\).

There is a harder residue. For general strong coupling, the paper says the minima of its function \(F\) are not generally known. Finding them, and then computing optimal weights, could support a worthwhile algorithmic study. But that would be computational complexity for an optimization problem already posed in the paper’s mean-field, large-population model. It would not create a new continuous mirror of a discrete computational problem. I would count it as a plausible follow-up to this paper, not as a surviving continuization anchor.

That distinction is also where the negative case is weakest: an algorithmic study of the unresolved strong-coupling case may be valuable to ChoCo. The paper itself, however, has already made the population continuous and solved the proponent’s chosen case.
