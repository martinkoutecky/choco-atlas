The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is narrow: this paper contains no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your rule. There is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Proposition, or Corollary asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity. Sections 4.1 and 4.2 describe COWPEA and COWPEA Lottery and argue for their voting criteria; the conclusion calls the lottery computationally simple. Those claims are not numbered complexity results. I would not present them as anchors.

There is still a plausible continuous-population question near the paper’s central method. Let a type be an approval set \(S\subseteq C\), since COWPEA uses only which candidates a ballot approves. Let \(\mu_S\) be the fraction of the electorate with ballot \(S\), with positive rational mass on each of the \(\tau\) supported types and \(\sum_S\mu_S=1\). A plausible regime is a large approval election with many voters but relatively few distinct ballot types—for example, millions of voters and a much smaller support of repeated approval patterns. That regime depends on the election: if nearly every ballot is unique, the high-multiplicity case is less useful.

The strongest candidate follow-up is: given \(C\) and the binary-encoded masses \((S,\mu_S)\), compute the exact COWPEA weight \(w_c\) for every candidate \(c\), where \(w_c\) is the probability that \(c\) is selected by the paper’s random-priority process. At each step, draw a ballot type with probability \(\mu_S\), intersect the remaining candidates with \(S\), ignore a draw that removes all remaining candidates, and continue until one remains; when the remaining candidates cannot be distinguished, use the paper’s equal-probability tie rule. The solution is the resulting vector \(w\in\mathbb{Q}^{C}\), summing to one. This is a computation of COWPEA’s output, not an optimization over candidate weights.

This is recognizably the same process: a uniform draw from an explicit electorate becomes a draw from its distribution over approval types. The population distribution is the continuous object here; the method’s randomized output is not the reason for calling the mirror continuous. Because rational masses can encode discrete voter multiplicities compactly, the question fits the programme’s high-multiplicity bridge. The paper does not, however, pose or analyze this exact-computation problem. So it is a proposed research question, not a qualifying anchor. If I had to nominate one computational follow-up, this would be the lead.

For that proposed question, approximation by sampling looks tractable: repeated executions can estimate the candidate weights, while exact computation can be expressed over the process’s remaining-candidate states. I cannot responsibly classify the exact problem from this paper; its natural state space can grow exponentially in \(m\), and the paper gives no complexity analysis. Further questions would include the complexity of exact weight computation, the running time of additive approximation in \(m,\tau,L,\epsilon\), and how those compare with the task of actually running COWPEA Lottery without computing the full weight vector.

The mirror covers only the computation associated with COWPEA’s output probabilities. It does not turn the paper’s criterion arguments into computational results, and it does not cover Optimal PAV or the cardinal-ballot conversion.

The weakest point is that the authors emphasize that COWPEA Lottery can be run without calculating the full probabilities. The continuous input may therefore look like a compact representation of a familiar election procedure, rather than a complexity question the paper itself raises. That makes the population mirror plausible as a new problem for ChoCo, but leaves the paper with zero eligible anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper offers no eligible computational result to mirror. Its central claims concern voting criteria and descriptions of methods; the statement that COWPEA Lottery is computationally simple is not a complexity result. The exact-probability problem the proponent proposes is a new follow-up, not an anchor in the paper.

That follow-up is well-posed, and its population model is sensible: approval sets are natural types, and large elections can have many voters but relatively few ballot types. The strongest version would give rational masses \(\mu_S\) and ask for the exact COWPEA outcome probabilities, with complexity measured in \(m,\tau,L\). I would not dismiss it as an invalid continuum or claim that high multiplicity is already prior art against it.

The objection is narrower: COWPEA’s own procedure already samples ballots from their distribution, so grouping identical ballots into types is a direct compression of its input. And the paper says COWPEA Lottery can be run without computing the full probability vector. Exact vector computation could be an interesting algorithmic problem, but it is not needed for the method’s stated use; the paper gives little reason to expect the continuous formulation to unlock a new computational advantage. Computing axiomatic criteria instead would leave the programme’s computational scope.

So the negative case is strong against calling this paper a successful continuous-mirror candidate, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile question exists. The probability-computation question survives as a plausible new project. What the paper does not supply is a named computational result—or enough motivation to make that follow-up a compelling continuization result.
