The continuous mirror question

Given a finite bipartite type graph \(H=(A\cup B,E)\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), integer per-member capacities \(b_t\), nonnegative rational edge values \(w_{uv}\), and nonnegative per-type shares \(q_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t=V(\mu)\), decide whether \(q\) is in the core. For every coalition mass vector \(0\le z\le\mu\), define \(V(z)=\max \sum_{(u,v)\in E}w_{uv}x_{uv}\), over \(x\ge0\) with \(\sum_vx_{uv}\le b_uz_u\) and \(\sum_ux_{uv}\le b_vz_v\); the question is whether \(\sum_tz_tq_t\ge V(z)\) for every such \(z\).

The model it lives in

A society of exchangeable provider and client types, each with mass \(\mu_t\), per-member capacity \(b_t\), and common per-member share \(q_t\), interacting under fixed type-pair values \(w_{uv}\). A coalition chooses masses \(z_t\) and feasible match masses \(x_{uv}\); core membership asks whether the maximum of \(\sum_{uv}w_{uv}x_{uv}-\sum_tq_tz_t\) over \(0\le z\le\mu\) and the matching-capacity constraints is at most zero.

The objection that survived

Generic graph instances, including the hardness reduction, may have almost all vertices in distinct complete types, so this mirror covers only the block-uniform high-multiplicity subclass; the proponent acknowledges this limitation but does not establish how much of Theorem 4's hardness survives there.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Theorem 4; the same typed model also supports Theorem 3's unstable-coalition question and Theorem 9's max-min and min-max fairness objectives. It does not claim analogues of Theorem 1's star characterization and polynomial algorithm, or exact leximin/leximax computation beyond Theorem 9's stated objectives.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Theorem 4. A plausible setting is a large service or transportation consortium: many providers and clients fall into a small number \(\tau\) of role types, each with the same capacity and the same surplus from each permitted counterpart type. A coalition can form the most valuable set of contracts among its members. The population size \(N\) can be much larger than \(\tau\), while \(\tau\) and the type graph describe the distinct roles.

Here is the continuous model. Let \(H=(A\cup B,E)\) be a bipartite graph of types. Type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t\), per-member capacity \(b_t\), and per-member profit share \(q_t\). Each permitted type pair \((u,v)\in E\) can match repeatedly, earning \(w_{uv}\) per unit. A coalition is a vector of masses \(z\) with \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\). Its worth \(V(z)\) is the maximum of \(\sum_{(u,v)\in E}w_{uv}x_{uv}\) over nonnegative match masses satisfying \(\sum_v x_{uv}\le b_u z_u\) and \(\sum_u x_{uv}\le b_v z_v\). The grand-coalition imputation condition is \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t=V(\mu)\); it is in the continuous core exactly when \(\sum_t z_tq_t\ge V(z)\) for every coalition mass vector \(z\).

This is a population limit with a concrete finite bridge. Replicate each role type many times and normalize total worth and profit by population size. For coalitions whose masses are multiples of \(1/N\), the bipartite \(b\)-matching formulation has integral capacities, so its optimum agrees with the corresponding finite replicated game. As \(N\) grows, those possible coalition masses become dense. The society and the seceding coalition have become continuous; the matching objective and the core question remain.

Lead — Theorem 4, proved in this paper. The paper proves that deciding whether a given imputation belongs to the core of an edge-unconstrained \(b\)-matching game on a general bipartite graph is co-NP-complete. Its continuous mirror, Core\(_\infty\) Membership, takes a finite bipartite type graph, rational masses and edge weights, capacities, and a rational per-type imputation \(q\) satisfying the grand-coalition budget. It asks whether \(q\) is in the core; a solution is the exact yes/no answer. For a proposed \(q\), search for a blocking coalition by maximizing \(\sum w_{uv}x_{uv}-\sum_t q_tz_t\), subject to the matching constraints above and \(0\le z\le\mu\). This is one LP, polynomial in the type graph size and input bit length. A positive optimum gives a blocking mass coalition and matching; an optimum at most zero certifies core membership. I expect Class A: the discrete hardness comes from choosing indivisible coalitions, while the continuous question admits direct optimization.

Theorem 3, proved in this paper, is a second useful anchor. It states that, given a \(b\)-matching game on a star graph and a profit share, deciding whether any unstable coalition exists is NP-complete. Its mirror, Star Mass-Blocking, uses the same continuous model restricted to a star-shaped type graph, with an arbitrary nonnegative per-type profit share \(q\); it asks whether some \(z\) has \(V(z)>\sum_tz_tq_t\). A solution is either such a coalition mass vector with a matching that witnesses the excess, or a certificate that none exists. The same LP decides it, so I also expect Class A. This is a separate question from Theorem 4 because its input need not be an imputation. It also makes particularly clear how the knapsack-style choice of leaves in the discrete reduction gives way to fractional coalition masses.

Theorem 9, also proved here, supplies a fairness anchor. It says that finding a core imputation that maximizes the minimum profit share is NP-hard, and that finding one that minimizes the maximum profit share is NP-hard. The continuous question, Fair Core\(_\infty\), takes the type graph and population masses and asks for a core per-type allocation \(q\) maximizing \(\min_t q_t\), or minimizing \(\max_t q_t\); the solution is an optimal \(q\) and its objective value. For indistinguishable members of a type, assigning the same share is natural, and symmetry lets these max-min and min-max objectives be optimized with a type-uniform allocation. Core membership has the LP separation procedure above, so the ellipsoid method can optimize either fairness objective in polynomial time. I expect Class A here as well. The continuous payoff vector remains an allocation among agents; the continuization is in the population and coalitions.

The authors have a good reason to recognize these as their questions. The paper itself identifies non-simple \(b\)-matching games with transportation games, and the worth of a coalition is still the best \(b\)-matching among its members. The cited finite transportation-game and Owen-set work is evidence that this game structure is natural. Its matching variables may be fractional in an LP, but that fact alone does not make the society continuous; the mirror above makes the role masses and possible seceding coalitions continuous too. I am mirroring Theorems 3, 4, and 9, not claiming a mirror for every result in the paper.

The weakest point is the type regime. The paper’s hardness reductions distinguish vertices by their weights, capacities, and assigned shares; in some instances the number of distinct profiles may be close to the number of agents. The mirror has its clearest population interpretation when many agents share each complete role profile, and its fractional coalitions can block an allocation that passes every finite coalition test. So this is not a claim that the continuous answer preserves each discrete instance’s yes/no answer. It is a claim that the same core and matching problem has a sensible high-multiplicity regime, where the new computational question is whether the discrete hardness survives when coalition membership is measured in mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection applies to all three anchors: in this paper, an agent is a vertex in a compatibility graph. Its full type must preserve its capacity, profit share, and compatibility and weight on every incident edge. On a generic instance, that can make almost every vertex a different type, leaving no substantial population to continuize. Replicating vertices as types instead imposes block-uniform compatibility: agents of a given pair of types can interact under the same rules. That is a real restriction on the paper’s graph model.

Theorem 3 makes this limitation especially visible. Its knapsack reduction encodes separate weights, values, capacities, and shares on the leaves, so the reduction’s leaves are generally distinct types. Grouping repeated leaves turns the discrete selection problem into a mass-selection problem. But that change alone is not a reason to reject the mirror: a market with many providers in a few capacity and surplus classes could have exactly that structure.

Theorem 4 has the same difficulty on arbitrary bipartite graphs. Its proposed mass model is a genuine continuous-population version when interactions are uniform within type pairs, and its blocking-coalition question has an LP formulation. The graph restriction narrows the regime in which this mirrors the paper; it does not make the question incoherent. Theorem 9’s fairness question also survives that restriction: when many agents share each role type, a common per-agent share \(q_t\) is a sensible object, and the minimum or maximum share across types captures the corresponding per-agent objective.

So the case against is limited to reach: these mirrors cover structured, exchangeable instances rather than the paper’s full range of individually specified graphs. That does not establish that no plausible high-multiplicity scenario exists, and the paper’s connection to transportation games supports one. I can’t honestly defeat the stronger versions of the Theorem 4 or Theorem 3 mirrors. The negative case is weak; on the material here, the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists is not supported.
