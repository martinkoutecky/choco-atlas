The continuous mirror question

Given \(C\), target \(w\), deletion budget \(\ell\), and an explicitly represented rational distribution \(\mu\) over types \(\theta=(\succ,A)\), where \(A\) is a nonempty proper prefix of \(\succ\), choose \(D\subseteq C\setminus\{w\}\) with \(|D|\le\ell\); restrict each type to \(C\setminus D\), apply the paper's rewrite rule, and decide whether \(w\) is the unique winner of the resulting mass-weighted approval election.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity SP-AV society with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) on complete ranking/approval-prefix types; the decision variable is the candidate-deletion set \(D\), and feasibility means that \(w\) uniquely wins after restriction and approval rewriting.

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Theorem 3.10 and its candidate-deletion hardness, while Theorem 3.11 supports fractional voter-control variants; partition control and the remaining auxiliary results are left untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity and parameterized complexity of this candidate-deletion problem as functions of \(|C|\), \(\ell\), and the type-support size?
  • Can fractional voter addition or deletion be rounded to finite electorates with useful additive or multiplicative guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a pair of complementary mirrors: one in which the discrete hardness dissolves because the controlled population becomes divisible, and one in which hardness survives because it is really carried by candidate selection.

The natural high-multiplicity type for SP-AV is a pair \(\theta=(\succ,A)\), where \(\succ\) is a complete ranking of the fixed candidate set \(C\), and \(A\) is a nonempty proper prefix of that ranking. Two agents have the same type exactly when they have the same preferences and approve exactly the same prefix. A continuous society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over such types. This is plausible for a large software-agent election, recommender platform, or institutional policy vote in which millions of agents are generated from a relatively small number of role templates. The paper itself motivates its complete-information model partly through large-scale software-agent environments.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.11, proved in this paper, although its proof sketches adapt constructions and algorithms from Hemaspaandra, Hemaspaandra, and Rothe. The theorem states that SP-AV is resistant to constructive control by adding voters and by deleting voters, and vulnerable to destructive control by adding voters and by deleting voters. Here “resistant” means susceptible but NP-hard, while “vulnerable” means susceptible and solvable in polynomial time. The constructive hardness portions use reductions from Exact Cover by Three-Sets.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous SP-AV Mass-Addition Control. An instance consists of:

  • a candidate set \(C\), a distinguished candidate \(c^\star\), and a base distribution \(\mu\) over SP-AV types;
  • an available-pool distribution \(\rho\), where \(\rho_\theta\) is the available mass of type \(\theta\);
  • a rational mass budget \(\lambda\).

The chair chooses \(x_\theta\) satisfying \(0\le x_\theta\le\rho_\theta\) and \(\sum_\theta x_\theta\le\lambda\). The resulting society has unnormalised type masses \(\mu_\theta+x_\theta\); normalising them does not affect the winner. The question is whether \(c^\star\) can be made the unique SP-AV winner.

Precisely, the solution is a vector \(x\) such that, for every \(d\neq c^\star\), \(\sum_\theta(\mu_\theta+x_\theta)\mathbf 1[c^\star\in A_\theta]>\sum_\theta(\mu_\theta+x_\theta)\mathbf 1[d\in A_\theta]\).

This is a faithful high-multiplicity version of adding voters. In the finite problem, one chooses a \(0\)-\(1\) subset of available named voters. In the mirror, one chooses how much of each repeated type-cohort to recruit. The preference rankings remain part of the type, even though with a fixed candidate set the winner calculation uses only the approval prefix.

I expect this mirror to be in Class A. Introduce a margin variable \(\delta\), maximise \(\delta\), and impose the linear constraints that every \(c^\star\)-versus-\(d\) approval margin is at least \(\delta\), together with the bounds on \(x\) and the budget constraint. The optimum is positive exactly when constructive control succeeds. Thus the continuous problem is solvable by linear programming in the number of represented types, candidates, and input bits.

This gives a meaningful continuization of the paper’s hardness result: the X3C obstruction in Theorem 3.11 is tied to selecting indivisible voters. Once a large cohort can be recruited fractionally, the exact-cover choice becomes a mass-allocation problem. The resulting LP, its integrality gap, and rounding bounds for finite electorates would be natural ChoCo questions. The paper’s other Theorem 3.11 cases also suggest continuous deletion, destructive control, and approximation variants, but the constructive-addition mirror is the cleanest lead.

A second, stronger hardness anchor is Theorem 3.10, proved here by a reduction from Hitting Set. It states that SP-AV is resistant to constructive control by deleting candidates. More concretely, from a Hitting Set instance \((B,\mathcal S,k)\), the paper constructs an election with candidates \(B\cup\{w\}\) such that \(w\) can be made the unique winner by deleting at most \(m-k\) candidates if and only if \(\mathcal S\) has a hitting set of size at most \(k\).

Its continuous counterpart is Continuous SP-AV Candidate-Deletion Control. The input is a candidate set \(C\), a target \(w\), a deletion limit \(\ell\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over SP-AV types. The chair chooses \(D\subseteq C\setminus\{w\}\) with \(|D|\le\ell\). Each type is restricted to \(C-D\); its approval prefix is then updated exactly according to the paper’s AV Strategy Rewrite Rule. If the restricted approval set is empty or contains every remaining candidate, it is rewritten to approve the top remaining candidate and disapprove the bottom remaining candidate. The question is whether \(w\) is the unique winner under the resulting approval masses.

I expect this mirror to remain NP-hard, hence Class B. Every finite election is embedded by setting \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/N\), where \(n_\theta\) is the number of voters of type \(\theta\). Candidate deletion and the rewrite rule then produce exactly the same approval comparisons, up to the common factor \(N\). The reduction in Theorem 3.10 uses only polynomially many type classes—those associated with the sets \(S_i\), the elements \(b_j\), and a few aggregate ballot forms—so its election becomes a compact rational distribution. Replicating every cohort arbitrarily many times makes the high-multiplicity interpretation literal without changing the answer. Here the combinatorics live in the candidate agenda and the hitting-set choice, not in the number of individually named voters.

These two mirrors cover only voter addition and constructive candidate deletion; they do not claim to continuize all 22 control variants. In particular, I would not confidently claim that Theorem 3.12’s voter-partition hardness survives unchanged. Its continuous version would split each type mass between two subelections, and the fractional split might destroy the exact-cover mechanism. That is a worthwhile open question rather than a safe transfer claim.

The weakest point is the lead mirror’s fractional recruitment assumption. In a small real election, adding \(0.37\) of a named voter is meaningless. The case becomes convincing only in the explicit large-cohort regime: a chair recruits a percentage of a large population of interchangeable agents of each type. The paper’s own repeated-voter representation and software-agent motivation make that regime plausible. The candidate-deletion mirror is less vulnerable: it leaves the procedural action entirely discrete and continuizes only the population, exactly as the ChoCo programme requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strong against the proposed voter-control anchor, but it does not defeat the candidate-control anchor. I therefore cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror.

Theorem 3.11 is a weak anchor for ChoCo. In adding or deleting voters, the candidate set is fixed and the rewrite rule is never invoked; the paper explicitly notes that the approval lines do not move. Consequently, SP-AV’s rankings are computationally irrelevant. A type \((\succ,A)\) can be quotiented down to its approval set \(A\), and the proposed mass-addition problem becomes ordinary fractional approval-score allocation:

\[ \max \delta \]

subject to

\[ \sum_\theta (\mu_\theta+x_\theta) \bigl(\mathbf 1[c^\star\in A_\theta]-\mathbf 1[d\in A_\theta]\bigr) \geq \delta \]

for every \(d\neq c^\star\), together with the capacity and budget constraints.

That is a legitimate high-multiplicity relaxation, but it is almost entirely generic. It does not use sincerity, rankings, admissibility, or the SP-AV rewrite rule. With explicitly listed support it is an immediate LP formulation of fractional approval control. With an implicitly represented distribution over the \(m!(m-1)\) ranking-prefix types, the proponent has not specified the representation of the available mass or supplied a pricing algorithm; the LP may simply have exponentially many variables. Thus the mirror is either a routine convexification of subset selection or under-specified at precisely the point where the ChoCo programme requires computational care. Adding type-dependent prices or quotas would remain an LP in the explicit-support model, but would still be a generic weighted approval problem rather than a substantive SP-AV result.

That criticism also applies to continuous voter deletion and to the destructive variants. They may be mathematically well-posed, but they are not strong mirrors of this paper’s distinctive content.

Theorem 3.10 is different. Its candidate-deletion mirror survives the negative case. Let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution over complete SP-AV types on \(C\). For a deleted set \(D\), restrict every ranking to \(C-D\), apply the paper’s rewrite rule to the approval prefix, aggregate the resulting masses, and ask whether \(w\) is the unique winner. This continuizes the population while leaving candidate control discrete, exactly as the programme permits.

The high-multiplicity interpretation is especially natural here. The construction in Theorem 3.10 already contains large groups of voters with identical relevant descriptions: \(2(k+1)\) voters for each \(S_i\), pairs associated with each \(b_j\), and aggregate voter blocks. Replacing those blocks by rational masses preserves every approval score after every candidate deletion, up to a common scaling factor. Hence the same Hitting Set instance reduces to the continuous problem with polynomially many types and rational masses.

This is not an objection that “the hardness remains.” It is a meaningful Class B result: it identifies a problem where population continuization does not remove the candidate-selection obstruction. The continuous mirror tests whether SP-AV’s resistance is genuinely independent of named voters, and the answer is yes. The fact that the combinatorics live in candidate deletion makes the mirror stronger, not weaker, under the ChoCo trichotomy.

So the best negative conclusion is limited: Theorem 3.11 does not provide a compelling SP-AV-specific mirror, because voter control discards the rule’s preference component and fractionalizes a generic approval-selection problem. But Theorem 3.10 supplies a sensible, natural, and computationally nontrivial continuous-population problem. I cannot honestly defeat that anchor; the paper therefore does have at least one worthwhile continuous mirror.
