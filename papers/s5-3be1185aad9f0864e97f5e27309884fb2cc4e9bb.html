The continuous mirror question

Given finite-support distributions over application types \(i\) and operator types \(j\), population sizes \(N_A,N_O\), declarations \(d_i,r_i,p_i\) and \(q_j,s_j,\ell_j\), and a threshold \(T\), decide whether two chains can reach total throughput at least \(T\). Choose masses \(x_{ic},y_{jc}\ge 0\) for \(c\in\{1,2\}\), subject to \(\sum_c x_{ic}\le\mu_i^A\) and \(\sum_c y_{jc}\le\mu_j^O\). Set \(D_c=N_A\sum_i d_i x_{ic}\), \(S_c=N_O\sum_j s_j y_{jc}\), \(R_c=\max_{i:x_{ic}>0}r_i\), and \(Q_c=\min_{j:y_{jc}>0}q_j\); require \(S_c\ge R_c\), and choose \(p_c\) between the maximum assigned operator price floor and minimum assigned application price cap. A chain lacking either role contributes zero; otherwise its throughput is \(p_c\min(D_c,Q_c)\). Is the sum across the two chains at least \(T\)?

The model it lives in

Use finite-support distributions of complete application and operator types, with masses \(\mu_i^A,\mu_j^O\). Decision variables \(x_{ic},y_{jc}\) allocate type mass across two chains; demand and stake scale by \(N_A,N_O\), while each chain’s supply remains the minimum \(q_j\) among operator types present. Maximize \(\sum_c p_c\min(D_c,Q_c)\) subject to the paper’s stake and price-feasibility conditions, and ask whether the maximum reaches \(T\).

The objection that survived

With two fixed chains, each chain’s supply remains capped by the capacity of its least-capable assigned operator, regardless of assigned operator mass, while demand scales with \(N_A\); the mirror therefore describes a fixed-capacity platform as its population grows.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s core, system-only threshold decision problem. It does not cover the paper’s full weighted objective, modular extensions, fairness or incentive claims; the simulations provide no separate guaranteed computational result.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the answer change when the number of chains is part of the input?

The case FOR (proponent)

Proposition 1 is a strong anchor for a continuous mirror because it asks a computational question about the population of applications: can their demand be assigned to chains to reach a throughput target? A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large managed execution platform with many independently assignable application deployments and validator operators. Repeated service tiers give applications the same declared demand, stake requirement, and price cap; standardized operator packages give operators the same capacity, stake, and minimum price. The regime is \(N_A,N_O\gg\tau\), where \(N_A,N_O\) are the population sizes and \(\tau\) is the number of distinct declaration types. This treats deployments as the units the paper calls applications; transactions or end users would not be a faithful substitute.

The paper’s Proposition 1 (NP-hardness), proved there by reduction from Partition, says that in the core model it is NP-hard to decide, given an instance and threshold \(T\), whether a feasible assignment has \(U^{\mathrm{sys}}\ge T\), even when \(\lambda_{\mathrm{sys}}=1\). The reduction gives applications demands \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) summing to \(2B\), two operators of capacity \(B\), and all prices equal to \(1\). Reaching throughput \(2B\) requires placing the operators on separate chains and splitting the applications into two groups each with total demand \(B\); that is exactly Partition. The proof sets application stake requirements to \(0\), despite the earlier positive-stake assumption. Setting each requirement to \(1\), matching each operator’s stake, repairs that issue without changing the reduction.

My lead mirror is Two-Chain Continuum Throughput. Its input is two finite-support distributions: application types \(i\in\mathcal A\), with masses \(\mu_i^A\) summing to \(1\), and operator types \(j\in\mathcal O\), with masses \(\mu_j^O\) summing to \(1\). The instance also gives population sizes \(N_A,N_O\), a threshold \(T\), and rational declarations. An application type has per-agent demand \(d_i\), stake requirement \(r_i\), and price cap \(p_i\); an operator type has capacity \(q_j\), stake \(s_j\), and minimum price \(\ell_j\). Types include every declaration relevant to this core problem.

For each of two chain slots \(c\in\{1,2\}\), choose nonnegative masses \(x_{ic}\) and \(y_{jc}\), with \(\sum_c x_{ic}\le\mu_i^A\) and \(\sum_c y_{jc}\le\mu_j^O\). Each individual belongs to at most one chain; only the mass of a type is divided across chains. Define

\[ D_c=N_A\sum_i d_i x_{ic},\qquad S_c=N_O\sum_j s_j y_{jc}, \]

and, when the corresponding type mass on the chain is positive,

\[ R_c=\max_{i:x_{ic}>0}r_i,\quad Q_c=\min_{j:y_{jc}>0}q_j,\quad \underline p_c=\max_{j:y_{jc}>0}\ell_j,\quad \overline p_c=\min_{i:x_{ic}>0}p_i. \]

A chain must satisfy \(S_c\ge R_c\), and its clearing price \(p_c\) must lie in \([\underline p_c,\overline p_c]\). Its processed gas is \(G_c=\min(D_c,Q_c)\); a chain missing either role contributes zero. The question is whether there is a mass allocation and feasible pair of prices with

\[ \sum_{c=1}^{2}p_cG_c\ge T. \]

The mirror keeps the paper’s chain assignment, feasibility conditions, bottleneck supply rule, and system-throughput objective. It changes the population representation: type mass can be apportioned across chains, while each member of the population still receives an integral assignment. That is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of the authors’ problem, and the two-chain restriction matches the structure used in their hardness reduction.

I expect this fixed-two-chain mirror to be tractable. Enumerate, for each chain, candidate types for the application price cap and stake requirement extrema, and for the operator price floor and capacity extrema. Once those extrema are fixed, type eligibility, stake feasibility, and mass constraints are linear. Introduce \(g_c\le D_c\) and \(g_c\le Q_c\), then maximize \(\overline p_c g_c\): in the system-only objective, the highest feasible price is optimal. There are polynomially many choices in \(\tau\) for two chains, and each choice gives an LP. The selected extrema need not actually be present in a candidate LP: their constraints can only make the price or supply more conservative, while the true extrema of any optimum are among the enumerated choices. This suggests a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\) and the input bit length, independent of \(N_A,N_O\) except through their encodings. Partition hardness does not transfer through this fluid assignment, because the application masses can divide.

The scope is Proposition 1’s core, system-only decision problem. It does not settle the paper’s full weighted objective, stability and diversity extensions, or its incentive and fairness claims. The next questions are whether the result extends to an input number of chains, whether the full objective permits a similar tractability result, and how well a continuous optimum rounds to a finite population.

The weakest point is the instance regime. Many blockchain ecosystems have relatively few independently assignable applications, so their application population may not satisfy \(N_A\gg\tau\). The mirror is persuasive for a large platform with many separately placeable app deployments sharing standardized profiles; it becomes much weaker if those deployments are really one application or if the proposed “agents” are merely end users. A referee could reasonably ask for evidence that this is a credible configuration regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the paper’s only named computational result, so there is one anchor to assess. Its reduction is a poor fit for a high-multiplicity regime: the applications carry the arbitrary demands \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), so in general there are \(n\) distinct types. The Partition hardness comes from assigning each whole workload to a chain. Replacing that list with divisible type mass removes precisely that indivisibility. That change could be valuable—the programme explicitly seeks cases where it makes a hard problem tractable—but the result alone does not establish that the relevant blockchain population has the repeated profiles a high-multiplicity model needs.

The proposed two-chain mirror has a more serious modeling problem. It scales application demand as \(D_c=N_A\sum_i d_i x_{ic}\), and operator stake as \(S_c=N_O\sum_j s_j y_{jc}\), but leaves chain supply at \(Q_c=\min_{j:y_{jc}>0}q_j\). Thus an arbitrarily small positive mass of an operator type unlocks its full per-chain capacity. With positive application stake requirement \(r\), a type with stake \(s\) can meet it using mass \(r/(N_Os)\), which tends to zero as the operator population grows. The capacity does not. This is a consequence of the paper’s bottleneck rule: one operator’s capacity can determine a whole chain’s throughput, even when that operator is negligible in the society-wide population.

With two fixed chain slots, total throughput is bounded by two per-operator capacities while aggregate demand grows with \(N_A\). Per-capita throughput therefore vanishes in the large-population limit. Keeping a fixed target eventually makes the question uninformative; scaling the target with population makes this fixed-two-chain system unable to reach it. The proposed LP’s apparent fluid tractability does not resolve that mismatch. Its two-chain restriction preserves the shape of the reduction while avoiding the scaling problem only by retaining a fixed-size infrastructure.

A stronger repair would let the number of chains grow with the population. But then the mirror needs a model of how chain count and chain configurations scale, in addition to a continuous population. Alternatively, making supply proportional to assigned operator mass restores extensive scaling but abandons the paper’s synchronized, slowest-operator bottleneck. Scaling each operator’s capacity with population has a similar problem: repeated operators would no longer have fixed per-agent declarations. These may be worthwhile models, but the first changes the fixed-two-chain question, and the others change a central operational assumption. The authors’ criterion remains whether they would recognize the resulting model as their problem; the paper gives little basis for assuming they would.

That is the best case against this anchor, but it does not establish the requested universal negative. A large platform with many independently assignable deployments and standardized operator packages could make repeated types credible. A corrected, scalable version could then ask a meaningful computational question about allocating fluid workloads among growing numbers of bottlenecked chains. Proposition 1 gives no high-multiplicity hardness result for that setting, but whether its continuous mirror is tractable is itself a legitimate question. So the specific two-chain mirror is weak, and the paper’s reduction offers limited support for it; the broader claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is weaker still.
