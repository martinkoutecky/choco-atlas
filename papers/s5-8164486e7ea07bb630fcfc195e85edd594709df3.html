The case FOR (proponent)

The honest qualification is that this paper does not contain a qualifying computational anchor. Its named results are structural and welfare theorems, not results asserting membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or a computational formulation that the paper analyzes. The complexity statements in the related literature are cited rather than proved here. So, under the programme’s strict anchor rule, this is not a compliant positive case.

The strongest substantive case nevertheless uses Theorem 1 as the lead. The theorem is proved in the paper and states that, under any stable-dominating mechanism, every school receives exactly the same numbers of advantaged and marginalized students as under DA; hence a school fully segregated under DA remains fully segregated under every such mechanism.

A natural mirror is the following Continuous Stable-Dominating Segregation problem. There is a finite school set \(S\), per-capita capacities \(q_s\), and a finite set of complete student types \(T\). A type \(t\) specifies a strict school preference, its priority position at every school, and its demographic group \(g(t)\in\{Y,Z\}\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students of type \(t\). Capacities scale with population, so \(q_s\) is also a population fraction.

The mass-DA procedure assigns type mass through the usual proposal rounds, allowing a type to be split at a capacity boundary only because it represents a continuum of individually assigned students. A candidate solution is an aggregate assignment \(x_{t,s}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\leq q_s\). It must weakly Pareto-dominate DA, and no feasible measurable reassignment may Pareto-improve it. The decision question is whether there exists such an assignment for which, at some school \(s\), the advantaged mass \(\sum_{t:g(t)=Y}x_{t,s}\) differs from its DA value. If not, the solution returns the invariant demographic composition of every school.

This is a credible population continuization. The many agents are students in a large district whose preferences and priority-relevant characteristics fall into a small number of repeated profiles: for example, catchment area, sibling status, socioeconomic tier, and a common preference ordering. The number of named students can be enormous while \(|T|\) remains modest. The action remains exactly the paper’s action—Pareto-improving reallocations over schools—not a lottery or a divisible-good assignment.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A if Theorem 1 lifts to atomless assignments. Mass-DA is computable by finite-type flow and priority simulation, and the theorem would make the composition question immediate: no efficient DA-dominating assignment can alter the group mass at any school. The interesting follow-up questions are whether the result extends to several priority tiers, whether one can optimize welfare within each demographic group, and how much priority relaxation is required before segregation can change.

A second, weaker structural anchor is Proposition 1, proved in the paper. It states that for every number \(n\) of schools, every stable-dominating mechanism has Rawlsian inequality exactly \(n/2\), and no mechanism has a larger ratio. It also states that every stable-dominating Pareto-efficient mechanism has rank-inefficiency exactly \(n/2\), while no Pareto-efficient mechanism has a larger ratio.

Its continuous counterpart would give a finite-type society together with a DA-dominating Pareto-efficient assignment \(x\), and ask for the ratios between \(x\)’s worst assigned rank and the continuous Rawlsian optimum, and between its average rank and the continuous rank-minimizing optimum. The latter optima are obtained over mass assignments \(x_{t,s}\), with objective \(\max\{\operatorname{rk}_t(s):x_{t,s}>0\}\) for the Rawlsian benchmark and \(\sum_{t,s}x_{t,s}\operatorname{rk}_t(s)\) for the average-rank benchmark. These are transportation-style optimization problems and should be tractable in the finite-type representation. The paper’s cyclic construction survives by giving each of \(n\) types mass \(1/n\), and then replicating those types arbitrarily many times, so the sharp \(n/2\) bound should remain valid in the continuous model.

The weakest point is decisive: neither Theorem 1 nor Proposition 1 is a computational result. Moreover, their finite proofs use individual envy cycles, so a real continuous theorem must carefully define Pareto efficiency for measurable assignments and show that allowing type mass to split does not introduce artificial trades unavailable to finite students. Thus the mirror is plausible and mathematically worthwhile, but it would be a new computational study motivated by this paper, not a continuous counterpart anchored in one of the paper’s qualifying complexity results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is decisive at the programme’s gatekeeping stage: this paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. Its named results are structural and welfare statements. Theorem 1 is an invariance theorem; Proposition 1 is a worst-case ratio theorem. Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, a decision problem, or a computational formulation whose continuous counterpart could be studied. The complexity results mentioned in the related-work section belong to other papers. Thus a positive case must first invent a computational problem inspired by the paper, rather than continuize one of its computational results.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 1 is the more credible of the two. A large district with repeated preference, priority, and demographic profiles is a perfectly sensible high-multiplicity regime. Per-capita capacities and mass assignments are also legitimate co-modifications. So the objection cannot be that school choice has no meaningful continuum.

The difficulty is that the proposed problem is not really the theorem’s problem. Theorem 1 already quantifies over every matching that Pareto-dominates DA and asserts that demographic occupancy is invariant. Asking whether a mass assignment violating that invariant exists merely wraps the theorem’s negation in an existential decision problem. With an explicit finite type set, the resulting feasibility test is an ordinary transportation or flow problem, and the finite cycle argument lifts directly after scaling rational masses. With an implicit type space, any new difficulty comes from an encoding choice absent from the paper, not from continuizing its result.

There is also a sharp dichotomy that defeats the stronger versions of this mirror. If one preserves the paper’s universal priority ordering—every advantaged student outranks every marginalized student—then the aggregate composition is rigid for the same structural reason as in the finite model, and there is no computational frontier to chart. If one relaxes that ordering to create a nontrivial pricing, optimization, or complexity problem, one is studying a new school-choice model rather than a continuous counterpart of Theorem 1. That could be worthwhile research, but it is no longer an anchor supplied by this paper.

Proposition 1 is a weaker negative target because its cyclic construction genuinely survives continuization. Giving each of \(n\) types mass \(1/n\) preserves the \(n/2\) Rawlsian and rank-inefficiency ratios, and arbitrary replication changes nothing. I would not pretend otherwise. But this does not rescue the paper as a computational mirror. Proposition 1 concerns a supremum over populations and profiles, not the computation of an outcome on a continuous society. Its continuous analogue asks for two standard assignment optima—one bottleneck and one linear transportation objective—and then compares their values. The paper supplies neither a mechanism-selection problem nor an algorithmic question about those optima. The population continuum is incidental: the same ratios are already obtained by a finite cyclic instance and remain unchanged under replication.

Consequently, the proponent’s constructions establish that continuous versions of the paper’s structural claims are coherent. They do not establish a worthwhile ChoCo problem. The positive case becomes persuasive only if the programme deliberately relaxes its anchor rule and accepts new computational questions generated from non-computational papers. Under the stated rule, however, the absence of any named computational result is fatal. The universal claim that no mathematically interesting continuum model can be invented would be too strong; the defensible claim is that this paper does not itself offer a qualifying computational mirror.
