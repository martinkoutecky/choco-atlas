What the mirror covers

No result qualifies for a population mirror; the equilibrium lemmas and experimental findings are left without a computational question over a society represented by type masses.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive foothold is the setting: a large professional partnership or cooperative could have many members but few complete types, distinguished by attributes such as productivity, endowment, investment cost, and whether contributions are observable. A continuous model could assign each type a population share \(\mu_t\), with members choosing investments and bargaining over the resulting fund. That is a plausible population extension of the paper’s subject.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor that mirror. Its nearest named results are Lemma 1, which characterizes the three-player SSPE—no investment, and, conditional on contributions, a proposer keeps two thirds and offers one third to a randomly selected partner—and Lemma 2, which gives SPE and efficiency claims. Those are equilibrium characterizations, not claims about computational complexity or algorithms. Lemma 2(1) and (2) cite earlier work; its third part is proved in the appendix. The paper’s conclusions about communication and efficiency are experimental findings, not numbered computational results.

A possible follow-on would be to take a rational distribution \(\mu\) over types in a large pool, form random three-person groups, and ask for a type-contingent stationary equilibrium of the paper’s investment-and-bargaining game, along with the induced expected investment and efficiency. But that would be a new computational question, not a continuous mirror of a named result in this paper. There is therefore no qualifying anchor, no anchor-specific problem to hand to a prover, and no class assignment the paper supports.

The weak point is also the limit of the positive case: if the many agents are spread across independent three-person games, the population limit aggregates group outcomes rather than continuizing the bargaining society itself. A single large partnership is a more direct population mirror, but changing the bargaining group size changes the game whose equilibrium Lemmas 1 and 2 characterize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is the paper’s own gap: it reports no computational result. Lemma 1 characterizes a three-player stationary equilibrium; Lemma 2 describes subgame-perfect equilibria and conditions for efficient or inefficient outcomes. Neither asks for an algorithm or establishes a complexity bound. The numbered conclusions summarize experimental findings, not computational claims. So there is no result here for ChoCo to classify as tractable or hard.

The proposed large-partnership setting makes a continuous population plausible, but plausibility alone does not supply a computational question. If partners are still randomly matched into groups of three, a distribution over types gives the frequencies of finite group compositions; it aggregates outcomes across those games rather than continuizing the bargaining group. If instead the whole partnership bargains at once, the model must specify how a proposer is selected, what counts as a majority, and how individual votes, communication, and rejection work in a population where each member has zero mass. Those choices could define a worthwhile new model, but they do not recover a problem posed or computed in this paper.

The best attempted mirrors of Lemmas 1 and 2 run into the same issue. Extending the equilibrium analysis to heterogeneous types in a large group might be interesting, but the paper supplies neither a computational task nor a particular large-group game to solve. The choices needed to make one would determine the task. Likewise, modeling the experiments’ fairness or efficiency outcomes as population statistics would continue the empirical analysis, not produce the computational social-choice question this programme targets. The fact that shares and funds are divisible does not change this: that is continuity of outcomes, while the experimental society remains groups of three.

I would not spend ChoCo effort looking for a continuous mirror of this paper. That verdict is strongest for the paper as written, rather than a proof that no related mass-bargaining model could be worthwhile. The proponent’s suggested large-partnership extension may merit a separate project; its value would come from the new model and its computational questions, not from mirroring a result in this paper.
