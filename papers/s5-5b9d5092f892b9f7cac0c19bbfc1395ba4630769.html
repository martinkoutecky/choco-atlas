The continuous mirror question

Given a pool of \(M\) indivisible items and a population of claimant types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), shared monotone bundle preferences, and entitlement weights \(w_t\), set \(a_{t,N}=w_t/(N\sum_u\mu_u w_u)\). For each type, let \(R_t=\{(l,d):1\le l\le d\le M,\ l/d\le a_{t,N}\}\). Find sets \(Q_t\subseteq R_t\) minimizing \(\sum_t\mu_t|Q_t|\), subject to every pair in \(R_t\) being dominated by a pair in \(Q_t\), using the paper’s dominance relation.

The model it lives in

A continuous distribution \(\mu\) over claimant entitlement types; each type has a per-person entitlement \(a_{t,N}\) and an audit set \(Q_t\) of integral MMS benchmark pairs. The objective is the mass-weighted number of checks, \(\sum_t\mu_t|Q_t|\), with coverage determined by Lemma 3.1’s pairwise dominance test.

The objection that survived

The checks decompose by type, so μ_t affects only the reported workload and not which checks are selected; the proponent acknowledges this, but does not establish that the aggregate audit is more than a weighted repetition of the paper’s procedure.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow: the paper has one plausible algorithmic anchor, but no named hardness result. Lemma 3.1, proved in the paper, characterizes its universal dominance problem: for \(1\le l\le d\) and \(1\le l'\le d'\), write \(d'=qd-r\) with \(q\ge1\) and \(0\le r<d\). Then \((l,d)\) dominates \((l',d')\)—meaning its maximin-share is at least as good for every finite item set and every monotone subset ordering—if and only if \(ql-\min(l,r)\ge l'\). Euclidean division and this integer comparison put that *dominance test* in polynomial time in the binary input lengths. It is proved here, not cited from elsewhere.

A continuous mirror I would defend is Population-Weighted OMMS Audit Basis. Consider a large cooperative dividing indivisible assets among many claimants. Claimants share the paper’s common ordering of bundles but belong to a small number \(\tau\) of entitlement classes. Class \(t\) has rational population mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and relative entitlement weight \(w_t\). At scale \(N\), its per-person entitlement is \(a_{t,N}=w_t/(N\sum_u\mu_u w_u)\). The pool has \(M\) indivisible items, with \(M\) scaling with the population; a type’s mass can be split across recipients of different integral bundles.

For each type, let \(R_t\) be the required benchmark pairs \((l,d)\) with \(1\le l\le d\le M\) and \(l/d\le a_{t,N}\). Choose a set \(Q_t\subseteq R_t\) of checks minimizing \(\sum_t\mu_t|Q_t|\), subject to every \(p\in R_t\) being dominated by some \(q\in Q_t\). A solution is the selected sets \(Q_t\) and their total mass-weighted check load. Checking a selected dominating benchmark covers the weaker benchmark it dominates; the irredundant bases are the maximal elements of the dominance relation. The paper’s Lemma 4.1 supplies the finite-item cutoff used here: for \(h\ge0\), if \(|X|\le d\), then the \(l\)-out-of-\(d\) MMS dominates the \((l+h)\)-out-of-\((d+h)\) MMS. This is the paper’s Section 4 audit procedure applied across a population of entitlement types.

The regime is plausible when \(N\) is very large and \(\tau\ll N\): for example, many beneficiaries with a small number of contract classes and a shared asset valuation. The mass represents the fraction of claimants in each class; the checked benchmarks and the bundles remain integral. The authors could recognize this as a high-multiplicity extension of their question about which OMMS conditions need checking. I expect this audit-basis problem to be Class A: the dominance predicate is the arithmetic test in Lemma 3.1, and the finite list can be generated and reduced in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(M\), and the input bit lengths.

This case covers the paper’s benchmark-reduction result, not the full problem of finding an OMMS-fair allocation or the comparisons between OMMS and WMMS in Propositions 5.1 and 5.2. Those propositions are examples, not computational anchors. The main weakness is that population mass only weights the audit workload; it does not alter the dominance relation. The paper also gives no hardness anchor, and its lemma does not solve the harder follow-on question of allocating the item mass among integral bundles. A continuous OMMS allocation problem with type-mass variables and per-capita item supplies would be a sensible next question, but its configuration pricing and complexity are not settled by this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only computational anchor is Lemma 3.1 and the audit procedure it supports. The lemma characterizes a relation between two integer pairs, quantified over every finite item set and every monotone ordering. Its input contains no society, agents, or population distribution. Section 4 adds an item-count cutoff, but its algorithm still depends on the entitlement and the number of items, not on how claimants are distributed across types.

The proposed population-weighted audit basis does not make that relation population-sensitive. Each type’s checks can be chosen independently; multiplying their counts by positive masses changes the reported workload, not the chosen checks. Increasing the number of entitlement classes repeats the same computation. That may describe an administrative workload, but it does not give the paper’s dominance result a high-multiplicity population to aggregate. If item supply is compressed as well as population, that requires a new input model absent from the paper.

A stronger candidate would assign mass of each entitlement type to integral bundles, with per-capita item supplies, and ask whether a fair allocation exists. That is a plausible high-multiplicity fair-division question, and the paper’s discussion of open OMMS-allocation existence gives it some connection to the authors’ concerns. But it is not a continuous version of Lemma 3.1 or the Section 4 audit result: it adds an allocation problem the paper does not solve. Its continuous analogue could be worthwhile, but this paper does not establish the computational anchor for it.

So the proponent’s audit-basis mirror does not survive as a population continuization of the result proved here. The broader claim that *no* scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror is less secure: a continuous allocation problem is plausible, though it would be an extension rather than a mirror of the paper’s algorithmic result.
