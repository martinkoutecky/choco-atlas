There is a plausible follow-on worth testing with the authors. Imagine a large clinical audit where many patients share the same finite condition vocabulary and sources have fixed expertise across patients. A patient’s type is their complete condition valuation \(v\in V\), with population mass \(\mu_v\). Suppose the observations give the rational mass of each *joint report profile*—which formulas each source reports as possible—rather than reports indexed by patient. One could ask whether a target condition’s prevalence \(\sum_{v\models\phi}\mu_v\) is uniquely determined among all population distributions and source partitions consistent with those observations; a solution would give the prevalence or two consistent models with different prevalences.

That is a concrete continuous-population inference problem, and it connects to the paper’s identifiability questions. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no qualifying anchors in the case for: the paper’s theorems concern learnability and identifiability, not the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1 characterizes which questions can be solved from complete streams; Theorems 2–4 and Corollary 1 describe what those streams identify; Theorems 5–7 characterize learning methods. None gives a running-time, hardness, or algorithmic result to mirror. The proponent is right to call their proposed problem an extension rather than a mirror.

The strongest natural extension would treat many patients as a population, with condition valuations \(v\) as types and shared source expertise across patients. If the expertise partitions are known, each patient’s complete report profile identifies the common-refinement cell containing their valuation. A target condition’s prevalence is then determined exactly when it is constant within each observed cell; otherwise the reports leave it ambiguous. Computing the determined prevalence amounts to summing the rational masses of relevant cells.

Unknown partitions make a richer inverse problem possible: find the range of prevalences consistent with the observed masses of report profiles. But that changes the paper’s case-indexed streams into aggregate data and makes prevalence, rather than truth-tracking of cases or identification of expertise, the target. Replicating cases does not add the coverage of valuation space that the paper’s expertise-identification results need: with fixed partitions, repeated valuations produce repeated report profiles. The proposed question may be worth studying on its own, but it has no computational result here to anchor it, and the continuous masses do not create the paper’s missing complexity analysis.

Its weakness is precisely that it is a new aggregate-inference problem, not a continuous mirror of a named result in this paper.
