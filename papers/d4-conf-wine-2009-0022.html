What the mirror covers

The proposed lead concerns Theorem 2’s welfare maximization among optimal strategies; Theorems 1, 3, and 4 concern dominant strategies and equilibria, not computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot honestly supply a named computational-complexity anchor from this paper. Theorems 1–4 and Lemma 1 concern strategy existence, welfare, and equilibrium; none classifies a problem as being in \(P\), NP-hard, or in another complexity class. The nearest constructive result is Theorem 2, proved here: in the sequential Bailey–Cavallo auction, for every valuation vector \(\theta\) and every vector \(s'\) of optimal strategies, the paper’s proposed strategy vector \(s\) gives \(SW(\theta,s)\ge SW(\theta,s')\). The paper also says its welfare is at least that of truthful bidding in the simultaneous Bailey–Cavallo auction. This is an algorithmic-looking result, but it does not state a computational bound, so it is not a qualifying anchor under your criteria.

The best positive mirror I can make from it is Continuous Sequential BC Welfare Optimization, with Theorem 2 as a *nonqualifying, provisional lead*. Take a large population spread across many independent copies of the paper’s \(n\)-player auction, each with the same fixed bidding order. A bidder type is their position \(i\) and value \(v\); mass is the fraction of all bidder slots of that type. Auction copies retain their \(n\)-slot grouping. More generally, an input may give a finite rational distribution \(\lambda\) over valuation profiles \(\theta\); it induces bidder masses \(\mu_{i,v}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\theta:\theta_i=v}\lambda_\theta\), while \(\lambda\) records which types meet in the same auction. The action is a strategy for each position, mapping its observed earlier bids and its own value to a bid. The problem: among strategy vectors satisfying the paper’s optimal-strategy condition, find one maximizing the population’s average final social welfare, \(\sum_\theta\lambda_\theta SW(\theta,s)\), and report that welfare.

For this finite-support version, Theorem 2’s strategy solves the problem profile by profile: bid truthfully when one’s value exceeds the current highest bid; otherwise, bid the current highest bid except at the last position, which bids the current second-highest. Since that strategy is optimal for every profile, averaging over \(\lambda\) preserves its welfare dominance. This suggests an easy tractable mirror, but that complexity conclusion is a deduction from the theorem, not a result the paper states. Further questions include whether welfare can be determined from bidder masses alone without the profile coupling, and whether a single auction with a continuum of sequential bidders has a meaningful Bailey–Cavallo extension.

The weak point is substantial: the paper studies one finite auction, while this mirror gets a large population by replicating finite auction cells. The strategic core is recognizable, but the mirror may be judged a continuization of repeated instances rather than of the paper’s bidder population. And even if accepted, it mirrors a welfare theorem, not a hardness result whose complexity might change in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 rules out dominant strategies for a class of auctions; Theorems 2–4 concern welfare and equilibrium. None formulates an algorithmic problem or establishes a complexity bound. Theorem 2 is the proponent’s only plausible anchor, but it proves a pointwise welfare-dominance statement: its strategy is an explicit rule based on the player’s value and the earlier bids. The paper does not ask how hard it is to find such a rule.

The proposed mirror adds a distribution over auction profiles and asks for a welfare-maximizing strategy across that distribution. With finite support, the stated strategy applies to each profile and the resulting welfare is averaged. That may be a useful model of many repeated auctions, but the added computation is an expectation over auction instances; it is not a complexity question raised by Theorem 2. If the input instead records only masses of bidder types, it loses information the auction needs. For example, with three positions, compare equal mixtures of \((10,0,0)\) and \((0,10,0)\) against equal mixtures of \((10,10,0)\) and \((0,0,0)\). Both have the same value marginals for every position. Yet under truthful bidding, the first mixture has final welfare \(10\) in each profile, while the second has welfare \(10/3\) in \((10,10,0)\) and \(0\) in \((0,0,0)\). The second-highest bid, and hence the Bailey–Cavallo transfers, depends on how bidders are grouped into auctions. Keeping that information means the continuous input is a distribution over whole auction instances, not just a society of bidder types.

A better mirror might put one large population into a single sequential auction. But the paper’s mechanism relies on a finite ordered list of bidders competing for one indivisible object. Passing to an atomless society leaves no ordinary first or last bidder; almost every position has a positive mass of predecessors. A single winner also has zero population mass, so integrated per-capita welfare does not represent the winner’s value. One could define a new limiting allocation, tie-breaking rule, and welfare scale, but those choices would construct a different model. They still would not supply a computational problem from this paper.

One could certainly study continuous value distributions, repeated markets, or asymptotic sequential auctions. Some such work might be worthwhile in its own right. But making it computational would require specifying new inputs and tasks—perhaps optimizing over a restricted strategy class or computing welfare from a compactly represented distribution. Any resulting difficulty would come from those additions, not from a computational question the paper formulates or answers. Under the programme’s stated criterion, the proponent has offered a possible extension of mechanism design, not a continuous mirror of a computational result.

This is a strong negative case for this paper as an anchor, rather than a proof that no related continuous model could ever be valuable.
