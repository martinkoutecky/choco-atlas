The closest results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in the paper. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the paper’s actual contribution: it proves economic identities, not computational results. Theorem 1 equates VCG transfers with largest net Walrasian prices for single-object traders; Theorem 2 gives a lower bound when Walrasian prices exist. Neither states the complexity of a computational problem, gives an algorithm or reduction, or specifies an input encoding. A question about computing transfers or prices in a continuous market could be interesting, but it would launch a new computational agenda rather than continuize a computational result from this paper.

The best attempt to build a mirror from Theorem 1 would give agents’ valuation and endowment types positive masses, then ask for each type’s VCG transfer density and the aggregate deficit. But the theorem’s transfer is defined by removing one agent and that agent’s endowment. In an atomless society, a single agent has zero mass, so that deletion is not an operation on the distribution that determines the market. A derivative with respect to type mass is definable, but it is a different object: it measures the effect of removing a cohort-sized increment, not an individual’s VCG externality. Treating a whole type cohort as the strategic agent would likewise change the mechanism’s incentives and the unit whose externality is measured.

Theorem 2 does not escape this problem. One could integrate each type’s largest net Walrasian price over its mass, but that integral need not represent a continuum VCG deficit. In regular atomless markets, individual pivotal effects disappear; the finite-market deficit can be a granularity effect that vanishes under per-capita normalization or survives only as a finite-size correction not determined by the limiting distribution. If masses at marginal types leave a price interval, the integral may remain nonzero, but the individual-removal interpretation is still missing. That is a question about price geometry unless an additional mechanism gives it a computational role.

There are plausible high-multiplicity asset-market scenarios, and the paper’s references to continuous assignment models show that a continuous economic formulation is sensible. That does not supply the missing complexity anchor. The honest negative case is therefore strong as a case against treating this paper as a ChoCo mirror: its central results depend on finite-agent externalities, and the paper makes no computational claim to continuize. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could grow from the paper. Computing welfare, equilibrium prices, or finite-support type-market quantities could be worthwhile, but those would be new questions rather than mirrors of Theorems 1 or 2.
