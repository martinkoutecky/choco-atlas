What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Theorem 1’s static-pricing limitation. It leaves the adaptive pricing guarantees, feasible variants, and revealed-preference algorithms untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

My positive case is narrow: the paper’s strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved here, but it is a performance lower bound, not an NP-hardness result. The theorem says that in the finite stochastic arrival model, there is a market instance where every static pricing algorithm has either expected regret or expected capacity violation \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\). Its example has \(n\) unit-budget users, two goods with capacity \(n\) each, and two equally likely utility types, \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\). The random number of users of each type fluctuates, so a price that works in expectation can leave one good over-consumed or reduce welfare. Corollary 1, also proved here as a consequence, says that even expected-equilibrium pricing with knowledge of the type distribution can have \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\) expected violation.

The mirror I believe in is the Fluid Static Fisher Clearing problem. Its instance consists of \(m\) goods, total population mass \(N\), per-unit-mass capacities \(d\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^m\), and \(K\) complete user types \(k=(w_k,u_k)\), each with mass fraction \(\mu_k>0\), where \(\sum_k\mu_k=1\). Each type specifies its budget and linear utility. The actual mass of type \(k\) is \(N\mu_k\), and total supply of good \(j\) is \(Nd_j\). Users can arrive sequentially over a normalized horizon, but the type proportions are exact in the fluid model: there is no sampling noise in the realized population.

The question is to find one uniform posted price \(p\) and a demand bundle \(x_k\) for each type, where
\[ x_k\in\arg\max_{x\ge0}\{u_k^\top x:p^\top x\le w_k\}, \]
such that aggregate demand respects capacities and every positive-price good clears. Measure its outcome against the full-information Eisenberg–Gale optimum
\[ U^*=N\max_{x_k\ge0}\sum_k\mu_kw_k\log(u_k^\top x_k) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_k\mu_kx_k\le d. \]
The posted-price welfare is \(U(p,x)=N\sum_k\mu_kw_k\log(u_k^\top x_k)\); its regret is \(U^*-U(p,x)\), and its total capacity violation is \(N\|(\sum_k\mu_kx_k-d)_+\|_2\). A solution is the price vector and type bundles, including a choice among tied best responses, that satisfy those market-clearing conditions.

For the anchor’s two-type example, the fluid input has mass \(N/2\) of each type. Prices \(p=(1/2,1/2)\) give type one bundle \((2,0)\) and type two bundle \((0,2)\). Demand exactly equals supply, and welfare equals the offline optimum \(N\log 2\). So the finite theorem’s \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\) loss does not persist for exact type masses. More generally, this is a standard Fisher market equilibrium computation: the type-level Eisenberg–Gale convex program supplies the allocation and its dual supplies prices. I expect this mirror to be tractable by continuous optimization.

The regime is a large voucher or artificial-currency market with many users but a modest number of recurring budget-and-preference profiles—for example, standardized benefit tiers and resource preferences in a large program. Within a type, users share everything the model uses: budget and utility. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the model retains the paper’s goods, budget-constrained demand, uniform posted prices, capacity constraints, and welfare benchmark. The co-modification from \(N\) users to type masses \(N\mu_k\) and capacities \(Nd_j\) is part of the mirror.

This is a fluid benchmark for Theorem 1, not a mirror of the paper’s whole online-learning problem. It covers the static-pricing limitation and asks where that limitation goes when the population itself is continuous. Further questions include how the finite-sample \(\sqrt n\) loss emerges as a correction to this zero-gap limit, and whether price updates using only aggregate revealed consumption can learn an equilibrium when the type-mass distribution is unknown.

The weak point is substantial: the theorem’s obstacle is precisely finite stochastic fluctuation. Replacing realized arrivals by exact masses removes that obstacle, so Fluid Static Fisher Clearing is much easier than the paper’s central online problem. The authors should recognize it as their market and welfare model in a high-multiplicity regime, but may reasonably say it does not preserve the uncertainty that makes Theorem 1 interesting. I would not use it to claim a continuous version of their adaptive algorithms; I would use it as a narrow, meaningful boundary result showing that the static-price loss can disappear when population multiplicities become exact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has picked a sensible high multiplicity regime: users with the same budget and utility are interchangeable, and a large voucher market could have many users per type. So the objection is not that mass is inappropriate. It is that this particular mirror is already the paper’s own type-mass computation.

Set \(q_k=\mu_k\) in the paper’s certainty-equivalent program \(\mathrm{CE}(d)\) in Section 4.2.1, and its type allocations \(z_k\) are exactly the proposed fluid bundles \(x_k\):
\[ \max_{z_k\ge 0}\ \sum_k \mu_k w_k\log(u_k^\top z_k) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_k\mu_k z_k\le d. \]
The proponent’s benchmark is \(N\) times this objective. The program’s dual prices give the same uniform prices and best responses. The paper already uses those prices for static expected-equilibrium pricing, and its adaptive algorithm repeatedly solves the same program as remaining capacities change. Thus “find prices and bundles for exact type masses” does not open a new computational question for this paper.

That equivalence also pinpoints what Theorem 1 is about. Its \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\) loss comes from random deviations between realized type counts and their expected proportions. With exact type masses, those deviations are absent and the certainty-equivalent equilibrium clears. A proposed study of the finite-sample correction would have to bring back random arrivals; that studies the original stochastic sequence around its fluid benchmark, rather than a distinct continuous-population problem. Giving the aggregate type masses their own persistent randomness could produce a different question, but it changes the uncertainty model that generates Theorem 1’s bound.

So the offered mirror fails the novelty test: the paper itself has already formulated and used its computational core. The negative case is strongest for this anchor, not airtight for the whole paper. A continuum-time analysis of revealed-preference price updates, or a model with uncertain aggregate type masses, might still be worthwhile; I cannot honestly rule those out universally. But they would need a separate case, and they do not rescue the proposed mirror of Theorem 1.
