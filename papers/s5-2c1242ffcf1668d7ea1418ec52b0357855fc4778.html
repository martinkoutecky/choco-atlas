The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result of the required kind: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic classification. Its rules and worked examples describe ways to aggregate rankings; they do not establish results about computing them. So there is no qualifying anchor for a continuous mirror under the brief’s rules.

The strongest candidate for a follow-up mirror would be the paper’s named Règle du produit transitionnel, but it is not a qualifying anchor. A continuous version would take \(m\) candidates and a list of \(\tau\) complete ranking types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). For each ordered pair, let \(q_{ab}=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t\). The task is to return a permutation \(\pi\) maximizing \(\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}q_{\pi_j,\pi_{j+1}}\). Its solution is the full social ranking. A plausible regime is a large online judging panel ranking the same fixed slate, with many judges but relatively few distinct rankings. That is recognizably the paper’s rule applied to a continuous population.

The weak point is decisive for this assignment: the paper neither studies the computational complexity of that optimization nor supplies a numbered result to anchor it. Presenting this candidate as though it were a result of the paper would overstate the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. It defines and compares aggregation rules through examples and normative discussion; it proves no complexity bound, gives no algorithmic classification, and identifies no computational problem as an open question. A complexity study would start from a rule in the paper, but it would add a research question the paper never investigates.

The product-transition rule is the best candidate, and its continuous version is perfectly coherent. Given ranking types with masses \(\mu_t\), define \(q_{ab}=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t\), then seek a permutation \(\pi\) maximizing \(\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}q_{\pi_j,\pi_{j+1}}\). A large panel with relatively few distinct rankings is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Neither type aggregation nor the continuum makes this question nonsensical.

That stronger version still does not supply a result from this paper to anchor a mirror. The objective depends only on pairwise masses, and rational masses can be scaled to integer voter counts: this recovers the same maximizing permutations, up to a common factor in the product. That makes the proposed complexity study a well-defined extension of the rule, but the paper reports no computational finding whose continuous counterpart it would be.

So the negative case is strong as a scope judgment: under the stated requirement to mirror a named computational result, this paper does not qualify. It is weak as the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The product-transition rule’s computational problem could be worth studying on its own; excluding it rests on the programme’s anchor criterion, not on a convincing objection to the continuous model.
