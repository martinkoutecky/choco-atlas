The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t overturn the narrow point that this paper proves no computational complexity result. The strongest challenge is that this point does not by itself establish that there is no worthwhile continuous computational question attached to a result in the paper.

Theorem 2 characterizes a concrete allocation rule, \(\phi^E\), for economies whose individuals may have different opportunity correspondences. A natural population regime is a large education system with many students of each type, where a type records the student’s full opportunity correspondence and resource-relevant characteristics. With type masses \(\mu_t\) and per-capita resources \(\bar x\), the continuous problem is to find bundles \(x_t\) satisfying
\[ \sum_t \mu_t x_t \leq \bar x, \]
while implementing equal opportunity levels \(G(c_t(x_t))\) across the types present in the population. The computational question is whether one can find such an allocation, or approximate it, from a finite description of the type-specific technologies.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity question: the model’s fairness criterion concerns opportunity sets, not who receives which bundle, and the types retain the relevant heterogeneity. Under rational polyhedral descriptions of the correspondences and ranking, continuous optimization becomes directly relevant. For example, one could ask for the complexity in the number of supported types, goods, and input bit length. The paper’s general axioms do not answer that question, but Theorem 2 supplies the rule whose implementation is at issue. Finite populations with type counts \(n_t\) correspond to masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), with total resources scaled to keep per-capita supply fixed.

That case has a real limit: it is a computational problem *derived from* the characterized rule, not a mirror of a computational theorem in the paper. And without a finite encoding of the opportunity correspondences and the common ranking, it is not yet a well-posed complexity problem. So the proponent is right about the absence of an eligible complexity result under a strict reading of “mirror.” The best argument against their broader conclusion is that Theorem 2 points to a recognizable, potentially useful high-multiplicity implementation problem. Whether that counts as a worthwhile ChoCo mirror depends on whether the programme admits computational questions built around an axiomatized rule, rather than requiring the paper itself to state a complexity classification.
