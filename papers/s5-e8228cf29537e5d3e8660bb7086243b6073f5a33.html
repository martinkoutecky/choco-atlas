The continuous mirror question

Given finite supports of complete manager and provider types with rational masses μ and ν, market rates and deadlines, and type-conditional rival bid and provider offer laws whose mass-weighted mixtures define \(F\) and \(G\), compute the focal manager type’s optimal second-price bid function \(b^*(r)\) over its participation horizon with regret at most ε and a certificate of that bound.

The model it lives in

A continuous market has manager types θ and provider types ξ carrying masses μ_θ and ν_ξ. Conditional rival bid and provider offer laws are supplied for each type; their mass-weighted mixtures give \(F\) and \(G\) in Corollary 3. The decision variable is a focal manager’s bid function over time, and the objective is to maximize that manager’s expected procurement utility under the paper’s second-price payment rule and deadline.

The objection that survived

The rival and provider laws remain exogenous, so this mirror computes a focal manager’s policy rather than an equilibrium of heterogeneous populations.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the second-price policy computation in Corollary 3; it leaves Theorem 1’s first-price result, the OBSAs and revenue analysis in Sections 3–4, and the rest of Section 5 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For piecewise-smooth mixture laws, what conditions ensure an optimal policy exists and is unique, and can a regret-ε policy be computed with a certified running-time bound in the support sizes and input bit length?
  • How sensitive is the computed policy and its regret certificate to errors in the type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: mirror Section 5’s problem of finding cloud managers’ bidding policies in the market between managers and private providers. The paper has no named result establishing a P, NP-hardness, or parameterized-complexity classification for its own problems. Its useful anchors are analytical results about bidding policies; they support a new computational question about calculating such policies for populations of agent types.

A plausible regime is a large set of regional or edge-cloud operators and private providers participating in many local resource markets. Let \(\Theta\) be a finite set of manager types, where \(\theta=(u_\theta,\gamma_\theta,T_\theta,\varpi_\theta)\) records the value of a server, discount rate, participation deadline, and fallback price. Let \(\Xi\) be a finite set of provider types, recording their offered prices and availability. The continuous society is given by type masses \(\mu_\theta\) and \(\nu_\xi\), each summing to one. Many operators and providers share each type because they have the same relevant economic parameters; their number \(N\) is large relative to the type support sizes \(\tau=|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\) and \(\sigma=|\operatorname{supp}(\nu)|\).

In repeated local markets, these masses determine the type composition of participants. To preserve the paper’s auction, each local event can retain its Poisson numbers of competing managers and providers, with types drawn from \(\mu\) and \(\nu\). Managers choose bids as a function of remaining participation time; providers’ offered-price behavior determines the ask distribution. This keeps the paper’s highest-bid winner, provider reserve price, payment rule, and deadline. The continuum is in the population’s type composition, not in auction outcomes or randomness. It is also a high-multiplicity extension, rather than a restatement: the paper assumes distributions over bids and offers, but does not represent the society itself by masses over complete agent types.

The clearest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For the paper’s first-price market, a manager with residual time \(r\), resource utility \(u\), time-preference rate \(\gamma\), and flat-market price \(\varpi\) has expected utility \(D(r)=(u-\varpi)e^{-\gamma r}\) and bid \(b^*(r)=\varpi e^{-\gamma r}\). The result is a policy for the manager’s underlying procurement problem, not a result about a mathematical encoding.

That gives the Continuous First-Price Procurement Policy problem. Its instance is the two type-mass distributions, market rates, a focal manager type \(\theta\), and that type’s residual horizon. It asks for the optimal bid and value functions \(b_\theta^*(r)\) and \(D_\theta(r)\) against the type-mass-generated market. A solution is those functions, or a representation that evaluates them at any \(r\). In the paper’s stated first-price regime, the answer is the closed form above, so I expect this version to be tractable. The next questions are whether the same simplicity survives heterogeneous type masses and how much the policy changes when the market composition is estimated with error.

My lead is Corollary 3, also proved here. It gives a bidding-policy equation for the second-price market and says the policy can be found numerically subject to the boundary conditions in (25). In the paper’s notation, with \(\alpha\) the active-manager fraction, \(\lambda_A\) the auction rate, \(\lambda_C\) and \(\lambda_P\) the manager and provider intensities, and \(F\) and \(G\) the bid and offer CDFs, its content is:

The Continuous Second-Price Procurement Policy problem takes finite rational type masses, market rates and deadlines, plus explicit type-level rival bidding and provider-offer laws inducing piecewise-smooth \(F\) and \(G\). It asks for the optimal bid function \(b_\theta^*(r)\) on the manager’s participation horizon. A computational solution should return a represented policy whose best-response regret is at most \(\varepsilon\), with a certificate of that bound. I expect Class A for regular inputs: the corollary reduces the task to solving a one-dimensional policy equation over a bounded horizon, with complexity to be measured in \(\tau,\sigma\), input bit length and \(1/\varepsilon\). Establishing existence, uniqueness, and certified running time is part of the proposed problem, not something the paper proves. Further questions include how policy error depends on errors in \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), and whether making the bid and offer laws endogenous—solving for a type-level market equilibrium—remains tractable.

The authors should recognize both mirrors: they preserve the procurement decision and payment rules they analyze, while replacing an untyped participant pool with populations of complete economic types. The scope is Section 5’s bidding-policy analysis. It does not mirror the options-based sequential auctions of Section 3, or claim new results on truthfulness, delayed entry, or welfare.

The weak point is the regime. Traditional cloud markets may have too few managers, and firms’ values, deadlines, and costs may vary enough to make \(\tau\) large. The more convincing setting is a broad market of repeated regional or edge operators with recurring workload and service classes. Even there, the paper optimizes a manager’s policy against given market distributions; it does not solve a full equilibrium among heterogeneous populations. That limits the claim, but leaves a genuine continuous-population policy-computation question anchored in the paper’s own results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed anchors are analytical results, not computational-complexity results. Theorem 1 and Corollary 3 derive bidding policies from an HJB model; they do not classify the complexity of a problem whose input is a society. Section 3 gives a simple winner rule, Section 4 analyzes revenue with a finite-state Markov chain, and Section 5 derives policy equations. The paper therefore offers no discrete computational problem with a population-size parameter for a high-multiplicity version to relax.

Theorem 1 is especially hard to turn into the proposed mirror. Its policy depends on the focal manager’s \(u\), \(\gamma\), and \(\varpi\); the other participants’ distributions do not enter the stated result. Applying it to manager types gives a policy for each type, but the type masses have no role in that computation. To make population composition matter, one would need to add an aggregate objective or make bidding behavior depend on the population. Neither is the problem Theorem 1 solves.

Corollary 3 is the stronger anchor because its equation uses the bid and offer distributions \(F\) and \(G\). But the paper takes those distributions as known inputs. If a proposed instance also supplies type masses and type-level bidding and offer laws, the masses merely provide one way to describe the distributions used by the paper’s single-manager policy problem. A more ambitious repair would make those laws endogenous: compute type-dependent policies whose aggregate bids and offers are consistent with the resulting population. That could be an interesting equilibrium problem, but it adds the equilibrium formulation and solution task that the paper does not provide. It is no longer a continuization of Corollary 3’s stated problem.

The same distinction holds if the mirror asks for a certified numerical policy with regret at most \(\varepsilon\). That is a computational question one can pose about the HJB equation, but the population is incidental unless its masses determine endogenous \(F\) and \(G\). With exogenous laws, it is a numerical optimal-control problem parameterized by distributions. With endogenous laws, it becomes a new heterogeneous-population equilibrium model. Neither follows from a discrete-to-continuous computational result in this paper.

I cannot make the universal negative airtight. A market of many similar operators and providers is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a population equilibrium study could be worthwhile. The negative case is strongest against these particular anchors as mirrors of the paper’s computational problems: there is no such complexity result here, and making the population computationally central requires adding a problem the authors did not analyze.
