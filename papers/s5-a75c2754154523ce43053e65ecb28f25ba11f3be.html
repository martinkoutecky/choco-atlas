Why no mirror

The type-share interpretation is sensible and maps exactly to the paper’s weighted power mean by setting \(d=\tau\) and \(w=\mu\). But Theorems 4.2(a) and 5.1(a) bound sample complexity, not algorithmic running time or computational hardness, so neither supplies the required computational anchor.

What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is learning welfare from pairwise policy choices in a large population whose members fall into a modest number of utility types. A type \(t\) is a resident’s complete utility function over the policies under consideration; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents with that function. Thus a city or country can have millions of residents but only \(\tau\) distinct types, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the population. This is plausible for the paper’s public-policy setting. The nonprofit example with 18 stakeholders is not the regime I would rely on.

For a policy \(a\), let \(u_a(t)\) be type \(t\)’s utility. With known population shares, define

\[ M_\mu(u;p)= \begin{cases} \left(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t u(t)^p\right)^{1/p}, & p\ne 0,\\[4pt] \exp\left(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log u(t)\right), & p=0. \end{cases} \]

This is the paper’s weighted power mean with weights equal to type masses. If a finite electorate has \(n_t\) residents of each type, setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) gives exactly its equal-weight discrete welfare value. The sample distribution over policy pairs remains a distribution of observations; the object being continuized is the population inside the welfare calculation.

My lead is Continuous Population Pairwise Welfare Learning, anchored in Theorem 4.2(a), proved in this paper. The theorem says that with known weights, i.i.d. utility-vector pairs and comparison labels, ERM over the power parameter has, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), excess \(0\)-\(1\) risk at most

\[ 16\sqrt{\frac{2(\log_2 d+1)\log(N+1)+\log(8/\delta)}{N}}, \]

where \(N\) is the number of samples. In particular, its sample complexity grows as \(O(\log d)\), up to the confidence and accuracy terms.

The continuous problem is: given a known finite rational measure \(\mu\) on \(\tau\) types and sample access to a distribution \(D\) over policy-utility pairs \((u,v)\) and observed preferences \(y\in\{-1,1\}\), return \(\hat p\) whose preference predictor

\[ \operatorname{sign}\!\left(\log M_\mu(u;\hat p)-\log M_\mu(v;\hat p)\right) \]

has population disagreement risk within \(\epsilon\) of the best predictor in this power-mean family, with probability at least \(1-\delta\). A solution is such a learner and a sample bound in \(N,\tau,\epsilon,\delta\). The paper’s theorem applies to this finite-support mirror by taking its utility-vector dimension to be \(\tau\) and its known weight vector to be \(\mu\). It therefore supplies an \(O(\log \tau)\)-scale PAC anchor for the stated question.

A second, useful anchor is Theorem 5.1(a), proved in this paper. For known weights and logistic comparison noise, it bounds the excess negative log-likelihood risk by

\[ 16\theta_{\max}\kappa \left( \sqrt{\frac{2\log 2+2\log N}{N}}+\frac{c}{\sqrt N} \right) +6\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2N}}, \]

where \(\kappa=\log(u_{\max}/u_{\min})\) and \(c>0\) is a constant. Section 5 describes fitting the noise temperature as well as the welfare parameter; the theorem’s displayed risk notation suppresses the fitted temperature.

Its mirror, Continuous Population Logistic Welfare Learning, has the same \((T,\mu)\) input and sample access to policy pairs, but labels follow

\[ \Pr(y=1\mid u,v) =\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left(-\theta[ \log M_\mu(u;p)-\log M_\mu(v;p)]\right)} \]

for unknown \(p\) and \(\theta\in[0,\theta_{\max}]\). The task is to return fitted parameters whose expected logistic loss is within \(\epsilon\) of the best model in this class, with confidence \(1-\delta\). I expect this to remain sample-efficient on finite support, following the paper’s known-weight complexity analysis. It also raises the natural question of what happens for an infinite type space or an unknown population measure.

The first question is my strongest: it is a direct extension of the paper’s ordinal task, and the finite-population-to-mass dictionary is exact when same-type residents have identical utility profiles and equal voice. The authors already study social welfare over individuals, explicitly include equal weights, and motivate national-scale policy decisions; I think they would recognize population shares as a sensible version of those weights. The mirror covers the paper’s pairwise-preference learning results, including the logistic-noise variant. It does not cover the cardinal-value task or claim that all of the paper has a continuous-population analogue.

The limitation is substantial: these anchors are sample-complexity results, not results proving polynomial running time. The paper itself notes that ERM can be difficult and gives a practical heuristic rather than a runtime guarantee. So this is an A-shaped mirror for statistical learnability, but it does not establish Class A under ChoCo’s worst-case computational-tractability standard. The paper contains no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result to anchor such a claim; its named theoretical results concern PAC bounds. That is the case’s weakest point and also its boundary: a further question would need to determine whether these continuous learning tasks admit efficient algorithms, especially when the support or the population weights are part of the input.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The national policy setting is a credible high-multiplicity regime. I would not argue that residents cannot sensibly be grouped into types. The stronger objection is that the proposed grouping leaves the paper’s mathematical problem unchanged.

For a finite type set, put each type’s utility for an action in one coordinate and set \(w_t=\mu_t\). Then the continuous society’s welfare is exactly the paper’s \(M(u;w,p)\). For every sampled action pair, its prediction is unchanged for every \(p\). Collapsing identical individuals into types is therefore a lossless re-expression of an instance the paper already allows: its weight vector ranges over the simplex, including population shares. The authors’ weights may have a normative interpretation, but the subcase where they equal population shares is already in the model.

That makes the first anchor, Theorem 4.2(a), a direct instance match. Its known-weight comparison learner applies with \(d=\tau\) and \(w=\mu\); the \(O(\log \tau)\) sample bound follows without a new continuization result. Theorem 5.1(a) has the same match for logistic comparisons. Even a stronger proposal that learns the type masses along with \(p\) lands in the paper’s unknown-weight setting, covered statistically by Theorem 5.1(b). A model that instead samples an unknown population distribution would need further specification, and would move toward distribution learning rather than create a new finite-type population problem.

There is also a mismatch with ChoCo’s computational aim. These theorems bound the number of observations needed for PAC generalization; they do not bound an algorithm’s running time or classify computational complexity. The paper itself says ERM is nonconvex and presents a practical heuristic without a worst-case runtime guarantee. One could ask whether ERM runs efficiently on a compressed type representation, but the same question applies to the paper’s existing weighted-vector learner: the compressed input is already its \(d\)-coordinate model with \(d=\tau\). Any new result would concern computation for that existing learning problem, rather than a distinct complexity landscape produced by making the population continuous.

So the negative case is strongest on model collision and programme fit, not on whether the scenario is sensible. It remains weak against a broader programme that includes statistical learning or efficient ERM: a large, low-support population is a natural application, and an algorithmic study of the learner could be worthwhile. I cannot make an honest case that no such research is worthwhile anywhere; I can make the narrower case that these two anchors do not establish a new continuous-population computational problem for ChoCo.
