What the mirror covers

The type-mass analogue recasts Theorem 1's success bound and Theorem 3's convergence guarantee; it leaves the paper's probability analysis and simulations as analytical contributions, with no computational complexity result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a prospective high-multiplicity mirror, but it does not meet the required anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational result. Theorem 1 proves a probability bound; Theorem 3 proves asymptotic convergence. Neither classifies a problem as tractable or hard, gives an algorithm with a complexity guarantee, or presents a formulation that the paper then computes with as a computational problem. The simulations do not supply such an anchor. So I can’t honestly present either theorem as evidence that a continuous computational problem is in Class A, B, or C.

The plausible scenario is a large pool of independent reviewers—or independent model replicas—judging binary fact-checking tasks, with many agents sharing a small number of calibration profiles. A type would include \(\theta_t=(p_t,\alpha_t,\beta_t,p_{\mathrm{crit},t},\tau_t)\): competence, initial Beta parameters, critical competence level, and abstention threshold. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with support size \(\tau\) much smaller than the population.

There is a natural continuum model. For \(H=T-1\) calibration rounds, type \(t\)’s publish probability is

\[ q_t=\sum_{k=0}^{H} \mathbf{1}\!\left[ 1-I_{p_{\mathrm{crit},t}}(\alpha_t+k,\beta_t+H-k)>\tau_t \right] \binom{H}{k}p_t^k(1-p_t)^{H-k}. \]

At the final decision, type \(t\)’s mass divides into correct votes \(\mu_t q_t p_t\), incorrect votes \(\mu_t q_t(1-p_t)\), and abstentions \(\mu_t(1-q_t)\). The signed vote mass is

\[ M=\sum_t\mu_t(2p_t-1)q_t. \]

A concrete continuous question is: given the type distribution and calibration parameters, does the confidence-gated continuum electorate give the true alternative a strict majority? Its answer is the sign of \(M\). Theorem 3 is the closest paper result to this mirror: it is proved in the paper and says that, for a sequence of finite electorates with fixed \(T\), average competence at least \(1/2+\Delta_p\), and a uniformly nondegenerate gate \(q_i(p)\ge q_{\min}>0\) for competent agents, the probability of a correct strict majority tends to \(1\) as \(N\to\infty\). Its proof gives the rate \(1-\exp(-2\Delta_p^2q_{\min}^2N/(T+3))\). That supports the finite-population limit underlying the mass model; it is not itself a computational-complexity result.

A second, narrower candidate mirrors Theorem 1, also proved in the paper. Under conditional independence, it gives the finite-electorate guarantee

\[ \Pr(\text{correct strict majority}) \ge 1-\exp\!\left( -\frac{\left(\sum_i(2p_i-1)\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathrm{vote},i}(T)]\right)^2} {2\sum_i((T-1)(2p_i-1)^2+4)} \right). \]

For a high-multiplicity instance with \(N\mu_t\) agents of each type, this becomes \(1-\exp(-NM^2/(2V))\), where \(V=\sum_t\mu_t[H(2p_t-1)^2+4]\). One could ask for this certificate from the type distribution, or for the minimum replication size needed to reach a target certificate. It is a precise, finite-support calculation, but the paper does not analyze its computational complexity.

These candidates stay close to the authors’ model: they preserve confidence-based abstention, calibration histories, and majority voting, while representing repeated agents by type masses. They continuize the population, not the outcome space. Their expected direction is tractable by summing over types and calibration counts, with running time depending on \(\tau\) and \(H\), subject to the cost of evaluating the Beta tails. They raise follow-up questions about gate design under participation constraints, finite-population rounding, and robustness when votes are correlated.

The weak point is substantial: the proposed continuum vote is a deterministic mass flow built from type-level vote probabilities, while the paper studies random votes by finitely many agents. Theorem 3 makes the limiting connection plausible, but does not formulate or compute this continuum object. Also, large reviewer pools or replica ensembles only fit the model if agents within each type truly share all relevant parameters and their errors are independent. So this is a credible direction to offer the authors, but the paper’s named results do not provide the computational anchor required for a full positive case under the stated rules.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Corollary 2 are probability bounds; Theorem 3 is an asymptotic convergence result. None poses a computational problem, gives an algorithm with a complexity guarantee, or studies how the running time depends on the population. The proponent acknowledges this gap, and their proposed calculations do not fill it.

The finite-support mirror is mathematically coherent. Grouping agents by their complete parameter tuple—competence, prior, critical level, and abstention threshold—is a sensible high-multiplicity model. For each type, the publish probability \(q_t\) follows from a sum over calibration success counts, and the expected signed vote mass is \(M=\sum_t\mu_t(2p_t-1)q_t\). But this is a type-by-type evaluation of the paper’s existing formulas, not a computational question the paper poses. The certificate likewise follows by substitution; its dependence on electorate size can be inverted directly to find a sufficient replication count. Theorem 1 supplies the probability analysis, while the proposed continuum calculation repackages it as mass arithmetic.

Theorem 3’s proposed mirror has the same gap. In the independent-replica scenario, the continuum records the limiting signed vote mass, and the theorem already gives a finite-population convergence guarantee under its assumptions. Asking whether the continuum mass has the correct sign is a well-defined decision question, but the paper does not make it an algorithmic task. It introduces no search over possible interventions or configurations: the type distribution and gate are fixed. This is not a novelty collision with an existing continuous computational treatment; it is a mismatch between the paper’s analytical contribution and ChoCo’s computational brief.

A stronger follow-on might choose abstention thresholds or allocate calibration rounds across types to maximize accuracy subject to a coverage requirement. That could be worthwhile research, but it adds design variables and constraints absent from the paper. It would be a new mechanism-design problem inspired by the model, not a continuous mirror of either theorem. Any computational challenge would depend on those additions.

The negative case is weak if read as a universal claim that no plausible continuum society exists. Large reviewer pools or independent model replicas with shared calibration profiles are plausible, and neither identity nor fractional mass blocks the model. The case is stronger and narrower: the proposed mirrors preserve the paper’s population setting, but they turn its analytical formulas into evaluations rather than exposing a computational problem that the paper itself contributes.
