The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 2.3

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 2.3 — survived: The theorem is a named approximation result; its limitation on finding a good allocation does not defeat the value-only mirror.

The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) item classes, \(\tau\) complete valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), nonnegative per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\), and compactly represented valuations \(v_t\) that are sums of weighted matroid rank functions, let \(x_{t,S}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(S\subseteq[m]\). Require \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le\rho_j\). Can the optimal mass Nash social welfare value \(\exp(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S))\), defined as zero if positive mass receives a zero-valued bundle, be approximated within a constant factor in time polynomial in the input size, including \(m\), \(\tau\), and the valuation descriptions?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic cohort with complete valuation types, rational mass \(\mu_t\), and per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\). Decision variables distribute each type’s mass across integral bundles; the objective is the mass-weighted geometric mean of bundle values.

The objection that survived

The continuous configuration LP introduces an exponential bundle-pricing problem that the paper’s individual-item relaxation does not resolve, so the theorem motivates this mirror but does not provide an algorithm for it.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers approximation of NSW value for sums of weighted matroid rank functions, including coverage. It does not establish efficient extraction of a high-value allocation or cover the paper’s matching-based and higher-dimensional matching valuation results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the configuration LP’s pricing problem for compact coverage or matroid-sum types, \(\max_{S\subseteq[m]}(\log v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j)\), be solved in polynomial time?
  • If pricing is hard, what approximation or hardness bounds hold for estimating the optimal mass-NSW value?
  • Can one efficiently compute a mass allocation achieving a constant-factor guarantee, beyond estimating the optimum value?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a coverage and rank valuation version of Mass-NSW. Theorem 2.3, proved in this paper, is the best anchor: for Nash social welfare with valuations that are sums of weighted matroid rank functions—including coverage valuations—the authors prove a factor \(c=\frac{1}{e}(1-\frac{1}{e})^2\) guarantee from their relaxation and Rounding 2. More precisely, their proof bounds the expected product of valuations by \(c^n\) times the relaxation objective, implying an allocation with geometric-mean welfare at least \(c\) times optimum. This is a guarantee about the *value*: the paper says it does not know how to find such an allocation in polynomial time. [Theorem 2.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.02278)

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. An instance has \(m\) item classes, a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_j\) for the item classes. Each type \(t\) has a valuation \(v_t\) that is a sum of weighted matroid rank functions, with all its matroids and weights included in the type description; coverage valuations are included. Seek a Mass-NSW allocation \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq[m]\), subject to
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq[m]}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le \rho_j \quad\text{for each }j. \]
Maximize
\[ \exp\!\left(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S)\right), \]
with zero-valued assignments treated in the usual way. The solution is a distribution of mass across integral bundles: each individual receives an indivisible bundle, while a type’s mass may be split among different bundles. A natural complexity question is whether one can approximate this optimum, and produce a mass allocation achieving the approximation, in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) for compactly represented matroid-sum or coverage valuations.

The regime is a large recurring allocation of many copies of standardized goods to a large cohort: people in the same segment have the same full valuation description, and each good class has a stable supply per person. The population size \(N\) is large relative to the number \(\tau\) of such types. This is a substantive high-multiplicity regime: it does not assume everyone has identical preferences, and it preserves the paper’s Nash objective and integral bundles for individuals.

I expect Class A when \(m\) is fixed or bundles are explicitly listed: the formulation is then a linear program over type-bundle assignments. For compact inputs with variable \(m\), I would expect the pricing problem to be the bottleneck and suspect continuum-specific hardness may remain. The dual asks, for each type, which bundle best balances \(\log v_t(S)\) against item prices. That retains combinatorics in the item set even though population mass is continuous. I would not claim hardness transfers without a reduction. The natural next questions are whether pricing is tractable for coverage or matroid-sum types, whether the continuous optimum gives useful bounds for finite high-multiplicity instances, and whether one can find a good mass allocation efficiently as well as estimate its value.

The paper’s introduction mentions NP-hardness and APX-hardness for additive NSW, citing prior work, but it gives no numbered hardness theorem for those claims. Theorem 2.3 is therefore a positive algorithmic anchor, proved here. It does not assert that an efficient allocation algorithm exists.

The weak point is the co-modification of the goods: the paper has one copy of each item, whereas this mirror groups goods into classes with per-capita supplies. That is a meaningful change to the instance regime. The population remains the object being continuized, and individuals still receive integral bundles, so the model seems like a recognizable continuous analogue of the authors’ underlying NSW problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 2.3 is a loose fit for the proposed computational task. The paper proves a constant-factor estimate of the optimum value, but says it does not know how to find a comparably good allocation in polynomial time. Its rounding argument yields an existence guarantee; it does not supply the efficient allocation algorithm the proposed Mass-NSW question asks for. [The paper’s abstract and publication record](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/1.9781611976465.69)

A tighter mirror could ask only for the continuous optimum’s value. But in the proposed model, the log objective is linear in the type-and-bundle masses, so this is a configuration LP. With bundles listed, or \(m\) fixed, it is straightforward. With compact coverage or matroid-sum valuations, the open issue is pricing over exponentially many bundles. That is a real computational question, but the paper’s stable-polynomial and contention-resolution arguments do not settle it. The link from Theorem 2.3 to this pricing problem is therefore an extrapolation, not a transferred result.

That gap does not defeat the better version of the mirror. Scaling supplies with population is necessary to avoid a degenerate limit in which most agents get nothing; the programme explicitly allows such co-modification. Integral bundles remain intact, and the mass objective is exactly the weighted geometric mean for a high-multiplicity cohort with repeated types. High-multiplicity Nash-welfare work also confirms that this is a sensible regime, though its existence supports the mirror rather than colliding with it. [Nguyen, Elbassioni, and Rothe on high-multiplicity social welfare](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/FAIA240881)

So the negative case can challenge how directly the theorem anchors the proposed algorithmic question, but it cannot identify a fundamental obstruction to a continuous mirror. I would call the case against weak: the proposed model is credible, and its unresolved pricing problem is itself a worthwhile computational question.
