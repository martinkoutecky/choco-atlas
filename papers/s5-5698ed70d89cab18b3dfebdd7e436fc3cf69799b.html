The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates, a scoring vector \(\alpha\), rational sincere mass \(\mu\) over complete rankings, rational strategic mass \(\lambda\), and preferred candidate \(p\), decide whether nonnegative masses \(x_r\) over rankings satisfying \(\sum_r x_r=\lambda\) can be assigned so that \(p\) is a winner, or uniquely wins, after adding \(x\) to \(\mu\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity strategic-voter model whose types are complete rankings together with strategic eligibility, whose mass variables \(x_r\) distribute the coalition across integral rankings, and whose objective is feasibility of the linear scoring inequalities; equivalently, solve the resulting LP using a ranking-pricing oracle.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the fixed-\(m\) winner and unique-winner weighted manipulation results of Theorem 2.1; it leaves Theorem 3.1's unbounded-candidate extension and the paper's ancillary observations untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can winner and unique-winner versions be solved exactly in time polynomial in \(m\) and the encoding length of \(\mu\) and \(\lambda\)?
  • What rounding guarantees convert a rational continuous solution into a finite election with unit voters?
  • Which indivisibility, quota, or eligibility restrictions restore NP-hardness?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous mirror of the paper’s weighted manipulation problem, anchored in Theorem 2.1 (the lead anchor). This theorem is proved in the paper, not merely cited.

Theorem 2.1 states that, for every fixed number \(m\) of candidates and every scoring vector \(\alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m)\), both weighted manipulation \(MP_\alpha\) and unique-winner weighted manipulation \(UMP_\alpha\) are in \(P\) when \(\alpha_2=\cdots=\alpha_m\), and are NP-complete in every other case. Here \(MP_\alpha\) asks whether a set of weighted strategic voters can cast their votes so that a preferred candidate \(p\) is a winner; \(UMP_\alpha\) asks whether they can make \(p\) the unique winner. The paper proves the hard cases by reducing from PARTITION, using the binary weights of the strategic voters to force an exact subset-sum balance.

I would mirror this with the following problem.

Continuous Weighted Manipulation\({}_\infty\). Fix \(m\), candidates \(C\), and a scoring vector \(\alpha\). An instance consists of a rational distribution \(\mu\) of sincere population mass over complete rankings of \(C\), a rational mass \(\lambda\) of strategic voters, and a preferred candidate \(p\). A solution is a collection of nonnegative masses \(x_r\), one for each ranking \(r\), satisfying \(\sum_r x_r=\lambda\). The resulting society has mass \(\mu_r+x_r\) on ranking \(r\). The question is whether some such \(x\) makes \(p\) a winner under \(\alpha\). The unique-winner version requires \(p\) to have strictly greater score than every other candidate.

The type is a complete ranking, together with the information that the voter belongs to the manipulable coalition. Mass is the fraction of society having that ranking, not a fractional ballot: every infinitesimal voter still submits one complete integral ranking. The action is to distribute the strategic mass among final ranking types. The objective is feasibility, exactly as in \(MP_\alpha\); an optimization version could instead minimize the strategic mass \(\lambda\).

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s problem. Consider a large election involving many repeated voter blocs—party members, organizational chapters, local constituencies, or demographic cohorts—with only a relatively small number of distinct rankings. A large coordinated coalition can determine how its members vote, while individual identities are irrelevant once their ranking and strategic status are fixed. The number of voters can be enormous while the number of sincere ranking types is at most \(m!\), and often much smaller. This is not an outcome-space relaxation: rankings remain discrete. Only the population of voters becomes divisible, exactly as in the proposed continuization programme.

The authors should recognise this as their problem rather than a simplified substitute. The candidates, scoring rule, preferred candidate, coalition, winner convention, and strategic choice of ballots are all retained. The only change is that a large collection of strategically controlled voters is represented by its mass distribution instead of by an explicitly listed collection of weighted voters. Indeed, the proof of Theorem 2.1 itself makes the relevance clear: its hardness construction encodes PARTITION in the binary weights of the strategic voters.

I expect Continuous Weighted Manipulation\({}_\infty\) to be in Class A for every scoring vector. For a fixed sincere distribution, write the score of candidate \(c\) after manipulation as a linear function of the variables \(x_r\). The requirements that \(p\) beat every other candidate are therefore linear inequalities. This gives an LP over ranking types. Although there are \(m!\) possible rankings, the pricing problem for a candidate ranking is polynomial: for any dual weights on the opponent constraints, one optimizes a linear assignment of candidates to score positions, which can be solved by sorting or a standard assignment algorithm. Thus the exponential family of possible manipulator ballots has a tractable pricing oracle.

The expected result is therefore a sharp continuum-specific reversal of Theorem 2.1: the discrete problem is NP-complete for every non-plurality-like scoring rule, but the high-multiplicity version is plausibly polynomial for all scoring rules. The PARTITION gadget no longer forces an indivisible choice of whole strategic voters into two sides. A mass of voters can be split fractionally between the two relevant rankings, so the exact subset-sum obstruction disappears. The continuous algorithm would also provide a natural rounding question: how much additional strategic mass is required to convert a rational continuous solution into an election with finitely many individual voters?

Further questions would include whether the same result holds for unique winners, how the running time depends on \(m\), the number of sincere types, and the encoding length of the masses, and which restrictions restore hardness. In particular, one could impose indivisible strategic blocs, type-dependent eligibility, quota constraints, or voter-specific costs. Those variants would test whether the NP-hardness returns when the population is no longer genuinely divisible. The paper’s Theorem 3.1 also suggests studying the corresponding mirror for a polynomial-time generated scoring function with an unbounded number of candidates, but that is not needed for the main case.

The weakest point is that the mirror deliberately removes the indivisibility exploited by the paper’s reduction. A referee could argue that manipulation is about choosing one complete vote for each strategic voter, whereas my model permits an arbitrarily fine split of a coalition across rankings. That objection would be valid if the strategic agents were intended to be a small number of indivisible institutional blocs. The case survives when the agents are instead a genuinely large coalition of ordinary voters: fractional mass then records different subgroups making different coordinated choices. This is precisely the high-multiplicity regime, and the disappearance of the PARTITION obstruction is not a defect but the central computational phenomenon the continuization programme is designed to investigate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative objection is that the proposed mirror does not really continuize the weighted manipulation problem; it convexifies the manipulators’ action set.

The hardness in Theorem 2.1 is carried by individually listed strategic voters with binary, indivisible weights. In the reduction, voter \(i\) has weight proportional to \(k_i\), and the manipulation must place that whole block on one ballot. The proposed variables \(x_r\) allow that block to be split arbitrarily across rankings. Thus the PARTITION gadget disappears by construction: the model has replaced “choose one ballot for each weighted bloc” with “choose a fractional mixture of ballots.” If the intended agents are parties, counties, or institutional blocs whose votes must remain coordinated, this is not their manipulation problem.

The same objection applies to the proposed story of a large coalition unless one is explicit about what the agents are. A genuinely large population of ordinary voters may be divided among rankings, but then the paper’s weighted voters have been reinterpreted as many independent unit voters. That is a legitimate new high-multiplicity problem, but it is not a preservation of the paper’s central indivisibility assumption. The LP is then primarily a convex-hull computation over possible collective ballots, with the sincere society entering only through fixed score offsets.

However, this does not defeat the best possible mirror. The programme explicitly treats high multiplicity as a relaxation in which repeated agents can be represented by mass. A coalition of many ordinary voters with the same strategic status and common target \(p\) is a coherent type population. A rational solution \(x_r\) can be expanded into finitely many unit voters, each still casting an integral ranking. The fact that the continuous version destroys the binary subset-sum obstruction is precisely the sort of Class A reversal the programme seeks.

Nor do the technical details rescue the negative case. The strategic mass can be incorporated into one normalized society by adding an “eligible/uncommitted” type. Unique-winner manipulation can be expressed through a positive score margin. The pricing problem is genuinely polynomial: a dual objective over rankings is an assignment problem. Theorem 3.1’s unbounded-candidate setting does not change that.

So the honest conclusion is that the negative case is weak. One can argue that the proposed formulation is a poor mirror of manipulation by indivisible weighted blocs, but one cannot defend the universal claim that no sensible population regime exists. Theorem 2.1 supplies a particularly strong continuous-population question: does the NP-completeness caused by binary coalition weights disappear when those weights represent a high-multiplicity population? That is a worthwhile mirror even if its answer is an LP in \(P\).
