The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result. My lead is the closest named computational-design anchor: Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that for a trading network with one possible trade between two players, if the type spaces are non-trivial—some type pair makes the trade’s total value negative, and for every possible type of either player there is a type of the other player that makes it positive—then no mechanism can simultaneously be DSIC, efficient, ex-post weakly budget balanced, and ex-post individually rational.

The mirror I believe in is a large market for a standardized good: many small sellers and buyers each handle at most one unit, and each has one of a few possible costs or values. A seller’s type is their unit cost \(c\); a buyer’s type is their unit value \(v\). The society is given by type masses \(\mu^S_c\) and \(\mu^B_v\), each summing to one on its side. This is plausible for repeated commodity or procurement markets with many small participants and a short list of recurring cost and value bands. The number of participants is large while the number of distinct types is small. Mass records population shares; each individual still gets either an integral trade or no trade.

Call the question Atomless Bilateral Trading Implementation. Its instance consists of finite rational type sets \(\mathcal C,\mathcal V\) satisfying the non-triviality conditions above, and a reported mass profile \((\mu^S,\mu^B)\) over those types. The task is to give one direct mechanism defined for every such mass profile. It must choose a feasible matching flow \(x_{cv}\), where \(x_{cv}\) is the mass of sellers of cost \(c\) matched to buyers of value \(v\), and per-agent transfers to the independent party. Feasibility requires \(\sum_v x_{cv}\leq\mu^S_c\) and \(\sum_c x_{cv}\leq\mu^B_v\). The allocation must maximize \(\sum_{c,v}(v-c)x_{cv}\). Transfers and allocation must also make truthful reporting a dominant strategy for each individual, leave each truthful agent nonnegative utility ex post, and give the independent party nonnegative total receipts for every population profile. For DSIC comparisons, an individual has zero mass, so changing that person’s report leaves the reported population distribution unchanged.

This question has a direct construction. Sort sellers by cost and buyers by value, then match the maximum-surplus mass. If trade occurs, let \(c^*\) be the highest cost among selected sellers and \(v^*\) the lowest value among selected buyers; efficiency gives \(c^*\leq v^*\). Select all strictly lower-cost sellers and strictly higher-value buyers, rationing the marginal types as needed. Pay each selected seller \(c^*\) and charge each selected buyer \(v^*\), with no transfer for non-traders. A seller of cost \(c\) then gets \(c^*-c\geq0\) per trade; a buyer of value \(v\) gets \(v-v^*\geq0\). Each agent faces a fixed cutoff because their report cannot move the aggregate cutoffs: reporting below the seller cutoff or above the buyer cutoff does not change the relevant payment, while crossing the cutoff in the wrong direction cannot help. The party’s receipts are \(Q(v^*-c^*)\geq0\), where \(Q\) is traded mass. Thus the continuous question looks tractable by sorting the finite support and filling mass to the cutoffs. Fractional allocation by type means selecting a fraction of that type’s agents, not giving anyone a fractional unit.

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s bilateral-trade impossibility, not the paper’s full trading-network model or its later mechanism-learning experiments. The main follow-up is how far this construction extends to networks with multiple contract classes, capacity interactions, or general valuation functions. Another is the finite bridge: with \(n\) agents, one person can affect reported masses and cutoffs, so the construction does not give exact DSIC for finite replications; a useful companion problem is to quantify the best approximate incentive guarantee as \(n\) grows.

The weak point is that Theorem 2 studies exactly two firms and one potential trade, while this mirror has atomless populations of sellers and buyers and matches them in one market. That is a meaningful high-multiplicity trading regime, but it changes the strategic force of a report: no individual is pivotal in the continuum. I would present it as asking whether the paper’s impossibility survives that natural large-market extension, not as a limit theorem for the paper’s two-player mechanism.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the anchor is about fit with ChoCo’s target. Theorem 2 is a mechanism-existence impossibility result, not a complexity result. The paper’s computational work is its LP over Groves pivot rules and its learning approximation. The proposed mass-market mechanism does not continuize that computation.

The gap is clearest in the network structure. Theorem 2 has one seller, one buyer, and one contract. Replicating that network gives separate bilateral pairs; pooling their participants so any seller can trade with any buyer creates a different, shared market. For the paper’s general networks, a histogram of firm types also loses which contracts each firm can make and the joint type information used by \(h_i(v_{-i})\) and the interim constraints. Encoding each firm’s network position in its type can make the support grow with the network.

The proposed pooled market is still a coherent and plausible adjacent problem. With finitely many seller and buyer types, its efficient mass allocation is a transportation or flow problem, and cutoff transfers give a natural implementation when each individual has zero influence on market totals. Continuum-economy work on strategy-proof mechanisms provides relevant background, though it does not by itself settle the computational question ([Hammond, “Multilaterally Strategy-Proof Mechanisms in Random Aumann–Hildenbrand Macroeconomies”](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/staff/pjhammond/privateinformationandincentiveconstraints/aumtor.pdf)).

That leaves a weak negative case: the clean mirror is a simple large-market double auction, while a closer mirror of the paper’s harder computation runs into network and joint-distribution structure that a type-mass vector cannot capture. I cannot honestly rule out that the double-auction question is worth studying; the atomless bilateral anchor survives as a sensible question, even if it seems peripheral to ChoCo’s complexity programme.
