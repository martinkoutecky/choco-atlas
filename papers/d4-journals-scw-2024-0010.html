Its results are empirical comparisons of RESPA and Majority Judgment before and after deliberation. So there is no eligible anchor for a continuous mirror under the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a paper-anchored mirror is strong: the paper reports no theorem or other named computational result to classify. Its main findings are empirical comparisons: rankings before and after deliberation, and larger observed rank changes under RESPA than under Majority Judgment. Representing respondents by masses of complete rating types would change the population model, but would not turn those findings into a computational problem. Given the types, one can sum RESPA scores and compute MJ medians and gauges directly. That calculation may be easy, but the more fundamental point is that the paper poses no algorithmic question for it to answer.

The harder mirror to dismiss is about deliberation. A distribution over individual rating types could reproduce the pre-deliberation aggregates in a large public consultation. It cannot, by itself, represent the paper’s central comparison: after deliberation, groups produce consensus rankings. Those outcomes depend on who deliberates together and how they interact. A type containing only a person’s grades omits that information; a type containing the whole group’s composition and discussion history makes the group, rather than an interchangeable individual, the unit. One could instead collect individual ratings after deliberation and model before-and-after transitions, but that would require a different study and data the paper did not collect.

A continuous robustness or manipulation problem is the strongest proposed extension. The paper discusses strategic manipulation as a property of aggregation rules, so it is not an arbitrary connection. But finding the least mass of voters, at specified costs, that would change an MJ or RESPA ranking introduces an intervention objective and cost model absent from the study. It would be a related new project, not a computational mirror of the paper’s reported result.

So the negative case is persuasive under the programme’s paper-anchoring standard: there is no computational result here to mirror, and the most plausible computational questions add a new objective or lose the deliberative process the paper studies. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous project could arise in any scenario is weaker. Large stakeholder populations are plausible, and these aggregation rules could support a separate continuous robustness study. That possibility is a reason not to overstate the negative verdict; it does not supply this paper with an eligible computational anchor.
