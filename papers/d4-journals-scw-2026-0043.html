The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(E\subseteq D\times L\times G\), rational vote masses \(\mu_e>0\) summing to \(1\), a seat supply \(h>0\) per unit of population, feasible target marginal seat masses \(m_{i,v}\) summing to \(h\) in each dimension, and rational cell bounds \(a_e\le b_e\), compute the minimizer \(x\) of \(\sum_{e\in E}x_e\log(x_e/\mu_e)-x_e\), subject to \(\sum_{e:e_i=v}x_e=m_{i,v}\) and \(a_e\le x_e\le b_e\).

The model it lives in

Types are supported vote categories \(e=(d,\ell,g)\) carrying population mass \(\mu_e\). The decision variable \(x_e\) is seat mass assigned to category \(e\), with per-capita seat supply \(h\), prescribed marginal seat masses, and cell bounds; the objective is the entropy projection of vote mass onto those constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s bounded approximate apportionment guarantee and the proportional allocation tensor used by the paper’s methods. It leaves the axiomatic characterization in Theorem 1, named-candidate selection, and the empirical comparisons of representativeness and voting power outside the model.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove finite-population convergence and error bounds when votes, seat supply, marginals, and cell bounds scale with population size \(N\).
  • Give bit-complexity guarantees for approximating the bounded entropy minimizer on sparse support.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow: the paper’s bounded 3-proportional apportionment problem, rather than the whole process of electing named candidates. It is a plausible high-multiplicity problem when a large federation or membership organization elects a delegate body whose size grows with membership. Members can be grouped by district and the candidate or slate they support; the supported candidate’s list and gender determine the corresponding apportionment cell. The number of members \(N\) can then be enormous relative to the number \(\tau\) of ballot types. Candidate gender here labels the seats being apportioned; it need not be a voter attribute.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper as an extension of earlier work. For a \(d\)-dimensional integer vote tensor \(V\), house size \(H\), prescribed marginals \(m\), and cell bounds \(I,U\), it says that if the linear relaxation of (10)–(15) is feasible, then for any nonnegative integer deviations \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d\) satisfying \(\sum_i 1/(\alpha_i+2)\le 1\), there exists an \(\alpha\)-approximate \(d\)-proportional apportionment. Its cell sums lie within \(\alpha_i\) of the prescribed marginals, and each cell allocation is a median of its proportionally scaled vote under the paper’s rounding rule and the cell bounds. For \(d=3\), the paper gives, among other choices, \((1,1,1)\) and \((0,1,4)\). The proof extends a prior discrepancy-based approach using an optimal solution to the LP relaxation. This is a constructive existence result, not a named polynomial-time classification; the paper reports that the apportionments can be found using LP techniques. The paper has no comparable hardness anchor, so this is the strongest computational anchor I would use.

I would hand a prover the following question, Continuous Bounded 3-Proportional Apportionment. Its input is a finite support \(E\subseteq D\times L\times G\), rational vote masses \(\mu_e>0\) summing to \(1\), a rational seat mass \(h>0\) per unit of population, target marginal seat masses \(m_{i,v}\) whose sum is \(h\) in each dimension, and rational cell bounds \(a_e\le b_e\). Assume the bounded marginal polytope is nonempty. The task is to compute the unique vector \(x\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{e\in E}x_e\log\!\left(\frac{x_e}{\mu_e}\right)-x_e \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{e:e_i=v}x_e=m_{i,v} \quad\text{for every dimension }i\text{ and value }v, \qquad a_e\le x_e\le b_e. \]
Here \(x_e\) is the mass of representative seats assigned to cell \(e\). Without active bounds, the optimizer has the paper’s multiplicative scaling form \(x_e=\mu_e\prod_i\lambda_{i,e_i}\); bounds constrain that fair-share allocation in the same cellwise way that the paper’s bounded rounding does.

For a finite population of size \(N\), take roughly \(N\mu_e\) votes, \(Nh\) seats, and bounds and marginals scaled by \(N\). Each actual seat remains indivisible, while \(x_e\) is the limiting allocation per unit of population. Theorem 2’s fixed additive marginal errors become at most \(\alpha_i/N\) after normalization. That makes this a direct high-multiplicity limit of its apportionment question, with the paper’s rounding deviations disappearing at scale. The expected direction is Class A for approximate computation: the mirror is a convex entropy minimization problem over a sparse tensor. A useful precise complexity target is an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation algorithm parameterized by \(|D|,|L|,|G|,\tau\), input bit length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\).

This scope covers the bounded apportionment tensor used by the paper’s proportional methods, including threshold-style cell bounds. It does not by itself solve the subsequent choice of named candidates. In particular, a plurality guarantee of one seat per district becomes negligible after normalization; preserving that feature would require a separate model for distinguished candidates or seats. Further questions include proving convergence and finite-\(N\) error bounds, establishing bit complexity for sparse instances, and determining which lower bounds survive in the limit.

The weakest point is that the paper already aggregates votes into tensors and defines a fractional fair-share tensor. For a legislature with fixed house size, merely rewriting vote counts as proportions could look like normalization rather than a new continuous-population problem. The stronger case depends on the delegate regime: population and representative supply both scale, and the question is the complexity of the resulting bounded allocation over a small support of types. That is a real co-modification of the model, but one the authors could plausibly recognize as the continuum limit of their own apportionment problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the wrong anchor for a complexity mirror. It proves an existence guarantee for an integral apportionment, conditional on feasibility of an LP relaxation; it gives no complexity classification. The paper’s claims about finding apportionments with LP techniques are computationally relevant, but the theorem itself does not establish a new algorithmic boundary for the programme to chart.

The proposed continuous problem is also very close to work the paper already does. Without cell bounds, its entropy minimizer is the fair-share tensor defined in Section 2 and characterized in Theorem 1; the paper says it can be computed by convex optimization. Adding box bounds leaves a convex optimization problem in the \(\tau\) supported cells, with linear marginal constraints. That may be a useful application, but it does not expose the sort of hard pricing problem or complexity landscape that motivates the programme. Theorem 2’s distinctive contribution is the integer rounding guarantee. When seats scale with population, its constant additive deviations become \(O(1/N)\) and disappear in the limit; the proposed entropy problem is then a different, already familiar continuous calculation.

Keeping the house size fixed gives a more faithful election model: voters have ballot types, their masses vary continuously, and seats remain indivisible. But for rational masses this is the same apportionment input expressed as vote shares rather than counts. The apportionment methods in the paper use those relative vote quantities, so this change in representation does not itself supply a new computational question. Including named candidate choices in the types is a coherent repair for plurality, but it does not alter that point.

That is a credible case against this mirror, not a proof that no worthwhile mirror exists. A continuous robustness question about how much ballot mass must move to change a seat allocation could be meaningful, but the paper does not study robustness. I cannot honestly rule out such a new question; it simply would not rescue Theorem 2 as the computational anchor offered here.
