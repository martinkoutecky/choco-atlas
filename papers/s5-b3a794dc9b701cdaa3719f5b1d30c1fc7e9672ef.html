The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror candidate is the static, bounded-length kidney-exchange optimization that the paper computes with its IP model. Take a large regional or national registry. A type is a donor–patient pair’s complete description for this problem: its clinically relevant compatibility properties, its utility-relevant properties, and therefore its compatibility and utility with every other type. Altruistic donors have their own types. Let \(T\) be the supported types, with mass \(\mu_t\) for each type, where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). The instance also gives the directed compatibility relations and arc utilities between types.

Let \(\mathcal C_k\) be all feasible cycles and altruist-rooted chains respecting the paper’s length cap \(k\). For each pattern \(C\), let \(a_{tC}\) count how many participants of type \(t\) it uses, and let \(q_C\) be its total utility. The continuous problem is to choose masses \(x_C\ge 0\) of each pattern:

\[ \max \sum_{C\in\mathcal C_k} q_C x_C \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{C\in\mathcal C_k} a_{tC}x_C \le \mu_t \quad\text{for every }t\in T. \]

The solution reports the exchange-pattern masses \(x_C\). With every compatible arc worth one, the objective counts transplants per unit of registry mass, matching the paper’s stated objective. Each infinitesimal participant still belongs to a whole exchange; fractional values describe the share of each type assigned to each pattern.

This is a plausible large-registry regime if many pairs share exact compatibility and utility profiles, so that the number of agents grows much faster than the number \(\tau=|T|\) of supported types. The paper already treats a pool as a compatibility graph and optimizes over node-disjoint cycles and chains. Its Eq. (20) gives the direct bridge: replace the binary selection of each pattern with a mass variable, and replace each individual’s capacity of one with the mass available in that type. That preserves the paper’s exchange objective, compatibility constraints, and operational length cap.

For fixed \(k\), including the paper’s experimental choice \(k=3\), this candidate is expected to be tractable: enumerate the type-level patterns and solve the resulting rational LP. The enumeration has polynomial size in \(\tau\) for fixed \(k\). If \(k\) is input, that argument no longer gives a polynomial bound. Further questions include whether finite registries can round these masses with useful guarantees, and how the model handles dynamic arrivals or failure risk. Those extensions are not needed for this static mirror.

The mirror covers the paper’s bounded-cycle and chain objective, including its type-to-type utility weights. It does not mirror ALO itself, its runtime comparisons, or the paper’s dynamic-environment suggestions. The weakest point is the type regime: exact HLA and crossmatch distinctions could make nearly every pair a distinct type. The case is strongest where a large registry really does contain repeated complete compatibility profiles; otherwise the continuous model may be mathematically clear but not a useful high-multiplicity account of the paper’s instances.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the attached proposal is that it has no qualifying result to mirror. The paper gives Algorithm 4, an integer-programming formulation in Eq. (20), and empirical comparisons, but no numbered theorem or other result establishing a complexity class or algorithmic guarantee. Its NP-completeness statement in §1.1 is background attributed to reference [25]. Under the stated anchor test, the proposed LP is a new question built from the paper’s benchmark formulation, not a continuous mirror of a result the paper proves.

There is also a practical weakness in the proposed type compression. A clinically faithful type must capture the features that determine compatibility and utility, including tissue compatibility and patient-specific priorities. If those distinctions are collapsed, the type-level cycles can misstate which exchanges are feasible; if they are retained, the number of types may grow close to the number of pairs. Kidney-exchange pools have been described as moderate and thin, with many highly sensitized patients, which makes a small-support, high-multiplicity regime a contingent fit for the paper’s setting ([sparse, heterogeneous pools](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3509)). The paper’s experiments do not report the number of repeated complete types, so they do not establish that the compression would help on its instances.

That objection does not defeat the better version of the mirror. A large registry with repeated complete donor–patient profiles and known type-to-type compatibility could use the proposed cycle-mass LP, with each type’s mass limiting its participation in exchanges. Fractional pattern masses still describe whole transplants allocated across many participants. And existing work showing that real kidney-exchange graphs can sometimes be represented with few attributes makes this regime more credible, not less ([small representations of kidney-exchange graphs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.07728)). That work is not itself a continuous-population treatment, but it undercuts a blanket claim that useful type compression is implausible.

So the negative case is strongest on the assigned anchor standard: there is no formal computational result here that demands a mirror, and the paper’s experiments do not demonstrate high multiplicity. I cannot honestly extend that into the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The type-level mass LP remains a plausible continuous computational question in a suitable registry regime.
