The continuous mirror question

Given α and a rational finite-support distribution μ over voter types ⁣(β,δ,a)⁣, with the paper's competitive-path uniqueness assumption, determine the time-τ equilibrium shares ⁣(θ,λ)⁣ under its separate-majority voting rule. Let ⁣κ_μ⁣ and ⁣χ_μ⁣ be the μ-weighted medians of ⁣κ(β,δ)=δ(1-β)/[(1+δ)(1-β+αβ)]⁣ and ⁣χ(β,δ)=(1+δ)(1-α)β/(1+δβ)⁣. Return the equilibrium pair given by ⁣θ=(κ_μ-κ_μχ_μ)/(1-κ_μχ_μ)⁣ and ⁣λ=(χ_μ-κ_μχ_μ)/(1-κ_μχ_μ)⁣, or all equilibrium pairs when the weighted medians are nonunique.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution ⁣μ⁣ over complete voter types ⁣(β,δ,a)⁣, where ⁣a⁣ records initial capital holdings and ⁣μ_t⁣ is each type's population mass. The policy variables remain the shares ⁣θ⁣ and ⁣λ⁣. The output is the equilibrium pair calculated from the mass-weighted medians of ⁣κ⁣ and ⁣χ⁣; capital holdings do not affect these shares under Theorem 1's assumptions.

The objection that survived

A mass distribution can have nonunique weighted medians, so the continuous equilibrium need not be unique as in the finite odd-electorate result; restricting to unique medians or returning all equilibrium pairs addresses this.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a rubric-compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational-complexity result. Its numbered results concern equilibrium existence, uniqueness, and characterization.

The closest candidate is Theorem 1, proved here. Under its assumption that the competitive equilibrium path is unique for each policy, it establishes a unique time-\(\tau\) voting equilibrium and gives its shares explicitly: \(\theta^*=(\kappa_{\mathrm{med}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{med}}\chi_{\mathrm{med}})/(1-\kappa_{\mathrm{med}}\chi_{\mathrm{med}})\) and \(\lambda^*=(\chi_{\mathrm{med}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{med}}\chi_{\mathrm{med}})/(1-\kappa_{\mathrm{med}}\chi_{\mathrm{med}})\), where the medians are over voters’ preference parameters. Theorem 5, proved here, says generalized intertemporal voting equilibria have those constant shares and exist from any non-degenerate initial state. Both are analytic results; neither classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard.

A population mirror is conceivable: treat agents sharing discount factor, public-consumption preference, and initial capital as one type, with mass equal to their population fraction. But asking for the resulting voting equilibrium would be an analytic extension of the paper’s question, not a computational mirror anchored in a named complexity result. So there is no honest per-anchor continuous computational problem to hand to a prover under the requested criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Its numbered results establish existence, uniqueness, and equilibrium characterization; none asks about an algorithm’s running time or classifies a computational problem. Theorems 1 and 5, the proponent’s anchors, are analytic results: Theorem 1 characterizes a time-\(\tau\) voting equilibrium under a uniqueness assumption, and Theorem 5 establishes generalized intertemporal equilibrium existence under a specified belief rule.

A population model is plausible here. One could group agents by their discount factor, public-consumption preference, and capital holdings, with mass as their population share. But that does not turn either theorem into a computational result. In the continuous version of Theorem 1, the policy shares would be characterized by weighted medians of the induced \(\kappa\) and \(\chi\) values. Asking an algorithm to compute those shares from a distribution is definable, but it adds an algorithmic task around the paper’s analytic characterization; it does not mirror a computational question the paper studied. Theorem 5 has the same problem: its contribution is existence from arbitrary initial states, while its shares remain those of Theorem 1. Asking for a computational treatment of the full economic trajectory would be a new equilibrium-computation project, not a mirror of the theorem.

So I would reject this paper as a ChoCo candidate on the stated gate, not on the plausibility of a high-multiplicity population or on whether the continuous answers differ. The negative case cannot prove that no worthwhile computational extension of this model could ever be invented. It can say that the extensions available here would add a computational problem to an analytic paper, rather than continuize one of its computational results.
