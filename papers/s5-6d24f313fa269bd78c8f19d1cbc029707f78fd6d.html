The continuous mirror question

Given \(p\) parties, finite type sets \(T_r\), rational type masses \(\mu_{r,t}\) summing to \(1\) within each party, strict rankings of other-party types, and a directed tree on the parties, decide whether a stable mass matching exists and output one if it does. A matching is an array \(x_{\mathbf t}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{\mathbf t:t_r=t}x_{\mathbf t}=\mu_{r,t}\); stability excludes any positive-mass regrouping in which every participant weakly prefers each proposed partner type to the incumbent and each strictly prefers at least one. What is the complexity of this search problem in \(p\), \(\tau=\sum_r|T_r|\), and the input bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A finite-support society for each party, represented by rational masses over complete preference types. Each unit of mass receives an integral family, while the aggregate family assignment \(x_{\mathbf t}\) may be fractional; members of one type have the same rankings of other-party types. The decision variables are the family masses, and the question is whether a stable assignment can be constructed efficiently.

The objection that survived

The paper’s strict lists distinguish named individuals, while the mirror makes members of a type interchangeable and introduces indifference; the tree-edge bijections and their composition may therefore fail for mass allocations.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the elemental stability and running-time results in Theorems 3 and 5. It does not cover Theorem 4’s count of elemental algorithms, the compound algorithm results in Theorems 6 and 7, or the listed open problems.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a stable mass matching always exist for these type rankings and the paper’s blocking condition?
  • Can the tree-based elemental construction be replaced by a polynomial-time mass-flow algorithm in \(p\), \(\tau\), and \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a narrow algorithmic mirror of Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says that an elemental algorithm for a \(p\)-party instance with \(n\) members in each party runs in \(O(pn^2)\) time: it runs Gale–Shapley once for each of the \(p-1\) directed edges of a tree, and the paper gives the bound \((p-1)(n^2-2n+2)\) on the total number of rounds. Its companion, Theorem 3, also proved here, says that every elemental algorithm produces a stable matching. The paper has no numbered hardness result for its own model; the cited hardness results concern other preference or stability variants.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is recurring team formation across several large professional groups. For example, many workers, employers, and training providers are grouped into recurring preference types, then matched into three-person placements. Each party might have hundreds of thousands of participants but only hundreds of distinct type descriptions. The type is a participant’s complete preference description for this market, and mass is the fraction of a party in that type. An individual placement still contains one member of each party; it is the aggregate assignment of the population that becomes divisible.

Here is a precise mirror to hand a prover. Call it Mass-\(p\) Stable Matching. For each party \(r\in\{1,\ldots,p\}\), the input gives a finite type set \(T_r\), rational masses \(\mu_{r,t}\) summing to \(1\), and each type’s strict ranking of the types in every other party. Members of the same type are indifferent among one another, so the ranking of types gives their complete preference over participants. The input also gives a directed tree on the parties, corresponding to the elemental algorithm.

A matching is a nonnegative mass array \(x_{\mathbf t}\), indexed by \(\mathbf t\in T_1\times\cdots\times T_p\). Here \(x_{\mathbf t}\) is the mass assigned to families with type tuple \(\mathbf t\). For every party \(r\) and type \(t\in T_r\), it must satisfy
\[ \sum_{\mathbf t:\,t_r=t}x_{\mathbf t}=\mu_{r,t}. \]
Thus every unit of mass is assigned exactly once.

Define stability by positive-mass blocking coalitions. A coalition can take mass from existing families, select one member of each party for each new family, and regroup them. It blocks if every selected member weakly prefers every proposed partner type to the incumbent partner type, and each selected member strictly prefers at least one proposed partner type. “No blocking family” in the continuum means no such regrouping of positive mass. A solution is a matching array \(x\) with no such blocking coalition. The prover’s question is whether, given the rational masses and rankings, one can construct such a stable matching efficiently in \(p\), \(\tau=\sum_r |T_r|\), and the input bit length \(L\), with a compact representation corresponding to the tree construction.

I expect this to be Class A, at least for fixed \(p\): the discrete algorithm’s work is repeated across individuals, while a type-level version could aggregate proposals and acceptances as mass flows. The tree is the useful structure, and the high-multiplicity regime is exactly where aggregating those flows might avoid work proportional to the population. Theorem 5 itself does not establish that compression; it gives the concrete algorithm and running time that make the question worth asking.

The paper’s theorem is a good, if modest, anchor because it concerns computing stable matchings, not merely describing a continuous outcome or proving an axiomatic fact. Its authors should recognize this as a mirror of their problem: the parties, families, stability condition, and tree-based construction remain central. The scope is only the elemental results. I would not use the paper’s cited hardness results for different \(3\)-party variants as anchors, or claim to mirror its open questions about optimal or egalitarian matchings.

The main soft spot is the move from strict rankings of named individuals to type rankings with indifference among members of a type. That is a real preference-model change, even if it is a natural consequence of making repeated participants indistinguishable. It also means Theorem 3’s discrete proof does not automatically carry over: its path argument uses strict individual rankings to infer that tree-edge partners must coincide. A continuous proof must handle ties and positive-mass blocks directly. That limitation keeps this from being a strong hardness-dissolution case, but it leaves a specific, recognizable computational question rather than a vague appeal to continuity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorems 3 and 5 depend on named individuals, while the proposed mirror gets its compression by replacing them with preference classes. In this paper, every agent strictly ranks every individual in each other party. If two people in a party are treated as the same type for stability, every other agent’s preferences must treat them identically too. But a strict list ranks those two people differently. So, under the programme’s requirement that a type preserve every distinction the problem uses, the parties do not have a small support of interchangeable types: the ranking structure distinguishes individuals.

The proponent’s market of workers, employers, and training providers repairs this by ranking *types* and treating members of a type as interchangeable. That is coherent, but it introduces indifference among individuals where the paper expressly uses strict lists. This is not a cosmetic change in the input. It changes the relationships the blocking condition tests. Theorem 3’s argument relies on Gale–Shapley producing bijections on tree edges, which can be composed to identify partners. In a mass allocation, a type’s mass can split across several counterpart types; those edge correspondences need not compose into a unique family. Theorem 5’s \(O(pn^2)\) bound, meanwhile, is the cost of running Gale–Shapley on named lists. The mass version needs a new matching process and a new stability analysis; the theorem supplies neither.

That makes the anchor weak as a mirror of this paper’s results. A continuous problem about stable allocation among preference classes may still be worthwhile, but the case against is that it would be a problem about a different preference and stability model, not the high-multiplicity version of the strict-list problem Theorems 3 and 5 analyze.

This is not an airtight case against *any* continuous mirror. The proposed placement market is plausible, and if the authors accept indifference within types as a natural version of their problem, it leaves a concrete question to study. The honest negative case is strongest against calling that question a mirror of this paper; it is weaker against spending research time on the related continuous matching problem.
