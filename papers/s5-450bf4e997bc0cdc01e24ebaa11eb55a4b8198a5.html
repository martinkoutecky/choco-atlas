The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational prejudices \(u_t\in\mathbb{Q}^d\), susceptibilities \(\lambda_t\in[0,1]\), nonnegative contact affinities \(a_{ts}\) with \(\sum_r a_{tr}\mu_r>0\), and a shared rational matrix \(C\), set \(p^\mu_{ts}=a_{ts}\mu_s/\sum_r a_{tr}\mu_r\), \(P^\mu=(p^\mu_{ts})\), and \(\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_t)\). Under the stability promise \(\rho(\Lambda P^\mu)\rho(C)<1\), compute each limiting type opinion \(x_t^*\) from \(x_t^*=\lambda_t C\sum_s p^\mu_{ts}x_s^*+(1-\lambda_t)u_t\), and the aggregate \(\bar{x}^*=\sum_t\mu_t x_t^*\).

The model it lives in

A population distribution \(\mu\) over complete behavioral types, each carrying an opinion vector, prejudice, susceptibility, and contact affinities. Type masses determine the row-stochastic contact matrix \(P^\mu\); the decision is to compute the stable equilibrium by solving the resulting rational linear system of dimension \(\tau d\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow population-level extension of the paper’s multidimensional Friedkin–Johnsen model. There is an important limit: the paper has no numbered computational-complexity result of the kind required for a firm anchor. Theorems 1–4 establish stability, convergence, and ergodicity. Section VIII gives convex formulations for estimating the coupling matrix, but they are not numbered complexity results. I would not present any of these as proving that a problem is in P, hard, or fixed-parameter tractable.

The closest numbered result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says the multidimensional model is stable exactly when \(\rho(\Lambda W)\rho(C)<1\), and, when stable, its limiting opinion is \(x'_C=(I_{mn}-\Lambda W\otimes C)^{-1}[(I_n-\Lambda)\otimes I_m]u\). It also says that when \(\rho(C)=1\), stability is equivalent to stability of the scalar Friedkin–Johnsen model, or equivalently to there being no oblivious agents. This is a precise equilibrium formula and stability criterion, but it is an analytic result, not a stated computational-complexity classification.

A plausible continuous follow-on is Type-Mass Multidimensional FJ Equilibrium Evaluation. Consider a large online deliberation or public-consultation population. Many participants share the same initial opinion vector, susceptibility to influence, and pattern of attention to behavioral groups. Let \(T\) be the supported set of these complete types, with \(|T|=\tau\ll n\), and let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s population fraction. Each type \(t\) has prejudice \(u_t\in\mathbb{Q}^d\), susceptibility \(\lambda_t\in[0,1]\), and contact propensities \(a_{ts}\geq 0\) toward types \(s\). The mass-dependent contact weight is \(p^\mu_{ts}=a_{ts}\mu_s/\sum_r a_{tr}\mu_r\), with positive denominators. A shared rational matrix \(C\) captures the paper’s interdependence between topics. The type-level state follows \(x_t(k+1)=\lambda_t C\sum_s p^\mu_{ts}x_s(k)+(1-\lambda_t)u_t\).

The computational question is: given these rational inputs, with stability promised, compute each limiting type opinion \(x_t^*\) and the population average \(\bar{x}^*=\sum_t\mu_t x_t^*\). The state vector is the output; this is equilibrium evaluation, not an intervention or optimization problem, since the paper does not optimize an action. Theorem 2’s formula carries over with the type-contact matrix \(P^\mu\) replacing \(W\): the equilibrium is obtained from a rational linear system of dimension \(\tau d\). I would expect this evaluation problem to be polynomial-time in \(\tau\), \(d\), and the input bit length \(L\). Its finite-population interpretation is straightforward: \(n\) participants have type frequencies approximating \(\mu\), while the number of behavioral profiles remains moderate.

This is a credible continuation of the paper’s underlying dynamical question, but it is not a computational mirror anchored by a qualifying result in this paper. Its main weakness is that the authors study a fixed finite interaction graph, whereas this proposal replaces it with a population-dependent, type-level contact kernel. They could reasonably regard that as a new mean-field model. I would limit the proposed scope to equilibrium and stability evaluation of the multidimensional FJ dynamics; gossip convergence and the paper’s identification procedures are separate questions. Useful next questions would be how finite cohorts approximate the type-mass equilibrium, and how much population mass must change type to move the aggregate equilibrium across a specified threshold.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, but it is an analytic stability theorem. It characterizes stability through \(\rho(\Lambda W)\rho(C)<1\) and gives a formula for the limiting opinion. The paper’s numbered results concern stability, convergence, and ergodicity; none states a complexity bound for a computational problem. Turning Theorem 2 into “given rational inputs, decide stability and compute the limit” supplies a new input-output problem and complexity question. Under ChoCo’s anchor gate, that cannot make the theorem a computational result to mirror.

The best version of the proposed mirror is still a credible model: large cohorts share prejudices, susceptibilities, and contact patterns, while their population fractions determine a type-level influence matrix \(P^\mu\). This gives a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the authors could recognize it as a block-structured extension of their dynamics. But it replaces the paper’s fixed, arbitrary interaction matrix \(W\) with a population-dependent contact model. Applying Theorem 2 to \(P^\mu\) studies that new model; it does not continue a computational result from the paper.

The other possible footholds do not repair the gap. Theorem 4’s gossip result is also an ergodicity theorem. Section VIII formulates convex procedures to estimate the MiDS matrix \(C\), but those procedures are not numbered complexity results, and estimation from observed trajectories is a separate task. A mass-based intervention or robustness problem could be worthwhile, yet it would need additional actions, costs, and a target condition absent from the anchored result.

So the negative case is strong at the programme’s eligibility gate: this paper supplies no qualifying computational result to mirror. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project exists is weaker. A type-mass extension for block-structured populations is plausible research; the case against is that it is an adjacent extension, not a ChoCo mirror anchored by this paper’s results.
