The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(S\), rational lower bounds \(b_s\), and a finite support of types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), linear preferences \(u_t(\rho,s)=v_{ts}\rho_s\), and ordered priority categories at each school, compute an RCE whose utility vector weakly dominates that of every other RCE, or report that none exists. An allocation consists of masses \(x_{ts}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\); for \(q_s=\sum_t x_{ts}>0\), require \(b_s\leq \rho_s\leq 1\) and \(\rho_s q_s=1\), and set \(\rho_s=1\) when \(q_s=0\). Every type assigned positive mass at multiple schools receives equal utility there; if a type strictly prefers a school to its assigned utility, that school must be at its lower bound and every type assigned positive mass there must have higher priority. Every empty school must be strictly worse for each type than its assigned outcome.

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over complete preference-and-priority types, with mass assignment variables \(x_{ts}\), per-capita resource ratios \(\rho_s\), exact clearing \(\rho_s q_s=1\) at nonempty schools, and priority-justified envy only at lower bounds. The output is an RCE with a greatest per-type utility vector, if one exists.

The objection that survived

The paper gives each student an individual strict priority, so aggregating students into priority tiers introduces ties; the proponent does not resolve how the paper's strict-priority fairness condition handles a tier split at a school.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 4's task of computing a maximal RCE on a restricted linear-preference domain. It leaves the equilibrium existence and structural results, strategy-proofness, and the paper's finite-student algorithm without a running-time bound as separate questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a greatest RCE be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\) on this type-level domain?
  • How does the type-level output relate to the discrete mechanism when \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), especially when priority categories split across schools?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible case is narrow: this paper does not give a named NP-hardness or polynomial-time result. Its one named algorithmic anchor is Proposition 4, proved here. It says that for linear preferences with rational coefficients satisfying NCBI, the algorithm in Section 7, run on the grid induced by the preferences, terminates after finitely many stages at a maximal RCE. The algorithm starts with every school’s ratio at \(1\), uses deferred acceptance for students demanding schools at their lower bounds, and lowers the ratios of a minimal overdemanded set by one grid step. This is a constructive computation result, but the paper gives no polynomial running-time bound. I would not use its existence, lattice, or strategy-proofness theorems as complexity anchors.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large school district where many thousands of students share a small number of complete profiles: the same preferences over schools and crowding, and the same priority category at each school. The paper itself says that real schools are large enough for one student’s effect on the resource ratio to be negligible. Here \(N\), the number of students, could be very large while the number of profiles \(\tau\) is modest. To keep per-capita crowding meaningful as the population grows, normalize school resources and capacity in per-capita units. That is the permitted co-modification of supplies, not a change in the object of continuization.

I would ask about Type-Maximal-RCE Computation. An instance has schools \(S\), rational lower bounds \(b_s\), and types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type specifies its complete linear preferences \(u_t(\rho,s)=v_{ts}\rho_s\), with rational \(v_{ts}>0\), and its priority rank at each school. The decision variable is \(x_{ts}\geq 0\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(s\), with \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\). Let \(q_s=\sum_t x_{ts}\). For a nonempty school, require \(\rho_s q_s=1\) and \(b_s\leq\rho_s\leq1\); for an empty school, set \(\rho_s=1\). Thus the continuum clears resources exactly, while the paper’s finite model permits its at-most-one-seat rounding discrepancy.

For fairness, all mass of a type must receive the same utility \(U_t\); if a type is split across schools, those assignments must give it equal utility. If \(u_t(\rho,s)>U_t\), school \(s\) must be at its lower bound and every type assigned positive mass there must outrank \(t\) at \(s\). Each empty school must be strictly worse for every type than its assigned outcome. Ask for a Pareto-greatest RCE: an allocation and distribution satisfying these conditions whose vector \((U_t)_{t\in T}\) is not weakly improved for every type, with a strict improvement for at least one, by another RCE. Any such pair is a solution.

My working expectation is Class A, tentatively, on this linear, finite-type domain: the population size disappears from the input, and the remaining problem may admit a continuous-optimization formulation. Proposition 4 does not establish that; its finite-grid algorithm might depend on \(N\), and the aggregate equilibrium conditions need not be linear or convex. The concrete questions are whether maximal RCE computation has an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and how its output relates to the discrete mechanism when \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). A result in either direction would answer something the paper leaves open.

The soft spot is the priority model. The paper gives each individual a strict priority position, which can make every student a different type under the programme’s definition of type. My scenario assumes operational, cohort-level priority categories, with ties within a type. That is plausible for category-based priority systems, but it is a real extension of the paper’s strict-priority model; if named lottery tie-breaks are essential, the type count can grow with \(N\). There is a second limit: Proposition 4’s grid and deferred-acceptance steps are built around individual students, and finite termination is not a complexity bound. So this is a credible mirror of its maximal-RCE computation, not a claim that its algorithm already extends.

The proposed mirror covers Proposition 4’s task of computing a maximal RCE on a restricted linear-preference domain. It does not turn the paper’s other main results into computational questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is that Proposition 4 is a weak anchor for the programme’s complexity questions. It proves finite termination of a grid-based algorithm for individual students with strict priorities; it gives no running-time bound. Its grid is tied to \(|N|\), and its deferred-acceptance steps operate on named students. A mass-allocation model with variables \(x_{ts}\) and crowding condition \(\rho_s q_s=1\) is therefore not a direct continuation of the proposition’s algorithm. It is a new equilibrium-computation problem whose tractability the paper does not address.

The main obstacle to a small-support mirror is priorities. In the paper’s strict priority orders, a student’s rank at a school is part of their complete type. Even if many students share preferences, distinct ranks can make the support size \(\tau\) grow with the population. Compressing students into priority tiers creates a different problem: if a tier is split at a school’s cutoff, an admitted member cannot outrank an unadmitted member of the same tier. The paper’s fairness condition may then reject the allocation whenever those left out strictly prefer that school. Random tie-breaking or a continuum of priority scores can repair the model, but they change the rationing rule or require a new representation of the type distribution. Neither is supplied by Proposition 4.

The other tempting computational mirror is to compute the student-optimal or maximal RCE underlying Proposition 3 and Theorem 4. That is a legitimate question, but its best formulation inherits the same priority issue. The strategy-proofness result itself is especially difficult to carry over: in a nonatomic population, one student has zero mass, so an individual report may leave the aggregate allocation unchanged. Strategy-proofness can become vacuous unless the model gives agents positive mass, which changes the incentive question.

These points make the paper’s named algorithm hard to mirror faithfully, but they do not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large district with repeated preference profiles and categorical priorities is plausible, and authors might recognize a mass-based extension with an explicit tie rule. A continuum of priority scores is another possible formulation. Those alternatives would ask genuinely new computational questions, and the paper’s finite grid does not show that they are trivial or already answered. So the negative case is honest but weak: it can challenge the directness of the proposed anchor, not support the universal claim that no scenario merits a continuous mirror.
