What the mirror covers

The distributional restatement covers Theorem 3's ranking-aggregation axioms; the paper's comparison with Theorems 1 and 2 and its discussions of escape routes and independence remain axiomatic.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is Theorem 3, but it is only a near-case, not a qualifying ChoCo anchor. The paper has no numbered result about computational complexity: Theorem 1 is Arrow’s impossibility theorem, cited from Arrow (1951/1963); Theorem 2 is the List–Pettit judgment aggregation impossibility, cited from 2002; and Theorem 3 is proved in this paper’s appendix. They establish that certain aggregation rules cannot satisfy specified axioms, not that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. So there are no eligible computational anchors here.

There is a clear continuous-population restatement of Theorem 3. Let the types be strict rankings of a finite alternative set \(X\), with \(|X|\ge3\), and let a society be a distribution \(\mu\) over those types. Seek a single rule \(F\) that maps every such \(\mu\) to a strict collective ranking. Require universal domain, anonymity, and the continuous analogue of independence of non-welfare characteristics: a common function \(f:[0,1]\to\{0,1\}\) determines whether \(x\) collectively outranks \(y\) solely from the mass \(\mu\) ranking \(x\) above \(y\), so \(xPy\) exactly when \(f(\mu\{t:x\succ_t y\})=1\). The question is whether any such rule exists. Its solution would be a rule satisfying those conditions, or an impossibility proof.

This is a persuasive population mirror: for example, a large electorate could have three ballot types in equal proportions, ranking \(x\succ y\succ z\), \(y\succ z\succ x\), and \(z\succ x\succ y\). The paper’s authors would recognize the model as a continuous version of their ranking aggregation problem. For that distribution, each edge of the cycle has support \(2/3\), so the common \(f\) either produces the cycle or rejects all three edges, which also violates strictness.

The weakness is decisive for this programme: this remains an axiomatic existence question. It has no algorithmic objective or computational complexity question, so it is outside the stated scope. A computational problem such as finding a Kemeny ranking for \(\mu\) might be natural, but this paper does not establish a named result about it, so it cannot serve as an honest anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 is Arrow’s impossibility theorem, Theorem 2 is the List–Pettit judgment aggregation theorem cited from 2002, and Theorem 3 is the paper’s own result. All three concern whether an aggregation rule can satisfy axioms while producing rational collective judgments. None studies an algorithmic problem or establishes a complexity result.

The proposed continuous version of Theorem 3 is a coherent axiomatic question. A distribution over rankings is a sensible population model, and the common function \(f\) gives a recognizable continuous analogue of systematicity. The paper’s assumptions already treat individuals symmetrically, so a population with repeated judgment types is plausible. But the question asks whether a rule exists that works across all societies. It does not ask an algorithm to optimize or decide something for a given society. Supplying a particular \(f\) leaves a finite check of its output; searching for a globally valid \(f\) restates the axiomatic impossibility question. The cyclic example illustrates that question; it does not supply a computational task.

The strongest computational follow-on would be to formalize one of the paper’s escape routes—for example, finding a minimum-cost mass of judgment changes that restores consistency, or choosing a priority set under a stated objective. Those could be worthwhile new problems. The paper gives no such objective, costs, or algorithmic result, though. Adding them would create a computational project inspired by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its results.

So the negative case is strong for the programme’s screening question: there is no paper-grounded computational anchor here. It is weaker as the literal claim that no worthwhile computational project could grow from the paper. The ranking distribution is a defensible continuous society; the obstacle is that the paper’s results concern axiomatic possibility, not computation.
