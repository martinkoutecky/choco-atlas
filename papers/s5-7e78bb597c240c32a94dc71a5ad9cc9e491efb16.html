The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Theorem 1, Corollary 1, and Theorem 2 are results about political support and tax reform; none classifies an encoded problem as being in P, NP-hard, or another complexity class. I can’t honestly present one as a qualifying anchor.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved by the authors (with its formal proof in Online-Appendix A): for a monotonic reform direction, a small reform benefits a majority exactly when it benefits the voter with median income. Corollary 1, also proved here, extends that equivalence to a large monotonic reform. Theorem 2 says that if the status quo is an interior Pareto optimum, then a simple reform cutting marginal tax rates in a bracket below median income, or raising them in a bracket above it, can be politically feasible. These results motivate a computational question, but they do not themselves assert a complexity result.

A credible computational companion would be Monotone Tax-Reform Support. An instance would give \(\tau\) agent types with rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to one; each type would specify a complete preference type, such as ability \(\omega_i\) in the paper’s utility model. It would also give a piecewise-linear status-quo tax schedule \(T_0\), a monotone proposed reform \(T_1=T_0+\lambda h\), and the paper’s rule for rebating the reform’s revenue change to everyone. For type \(i\), calculate the utility-maximizing income before and after the reform and the resulting utility change \(V_i\). The question is whether
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\,\mathbf{1}\{V_i>0\}\ge \tfrac12. \]
The answer is yes or no; an exact solution can also report the supporting mass. Here mass is the fraction of the taxpaying population sharing each complete ability type, and the policy being evaluated is the reform itself, including its behavioral and revenue effects.

This is a plausible Class A question for a specified finite encoding—for example, quadratic effort costs and rational piecewise-linear schedules. Typewise income choices can then be computed by checking the tax brackets, and Theorem 1 gives a median-voter shortcut for monotone reforms. That tractability claim is my inference about this encoded problem, not a result established by the paper. The authors would likely recognize it as their political-feasibility question: it preserves endogenous earnings, the tax-revenue rebate, and majority support.

The regime is also plausible as a high-multiplicity one: many taxpayers can share the same ability and preferences, with the number of taxpayers much larger than the number of distinct types. But there is a major qualification: the paper already models a continuum population of measure one with a distribution \(F\) over abilities. This would be a computational formalization of its existing continuous-agent model, not a new continuization of a discrete model. Its positive case is that computational questions about that model remain open in the paper—not that the population becomes continuous for the first time.

This mirror covers only monotone reform support in Theorem 1 and Corollary 1. It does not cover the paper’s empirical findings or its Pareto-bound analysis. Further questions include optimizing over all simple reforms that are both politically feasible and welfare-improving, handling non-monotone reforms, and establishing a precise relation to finite discrete electorates. The weakest point is the missing qualifying anchor: without a named complexity result in the paper, this is a promising computational extension, but it cannot satisfy the requested anchor-based case for a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case for this mirror fails the stated anchor test. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 characterize political support for monotonic reforms; Theorem 2 characterizes politically feasible simple reforms. None asserts the complexity of a computational problem. The proponent acknowledges this, then proposes a new decision problem. That may be a legitimate research question, but it is not a computational result of this paper for a continuous mirror to take up.

The strongest version of their proposed question is still easy to formalize: given rational masses of ability types, a piecewise-linear tax schedule, and a monotonic reform, decide whether at least half the population gains. But the paper’s own median-voter theorem reduces this to checking the median type. With quadratic effort costs, each type’s income choice can be found by checking the tax brackets; then one compares that type’s utility before and after the reform. If the task is to report the full supporting mass, evaluate each type and sum its mass. For \(\tau\) types and \(B\) brackets, this takes roughly \(O(\tau B)\) bracket checks, apart from exact arithmetic. The result is a well-posed computation, but its work lies in evaluating separable one-person choices and aggregating weights. There is no population-driven search or exponential family of configurations for continuous optimization to unlock.

A stronger search question would ask whether there exists a simple reform that is both politically feasible and welfare-improving. But Corollary 3 already reduces that question to inequalities in sufficient statistics at income levels. Under a finite piecewise encoding, one can check the relevant intervals and breakpoints. Allowing unrestricted reform functions could make the search difficult, but then the complexity would come from the chosen policy-function representation; the paper offers no named computational result anchoring that problem in its population axis.

There is also a basic scope concern: this paper already models a continuum of individuals with a distribution over types. Its principal results and empirical analysis operate on that continuous population. So the proposed work would computationalize an existing continuous-agent model; it would not supply the programme’s central modeling move of making society continuous. That distinction alone does not close the computational gap—the paper does not answer computational questions—but it weakens the claim that this is a continuous mirror.

That is the strongest honest negative case, and its limit matters. I cannot rule out a worthwhile computational follow-up: the median-support question is one the authors could recognize, and a complexity study of richer reform-search problems could produce useful results. But under the instructions given, the proposed case does not survive as an anchored mirror: it supplies no named computational-complexity result from the paper. The broader claim that no scenario is worth the programme’s time is much harder to sustain.
