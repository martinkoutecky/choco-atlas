Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 3.7 — survived: The mass-allocation version preserves the matching and fairness question for recurring types, and the opponent concedes that this mirror survives.
  • Theorem 3.4 — survived: The opponent also accepts the overlapping-group mass version as a recognizable continuous question, though it is less exact than the Theorem 3.7 mirror.

The continuous mirror question

Given platforms \(P\), supported types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), eligibility sets, rankings, disjoint group labels, type-level top-\(k\) bounds \(L_{t,k},U_{t,k}\), and per-capita platform and platform-group quota bounds, compute a mass matrix \(x_{t,p}\) maximizing \(\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}\), subject to \(\sum_p x_{t,p}\leq\mu_t\), \(L_{t,k}\mu_t\leq\sum_{p\in R_{t,k}}x_{t,p}\leq U_{t,k}\mu_t\), and the quota bounds. Give an induced randomized assignment in which each person receives at most one platform, every realization meets the quotas, and each type’s top-\(k\) assignment probabilities meet the stated bounds.

The model it lives in

Represent each type as an atomless population block of measure \(\mu_t\). The variables \(x_{t,p}\) are assigned masses, with unmatched mass allowed; maximize total assigned mass under the type-level probability constraints and per-capita quotas. A randomized shift of a partition of each type block realizes the masses in every outcome and gives each person the corresponding marginal assignment probabilities.

The objection that survived

The continuous formulation follows directly from the paper’s marginal LP aggregated by type, so it introduces little new algorithmic work and may be a lower-priority mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Theorem 3.7 yields an exact mirror for disjoint groups with platform and platform-group lower and upper quotas. Theorem 3.4 suggests an overlapping-group mirror with upper quotas; these mirrors do not cover the paper’s other bicriteria guarantees or extensions to maxmin and mindom fairness.

Open questions for a prover

  • Quantify how the continuous optimum and feasibility relate to finite populations when type counts and per-capita quotas must be rounded.
  • Determine whether an exact mass-allocation characterization extends to overlapping groups with lower quotas.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for a high multiplicity matching regime: a large school district or hiring platform with many applicants but relatively few recurring profiles. A type records everything the paper’s matching problem uses: which platforms the person can be assigned to, their ranking of those platforms, their group memberships, and their individual fairness bounds. The regime is convincing only when these profiles and rule-based bounds recur often enough that the number of supported types \(\tau\) is much smaller than the number of people \(N\). A seven-person committee would not qualify; a large applicant pool with repeated eligibility and preference profiles plausibly could.

My lead is Theorem 3.7, proved in this paper. It says that when each item belongs to exactly one group, a polynomial-time algorithm either computes a probabilistic individually fair distribution over strong group-fair matchings or reports infeasibility. In the paper’s framework, the distribution maximizes expected matching size subject to the individual fairness bounds and the platform and group lower and upper bounds. This is the cleanest anchor because it preserves both sides of the paper’s guarantee: each realized assignment satisfies group fairness, while each individual’s probability of receiving a sufficiently preferred platform meets the specified bounds.

Here is its continuous counterpart. The input is a finite platform set \(P\), a finite set of supported types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each type’s eligible platforms and ranking, disjoint group memberships, rational individual fairness bounds \(L_{t,k},U_{t,k}\), and per-platform and per-group lower and upper bounds expressed as fractions of the population. The decision variables are masses \(x_{t,p}\geq 0\) assigned from type \(t\) to platform \(p\), with \(\sum_p x_{t,p}\leq\mu_t\). For every type and ranking prefix \(R_{t,k}\), require
\[ L_{t,k}\mu_t\leq\sum_{p\in R_{t,k}}x_{t,p}\leq U_{t,k}\mu_t. \]
Require also that the total mass assigned to each platform and each platform–group pair meets its specified bounds. The question is to maximize \(\sum_{t,p}x_{t,p}\); a solution is a feasible rational mass matrix attaining that maximum, together with its induced randomized assignment.

Each person still receives at most one platform in any realization. The continuum is in the population: within a type, atomless mass can be partitioned among platforms. Randomly shifting that partition gives every individual the same marginal assignment probabilities while keeping the assigned mass of each type–platform pair fixed in every realization. Thus group bounds hold ex post, and the top-\(k\) bounds hold ex ante, as in the paper. Since the constraints and objective are linear, this mirror is a Class A problem: an LP of size polynomial in the supported types, platforms, group incidences, and input bit length \(L\), rather than in the total population size.

A second, broader anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved here in its formal form as Theorem 4.1. It concerns overlapping groups, with each item in at most \(\Delta\) groups and group upper bounds. For any \(\epsilon>0\), the theorem gives a polynomial-time algorithm that returns a distribution over group-fair matchings with expected size at least \((\mathrm{OPT}+\epsilon)/f_\epsilon\), where \(f_\epsilon=O(\Delta\log(n/\epsilon))\). For each item \(a\) and ranking prefix \(R_{a,k}\), its probability of being matched within that prefix lies between \((L_{a,k}-\epsilon)/f_\epsilon\) and \((U_{a,k}+\epsilon)/f_\epsilon\); the algorithm reports infeasibility if no distribution meeting its stated conditions exists.

Its continuous mirror uses the same mass-matrix objective and top-\(k\) constraints as above, but permits overlapping group memberships and imposes only the group upper bounds, scaled per capita. It asks for the maximum matched mass with the original individual fairness bounds satisfied exactly. This too is an LP of size polynomial in \(\tau\), the platform and group data, and \(L\). I would expect tractability. The continuous problem is not a claim that Theorem 3.4’s finite-population approximation guarantee transfers verbatim: it is the same underlying optimization question when people are represented by type masses. A useful follow-up is to quantify how its optimum and feasibility compare with finite populations as \(N\) grows, including the rounding error needed to recover integral matchings.

Theorem 3.7 is the stronger anchor for my case; Theorem 3.4 shows that the mirror is not limited to disjoint cohorts. These are algorithmic anchors. The paper’s opening hardness results are attributed to earlier work in §3.1.1, but are not numbered results of this paper, so I am not using them as anchors. For Theorem 3.7, a further question is whether the exact continuous characterization extends to overlapping groups with lower quotas. For both mirrors, one can ask when a mass solution can be rounded into a distribution over finite, group-fair matchings with small loss.

The fit is close to the authors’ stated problem: matching, preference rankings, individual top-choice probabilities, group bounds, and expected matching size all remain. The lottery in the paper is over outcomes for a finite society; here the society itself has mass. Fractional type-level assignment does not mean a person receives a fractional platform: it means that different members of a type receive different integral platforms, and randomization gives each the required marginal probability. Capacities and quotas expressed per person are a natural co-modification for a population limit.

My weakest point is the regime assumption. If rankings, eligibility, group membership, or fairness parameters vary idiosyncratically by person, then \(\tau\) may approach \(N\), and the high multiplicity benefit disappears. The continuum also removes finite indivisibility at the type-mass level, so Theorem 3.7’s finite integrality argument is not itself what makes the mirror tractable; the continuous solution follows from the mass LP. That is a real change in the mathematical route, but the underlying allocation question and its fairness guarantees remain recognizable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is about priority, not validity. Once a repeated applicant type includes its eligibility, platform ranking, group memberships, and fairness bounds, the proposed continuous problems look like the paper’s own marginal LPs with individuals aggregated by type and the quotas normalized by population. There is no difficult pricing problem or new optimization machinery to uncover. The exact discrete result in Theorem 3.7 is already polynomial-time, and the mass version appears to follow directly from its LP formulation. That may make these less urgent targets than papers whose continuous mirrors require substantial new work.

But that does not defeat Theorem 3.7’s mirror. In a large allocation system with recurring applicant profiles and capacities that scale with the population, a type’s mass can be partitioned among platforms. Randomly shifting those partitions gives each person the type’s specified assignment probabilities, while preserving the type-platform masses—and therefore the group quotas—in every realization. The individual-level guarantees and ex-post group fairness survive. The fact that the finite theorem’s integrality argument is no longer needed changes the proof route, not the question the mirror asks.

Theorem 3.4 is harder to dismiss. With overlapping groups, the type-mass LP can impose each platform’s upper bound for every group. Atomless mass can still be partitioned to realize the assignment, so overlap does not reintroduce an individual indivisibility obstruction. The resulting exact LP mirror could be a useful Class A finding, even if its solution is immediate once the model is written down. Saying it is easy, or that the answer may be unsurprising, is not enough to show the question is unworthy.

There is a real limitation: complete types may be rare when rankings, eligibility, or fairness bounds are personalized, leaving support size close to population size. That weakens the high-multiplicity benefit in many applications. Yet standardized hiring cohorts or other large allocation pools with recurring profiles remain plausible regimes, and the paper’s own framework makes the type-level aggregation recognizable. Prior work also studies divisible-item matching with fairness algorithms, though with different fairness notions; that makes the mirror less isolated, but does not establish that the continuous-population question is already answered.[^1]

So my negative case does not defeat either anchor. It can argue that these are comparatively low-priority mirrors because their mass formulations are close to existing LP machinery. It cannot support the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.

[^1]: [Class Fairness in Online Matching](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/25704) studies fractional matchings for divisible items with class fairness guarantees.
