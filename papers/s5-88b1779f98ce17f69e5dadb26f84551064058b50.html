Proposition 1

The continuous mirror question

Given finite sets of patient-pair types and non-directed-donor types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), directed type-pair compatibility arcs with weights \(w_{tu}\) and independent failure probabilities \(p_{tu}\), and cycle and chain limits \(K,L\), compute a maximum-expected-weight allocation of mass to feasible exchange templates. Each template uses distinct participants, even when a type appears more than once. If \(r_{tq}\) is the number of participants of type \(t\) used by template \(q\), and \(U(q)\) is its expected weight—\(U(c)=(\sum_{e\in c}w_e)\prod_{e\in c}(1-p_e)\) for a cycle and \(U(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}w_{e_i}\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-p_{e_j})\) for a chain—optimize \(\sum_q x_q U(q)\) subject to \(\sum_q r_{tq}x_q\le\mu_t\) for every type and \(x_q\ge0\).

The model it lives in

A fractional packing LP over capped exchange templates in a finite-type, nonatomic population. Type masses are capacities; template variables allocate portions of each type to exchanges, whose expected values use the paper’s cycle and chain formulas.

The objection that survived

The paper’s graph relies on pair-specific compatibility and edge data, and its experiments assign independent continuous failure probabilities, which almost surely make full relational profiles distinct; the proponent acknowledges that unique signatures weaken the case but does not address how rarely the paper’s demonstrated regime has repeated complete profiles.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s expected-matching-weight problem with nonidentical edge failure probabilities. It leaves the CVaR/SAA formulation and the paper’s empirical comparisons outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • When cycle or chain limits are part of the input, can the type-template LP be solved efficiently through pricing, and what parameterized complexity bounds apply?
  • What finite-population approximation guarantees follow when template masses are rounded to participant counts?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Continuous Inhomogeneous-Risk Kidney Exchange (\(\mathrm{KEP}_\infty\)), for the paper’s expected-matching-weight problem.

The anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It says that Optimization (4)—choosing vertex-disjoint cycles and chains to maximize expected weight when edges have nonidentical failure probabilities—is equivalent to the mixed-integer linear program (6). The objective gives each cycle \(c\) value \((\sum_{e\in c} w_e)\prod_{e\in c}(1-p_e)\); for chains, position-indexed variables track the probability that each edge is reached and succeeds. The reformulation replaces the nonlinear products of the binary chain-use variables \(y_{ek}\) and discount variables \(o_{ek}\) with variables \(O_{ek}\), linked by \(O_{ek}\le y_{ek}\), \(O_{ek}\le o_{ek}\), and the paper’s discount-propagation constraints. The paper uses this formulation in its experiments as KEP-NP. Proposition 1 is an exact solver formulation, not a claim that the discrete problem is in P; that is the kind of anchor the prompt permits when the paper computes with the formulation.

Here is the continuous problem I would ask a prover to study. An instance has patient–donor-pair types \(T_P\), non-directed-donor types \(T_N\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\). It also has directed compatibility arcs between donor and recipient types, with a weight \(w_{tu}>0\) and failure probability \(p_{tu}\in[0,1]\) for each arc, and cycle and chain limits \(K,L\). The type descriptions must be complete for this problem: members of a type have the same compatibility, edge weights, and failure probabilities to every type. Thus a type might encode a donor–patient medical profile and clinical-risk class, with arc data determined by the two endpoint types.

Let \(\mathcal Q_{K,L}\) be the feasible exchange templates: cycles of at most \(K\) edges and chains starting at an NDD type with at most \(L\) edges. A template may repeat a type label, provided its repeated positions are filled by distinct people of that type. Let \(r_{tq}\) be the number of people of type \(t\) used by template \(q\), and let \(x_q\ge0\) be the mass assigned to it. Ask for a feasible \(x\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{q\in\mathcal Q_{K,L}} x_q U(q) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{q\in\mathcal Q_{K,L}} r_{tq}x_q\le\mu_t \quad(t\in T_P\cup T_N). \]
For a cycle \(c\), \(U(c)=(\sum_{e\in c}w_e)\prod_{e\in c}(1-p_e)\). For a chain with ordered edges \(e_1,\ldots,e_\ell\), \(U(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}w_{e_i}\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-p_{e_j})\), the expected value used in the paper’s chain calculation. The solution is the mass of society assigned to each template. Each infinitesimal member participates in one integral exchange or none; fractional values of \(x_q\) describe portions of a type cohort assigned to different exchanges.

A convincing regime is a large clearinghouse whose participants fall into repeated, clinically equivalent profiles—for example, common donor and recipient profiles whose compatibility, utility, and risk parameters are determined by type pairs. The case depends on having many participants but comparatively few *complete* types, not merely few blood groups. If individual compatibility or clinical data make almost every participant’s signature unique, then \(\tau\) approaches the population size and the high-multiplicity motivation weakens. In the regime where \(N\gg\tau\), though, this is recognizably the paper’s exchange problem: the same capped cycles and chains, the same edge-specific failure model, and the same expected-weight objective, with cohort mass replacing individual counts.

For fixed \(K,L\), this mirror is expected to be Class A. There are polynomially many type-labelled templates in \(\tau\), specifically \(O(\tau^{\max(K,L)})\) for fixed caps, so the stated optimization is a rational LP of polynomial size. This is a consequence of the proposed mirror, not a complexity theorem claimed by the paper. The high-multiplicity bridge is concrete: in an \(n\)-person clone instance, every discrete matching gives a feasible mass solution by dividing template counts by \(n\). Conversely, an optimal basic LP solution uses at most \(\tau\) templates; rounding each \(n x_q\) down gives a feasible discrete matching, losing at most \(\tau U_{\max}/n\) in per-capita value, where \(U_{\max}=\max_q U(q)\). With variable cycle or chain limits, template enumeration is no longer polynomial by this argument; pricing over templates is a natural further question.

I would scope this case to Proposition 1’s expected-weight result. I would not use Proposition 2’s CVaR/SAA result as another anchor: with independent failures across infinitely many members of each type, aggregate realized weight self-averages and tail risk can collapse to the mean. Preserving a nontrivial CVaR objective would require a different source of aggregate uncertainty, such as common shocks, and thus a less faithful mirror of the paper’s failure model.

The weakest point is the regime assumption. Exact type equality in all compatibility, weight, and failure data may be demanding in real kidney-exchange pools. The mirror stands if a large pool has substantial repeated profiles under those full signatures; it becomes much less persuasive if the only way to preserve the paper’s graph is to give nearly every vertex its own type.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The expected-weight anchor is a real mirror. A type-mass LP over capped exchange templates preserves the paper’s expected utility, including the product of edge success probabilities, and it has a concrete high-multiplicity interpretation. I would not reject it because its answer may be easy: with fixed cycle and chain caps, there are polynomially many type-labelled templates.

The strongest objection is that a kidney-exchange type must capture a participant’s *relationships* to other participants, not just a clinical category. The paper’s feasible cycles and chains depend on specific directed compatibilities, edge weights, and failure probabilities. In practice, compatibility depends on comparing a donor’s HLA antigens with a recipient’s antibodies, so it is pair-specific; the graph’s arcs encode those particular donor–recipient relations. [A clinical kidney-exchange model describing virtual crossmatches](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4004760/) In an arbitrary instance, preserving all those relations in a type requires identical rows and columns of the weighted, probabilistic graph. Similar blood groups or broad clinical profiles do not suffice.

The paper’s own experiments sharpen that concern: they assign each edge an independent failure probability drawn from a continuous interval. With that construction, vertices with incident edges almost surely have distinct complete failure profiles. Thus \(\tau\) grows with the number of vertices, and the proposed mirror loses its high-multiplicity advantage on the paper’s demonstrated input regime.

A stronger rescue would posit large cohorts whose compatibility, weights, and failure probabilities are determined by endpoint types. That is coherent and could yield a genuine mirror. But it imposes a block structure on the graph. If broader types are used to include people with differing pairwise crossmatches, replacing their arcs with average compatibility or average risk changes which cycles and chains exist; their feasibility depends on the particular combination of arcs. The rescued model therefore applies to pools with unusually repeated *complete* relational profiles, rather than following from a large pool alone.

That is a substantial practical limitation, but it does not defeat the mirror universally. A deliberately structured pool with repeated profiles and type-pair edge data is plausible, and its continuous LP is recognizable as kidney exchange. Under the stated rule that one surviving anchor is enough, I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile scenario exists. The negative case is strongest on the gap between broad clinical categories and complete types; it remains weak against the structured-profile scenario.
