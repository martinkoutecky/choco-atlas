Why no mirror

Theorem 3.1 is a quantitative distortion lower bound, which the stated gate excludes even though its proof uses the LP. The distribution-over-cohorts optimization is well-posed and recognizable, but the paper has no qualifying named computational result for it to mirror.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the type-homogeneous version of Theorem 3.1's seven-client profile and its instance-optimal LP; it leaves the paper's broader claims about randomized distortion untouched.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s instance-optimal randomized distortion problem, restricted to societies with a small number of homogeneous voter types. The paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or similar. Its useful named anchor is instead a quantitative optimization result.

Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper, says that for a particular profile with seven clients and seven facilities, every randomized social choice function has worst-case metric distortion at least \(2.063164\). The profile has three clients ranking \(c \succ e \succ b \succ a \succ f \succ g \succ d\), three ranking \(d \succ g \succ f \succ a \succ e \succ b \succ c\), and one ranking \(b \succ a \succ f \succ g \succ e \succ c \succ d\). The ratio is expected total client cost divided by the minimum total cost of any facility, maximized over metrics consistent with those rankings.

The continuous version has input a facility set \(F\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) ranking types, and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. In each metric realization, all mass of a type shares one representative voter location, so its members have the same distances as well as the same ranking. Write \(S_d(i)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t d(i,t)\) for facility \(i\)’s per-capita social cost. The problem is to find a distribution \(q\in\Delta(F)\) minimizing \(\max_{o\in F}\sup_{d:\,S_d(o)=1}\sum_{i\in F}q_iS_d(i)\), where the metric respects every type’s ranking. Normalizing each candidate’s cost in turn expresses the worst-case ratio to the cheapest facility. A solution is the optimal distribution \(q\) and its distortion value.

For Theorem 3.1’s profile, the continuous society has three types with masses \(3/7,3/7,1/7\). Replicating the seven clients \(k\) times gives \(7k\) voters with those same proportions, and scaling from total cost to per-capita cost leaves every distortion ratio unchanged. The paper’s lower-bound metric witnesses place each of the first two ranking groups at a common location, so the bound survives this type-homogeneous restriction: every randomized choice for this continuous society still has distortion at least \(2.063164\). The paper also supplies matching primal and dual values for its instance, giving the same optimum up to the displayed rounding.

The regime is a large election with three dominant preference and location cohorts: for example, many residents represented by three common neighborhoods, with \(N=7k\) and \(k\) large. The meaningful scale is \(N\gg\tau\), not seven individually named people. The mirror asks the authors’ question—how well can an ordinal-only randomized rule choose a facility under hidden consistent metrics—while making the society a distribution over types.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). For each candidate \(o\), the adversary’s problem is an LP over distances among the \(m\) facilities and \(\tau\) representative voter locations, with ranking, triangle-inequality, and normalization constraints. Dualizing those finitely many metric LPs gives an LP for \(q\) and the worst-case value, polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). That gives a precise follow-up: does this type-level LP preserve the paper’s exact instance value, and how does the optimum vary with rational mass vectors?

My weakest point is the homogeneity restriction. The original ordinal profile allows voters with the same ranking to occupy different metric locations; this mirror requires them to share one location in each realization. That makes it a genuine high-multiplicity regime, and the theorem’s witness already has that structure, but it covers only that regime. Also, this anchor is about distortion quality, not complexity hardness, so it does not test whether continuization dissolves a discrete hardness result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 3.1 is a distortion bound for one seven-voter profile, not a result about computational complexity. The paper’s computational contribution is instead the LP in Section 3 for finding an instance-optimal randomized rule. That distinction matters: there is no complexity result here whose population dependence the continuous programme could chart.

The best version of the proposed mirror is coherent. Treat each ranking-and-location cohort as a type of mass \(\mu_t\), and measure a facility’s per-capita cost by \(S_d(i)=\sum_t\mu_t d(i,t)\). The theorem’s witness already puts clients with the same ranking at the same location, so its profile becomes masses \(3/7,3/7,1/7\); replication preserves the distortion ratio. A large election made up of such cohorts is a plausible regime. I would not reject it on the grounds that its agents lack multiplicity.

The difficulty is that its computational question appears to be answered by a routine weighted form of the paper’s own LP. For homogeneous cohorts, replace each client sum with a mass-weighted sum and collapse identical client locations. The metric constraints still range over the type locations and facilities; dualizing gives a polynomial-size LP in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the mass bit length. The fixed lower bound follows by replication, and varying the masses amounts to varying the coefficients of that LP. This is a legitimate continuous formulation, but it offers little new computational terrain: no new separation problem, complexity boundary, or algorithmic obstacle is apparent. If one allows voters with the same ranking to have different hidden distances, ranking masses no longer summarize the relevant population; a richer latent-type model would need separate motivation.

So I would argue against spending ChoCo’s time on this mirror as stated: its clean version is an immediate weighted extension of the paper’s LP, while the more faithful heterogeneous version needs a different population model. That case is about limited research novelty, not a fundamental failure of the high-multiplicity regime. It remains weak against the universal claim: a parametric study of how optimal distortion varies across mass vectors could be worthwhile, and the paper’s authors might recognize it as their problem.
