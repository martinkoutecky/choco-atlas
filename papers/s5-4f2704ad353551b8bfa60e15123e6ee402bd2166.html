The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires: it classifies no problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar, and gives no algorithmic formulation it computes with. So there are no qualifying anchors from which to state one continuous computational problem per anchor.

The closest numbered results are structural. Proposition 1, proved here, says that for an inner-product norm, sequential issue-by-issue voting is order-independent exactly when the basis is orthogonal. Proposition 3, proved here using the cited characterization of strategy-proofness, says that a LAMO basis makes voting order-independent and gives the same outcome as the static strategy-proof issue-by-issue median. Theorem 3, proved here using a cited result of Bosznay and Garay, says that in dimensions greater than two, every norm has arbitrarily close norms with no LAMO basis. These are substantial mathematical results, but they are not computational-complexity anchors.

There is a plausible population setting: many constituents with ideal points in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), represented by a distribution \(\mu\), where mass is population share. One could ask whether the order-robustness results persist for continuum limits of large electorates. But a continuum voter has zero individual mass and cannot be pivotal, so that question needs a limiting or coalition-based equilibrium model. It would be a new extension of the paper’s analysis, not a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest objection is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its propositions and theorems characterize equilibrium outcomes, strategy-proofness, and geometric conditions on bases; none gives an algorithm or a complexity classification. The paper does not formulate a computational problem over an electorate. A continuous version might be mathematically interesting, but it would not fill ChoCo’s stated gap without adding a new computational objective.

A large electorate with repeated ideal points is plausible, so lack of multiplicity is not the issue. The difficulty is that the paper’s results depend on individual voters being pivotal. In an atomless society, a single voter has measure zero and cannot change a majority outcome. If voters report sincerely, the outcome is just the vector of marginal medians, independent of the order for any basis. If strategic reports and ex-post perfect equilibrium are retained, unilateral deviations have no effect, so every report is a best response and the model selects no informative outcome. Positive mass at a peak does not fix this: each person in that group is still individually nonpivotal. Treating the group as a coordinated bloc would change the strategic players.

That problem defeats the proposed anchors. For Proposition 1, sincerity makes order independence automatic, while the continuum equilibrium lacks the pivotal incentives behind the orthogonality characterization. For Proposition 3, the same issue removes the strategic force of LAMO; computing marginal medians for a fixed basis from finite support is straightforward, while finding a LAMO basis is a question about the norm and geometry, not the population. Theorem 3 is even further from a population mirror: it concerns nearby norms and the existence of suitable bases, and adding a voter distribution does not change that geometric statement. Turning the norm itself into the continuous object would miss ChoCo’s population focus.

One could preserve pivotality by studying coordinated positive-mass blocs, or ask how much population mass must be persuaded to cause an order reversal. Those might be worthwhile computational problems. But they require new assumptions about coordination or persuasion costs and a new objective; they are not continuizations of the results this paper proves. So the negative case is strong against a *direct, faithful computational mirror*. It cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no useful project could be inspired by the paper: a mass-manipulation problem could be valuable, but it would be new work rather than the paper’s continuous mirror.
