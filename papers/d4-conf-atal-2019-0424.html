The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no anchor to defeat: the proponent names none, and the paper supplies none. Its results are experimental findings, learned behavior models, and simulation analyses. Section 5.3 studies node placement, and Section 5.2 uses coordinate greedy search to tune parameters, but neither states a computational problem with a complexity result. A continuous complexity question built from either would be a new problem, not a mirror of a result in this paper.

The best candidate for a stronger mirror is to study a large organization, represent its agents by masses of regular, trusted, and adversarial types, and optimize placement to maximize consensus. But the paper’s behavior depends on who is connected to whom, local states, and the global consensus event. Masses of agent types alone lose that structure. Adding a network kernel or a distribution of local neighborhoods could retain more of it, but would require a new model and objective. A dense graph limit also changes the paper’s mostly sparse network regime; keeping sparse local interactions and their randomness requires still more structure. Replacing exact consensus with near-total population agreement may be sensible, but it defines another new problem.

The paper itself studies deliberately small games: 20 consensus players and at most 5 adversaries. A large-organization setting is imaginable, but the paper does not establish that repeated agents in such a setting preserve its interaction model or its research question. The negative case is therefore strong for this paper as a ChoCo mirror: it offers no named computational result to continuize, and rescuing one requires substantial new modeling. It cannot prove that no worthwhile project could grow out of the setting; a formal computational study of placement on large networks might be valuable in its own right. That would be an extension, not this paper’s continuous mirror.
