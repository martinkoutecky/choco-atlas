The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses μ, additive valuations \(v_t\) over \(q\) good types, per-capita supply \(b\), a bundle cap \(K\), and \(\varepsilon>0\), compute a finite-support lottery over feasible mass allocations \(x_{t,z}\), where each bundle \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^q\) has \(\sum_g z_g\le K\), \(\sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_g=b_g\). Each type’s expected value must be at least its proportional share \(v_t(b)\); in every outcome, every recipient must receive at least \((0.9-\varepsilon)M_t\), and each recipient below \((1-\varepsilon)M_t\) must be EFX-satisfied against every bundle assigned positive mass. Here \(M_t\) is the greatest value threshold attainable by assigning the same per-capita supply \(b\) among a unit-mass population with valuation \(v_t\), using integral bundles from the capped bundle set.

The model it lives in

A population of mass \(1\) has \(\tau\) complete additive valuation types with masses \(\mu_t\), and per-capita supplies \(b_g\) of \(q\) good types. Recipients receive integral bundles \(z\); allocation variables \(x_{t,z}\) describe the mass of each type receiving each bundle, and the lottery randomizes over feasible mass allocations.

What the mirror covers

The mirror builds on Theorem 5.1’s polynomial-time three-agent BoBW guarantees. It leaves the two-agent result, the existential three-agent theorem, and the separate EEFX-preserving approximation result outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • For variable \(q\), \(K\), and \(\tau\), do all feasible instances admit a lottery with these guarantees?
  • What is the complexity of deciding feasibility and computing a valid lottery as a function of \(q\), \(K\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 5.1, the paper’s “Polynomial-time BoBW for three agents” result, proved here. For any fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), it gives an FPTAS which, for three agents with additive valuations over indivisible goods, outputs a lottery that is ex-ante proportional. In every allocation in its support, one agent is EFX-satisfied and receives her proportional share; a second either receives at least \((0.9-\varepsilon)\) of her MMS and is EFX-satisfied, or receives at least \((1-\varepsilon)\) of her MMS; and the third receives at least \((1-\varepsilon)\) of her MMS. Thus every agent gets at least \((0.9-\varepsilon)\)-MMS, and an agent falling below \((1-\varepsilon)\)-MMS is EFX-satisfied. The paper mentions that exact MMS computation is NP-hard, but that appears as a citation in the prose, not as a numbered hardness result by these authors; I would not present it as a second anchor.

I would mirror the underlying BoBW allocation problem in a high-multiplicity market for standardized grants or equipment. There are many recipients, grouped into a small support of complete valuation types: agents of type \(t\) have the same additive values \(v_t\) for each of \(q\) good types. The population has mass \(1\), type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), and the supply of each good type is \(b_g\) units per person. Supply scales with population size. Each person still receives an integral bundle; only the fraction of a type’s mass receiving each bundle can be fractional. A per-person bundle cap \(K\) keeps the bundle-pattern set finite, \(\mathcal B_K=\{z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^q:\sum_g z_g\le K\}\). This co-modification of supply is needed for a growing population with stable per-capita resources.

Call the proposed problem BoBW Fair Allocation for a High-Multiplicity Society. An instance consists of \((q,K,\mathcal B_K,\mu,(v_t)_{t=1}^{\tau},b,\varepsilon)\), with rational data and feasible per-capita supply. A deterministic mass allocation is a set of numbers \(x_{t,z}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,z}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(z\). It must satisfy \(\sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_g=b_g\) for each good type \(g\). For each type, define its continuum MMS \(M_t\) as the greatest \(\theta\) for which the entire population, if it had type \(t\)’s valuation, could be assigned bundles from \(\mathcal B_K\) using mass proportions that consume \(b\) and give every recipient a bundle worth at least \(\theta\). This is the high-multiplicity relaxation of partitioning the goods among identical agents.

The question is whether one can produce a finite-support lottery over deterministic mass allocations such that each type’s expected per-person value is at least its proportional share \(v_t(b)\), and, in every lottery outcome, every recipient gets at least \((0.9-\varepsilon)M_t\). Also, anyone receiving less than \((1-\varepsilon)M_t\) must be EFX-satisfied: if their bundle is \(z\), then for every bundle \(w\) assigned positive mass to any type and every good \(g\) in \(w\), \(v_t(z)\ge v_t(w-e_g)\). A solution is the lottery itself, with its allocation masses and probabilities. This keeps the paper’s ex-ante lottery and ex-post fairness question intact while making the population the continuous object.

The regime is a large cohort of residents competing for a per-capita-scaled supply of standardized benefits, with finitely many stable valuation types and \(N\gg\tau\) as the finite-population counterpart. Type membership includes the full valuation vector, so agents grouped together are interchangeable for the problem. In denominator-compatible finite populations, rational masses correspond exactly to counts; other populations can approximate those proportions. The mirror does not make goods divisible or replace the lottery with outcome-space continuity.

My prior for the full problem, with \(q,K,\tau\) varying, is Class C: mass removes population-count granularity, but the search for bundle patterns that are simultaneously MMS-worthy and mutually EFX-compatible remains. That is a new computational question, not a consequence of Theorem 5.1. A promising tractable boundary would be fixed \(q,K,\tau\), where bundle configurations are finite and mass assignment is naturally expressed with configuration-LP variables; pricing or EFX-compatible support selection is the likely bottleneck. The theorem motivates that investigation, but it does not establish that its guarantees extend to a large population.

The soft spot is the change from three named agents with divider, subdivider, and chooser roles to many recipients divided into valuation cohorts. That is a real generalization, and these authors might regard the scaled EFX/MMS objective as theirs while declining to view the role-based theorem as directly mirrored. I think the population-level fairness question is still a plausible continuation of their underlying problem—especially since the paper already studies distributions, proportionality, MMS, and envy across additive agents—but the case rests on that broader recognition, not on a limit of their three-agent proof.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 5.1, the three-agent FPTAS. It is a genuine computational result, so I would not attack it on the ground that the paper has nothing to mirror. The better objection is that its population axis is built into the theorem: every instance divides goods among exactly three named agents, uses \(\mathrm{MMS}(v_i,3)\), and constructs allocations through divider, subdivider, and chooser roles.

There are two ways to scale that setting. If we replicate independent three-agent instances, each can be solved by the paper’s algorithm; aggregating their outcomes adds no shared allocation problem. If we pool the recipients and goods into one market, as the proposed mirror does, the number of claimants grows and the benchmark becomes an \(N\)-recipient MMS. EFX must then hold across all recipients’ bundles, and the three-role construction disappears. That is a coherent large-population fair-division problem, but it is a substantial move from the theorem’s few-agent question.

The proposed model also chooses a particular new economy: standardized good types, a per-capita supply vector, and a bundle cap \(K\). Those choices make aggregate allocations over a finite set of integral bundles well-defined. With \(q\), \(K\), and \(\tau\) fixed, population size itself disappears from the decision problem; the remaining work is over a fixed bundle alphabet and mass variables. When those parameters grow, the hard dimensions are the goods and bundle patterns, not population multiplicity. That observation does not make the question uninteresting—the resulting LP or configuration formulation could be useful—but it makes the proposed question look like a new parameterized fair-division model, rather than the continuous version of the paper’s result.

That is the best case against the anchor: preserve the three-agent problem and continuization is vacuous; pool agents and the mirror becomes a large-\(N\) extension. The case is weak, though. The paper itself identifies extending beyond three agents as an open direction, and the proposed high-multiplicity setting is a plausible, precise way to pursue it. I can argue that this is adjacent work rather than a direct mirror, but I cannot honestly say that the best version fails the programme’s recognition test or that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
