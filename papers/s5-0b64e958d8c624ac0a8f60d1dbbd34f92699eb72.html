Why no mirror

The distributional undomination search is coherent and closely matches the paper’s stated open question about finding such committees. But none of the paper’s numbered results asserts an algorithm, a complexity bound, a hardness result, or a computational formulation; its structural bounds and existence corollaries therefore fail bit (a).

What the mirror covers

The proposed search covers committee selection by undomination and its connection to deterministic metric distortion. It does not provide a computational mirror of any numbered result in the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a positive case for a continuous-population version of the committee-selection problem, but the paper has no qualifying computational anchor: none of its numbered results says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. Theorem 2, proved here, bounds a committee’s distortion by \(\max_{c\notin W} 2n/M(W,c)-1\); Corollary 3 turns \(\alpha\)-undomination into the bound \((1+\alpha)/(1-\alpha)\). Corollary 4 says that, when there are at least five candidates, some size-five committee has distortion at most \(2.7384\), using an existence result of Song, Nguyen, and Lin. Theorem 6, also proved here, gives a converse relation between distortion and undomination. These are structural or existence results, not complexity results. The paper itself presents efficient committee-finding algorithms as an open question.

The strongest candidate mirror is Continuous Undominated Committee Search. Its input is a candidate set \(C\), an integer \(k\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) observed complete rankings, with each type’s mass a binary rational and the masses summing to one. For a committee \(W\subseteq C\), define
\[ q_\mu(W,c)=\sum_{t}\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[c\succ_t w\text{ for every }w\in W\right]. \]
This is the fraction of society that prefers the outside candidate \(c\) to every member of \(W\). The optimization question is to find a size-\(k\) committee minimizing \(\max_{c\notin W} q_\mu(W,c)\). Equivalently, given a threshold \(\alpha\), return a size-\(k\) committee that is \(\alpha\)-undominated, or report that none exists. The committee \(W\) is the decision variable; its members are candidates, while the input mass is distributed across ranking types.

A natural regime is a large ranked election or public consultation with many participants but relatively few distinct rankings: \(n\) may be very large while \(\tau\ll n\). Each type is a complete ranking, and its mass is the share of participants with that ranking. This is high multiplicity applied directly to the paper’s problem: its undomination criterion already measures fractions of voters. The metric results have a natural mass-weighted version too: Theorem 2’s bound becomes \(\operatorname{dist}_\mu(W)\le \max_{c\notin W}(2/M_\mu(W,c)-1)\), where \(M_\mu(W,c)\) is the mass preferring some member of \(W\) to \(c\). Thus Corollaries 3 and 4 motivate the search objective without changing the paper’s committee question.

For the fixed-size case emphasized by Corollary 4, especially \(k=5\), this search is tractable by enumerating committees and evaluating their margins in \(O\!\left(\binom{m}{k}m\tau\,\mathrm{poly}(L)\right)\) time, where \(L\) is the input bit length. Corollary 4’s existence guarantee means the search can find a size-five committee with the stated distortion bound. That algorithm is a deduction, not a result claimed in the paper. When \(k\) is part of the input, I would expect hardness to transfer if the corresponding finite-profile search is hard: every finite profile embeds as masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and the hard choices would be among candidates. This note supplies no such hardness result, so that expectation remains a question for follow-up work.

The mirror covers committee selection by undomination and its link to deterministic metric distortion. I would leave Corollary 7’s lottery-support statement aside: its randomization concerns the outcome, whereas the proposed mirror continuizes the population. The weakest point is that the committee remains a discrete subset of candidates; the continuum is in the input distribution, and the paper’s bounds already depend on voter fractions. This is a plausible high-multiplicity computational question that the authors would recognize, but the paper does not provide a named complexity anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 and Corollary 3 are bounds, Corollary 4 combines such a bound with a prior existence theorem, and Theorem 6 proves a converse structural implication. None classifies an algorithmic problem. The proposed search is therefore a follow-up inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its computational results.

That follow-up is coherent: a distribution over rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity input, and the undomination objective remains meaningful under mass. But for the paper’s highlighted case \(k=5\), enumerate the \(\binom{m}{5}\) committees and evaluate each candidate’s undominated mass across the \(\tau\) types. This takes \(O\!\left(\binom{m}{5}m\tau\,\mathrm{poly}(L)\right)\) time. It constructs a committee with Corollary 4’s guarantee because the corollary says one exists. The mass formulation adds no continuous-optimization step or new structural insight to that result.

The stronger version—minimize maximum undominated mass when \(k\) is part of the input—could be a worthwhile high-multiplicity problem. But it is not a complexity result from this paper. A rational distribution can be scaled to an integer profile, and the committee choices and objective are unchanged. That equivalence is not itself an objection: high multiplicity is a valid regime. It does, however, mean this proposed question stands or falls as a new algorithmic follow-up, not as a mirror of the paper’s theorems.

The metric claims do not supply a separate computational anchor. The bounds in Theorem 2 and Corollary 3 carry over by replacing voter counts with masses; the witness in Theorem 6 can likewise be weighted by type mass. Those are continuous statements about distortion and undomination, not computational questions. Corollary 7’s lottery support concerns outcome randomization, which the programme’s scope excludes.

So the negative case is strong only under the stated anchor requirement: the proponent has not identified a named computational result to continuize. It is weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The paper explicitly asks for algorithms to find undominated committees, and its high-multiplicity version is plausible. I cannot honestly rule that out as a useful follow-up; I can argue that it is a new question rather than a mirror of this paper’s results.
