The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a computational continuation of the paper’s group-based game, not a discrete-to-continuous mirror. The paper already takes the electorate to be a continuum: in its baseline, types \(a,b,c\) have masses \(\alpha(1-k)\), \((1-\alpha)(1-k)\), and \(k\), and turnout is uncertain. Recasting that same electorate as a distribution over types would restate the paper’s model.

There is also no qualifying named computational anchor. The closest is Proposition 11, proved in the paper: with plurality and a group for each strict preference ordering, sincere voting is an equilibrium iff \(W/Y\) is at least the maximum, over voter types \(ij\), of the ratio between (i) the change in the probability that the voter’s third choice is defeated when that group votes sincerely rather than strategically and (ii) the change in the probability that its first choice wins. The probabilities are defined from the sincere vote totals \(v_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}q_{ij}\). That is an equilibrium characterization, not a result classifying the complexity of a computational problem as in P, NP-hard, or similar. I would not present it as an anchor under your rule.

A plausible unanchored follow-on is *Pure Group Equilibrium under Aggregate Turnout*. An instance has \(m\) candidates and \(\tau\) voter types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Each type specifies a strict ranking, a rational cardinal utility for each candidate, and an independent turnout fraction \(Q_t\sim U[0,1]\). A pure strategy assigns each type one candidate; when \(Q_t\) is realized, mass \(\mu_tQ_t\) votes for that candidate. Ties are broken uniformly. Ask whether a pure group-level Nash equilibrium exists, and, if so, output an assignment in which no type can improve its expected utility by switching candidates.

A plausible regime is a large national or local election with many voters but a moderate number of shared preference and interest types. This preserves the paper’s group-level decision and welfare objective, while making \(\tau\) a variable part of the input. The authors would likely recognize the equilibrium problem as theirs; the new question is its computational complexity. My provisional expectation is Class C: exact deviation payoffs involve probabilities over a \(\tau\)-dimensional turnout cube, alongside a search over type-to-candidate assignments. That is a conjecture, not an established hardness result; a discrete embedding could instead make it Class B. Natural follow-ups are whether equilibrium existence differs from finding one, whether the problem is tractable for fixed \(\tau\) or fixed \(m\), and whether mixed equilibria have a more manageable description.

The weak point is decisive for the requested case: this follow-on does not have a qualifying named anchor in the paper, and the population is continuous there already. So it may be a worthwhile computational study of the authors’ model, but I cannot honestly claim the paper supplies the required anchored continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result to mirror, and the population is already continuous. The paper’s baseline explicitly has a continuum of voters with masses \(\alpha(1-k)\), \((1-\alpha)(1-k)\), and \(k\). Replacing it with a distribution over voter types would reproduce its model. The paper instead proves equilibrium characterizations and comparative statics.

Proposition 11 is the closest candidate, but it says when sincere voting is an equilibrium; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Propositions 1–15 similarly characterize equilibrium existence, uniqueness, or behavior. Proposition 10’s \(n\) counts strategic subgroups: increasing it changes who coordinates and can change the equilibria. It does not count interchangeable voters within a type, so it supplies no high-multiplicity problem whose population dimension can be relaxed.

The proposed pure-equilibrium computation is the strongest follow-on. It would ask a new algorithmic question about the paper’s existing continuum game. For the paper’s three-candidate setting, there are at most six strict-ranking types and at most \(3^6\) pure action profiles; the population size supplies no growing input dimension. A version with variable \(m\) and \(\tau\) might become difficult because it generalizes the game and its strategy search. That difficulty would concern equilibrium computation and turnout-probability calculations in a continuum model, rather than a computational problem made tractable or newly hard by continuizing the population.

A finite-voter stochastic model followed by a limit theorem is another possible connection. If it reproduces the paper’s group-level turnout shocks, the limit recovers a model the paper already studies; if it uses independent individual turnout, the limiting uncertainty can differ. Either route might yield a result about convergence, but it would not furnish an anchored computational mirror.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s anchor test: every plausible mirror either restates the paper’s continuous population or introduces a new computational problem without a discrete result to continuize. The honest limitation is that equilibrium computation for this game could still be a worthwhile separate project. Its potential value does not establish a continuous mirror of this paper.
