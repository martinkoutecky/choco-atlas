The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\), a list of \(\tau\) rankings \(t\in\mathcal{S}_m\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and rational \(\epsilon<0.05\), suppose \(\mu=(1-\eta)M(\phi,\pi^*)+\eta\nu\) for unknown \(\phi<1\), central ranking \(\pi^*\), \(\eta\le\epsilon\), and arbitrary distribution \(\nu\) over rankings. Output \((\tilde\phi,\tilde\pi)\) with probability at least \(2/3\) such that \(\|v_{\pi^*}-v_{\tilde\pi}\|_2\le O(\epsilon\log(1/\epsilon)/(1-\phi))\), \(|\phi-\tilde\phi|\le O(\epsilon/\sqrt m)\), and \(D_{\mathrm{TV}}(M(\phi,\pi^*),M(\tilde\phi,\tilde\pi))\le O(\epsilon\log(1/\epsilon))\), in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\), where \(L\) is the input bit length.

The model it lives in

Treat each complete ranking \(t\in\mathcal{S}_m\) as a voter type with population mass \(\mu_t\). The honest population has Mallows masses around \(\pi^*\), while an adversarial subpopulation of mass \(\eta\) may hold any rankings. The task is to recover \(\phi\) and \(\pi^*\); the decision variables are the estimated parameters, and the guarantee measures ranking-position error and total variation between type distributions.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.6’s robust recovery of the central ranking and Mallows parameters, capturing the coalition-corruption component of Corollary 1.8. It does not transfer the finite-sample noise term in that corollary or claim continuous versions of the paper’s other results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can exact rational masses improve the recovery guarantees or bit complexity compared with drawing synthetic rankings and applying Theorem 1.6?
  • What finite-electorate dictionary relates this mass-input problem to Theorem 1.6’s sampling and corruption bounds?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is robust Mallows recovery when the electorate itself is given as a distribution over ranking types.

Take \(m\) alternatives and let each type \(t\in\mathcal S_m\) be a complete ranking. The society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\): \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of people reporting ranking \(t\). A plausible regime is a very large electorate with a fixed or moderate candidate set. Then the number of distinct ranking types is at most \(m!\), while the electorate size \(N\) can be much larger than the support size \(\tau\). Honest voters’ rankings follow a Mallows model around a common central ranking; a coalition controls at most an \(\epsilon\) fraction of the society and can report any rankings. Ranking is a complete type for this problem because the estimator uses no other voter-specific attributes.

My anchor is Theorem 1.6 (Main), proved in this paper. It says that, for \(\epsilon<0.1\), given \(s\ge (n/\epsilon)^2\operatorname{poly}(\log(n/\epsilon))\) samples from an unknown Mallows model \(M(\phi,\pi^*)\), with up to an \(\epsilon\) fraction arbitrarily corrupted, a \(\operatorname{poly}(s)\)-time algorithm succeeds with probability at least \(1-(\epsilon/n)^8\). It outputs \(M(\tilde\phi,\tilde\pi^*)\) with
\[ \|v_{\pi^*}-v_{\tilde\pi^*}\|_2 \le O\!\left(\frac{\epsilon\log(1/\epsilon)}{1-\phi}\right), \qquad |\phi-\tilde\phi|\le O(\epsilon/\sqrt n), \]
and
\[ D_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\left(M(\phi,\pi^*),M(\tilde\phi,\tilde\pi^*)\right) \le O(\epsilon\log(1/\epsilon)). \]
The paper proves this theorem using Mallows-specific analysis and cited robust mean-estimation subroutines. The paper has no named hardness result to anchor on.

Continuous problem — Robust Mallows Recovery from a Population Measure (lead). An instance consists of \(m\), a support list of \(\tau\) rankings with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and a rational \(\epsilon<0.05\). The promise is that, for some unknown \(\phi<1\), central ranking \(\pi^*\), \(\eta\le\epsilon\), and arbitrary distribution \(\nu\),
\[ \mu=(1-\eta)M(\phi,\pi^*)+\eta\nu. \]
The task is to output \((\tilde\phi,\tilde\pi)\) satisfying the three error bounds above with \(n\) replaced by \(m\), with probability at least \(2/3\). Count running time in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\), where \(L\) is the input bit length.

This is a credible Class A question. Draw \(s\) synthetic rankings from the input mass distribution \(\mu\), then run the algorithm of Theorem 1.6. Each synthetic draw is Mallows-generated with probability at least \(1-\epsilon\); with high probability, at most a \(2\epsilon\) fraction are corrupt. The paper’s strong-contamination guarantee therefore applies at corruption rate \(2\epsilon<0.1\), preserving the stated error bounds up to constants. Sampling from the rational support and running the paper’s algorithm takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\). The algorithm’s samples are an internal way to process the mass input; in the model, \(\mu_t\) still means a fraction of the population.

This should be recognizable to the authors: it keeps their target, Mallows assumption, and arbitrary coalition, and changes the input from individual reports to the electorate’s type masses. Mallows probabilities serve here as population fractions of ranking types, not as probabilities over outcomes. The mirror covers Theorem 1.6’s robust recovery result; it does not claim a continuous version of every result in the paper.

The main limitation is that the Mallows assumption is restrictive, and for \(0<\phi<1\) its support can be all \(m!\) rankings. Thus this is most convincing with fixed or moderate \(m\); when \(m\) grows, \(\tau\) may be enormous. There is also a real distinction between an exact population measure and the paper’s finite random sample: this mirror is a high-multiplicity population version, not a replacement for the finite-sample theorem. Natural follow-ups are to quantify the finite-electorate dictionary, including sampling error, and to ask whether exact masses can improve the sample complexity or error bounds.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is about the instance regime the proposed mirror actually reaches. For every \(0<\phi<1\), a Mallows model assigns positive mass to every ranking. Since \(\eta<1\), the promised society \(\mu=(1-\eta)M(\phi,\pi^*)+\eta\nu\) therefore has \(\tau=m!\), whatever the contaminating distribution. The explicit mass-list input is factorial in the number of alternatives, while the paper’s theorem works from only \(\operatorname{poly}(m,1/\epsilon)\) sampled ballots. So this mirror does not deliver the especially compelling high-multiplicity regime of a large electorate with relatively few observed ballot types; its exact society description can be much larger than the finite sample the paper already handles.

That objection weakens the mirror’s practical and computational motivation, but it does not defeat it under the programme’s rules. Fixed \(m\) still gives a sensible large-population scenario, and the permitted running-time parameters explicitly include \(\tau\). The better empirical-frequency version, with at most \(s\) supported rankings, is a valid high-multiplicity formulation too. It compresses repeated ballots but preserves exactly the data the estimator uses.

Nor can I dismiss the anchor because the paper already gives an algorithm: its theorem supplies precisely the reduction that solves the mass-input problem. That makes the mirror easy to answer, but a known or dull answer is not a reason to reject a well-posed question. The Mallows distribution is part of the paper’s statistical model, but the paper’s input is a finite corrupted sample, not an exact society measure, so calling this a collision would overstate the case.

The anchor survives. I find no honest argument here that defeats robust Mallows recovery as a continuous-population mirror; the negative case is limited to the fact that its exact-input regime is less compelling than the proponent suggests.
