The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. The numbered results are about equilibrium and welfare: Theorem 1 proves uniqueness of the weak PBE; Theorems 2 and 3 give conditions for agents to prefer the dynamic mechanism; and Theorem 4 proves that expected total surplus converges to the Vickrey-like auction’s surplus as \(M\to\infty\). They do not assert that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classify its complexity.

The strongest foothold for a continuous-population case is Theorem 4, proved here: the paper explicitly studies a large-market limit. But that is an analytical limit of finite order statistics, not a computational result about a society represented by masses of types. The model also assumes private values drawn from \(F\); it does not give the finite-support, binary-rational population input needed for the programme’s central computational questions. Turning it into such a problem would require proposing a new model and anchor, rather than mirroring a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

This paper has no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1 characterizes a weak perfect Bayesian equilibrium; Theorems 2 and 3 compare expected payoffs; Theorem 4 compares welfare in a large-market limit. None gives an algorithmic classification for bribery, control, campaigning, robustness, or another computational task. A question about computing one of these quantities would need a new computational problem and input model, rather than a continuous mirror of a result the paper proves.

Theorem 4 is the strongest candidate for a mirror: as \(M\to\infty\), the mechanism’s expected total surplus approaches the Vickrey-like auction’s. But the result tracks the highest individual valuations in a finite sample. In an atomless population with distribution \(F\), the corresponding winners sit at the upper edge of the support, which has zero mass when \(F\) is continuous. A mass allocation cannot assign two indivisible objects to those zero-mass types. Per-capita normalization makes the fixed supply vanish; retaining total surplus requires tracking selected individuals or extreme order statistics, which a distribution of population mass does not encode. Scaling supply with the population could avoid that degeneration, but it would require a mechanism with an extensive number of contracts and auction allocations. That changes the object whose welfare Theorem 4 compares.

One could instead propose a computational question about finding the equilibrium or evaluating the welfare threshold. The paper already gives the equilibrium strategy and an integral condition for the contracted intermediary’s choice. With a finite, explicitly represented valuation distribution, that condition becomes a finite calculation. With a general density \(F\), a complexity question needs a further encoding or oracle model. Either route may motivate a separate computational study, but neither supplies a computational result in this paper for continuization to mirror.

This is a strong case against a mirror anchored in the paper’s results. It cannot rule out every future extensive-supply model inspired by the paper; such a model could be worthwhile in its own right, but its computational questions would need to be formulated and justified separately.
