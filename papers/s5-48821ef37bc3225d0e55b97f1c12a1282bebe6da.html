The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), observed masses \(0\leq\nu_t\leq\mu_t\), signals \(\tilde q_t\), and accuracies \(\epsilon,\eta>0\), suppose there are \(w_t\geq0\) and \(\theta\in\mathbb R\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t w_t^2=1\) and \(\sum_t\nu_t(\tilde q_t-q_{w,\theta}(t))^2\leq\eta^2\). Find normalized weights \(v_t\geq0\) and a threshold \(\phi\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_t v_t^2=1\) and \(\bigl[\sum_t\nu_t(q_{v,\phi}(t)-\tilde q_t)^2\bigr]^{1/2}\leq\epsilon+\eta\), where \(q_{v,\phi}(t)=\mu_t^{-1/2}\mathbb E[\operatorname{sgn}(\sum_s\sqrt{\mu_s}v_s Z_s-\phi)Z_t]\u005c) for independent standard Gaussian Z_t\).

The model it lives in

A society with \(\tau\) homogeneous voting tiers of masses \(\mu_t\), where each tier has a common nonnegative voting weight. The rule acts on Gaussian aggregate coalition fluctuations with variance normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t w_t^2=1\); the observed type signal is the correspondingly normalized degree-one correlation. In this model \(q_{w,\theta}(t)=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,e^{-\theta^2/2}w_t\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 8’s partial Chow reconstruction result; it leaves the paper’s partial Shapley reconstruction result and its supporting structural results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give a bit-complexity algorithm for partial reconstruction in \(\tau\), the input bit length, and the accuracy parameters, including a characterization of feasible completions for unobserved types.
  • Bound the finite-population error between the rescaled tier-level Chow parameters and \(q_{w,\theta}(t)\), uniformly over thresholds and tier masses in the near-balanced regime.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of the paper’s partial Chow reconstruction problem, in a large weighted-voting society with a small number of homogeneous voting tiers. For example, a large shareholder body might have many members in each of a few tiers, with members in a tier carrying the same vote weight. The regime is \(N\) voters and \(\tau\) tiers, with \(N\gg\tau\). The power-index observations can cover only some of the voters in some tiers.

Here is a precise mirror. Let \(T\) be the set of \(\tau\) tiers. The instance gives each tier’s population mass \(\mu_t\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); the mass \(\nu_t\leq\mu_t\) whose power indices were observed; and approximate observed indices \(\tilde q_t\) for tiers with \(\nu_t>0\). The unknown rule assigns a common nonnegative weight \(w_t\) to each voter in tier \(t\), and has threshold \(\theta\). Normalize \(\sum_t\mu_t w_t^2=1\), since scaling all weights and the threshold together leaves the rule unchanged.

For the continuous model, take independent standard Gaussians \(Z_t\), and define the voting rule on aggregate coalition fluctuations by
\[ F_{w,\theta}(Z)=\operatorname{sgn}\!\left(\sum_{t\in T}\sqrt{\mu_t}\,w_t Z_t-\theta\right). \]
The type-level power signal is
\[ q_{w,\theta}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_t}}\, \mathbb E\!\left[F_{w,\theta}(Z)Z_t\right]. \]
The problem, Continuous Partial Chow Reconstruction, is: given \((T,\mu,\nu,\tilde q,\epsilon,\eta)\), promised that the observations are within \(\eta\) of the signals of some such hidden rule, output nonnegative weights \(v_t\) and a threshold \(\phi\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t v_t^2=1\), whose observed type signals satisfy
\[ \left(\sum_{t\in T}\nu_t\bigl(q_{v,\phi}(t)-\tilde q_t\bigr)^2\right)^{1/2} \leq \epsilon+\eta. \]
The solution is a full weighted voting rule, judged by its error on the measured population mass.

This is a population limit of the Chow model, with the index normalization made explicit. In the finite society take \(N\mu_t\) voters of tier \(t\), each with weight \(w_t/\sqrt N\), and threshold \(\theta\). The random coalition score converges to \(\sum_t\sqrt{\mu_t}w_tZ_t\); for a voter in tier \(t\), \(\sqrt N\) times that voter’s Chow parameter converges to \(q_{w,\theta}(t)\). If \(N\nu_t\) voters in tier \(t\) are observed, the paper’s partial Euclidean error converges to the weighted error in the stated problem. The Gaussian description is the limit of the existing Chow index under a many-voter regime; the object being reconstructed remains the voting society’s rule.

The anchor is Theorem 8, proved in this paper and given as the detailed version of its introductory Theorem 5. It says that, given \(n\), an error parameter \(\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac12)\), a confidence parameter \(\delta>0\), and partial Chow parameters from some unknown \(n\)-variable LTF, an algorithm outputs a weighted representation of an LTF whose partial Chow distance on the supplied coordinates is \(O(\epsilon)\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\), in time \(n^2\log n\cdot\log(1/\delta)\cdot2^{\mathrm{poly}(1/\epsilon)}\). I rely on its voter-coordinate case \(S\subseteq[n]\), omitting the optional constant coefficient. [Theorem 8 in the published paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v134/bennett21a/bennett21a.pdf) is a direct algorithmic result on reconstructing a voting rule from partial power-index information.

The authors have good reason to recognize this as their problem’s continuous analogue: it keeps the unknown weighted threshold rule, partial Chow observations, and the task of producing a rule that matches those observations. The type quotient follows the paper’s own setting when many voters have identical weights. The continuum problem should fall in Class A. In fact, under the Gaussian limit its signal has the closed form
\[ q_{w,\theta}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,e^{-\theta^2/2}w_t. \]
Thus observed signals reveal weight ratios across observed tiers, and the remaining mass can be completed subject to the normalization. This suggests a small-support algorithm; the paper’s finite-\(n\) running-time bound does not itself prove that algorithm.

The scope here is deliberately one result: partial Chow reconstruction. The paper supplies no comparable named hardness result to anchor a claim that discrete hardness dissolves. Further questions include proving explicit finite-\(N\) error bounds for the mirror, studying regimes where the number of tiers grows with the population, and defining a corresponding type-level Shapley reconstruction problem.

The weakest point is the scaling and limit: individual Chow parameters shrink as the population grows, so the mirror uses their \(\sqrt N\)-rescaled type signal and a Gaussian fluctuation limit. That is a real high-multiplicity bridge for near-balanced voting systems, but it makes the continuous problem easier than the finite Boolean-cube reconstruction problem. The case stands as a faithful continuous question about the same reconstruction task, not as a claim that Theorem 8 transfers unchanged.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 8’s continuous counterpart depends on a particular fluctuation limit, rather than following directly from replacing voter counts by masses. For a fixed quota away from half the total weight, the standardized threshold moves out of the Gaussian window as the electorate grows, and the Chow signals collapse. The proposed \(q_{w,\theta}\) stays informative by scaling individual weights as \(1/\sqrt N\) and keeping the quota within \(O(\sqrt N)\) of the mean. That gives a coherent near-balanced regime, but it makes the mirror a second-order model of random coalition fluctuations. In a deterministic nonatomic voting society, an individual has zero pivotal power; the Gaussian signal needs an additional sampling model to recover a nonzero type-level quantity.

A stronger version could define power through random coalition fractions and take their central-limit fluctuations. That is mathematically natural, and the finite-\(N\) derivation gives it a credible bridge. But it still selects a specific fluctuation scale and quota regime. Extending it to general quota proportions leads to different asymptotics, where the ordinary Chow signals vanish and a useful replacement would need to be chosen. This is a real limitation on how broadly the mirror represents weighted-voting reconstruction.

It does not defeat the anchor. Theorem 8 is plainly a named computational result, the tier model makes the population mass explicit, and the paper’s Chow index supplies a principled route to the Gaussian signal. Near-balanced voting is a plausible regime, and the programme allows model co-modification when population continuization requires it. The fact that the limit may be easier is not a valid objection here. So the negative case is weak: it narrows the mirror’s reach, but does not show that this scenario is unworthy of study.
