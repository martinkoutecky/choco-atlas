The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor test yields no qualifying anchor. The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. “Max-GP,” “Min-GP,” and “Fixed-GP” are named problem definitions; equation (20) is an ILP objective, not a numbered result. The paper solves instances and reports case-study findings, but those are not numbered results of the required kind. Its introduction cites NP-hardness for redistricting “in many of its formulations” from [15]; that is background, not a proved result here for this paper’s particular Max-GP. So there are no formal anchors to mirror under your rubric.

The strongest positive candidate is nevertheless clear: a population mirror of the paper’s explicitly formulated Maximum Gerrymandering Problem (Max-GP). This is a mirror of a named problem definition, not a qualifying result anchor.

Take a connected locality graph \(G=(V,E)\), \(K\) specified district centers, and \(M\) parties. Let a voter type be \((i,p)\), where \(i\) is the voter’s locality and \(p\) is their party choice, with an additional abstention category if needed. The type must include locality because location determines which districts the voter can belong to. Let \(\mu_{i,p}\) be the fraction of the registered electorate of that type, with \(\sum_{i,p}\mu_{i,p}=1\). The population mass of locality \(i\) is \(d_i=\sum_p\mu_{i,p}\). District population bounds are fractions \(a,b\) of the electorate.

A solution is a partition \((D_1,\ldots,D_K)\) of the localities: every locality is assigned whole to one district, each district contains its specified center, each induces a connected subgraph, and \(a\leq\sum_{i\in D_k}d_i\leq b\). Party \(m\)’s vote mass in district \(D_k\) is \(\sum_{i\in D_k}\mu_{i,m}\). Using the paper’s strict-plurality convention, count a district for \(m\) only if that mass exceeds every other party’s; maximize the number of districts counted for \(m\). The output is an attaining district map and its seat count. This keeps the districting objective, locality-level geographic constraints, and plurality rule intact. It continuizes the electorate, not seats, district maps, or uncertainty over maps.

A plausible regime is a countrywide election with millions of registered voters, hundreds or thousands of localities or census units, and a small set of party choices. Many voters then share each locality-and-choice type, so the number of agents can greatly exceed the support size. In the paper’s own case study, the vote data are already aggregated over 838 localities and four parties in MD32. The authors could plausibly recognize the mirror: their inputs \(p_i\) and \(v_i^m\) become normalized masses, while their district map and objective remain the same.

My forecast is hardness transfers (Class B) if the corresponding discrete graph-constrained Max-GP variant is hard. A discrete input with counts \(n_{i,p}\) maps to \(\mu_{i,p}=n_{i,p}/n\); after scaling the population bounds by \(n\), every feasible map and winner is preserved. Conversely, rational masses can be cleared to integer counts. Thus fractional vote masses do not dissolve the geographic partitioning combinatorics. The article does not prove hardness for this exact variant, so that forecast is conditional rather than an anchor-backed conclusion.

This mirror covers Max-GP, and naturally raises its Fixed-GP sibling: given a seat-count vector, does a feasible connected partition realizing it exist? One can also ask for complexity parameterized by \(K\), the number of parties, graph structure, and the support size and bit length of \(\mu\). The paper’s fairness ranking over feasible outcomes is downstream of those computational questions, not a separate result anchor.

The weak point is that the paper already aggregates votes by locality. Continuizing those counts gives a faithful high-multiplicity description, but it may not simplify the computation: the hard choices still concern how to partition the geographic graph. That makes this a credible population mirror, but a modest positive case for a new tractability story.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the anchor rule itself: this paper states Max-GP, Min-GP, and Fixed-GP, gives an ILP formulation, and reports computational case-study results, but it proves no numbered complexity or algorithmic result. The cited NP-hardness concerns districting formulations generally, not a result established here for the paper’s particular problem. So none of the proposed mirrors is a mirror of a qualifying result. On the rubric as written, the proponent supplied no qualifying anchor.

That is a narrow win, though. Max-GP has a coherent population mirror. Fix the locality graph and district centers, and let each voter type be a locality and party choice, with abstention included if needed. The mass at \((i,m)\) is the fraction of the electorate in locality \(i\) supporting party \(m\). Districts still consist of whole localities, obey the same contiguity and population bounds, and award a seat when one party’s vote mass strictly exceeds every other’s. Maximizing the number of seats for a party is then a well-posed computational question over a continuous society.

This is not defeated by the fact that the locality partition remains discrete. Continuization concerns the population; geographic units and district maps may remain discrete. Nor does the exact correspondence with integer instances make the question unworthy: the programme explicitly treats a discrete-to-continuous identity or transferred hardness as a possible result, not as an objection. Fixed-GP inherits the same answer: asking whether a given seat-count vector is attainable is a natural feasibility question for that mass model.

A richer model with voter densities over continuous territory and districts as connected regions might also be proposed. It would require replacing the paper’s locality graph and whole-locality assignment rules, so it is a less faithful mirror. But the simpler locality-and-party type model needs no such redesign, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as their problem with normalized vote counts.

So I cannot make a strong universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The defensible negative is that the paper fails the requested *result-anchor* test; the proposed Max-GP and Fixed-GP mirrors survive as plausible questions if problem definitions are allowed to count.
