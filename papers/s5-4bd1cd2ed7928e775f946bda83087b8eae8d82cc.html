The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a research direction, not a continuous mirror anchored in this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, proposition, or computational formulation result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally classified. Its numbered formal statement, Definition 1 (IvFoC Revelation Principle), defines a behavioral condition; it gives no computational result. The findings that 85% of spectators reveal intrinsic value of freedom at least once and that 68% use cardinality-family rules for freedom are empirical results, not complexity anchors. So there are no qualifying anchors here, and no per-anchor prover questions or expected complexity classes to report.

There is a plausible seed for a separate project: model a large population of policy spectators as a distribution \(\mu\) over types \(\theta=(R_\theta,\succeq_F^\theta,\succeq_W^\theta)\), where each type records preferences over hospitals and rankings of feasible menus by freedom and welfare. One could then study how to aggregate those rankings or choose among menus. But this paper does not define that collective decision problem, its objective, or its constraints; it estimates individual response patterns from a survey. Presenting a newly specified aggregation or menu-design problem as a mirror of one of the paper’s computational results would invent an anchor the paper does not provide.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no anchors here to defeat: the proponent correctly notes that the paper states no computational problem or complexity result. Definition 1 formalizes a behavioral condition; the headline percentages and rule classifications are empirical findings. Replacing respondent counts with population masses would restate those findings as proportions, not mirror a computational result.

The strongest proposed extension is a population distribution over spectator types, followed by a problem such as choosing a hospital menu for that population. A large population of people with recurring menu judgments is plausible. But the paper supplies neither the policy choices that are feasible nor a rule for turning individual, ordinal welfare judgments into a collective objective. Summing those judgments would require extra cardinal information; choosing majority rule or another aggregator would add a substantive model the paper does not provide. Adding capacities, budgets, or a larger menu-design space could create a real optimization problem, but its computational structure would come from those additions, not from a result in this paper.

The paper’s actual calculations also offer little computational obstacle to mirror. It classifies each response pattern against 23 rules over 15 comparisons. With a population distribution over such patterns, aggregate frequencies can be computed directly; individuals’ answers do not interact. A continuous population representation may be natural for describing the survey population, but it does not by itself expose a complexity question.

So the negative case is strong against a *mirror of this paper’s results*: there is no computational result to mirror, and the proposed menu-design problem needs substantial new modeling. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could grow from the paper. A policy-design project using population distributions might be worthwhile, and the authors could recognize it as a follow-up. I would classify that as a new computational question motivated by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.
