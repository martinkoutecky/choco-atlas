The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly supply a result-anchored positive case: this paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. It states Arrow’s impossibility theorem in prose, and reports a result of Plazola and Guillén (2008), but neither is a named computational result of this paper. The reported result is also about axiomatic aggregation, not computational complexity.

The closest plausible population mirror is the paper’s additive aggregation rule, equation (1). Let \(A\) be the finite set of alternatives, and let each type \(t\) have the evaluation vector \(v_t:A\to[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\) used by that rule. A society is a finite support distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with rational masses summing to \(1\). Per-capita Additive Aggregation asks for the group’s weak order on \(A\), defined by
\[ a\succeq_\mu b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_t\mu_t v_t(a)\ge \sum_t\mu_t v_t(b). \]
A solution is that weak order, including its ties. The mass is each type’s share of the population; the decision is the resulting social ranking. In a large electorate where many people use the same normalized evaluation vectors, \(N\) can greatly exceed the number of distinct types \(\tau\). Averaging rather than summing preserves the paper’s ranking.

This is a recognizable continuous restatement of the additive constitution, and its computational direction is straightforward: compute the \(m\) weighted sums and sort them, in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. But it is only a candidate mirror, not an anchor-backed result. The paper’s central proposal concerns how second-order preferences could justify or measure preference strength. It does not specify a computational procedure that turns a ranking over possible social orders into the evaluation vector \(v_t\). The problem above takes those vectors as input, so it bypasses that issue and offers little complexity landscape to chart. Whether the authors would accept it as the continuous form of their *main* question is therefore doubtful.

My strongest positive claim is narrow: the paper’s explicit additive aggregation formula has a sensible high-multiplicity, population-distribution interpretation. The likely answer is tractability, and further questions would concern how to derive or identify the \(v_t\) vectors from second-order rankings and how the output changes under the paper’s admissible common scale transformations. Those questions remain underspecified here. The weakness is decisive for the requested anchoring standard: the paper supplies no named computational result for the mirror to inherit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Arrow’s impossibility theorem is an axiomatic result; the paper’s own proposal and its reported result from Plazola and Guillén concern aggregation and axioms, not complexity. There is no stated computational problem with a theorem, lemma, or corollary about its complexity. That is a gap in the proposed case, not an argument that a continuous answer would be unchanged or uninteresting.

The proponent’s additive aggregation model is a fair population interpretation. A large society can have many people sharing a complete, calibrated evaluation type \(v_t\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\). I would not reject it for lacking multiplicity. But computing
\[ v_\mu(a)=\sum_t \mu_t v_t(a) \]
and ordering the alternatives does not mirror the paper’s central question. The paper is concerned with how preference strength can be measured and compared, and whether second-order preferences can provide that information. Taking \(v_t\) as input assumes that question has already been answered.

A stronger version would make second-order preferences themselves the types: give each type’s ranking over possible social orders and ask for an aggregate order consistent with the paper’s equal-influence proposal. That runs into the same missing specification. The paper does not give a computational map from such a ranking to a value function \(v_t\), nor a procedure that fixes the relevant scales. If the type includes \(v_t\), the question reduces to weighted summation; if it includes only the second-order ranking, the paper has not specified the rule whose output the algorithm should compute. Defining that rule could be worthwhile work, but it would supply a new formal model rather than continuize a computational result in this paper.

One could also add costs and ask how much mass must change type to alter the social order. That would be a sensible new population optimization problem, but the paper defines neither such interventions nor their costs. It would be inspired by the aggregation formula, not a mirror of a result the authors establish.

The negative case is therefore strong under the requested result-anchored standard, but it is not proof that no useful project can grow from the paper. Its weak point is exactly that distinction: a later, fully specified model might make a worthwhile continuous computational question. The paper as given does not provide the computational target for one.
