The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly give this paper a qualifying anchor. Theorem 1 proves an impossibility tradeoff between what the auctioneer and bidders learn; Theorem 2 gives sufficient conditions for a protocol to lie on the Privacy Frontier; and the propositions characterize particular protocols. None asserts a computational complexity result or reports an algorithm for computing the frontier.

A continuous population model is imaginable—for example, cohorts of bidders represented by masses over valuation types—but that would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a named result here. The paper’s privacy comparisons depend on which individual type profiles and bidder experiences a protocol distinguishes. Aggregating bidders into masses risks erasing exactly that information; keeping identities and their roles may defeat the high-multiplicity compression. A distribution over values alone would also be uncertainty about a finite population, not continuization of the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a mirror starts with the missing computational result. Theorem 1 proves an information tradeoff, Theorem 2 gives sufficient conditions for frontier membership, Proposition 1 characterizes static protocols, and Propositions 2–6 analyze particular auctions. None states a complexity bound or gives an algorithm for a computational problem. A continuous version of one of these results would therefore not mirror a computational result in the paper.

The strongest candidate for a new computational question is to design or verify protocols on the Privacy Frontier. But the object being compared is a partition of *labelled type profiles*: it records which profiles a particular bidder and the auctioneer can distinguish. The paper’s privacy claims are worst-case over profiles; the prior distribution over values does not enter them. Replacing bidders with a distribution over valuation types does not supply the missing computational input. It removes the correspondence between each bidder and their observations. Keeping bidder identities and roles in the type description preserves that correspondence, but then the number of types grows with the bidders and the proposed population compression disappears. Grouping identical valuations is legitimate; the difficulty is that equal valuations alone do not encode who received which questions or what that person inferred.

The natural large-auction limit does not repair this. In a single-unit auction with many atomless bidders, any one bidder has zero mass, while the paper’s pivotality arguments turn on changing one bidder’s type and tracking the consequences for that bidder’s experience. Treat a positive-mass cohort as the relevant actor and the model becomes one in which a cohort reports or receives information collectively. Scale supply with population to make cohorts affect allocation, and the choice rule changes into a market-allocation problem. Either could support worthwhile research, but neither preserves the paper’s individual privacy question.

So the proponent is right that there is no qualifying anchor here. A worthwhile algorithmic study of protocol synthesis may be possible, and the universal claim that no conceivable redesigned setting could merit attention is not provable from this paper. But once the candidate is made precise, its computational input is the choice rule, protocol tree, and labelled profiles—not a continuous society. On the programme’s terms, that is an adjacent project, not a continuous mirror of these results.
