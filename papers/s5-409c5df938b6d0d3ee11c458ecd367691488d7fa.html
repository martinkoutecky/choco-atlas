The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give a compliant positive case under your anchor rule: the paper proves no named result about whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally tractable. Its theorems concern price of anarchy, a game-theoretic welfare measure.

The closest negative-side anchor is Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper: for the asymmetric one-sided allocation problem with multi-unit demand agents, the price of anarchy of both Probabilistic Serial and Cardinal Probabilistic Serial is at least \(\Omega(\min\{n,\log m\})\). The closest upper-bound results are Theorems 3.3 and 3.4, also proved here: in that setting, the price of anarchy is \(O(\sqrt n\log m)\) for Cardinal Probabilistic Serial and for Probabilistic Serial, respectively. These are meaningful results about strategic welfare, but none is a computational-complexity anchor of the kind requested. The paper’s earlier Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 are likewise price-of-anarchy results, cited from [10].

There is a coherent *adjacent* continuum question. Let a society have total mass \(N\), with finitely many true valuation types \(t\in T\), each an additive unit-sum vector \(v^t\in\Delta_m\), and type masses \(N\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each type’s agents may distribute their reports across PS rankings or CPS valuation vectors. Goods have unit supply; each infinitesimal agent consumes at rate one, so aggregate consumption runs at rate \(N\). Define a nonatomic equilibrium by requiring each type’s reports to be best responses to the aggregate report distribution, with a single agent’s deviation leaving that distribution unchanged. Welfare is the integral of true valuations over the resulting bundles; the benchmark optimum assigns each item across type masses to maximize that welfare.

For large cohorts sharing a small number of complete valuation types, the concrete question would be: does the worst-equilibrium price of anarchy in this nonatomic PS or CPS game have a lower bound of \(\Omega(\min\{N,\log m\})\), as in Theorem 3.5? This is recognizable as a population-scale continuation of the paper’s mechanism and welfare question. Its weakest point is substantial: an individual deviation changes the consumption process in the finite game but has no effect on it in the nonatomic game. That can change the equilibrium set, so Theorem 3.5’s lower bound does not automatically carry over. I would treat this as a plausible new game-theoretic extension, not as a computational mirror anchored by the paper’s results. The ChoCo tractability/hardness trichotomy does not apply to the stated question.

The strongest repaired version of the proponent’s anchor is a finite-type society with masses of agents sharing complete valuation vectors, and a question about whether the price-of-anarchy lower bound in Theorem 3.5 persists for nonatomic PS or CPS. That is a coherent adjacent question. But it changes the strategic model: an individual of measure zero cannot change the aggregate consumption process. The finite-player incentive effects used in the paper’s equilibrium analysis therefore do not carry over automatically. One could instead study finite-player or approximate equilibria, but that retains the population count as part of the game; or define a nonatomic equilibrium and ask about its welfare, which is a new game-theoretic question. The same limitation applies to the upper bounds in Theorems 3.3 and 3.4: they motivate continuum welfare inequalities, not a continuous version of a computational result.

That defeats the anchors as ChoCo mirrors, but it does not prove that every adjacent project is unworthy. A computational study of worst-equilibrium welfare for finite-support nonatomic PS/CPS could be worthwhile; it would need a newly posed computational problem, rather than a named result from this paper to mirror. So the honest negative case is strong on programme fit and weak as a universal claim that no continuous-population scenario could be worth studying.
