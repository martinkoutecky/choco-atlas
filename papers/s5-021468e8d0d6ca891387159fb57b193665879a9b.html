The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: the paper’s count-based rules have natural mass-valued versions in a large membership pool. But the paper contains no named computational result of the kind this task requires as an anchor. Its theorems characterize preference relations axiomatically; none establishes a problem’s complexity or computes with a formulation. So there are no valid anchors, and I can’t honestly present a per-anchor prover question.

The closest foothold is Theorem 3, proved in the paper: IND, LM, and LD characterize the difference rule, under which \(A \succeq_d B\) exactly when \(|A\cap G|-|A\setminus G| \ge |B\cap G|-|B\setminus G|\). Theorem 2, also proved there, characterizes the bad-elements-priority rule: compare sets first by preferring fewer bad elements, then—if tied—more good elements. These are substantive results, but they are not computational anchors.

A plausible population regime is a large pool of prospective club or committee members, each classified as qualified or unqualified, with members in each category interchangeable for the decision at hand. The pool has \(N\) people but only two types. In the continuous version, let \(\mu_g,\mu_b\) be the fractions of each type, and represent a proposed group by selected masses \(q=(q_g,q_b)\), where \(0\le q_g\le\mu_g\) and \(0\le q_b\le\mu_b\). The direct mass analogue of Theorem 3 ranks \(q\) above \(r\) exactly when \(q_g-q_b\ge r_g-r_b\). Theorem 2’s analogue compares \(q\) and \(r\) lexicographically: fewer unqualified members first, then more qualified members.

One possible, unanchored computational question is: given rational \(\mu_g,\mu_b\) and two feasible group vectors \(q,r\), decide their ranking under either mass rule. The difference-rule query is an exact rational comparison; maximizing that score over a supplied polytope would be a linear program. This covers the paper’s count-based rules, not its full research agenda.

The weak point is that the paper studies how one decision maker ranks subsets, not a computational social-choice problem. The mass model is a plausible continuous extension of its rules, and the large pool makes the population interpretation credible, but the comparison task is straightforward and the optimization task requires adding structure absent from the paper. I would offer this as a research seed, not as evidence that a named computational result in this paper has a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case is the one the proponent already concedes: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorems 2 and 3 characterize preference relations on subsets from axioms; Theorem 1 gives implications from those axioms. The paper poses no decision, search, or optimization problem. So there is no complexity claim whose continuous analogue could fill ChoCo’s stated gap.

The large pool of interchangeable committee members is a credible high-multiplicity setting. It gives Theorem 3 a natural mass extension: compare \(q_g-q_b\) for selected good and bad masses. Theorem 2 likewise extends to a lexicographic comparison of bad mass and then good mass. Those are sensible continuous preference models, and I would not reject them on the grounds that homogeneity is implausible.

The difficulty is making them into worthwhile computational mirrors. Given two mass vectors, each comparison is a direct rational calculation. To ask for an optimization result, one must add a feasible family of groups and ask which group is best. The paper supplies no such feasibility model. With aggregate quotas on just the good and bad masses, the resulting problem is a two-variable linear or lexicographic optimization problem. If a richer constraint system supplies the computational difficulty, that difficulty belongs to the added selection problem; it does not mirror a computational result in this paper. A continuous axiomatic characterization could be interesting, but it would be outside ChoCo’s computational brief.

So the negative case is strong about the programme fit, not about the possibility of a useful continuous extension in some broader research agenda. A plausible continuous population exists, and the mass rules are well defined. What is missing is a computational problem from this paper for that population to illuminate.
