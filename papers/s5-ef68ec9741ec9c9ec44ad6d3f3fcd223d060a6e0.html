The continuous mirror question

Given a binary-rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many applicant classes, where each class specifies priority, a prior over preferences, search cost, and payoff vector, together with per-capita university capacities \(q_c\), compute each class’s optimal search-stopping and selection policy under the paper’s search technology and sequential priority process, then compute the resulting aggregate assignment and expected net student welfare. Each applicant receives one indivisible seat; assignments may be fractional only when reported as type-level mass.

The model it lives in

A continuum of applicants represented by mass over priority-ordered types, with per-capita capacities that scale with population size. Individual applicants retain the paper’s private preference uncertainty, search costs, utilities, and one-seat assignments; type-level mass records aggregate flows as sequential choices consume capacity.

The objection that survived

Proposition 4 gives a stopping rule for an individual with a known budget set; aggregating it over a population requires analyzing how capacity exhaustion changes the available set along the priority sequence.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers optimal search and aggregate assignments under sequential serial dictatorship; leaves the welfare comparison proofs, historical-cutoff treatment, and experimental behavioral results as studied in the finite market.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the optimal type-level policies, aggregate assignment, and expected net welfare be computed in time polynomial in the number of universities, support size, and input bit length?
  • How does the SeqSD versus DirSD welfare comparison behave when the finite priority sequence becomes a priority-ordered mass flow?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a large admissions system with many applicants, a small number of repeated score bands and preference or search-cost classes, and university capacities that scale with the population. A type could record an applicant’s priority band, preference state, information cost, and payoff vector; mass would be the fraction of applicants in that class. The decisions would be search policies and university submissions, with aggregate student welfare net of search costs as the objective. This regime is plausible for the authors’ model: they explicitly discuss sequential decisions by groups ordered by score.

But the paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says each student is weakly better off under SeqSD than DirSD under optimal information acquisition and truthful submission. Corollary 1 extends the comparison to noisy budget-set information. These are welfare results, not results about computational complexity or algorithms. Proposition 4 characterizes optimal stopping under SeqSD, but does not classify a problem as tractable or give a complexity result. Calling any of these a computational anchor would overstate what the paper proves.

So there is a plausible continuous-population research direction, but I can’t honestly present it as a mirror anchored in this paper’s named computational results. There is no qualifying anchor to restate or assign a tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hardness prediction.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

A large admissions system with repeated score bands, common information costs and preference priors, and capacities that scale with the population is a sensible continuous economic model. The paper itself points to sequential decisions by groups. So I would not base the case against a mirror on claiming that high multiplicity is implausible.

The problem is that this paper supplies no computational result for such a mirror to continue. Theorem 1’s strongest continuous version would ask whether aggregate welfare under SeqSD is at least aggregate welfare under DirSD for a distribution of applicant types. Theorem 1 already gives the individual comparison for arbitrary ex ante distributions over budget sets; weighting those comparisons by type masses gives the aggregate inequality. That is a valid welfare question, but it remains an analytical comparison, not a complexity or algorithm question.

Corollary 1 has the same limitation. A population distribution plus a signal model for historical cutoffs yields an aggregate version of its welfare comparison, again by applying the result to each type and aggregating. A computational question about choosing the best disclosure policy could be interesting, but the paper studies one cutoff treatment; it does not formulate an optimization problem over disclosure policies. That would be a new research problem, rather than a computational mirror of the corollary.

Proposition 4 is the closest candidate for a computational anchor because it characterizes optimal stopping. In a population model with finitely many applicant classes, one could calculate each class’s stopping behavior and aggregate search costs. But the proposition gives a marginal-benefit threshold for a student searching among available universities. Turning it into a population calculation does not create a complexity landscape. A harder version with general priors, correlated preferences, or equilibrium search would require specifying and solving a richer Bayesian matching model that the paper does not analyze computationally.

There is also a modeling tradeoff: preserving the paper’s strict priority order makes an applicant’s rank position consequential, so the natural type space grows with the market. Fixed score bands keep the support small, but introduce group-level decisions that are a variant of the paper’s individual serial dictatorship. The authors’ discussion makes that variant plausible; it does not supply a computational result for it.

So the negative case is strongest as a judgment about fit with ChoCo: the paper may support a worthwhile continuous economic analysis, but its results do not establish a computational question for the programme to mirror. The case is weaker as a universal claim. A newly formulated algorithmic problem about group admissions or disclosure design could be valuable; I cannot rule that out. It would, however, need its own motivation and model, rather than being anchored in a computational result of this paper.
