What the mirror covers

No named result is covered by a continuous computational mirror; a type-mass model for repeated service markets would be new work beyond the paper’s characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting exists: a large service platform with many providers who share the same skills, costs, valuation functions, and execution probabilities. A type would have to include the full valuation and probability-of-success profiles used by the paper; in the trust extension, it would also include the relevant opinions. The mass of a type would be its fraction of providers. One could then study assigning mass to task bundles to maximize expected total welfare, with payments conditioned on successful completion.

But this paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor that question. Theorem 1 proves that the PEV mechanism is ex-post truthful when valuations are multilinear in probabilities of success; Theorem 2 proves that condition is necessary for ex-post truthfulness across all type profiles. Corollary 1 extends the characterization to trust aggregation, and Proposition 1 characterizes individual rationality. These are mechanism-design characterizations, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. The discussion says efficient allocation can be computationally hard in some settings, but gives no named result establishing that here.

So I can offer a plausible research extension, but not the required anchor-based continuous problem. There is also a real modeling gap: the paper allows arbitrary task allocations and interdependencies among agents, while a mass-allocation model needs explicit structure for assigning task bundles to provider types. And its PEV argument uses an individual’s effect on allocation and the counterfactual without that individual; for an infinitesimal member of a continuum, that effect may vanish. A continuous mechanism would need to resolve that before the paper’s truthfulness results could be claimed as its mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper gives no computational result for a continuous mirror to inherit. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize when PEV is ex-post truthful; Corollary 1 extends that characterization to trust; Proposition 1 characterizes individual rationality. None establishes a complexity bound or an algorithm for a computational problem. The paper only warns that finding an efficient allocation can be hard in some settings, and points to other work. Its allocation set \(T\) and valuation functions are unrestricted, with no input encoding that would determine a complexity question. With \(T\) explicit, one can scan it; with \(T\) succinct, the complexity depends on the representation.

The platform example is a credible high-multiplicity setting: providers with the same complete type can be grouped by mass. But turning it into an optimization problem over mass assignments requires specifying how repeated tasks scale, which type-level assignments are feasible, and how their welfare is represented. That could be worthwhile research. Its computational results, however, would concern those added market structures; they would not be a continuous version of a complexity result in this paper, because the paper proves none.

The best candidate mirrors of the named results do not supply the missing computational question. For Theorems 1 and 2, multilinearity is a pointwise identity about each agent’s execution uncertainty. Replicating agents by type leaves the characterization unchanged. In an atomless population, one person’s report changes no mass, so an allocation based only on the distribution is unchanged by that report and the PEV counterfactual externality vanishes. A mass-level reporting model could retain strategic effects, but then it asks about coalitions of positive mass rather than the paper’s individual ex-post truthfulness. Rescaling payoffs might preserve a per-agent condition; it would still be the same characterization, not a new computational frontier.

Corollary 1 has the same limitation: multilinearity of trust aggregation is a condition on probabilities and opinions, not a computational result about a continuous population. The fact that these quantities already range over \([0,1]\) is continuity of the uncertainty model, not continuization of the society. Proposition 1 likewise gives a per-agent rationality condition; lifting it to types gives a population-weighted statement, not an algorithmic problem.

So the proponent has a plausible agenda for a new model of repeated service markets, and high multiplicity is not itself an objection. The negative case is strongest—and narrower—on the assignment the reader was given: this paper has no named computational result to mirror, and its named results either lift pointwise or lose their individual counterfactual in an atomless model. I cannot honestly establish that every possible follow-up is unworthwhile; the repeated-market scenario could be. But that would be a new computational study inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.
