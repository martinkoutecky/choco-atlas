The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give this paper a genuine anchor for a continuous mirror. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its contributions are the VoteLab platform and a 120-participant proof-of-concept study; the reported findings describe voting outcomes and their consistency across methods. Those findings are not named complexity results of the kind the question requires.

The strongest plausible population scenario is a large citywide consultation in which many residents submit ballots drawn from a relatively small set of complete response types. A type could record a resident’s responses across the campaign’s questions and voting methods, and its mass would be the fraction of residents with that response profile. Given rational masses, one could ask for each method’s aggregate outcome and the consistency of those outcomes. But that is a proposed computation, not a problem the paper studies as a computational question or supports with a named result. As stated, it also appears to be straightforward aggregation, rather than a substantial complexity question.

So I would not offer that proposal as an anchored mirror. The paper’s continuous-population angle is conceivable, but there is no qualifying result here to mirror, and inventing a harder optimization problem would make the mirror less faithful to the authors’ work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent named no anchor. That is the strongest objection: VoteLab has no theorem, lemma, or corollary about a computational problem, and makes no complexity claim. Its contributions are a software platform and a 120-person experiment. The reported differences in voting outcomes and consistency are empirical findings, not computational results to continuize.

The most plausible population model would give each type a resident’s complete response profile across the questions and voting methods, preserving the correlations between that person’s answers. A citywide consultation could then be represented by a distribution \(\mu\), and the method outcomes and consistency summaries computed from its masses. That is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario, but it turns the paper’s sample summaries into population summaries; it does not mirror a computational result in the paper.

A stronger proposal might ask for the least mass of changed ballots needed to alter a winner or make methods disagree. But that requires adding an intervention model, a cost for changing responses, and a target objective. The paper specifies none of these. Optimizing which groups receive which treatments would likewise add a new problem; VoteLab’s tags route campaigns, rather than define an optimization task. Those could be worthwhile follow-on questions, but they are not continuous versions of results this paper establishes.

So the negative case is strongest for a *mirror of this paper*: there is no computational result to anchor one to. It cannot prove that no related population-based question would ever be worthwhile. The citywide scenario is plausible; what it lacks is a paper-defined computational question for ChoCo to continuize.
