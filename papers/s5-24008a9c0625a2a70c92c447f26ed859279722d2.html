The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many roommate types, each with a ranking of acceptable partner types, and a rational profile threshold \(\sigma\), does there exist a stable mass-pair assignment whose profile \(p(x)\) is lexicographically at least \(\sigma\)?

The model it lives in

Types are interchangeable residents, and \(\mu_t\) is the mass of type \(t\). Use symmetric pair-mass variables \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\) and unmatched mass \(u_t\), with \(\sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}+2x_{tt}+u_t=\mu_t\). Stability excludes any pair of types with positive mass on both sides preferring each other to their current assignments; the profile coordinate \(p_k(x)\) counts mass whose partner type is ranked at position \(k\). The decision asks whether such a stable assignment meets \(p(x)\succeq_{\mathrm{lex}}\sigma\).

The objection that survived

Theorem 6.3's reduction does not establish hardness when the population has few types, so its hardness may disappear in the proposed regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 6.3's stable-roommates profile-threshold decision problem. It leaves Theorems 4.11 and 5.3 on two-sided matching, the other stable-roommates hardness results, and the experiments unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • When the support size \(\tau\) varies, is the typed stable-roommates profile-threshold problem polynomial-time solvable, or does it remain NP-hard despite fractional mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s stable-roommates hardness result, because it asks about the same two ingredients—stability and profile-based fairness—that define a continuous matching problem.

Theorem 6.3, proved in this paper, says that RMSR-D is NP-complete: given a stable-roommates instance \(I\) and a profile threshold \(\sigma\), decide whether \(I\) has a stable matching \(M\) with \(p(M)\succeq_{\mathrm{lex}}\sigma\). The paper proves this by reduction from vertex cover in cubic graphs.

A natural high-multiplicity setting is a large university or co-living programme where thousands of residents rank a small set of standardized roommate archetypes. A type is a complete preference order over those archetypes, including its acceptable types; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents of type \(t\). There may be many residents but only \(\tau\) distinct types. Each resident still receives one roommate. The matching variable records the mass paired between types, not fractional roommates for an individual.

Precisely, let \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\) be the mass of pairs between types \(t\) and \(u\), and let \(x_{tt}\) be the mass of pairs within type \(t\). Let \(u_t\) be unmatched mass. Feasibility requires
\[ \sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}+2x_{tt}+u_t=\mu_t. \]
For \(u\ne t\), type-\(t\) mass matched to \(u\) is \(x_{tu}\); for \(u=t\), it is \(2x_{tt}\). The profile coordinate \(p_k(x)\) is the total population mass whose partner’s type is at position \(k\) in their ranking.

Stability means there is no mutually acceptable pair of types with positive mass on both sides who prefer each other’s type to their current partner type (or to being unmatched). For a same-type pair, any positive mass of type-\(t\) residents who prefer their own type to their current assignment can pair among themselves. The lead mirror, Type-Continuum RMSR-D, takes rational masses \(\mu\), type rankings, and a rational profile threshold \(\sigma\). It asks whether there is a feasible stable pair-mass assignment \(x\) with \(p(x)\succeq_{\mathrm{lex}}\sigma\). A solution is such an assignment, with unmatched masses where needed.

This preserves the paper’s core question: among stable roommate matchings, can we meet a profile threshold? The population is the continuous object; pair mass is the natural aggregate decision variable needed to express a matching of that population. I would expect this formulation to be fixed-parameter tractable in support size \(\tau\): each type pair’s stability condition is a disjunction saying that one side has zero mass who would prefer the other type, and choosing a side for each pair leaves linear constraints. Enumerating these choices and the possible first coordinate where the profile threshold is met reduces the question to rational linear programmes, giving a \(2^{O(\tau^2)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) route. That is parameterized tractability, not a claim of a polynomial algorithm in unrestricted \(\tau\).

The main weakness is that the paper ranks named individuals with strict preferences, whereas this mirror ranks roommate types and treats residents within a type as interchangeable. That changes both profile ranks and the blocking-pair semantics. The paper’s hardness reduction uses graph-specific preference orders, so it does not show that hardness survives when a large population has few types; fractional mass can also dissolve discrete choices. I would therefore cover Theorem 6.3 only, and leave open whether its hardness persists in a high-multiplicity regime or whether the support-parameterized algorithm can be improved to polynomial time.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the proposed anchor is that Theorem 6.3’s hardness is carried by named agents and their individual preference lists. Its reduction encodes a graph’s incidence through the lists of particular agents. Under the programme’s complete-type criterion, agents with different relevant neighborhoods are different types, so the reduction does not establish hardness for a population with small support \(\tau\).

The proposed archetype model repairs that by having people rank partner *types*. That is coherent, but it changes what is being ranked and when a blocking pair exists: profile coordinates count mass at category-ranks, and a block is formed by positive masses of two types. The proposed \(2^{O(\tau^2)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) method makes the change consequential: it solves the bounded-support version by choosing stability conditions for each type pair. It does not explain what Theorem 6.3 says about that version. To retain the paper’s named-person rankings, types would generally need to preserve identity-specific preferences, so support could grow with the population.

That is a serious limit on using the reduction as evidence for a continuous mirror. It is not, though, a decisive objection to the typed problem itself. A large programme with standardized roommate categories is plausible, and the mass-pair model is a recognizable computational question. The paper also has a stronger alternative anchor in Theorem 4.11: rank-maximal stable matching for two-sided markets has an evident cohort setting. So the best negative case is that this paper’s person-specific hardness result does not validate the proposed few-type mirror. I cannot honestly extend that to “there is no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario”: the typed roommate problem, and especially the two-sided cohort version, survive the objections.
