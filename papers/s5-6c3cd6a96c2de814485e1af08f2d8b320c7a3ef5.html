The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named complexity result that meets your anchor rule: none says that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. The closest bridge is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It says that, given the unique Nash equilibrium from Theorem 1, the equilibrium bandwidth allocated to each MVNO is the solution to maximizing \(\sum_m \hat v_m(r_m)\), subject to \(\sum_m r_m\le R\) and \(r_m\ge0\), where \(\hat v_m\) is the penalty-adjusted valuation defined in equation (15). Theorem 1 establishes the uniqueness of that equilibrium under its stated assumptions. These are equilibrium and optimization characterizations, not tractability results; Algorithm 1 does not supply a named complexity theorem. So Proposition 3 is a useful bridge, but it is not a qualifying anchor under the strict test.

My strongest positive candidate is Atomless GKM allocation, a population-limit mirror of Proposition 3. Take a large wholesale market with \(N\) MVNO-like tenants and \(\tau\ll N\) types. A type completely specifies a tenant’s penalty \(q_t>0\) and its per-capita valuation function, including its user and channel profile. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of tenants of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Tenants of one type are interchangeable for everything the model uses. If the InP’s bandwidth supply grows with the market, write \(R\) for supply per unit of population mass and \(y_t\) for the bandwidth allocated per tenant of type \(t\).

For the paper’s user model, one possible explicit valuation is
\[ v_t(y)=\max_{\substack{z_{tj}\ge0\\ \sum_j z_{tj}\le y}} \sum_j \log(1+\alpha_{tj}z_{tj}), \]
where \(\alpha_{tj}\) describes the channel efficiency of users in the type’s profile. The continuous problem is: given the rational masses \(\mu_t\), penalties \(q_t\), supply \(R\), and these type profiles, compute an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal feasible allocation \(y_t\) for
\[ \max_{y_t\ge0}\ \sum_t \mu_t\frac{v_t(y_t)}{q_t} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_t\mu_t y_t\le R. \]
A solution can also report a supporting price \(p\), with each type choosing \(y_t\in\arg\max_{y\ge0}\{v_t(y)-q_tpy\}\) and the market clearing \(\sum_t\mu_t y_t=R\). In the atomless limit an individual tenant has zero market influence, so its bid does not change that price.

This is a genuine population continuization: the continuous object is the distribution over tenant types, not the already-divisible bandwidth. It also follows a limit the authors themselves point to when they say that an individual MVNO’s market influence vanishes as the number of MVNOs grows. Proposition 3 gives the finite-market equilibrium characterization; in the per-capita-supply limit, its adjusted valuation approaches \(v_t(y)/q_t\). That makes the proposed objective a reasoned limit of their result, not a claim the paper already proves.

I would expect this stated problem to be Class A: it is separable concave optimization with one aggregate capacity constraint, and the paper’s user-allocation structure offers a natural way to compute type demand. Further questions include the bit complexity of an \(\varepsilon\)-algorithm for explicit user profiles, how quickly the finite-market equilibrium approaches this limit, and what happens when the type support is succinct rather than listed. The scope here is only the upper-level resource allocation around Proposition 3; it does not stretch to the paper’s incomplete-information or multiple-resource extensions.

The regime is plausible for a large wholesale slicing marketplace with many small tenants on standardized service plans and relatively few demand/channel profiles. The important weakness is that the paper’s distinctive GKM feature is price anticipation, and the atomless limit removes it. Its simulations use only four MVNOs, and real operators can be highly differentiated. The positive case therefore rests on a specific many-tenant regime the paper itself discusses, not on a claim that its finite-MVNO mechanism is already continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no qualifying computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 proves uniqueness of a Nash equilibrium; Proposition 3 characterizes its allocation through an optimization problem; the lower-level section derives a KKT allocation. None gives a complexity classification for a computational problem. Algorithm 1 reports an iterative procedure, but the paper does not prove a runtime or iteration bound for it. Under the anchor rule, Proposition 3 is not a qualifying anchor.

The proposed atomless market is the strongest possible extension of that proposition, and its limit is mathematically plausible. Replicate tenants with the same \(q_t\) and \(v_t\), scale capacity with population, and let each tenant’s market influence vanish. But the resulting question changes the object being studied: it replaces strategic equilibrium and the InP’s penalty updates with a price-taking market whose allocation solves a weighted concave welfare problem. In the paper, \(q_m\) is a penalty set by the InP and updated using market information; treating it as a fixed attribute of a tenant type changes its role. Making it endogenous again would require a new mechanism and analysis. The paper’s introduction does discuss the many-MVNO price-taking limit, so the authors might recognize this as a related extension. It still does not mirror a computational result that the paper proves.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a wholesale market with many small tenants on a few standardized plans. That keeps the population interpretation sensible if tenants of each type truly share the same valuation and penalty. The paper’s worked setting, however, has four differentiated MVNOs, and its central mechanism concerns their ability to influence the market price. The continuum removes that feature. This weakens the case that the proposed limit is a worthwhile mirror of this paper, though it does not rule out such a market as an independent research problem.

A second plausible population is the end users within each MVNO, grouped by channel and service type. The paper already allocates bandwidth to users from those characteristics and derives the allocation by KKT conditions. A population model could be a natural extension of that layer, but it would not continue a named complexity result either.

So the negative case is strong under the requested anchor standard: there is no computational result here to mirror, and the best candidate is a related price-taking model with a different strategic object. The broader claim that no continuous mirror could be worthwhile in any scenario is weaker. The standardized-tenant market is plausible enough that I would not call that universal claim airtight.
