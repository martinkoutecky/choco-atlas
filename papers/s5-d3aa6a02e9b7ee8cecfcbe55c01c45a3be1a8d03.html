What the mirror covers

Theorem 4.6 and Corollary 4.7 support an axiomatic mass-threshold analogy, but neither supplies a computational result to mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The closest positive foothold is Corollary 4.7 (proved here). It characterizes anonymous, top-selecting, strategy-proof rules on the endpoint-peaked domain: for some \(1\le q_1\le q_2\le n\), the rule selects \(\{x_1\}\) when fewer than \(q_1\) agents peak at \(x_m\), selects \(\{x_1,x_m\}\) when the count is between the thresholds, and selects \(\{x_m\}\) once it reaches \(q_2\). Its more general companion, Theorem 4.6 (proved here), says that top-selection and strategy-proofness are equivalent to two monotone endpoint committees, each excluding the empty coalition and jointly satisfying a covering condition. These are strong structural results, but neither asserts a computational complexity bound or hardness result.

If a structural characterization can motivate a mirror, Corollary 4.7 suggests a concrete one. Consider a large municipality choosing an endpoint location for an undesirable facility, with a shortlist of both endpoints allowed. Let the two relevant types be residents whose complete preferences, for purposes of these three outcomes, favor \(x_1\) or \(x_m\). Their rational masses are \(\mu_1,\mu_m\), summing to \(1\). Given rational thresholds \(0<\theta_1\le\theta_2\le1\), the continuous rule returns \(\{x_1\}\) if \(\mu_m<\theta_1\), \(\{x_1,x_m\}\) if \(\theta_1\le\mu_m<\theta_2\), and \(\{x_m\}\) otherwise. Here \(n\) can be very large while the number of outcome-relevant types is two. Residents sharing a type must agree on the preferences the rule uses; grouping rankings with the same endpoint peak is appropriate because responsive dominance over these three outcomes depends only on that peak.

That is a recognizable continuous-population counterpart of the anonymous quota characterization. Its computational task, however, is only to evaluate a specified rule from two rational masses and two thresholds: compare \(\mu_m\) with the thresholds. That is plainly polynomial in the input bit length, but the paper does not prove that result, and it offers no optimization objective that would make the task substantial. Designing the thresholds to meet some added fairness or welfare goal would introduce a new problem absent from the paper.

So the honest case for the mirror is that the population regime is plausible and the quota-to-share translation is direct. Its decisive weakness is that the paper contains no named computational result at all: its results characterize strategy-proof rules rather than classify the complexity of a computational problem. There is also an incentive issue in the continuous model: a single infinitesimal resident cannot change the aggregate mass, so the paper’s individual strategy-proofness does not transfer without defining a positive-mass deviation model. I would not present the threshold translation as a qualifying ChoCo computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.6 and Corollary 4.7 characterize which rules satisfy top-selection and strategy-proofness; Theorem 3.8 does the same for interval rules. None poses an algorithmic problem or proves a complexity bound. Recasting one of these characterizations over a continuous population could be a worthwhile axiomatic question, but it would not fill ChoCo’s computational gap.

Corollary 4.7 has a natural mass version: replace the count of agents favoring \(x_m\) with their share and the integer quotas with thresholds. That makes a plausible population model, since the three outcomes depend only on endpoint peaks. But evaluating a specified rule requires only comparing a rational share with its thresholds. Characterizing all such rules remains an axiomatic result. Optimizing the thresholds would need a new objective—fairness or welfare, for example—that the paper does not supply. Theorem 3.8 has the same limitation: its anonymous threshold rule also translates directly to a share cutoff, without producing a computational task.

Theorem 4.6’s general committee result is harder to carry over. Its committees can depend on arbitrary coalitions of named agents. Encoding each identity as a distinct type preserves that structure but gives no high-multiplicity regime; imposing anonymity restores the mass-based version and reduces it to the quota characterization. That is a modeling limit on the general theorem, not an objection to grouping agents by complete types.

There is also an incentive problem in the continuum. A single agent has zero mass, so changing one report cannot change an outcome determined by the population distribution. Individual strategy-proofness then becomes vacuous. Replacing it with coalition strategy-proofness gives a meaningful analogue, and the paper proves group-strategy-proofness for its characterized rules. But in the anonymous case this again leads to monotone cutoffs, not a computational landscape.

The proponents’ strongest point survives: the endpoint-peak share model is a sensible continuous population. It could support a clean continuous axiomatic analogue. The negative case is that this paper supplies no computational question for that analogue; making one would require adding a problem the authors did not study.
