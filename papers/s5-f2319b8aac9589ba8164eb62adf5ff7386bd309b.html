The continuous mirror question

Given τ resident cohorts with positive rational masses μ_i summing to \(1\), a symmetric rational interaction matrix \(q_{ij}\in[0,1]\), and one indivisible house per resident, with total supply \(1/2\) of each of two house types valued \(0\) and \(1\) by everyone, choose cohort fractions \(p_i\in[0,1]\) satisfying \(\sum_i μ_i p_i=1/2\) to minimize \(E(p)=\frac12\sum_{i,j}μ_iμ_jq_{ij}[p_i(1-p_j)+(1-p_i)p_j]\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population divided into τ cohorts, where each type specifies the common valuation and interaction intensities \(q_{ij}\); residents receive indivisible houses, while \(p_i\) records the fraction of cohort \(i\) receiving type \(B\).

The objection that survived

The bounded kernel averages over all cohort pairs, so the reduction’s sparse graph has energy \(O(1/n)\) and does not preserve a nonzero normalized sparse-network limit.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1’s NP-hardness result for two house types. It leaves the paper’s approximation, parameterized, and exact algorithmic results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does NP-hardness persist in sparse-kernel variants whose normalized per-capita envy remains nonzero?
  • Which structural restrictions on \(q\) make the mass-split optimization tractable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4.1, the section 4 result proved in this paper: ME-GHA is NP-hard even when there are only \(\ell=2\) house types. Its reduction starts from Minimum Bisection on an \(n\)-vertex graph with \(n\) even. It uses that graph as the social graph, gives every agent the same binary valuation, and divides the houses equally between two types valued \(0\) and \(1\). An allocation then determines a bisection: the agents receiving each house type form one side. Its total envy equals the number of social edges crossing the bisection, so an optimal allocation gives a minimum bisection.

A continuous mirror I would defend is Two-Class Continuum ME-GHA on Block Interaction Kernels. Its instances have \(\tau\) resident cohorts, with positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\). Every resident receives one indivisible house of type \(A\) or \(B\), and the total supply of each type is \(1/2\). All residents value \(A\) at \(0\) and \(B\) at \(1\). A symmetric rational matrix \(q_{ij}\in[0,1]\) specifies the social interaction intensity between residents in cohorts \(i\) and \(j\), including among distinct residents of the same cohort.

Let \(p_i\in[0,1]\) be the fraction of cohort \(i\) receiving \(B\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_i\mu_i p_i=1/2\). The question is to choose feasible \(p\) minimizing

\[ E(p)=\frac12\sum_{i,j}\mu_i\mu_jq_{ij} \bigl[p_i(1-p_j)+(1-p_i)p_j\bigr]. \]

This is expected total envy over the interaction kernel: a social interaction contributes envy exactly when its two residents receive different house types. A solution is an optimal mass-split vector \(p\); because the kernel is constant within and between cohorts, any measurable assignment realizing that vector has the same cost. For a decision version, add a rational threshold \(B\) and ask whether some feasible \(p\) has \(E(p)\le B\). The houses remain indivisible; only the amount of each cohort receiving each type is fractional.

The setting could be a large municipal housing allocation, with many residents in each of a smaller number of communities whose members share valuations and have common interaction intensities with each community. Thus the population can be much larger than the number of distinct roles, while a type captures both valuation and social-interaction behavior. The authors should recognize the mirror: it retains their local pairwise envy objective, their two discrete house types, and their allocation constraint, while replacing the finite social graph by its type-level interaction description.

I expect this problem to be Class B: hardness transfers. In the reduction above, give each graph vertex its own cohort, set \(\mu_i=1/n\), and let \(A\) be the source graph’s adjacency matrix. Set \(q_{ii}=1\), \(q_{ij}=\beta=1/(\Delta+1)\) when \(ij\) is an edge, and \(q_{ij}=0\) otherwise, where \(\Delta\) is the maximum degree. Then \(Q=I+\beta A\) is positive semidefinite. The objective is a concave quadratic in \(p\), so its minimum over \(0\le p_i\le1\), \(\sum_i p_i=n/2\), is attained at an extreme point. Those extreme points are integral bisections. At an integral point, within-cohort interactions contribute no envy, and each crossing edge contributes \(\beta/n^2\). The continuous optimum therefore encodes the minimum bisection. Cohorts can each contain arbitrarily many residents; hardness here comes from the number of distinct social roles, not from having only one person per role.

This case covers Theorem 4.1’s two-house-type hardness result, not the paper’s other algorithms. Its weakest point is the interaction model: a type-level weighted kernel replaces an individually specified, unweighted social graph. A referee could regard that as a meaningful extension of the authors’ problem rather than its most literal limit. Still, the change is tied to making the population continuous, and the envy and allocation questions remain recognizable. Follow-up questions include whether the threshold problem is hard under stricter interaction kernels, what restrictions on \(Q\) yield tractability, and when an optimal solution can be chosen cohort-pure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 4.1’s NP-hardness with two house types. The strongest case against its mirror is that the hard information lives in the social graph’s vertex-by-vertex adjacency. To make residents in a cohort interchangeable, the proposed model replaces that graph with a type-pair interaction matrix. With a small support, that leaves only a block-symmetric network; to encode an arbitrary Minimum Bisection instance, the reduction instead uses one cohort per graph vertex, so \(\tau=n\), and puts the graph into \(q\).

There is also a limit issue in that reduction. With \(\mu_i=1/n\) and bounded \(q_{ij}\), its bisection cost is \(\beta\,\mathrm{cut}/n^2=O(1/n)\). The normalized interaction energy therefore vanishes along these sparse instances. Rescaling the objective or using a sparse network limit could preserve the distinction, but that requires a more elaborate model than a bounded type-pair kernel. The reduction is strongest as evidence for a continuous model of weighted, block-structured networks, rather than as a direct limit of the paper’s unweighted social graphs.

Those objections narrow the mirror; they do not defeat it. A weighted interaction kernel is a plausible co-modification when the population becomes continuous, and the cohorts can each contain arbitrarily many residents while retaining the stated masses. The resulting optimization problem is well-posed and computational, and its complexity in \(\tau\) remains meaningful even when the reduction uses \(\tau=n\). The hard answer or the fact that the combinatorics remain in the distinct social roles cannot itself count against asking the question.

So I cannot honestly make the universal negative case the prompt calls for. The mirror’s vulnerability is its move from unweighted graphs to weighted block interactions, especially for sparse networks. Whether that is a worthwhile model is a genuine scope question, but the proposed construction is plausible enough that this anchor survives.
