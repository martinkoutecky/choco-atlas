The case FOR (proponent)

I can see a plausible computational mirror, but I can’t honestly give it a qualifying anchor under your rules. The paper’s numbered theorems and corollaries establish learning rates and probability limits; none classifies a problem as tractable, NP-hard, or parameterized. Appendix F describes a numerical method and mentions computational hardness in related work, but that hardness is cited from elsewhere, not proved here as a numbered result.

The closest foothold is Theorem 3 (proved here): with a uniform prior, signals distributed as \(\mathcal N(\pm1,n)\), and agents choosing the action that matches the state, for every fixed \(t\), the probability that all agents in periods \(2,\ldots,t\) choose the first-period majority action tends to \(1\) as \(n\to\infty\). This is a population-limit result, but it is not a computational result.

A reasonable *unanchored* question suggested by the paper would be: given a finite support of agent types, with each type specifying its mass, signal distributions, action set, and payoff table, plus a prior and horizon \(H\), compute the exact mass choosing the wrong action at period \(H\) in a nonatomic population where agents observe the aggregate action masses and update rationally. Many sellers in separate markets, or forecasters with a small number of shared signal and payoff profiles, could provide a credible high-multiplicity regime. That asks about the population itself, and the paper’s discussion of the difficulty of computing Bayesian behavior makes it a natural computational direction.

The main obstacle is that Theorem 3 uses signal variance \(n\). In that regime the first-period action-share difference between states vanishes in the ordinary continuum limit, while the random finite-population majority that drives later consensus does not. Preserving that effect would require tracking a scaled fluctuation alongside the population mass. So even this candidate is not a clean direct mirror of the theorem. It is a research question suggested by the paper, not a continuous problem anchored in one of its named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying computational result in this paper to anchor a mirror. Its numbered results establish learning rates, error probabilities, and probability limits. Appendix F describes numerical computation and notes hardness in related work, but the paper proves no complexity classification or algorithmic bound of its own.

Theorem 3 is the closest anchor, but its key effect depends on a finite-population fluctuation. With the paper’s signal variance \(n\), the first-period high-action share converges to \(1/2\) in either state. The random majority that drives later consensus survives at finite \(n\), but disappears from the ordinary continuum mass. If instead signals keep fixed variance, the conditional action shares differ by state; in the paper’s binary normal example, observing the exact continuum share reveals the state immediately. So the standard continuum either removes the public information that creates groupthink or makes learning from actions trivial.

One could retain a nontrivial majority by tracking the scaled fluctuation around the mass, such as a Gaussian limit. That may be a worthwhile model, but its object is a random fluctuation process, not the distribution over types that ChoCo continuizes. Likewise, computing wrong-action mass for a finite-support continuum population could be a new computational question, but it is not a mirror of a named computational result here.

That is a strong case against the proposed anchors, not proof that no worthwhile continuous question can be built from social learning. The paper’s computational difficulty makes that universal claim hard to sustain; such a question would be a new project rather than the continuous mirror this paper supplies.
