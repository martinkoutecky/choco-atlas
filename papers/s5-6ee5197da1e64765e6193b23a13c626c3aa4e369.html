The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says the Strong Demand Matching (SDM) mechanism gives every bidder a fraction of their proportionally fair (PF) utility of at least
\[ \rho=\min_j \frac{p_j^*}{\lceil p_j^*\rceil}, \]
where \(p_j^*\) is the PF price of item \(j\). If the minimum PF price is \(k\), the guarantee is at least \(k/(k+1)\). The mechanism’s truthfulness is established separately by Corollary 1, also proved here. The theorem gives a particularly apt anchor: its guarantee loses accuracy through the integer capacities used to assign bidders to goods.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large population allocating shares in a small catalogue of firms or other resources. Take many citizens, or many institutional teams, whose normalized valuation vectors come from a modest set of recurring portfolio profiles. If the population has scale \(N\) and type distribution \(\mu\), then type \(t\) has mass \(N\mu_t\), with \(N\gg m,\tau\). The voucher-auction example makes a very large population competing over relatively few items plausible; recurring, exactly shared valuation profiles are an additional assumption, most convincing where a protocol or institution creates standard profiles. When valuations are truly individualized and \(\tau\) is near \(N\), this mirror is much less useful. A type here is the complete normalized valuation vector; all agents also have the paper’s equal artificial budget. That budget defines PF prices and does not involve actual payments.

I would hand a prover this problem:

Continuum Strong-Demand PF Allocation. An instance consists of \(m\) divisible goods, each with supply one; a population scale \(N\); and a listed support of \(\tau\) rational valuation types \(v^t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^m\), with \(\sum_j v^t_j=1\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. Each type has mass \(N\mu_t\), and each agent has a unit artificial budget. Find item prices \(p_j\) and a feasible assignment of population mass to single-good bundles \(e_j/p_j\), such that agents reporting type \(t\) receive goods that maximize their bang per buck \(v^t_j/p_j\). The objective is to maximize the minimum, over types in the support, of the truthful per-agent utility divided by that type’s PF utility. Truthfulness means that an individual agent cannot improve their utility by reporting another valuation type.

In the continuum, the assignment can use real masses. At PF prices \(p^*\), seek flows \(f_{tj}\) satisfying
\[ \sum_j f_{tj}=N\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t f_{tj}=p_j^*,\qquad f_{tj}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ j\in\arg\max_\ell\frac{v^t_\ell}{p^*_\ell}. \]
An agent assigned to good \(j\) receives \(1/p_j^*\) of it, so the flow to that good uses exactly its unit supply. Every truthful type receives its PF utility, since every good assigned to it is a bang-per-buck maximizer. If an agent lies and receives a good \(j\), their true utility is \(v^t_j/p_j^*\), which cannot exceed their truthful maximum. Thus the price menu is truthful for an individual atomistic agent, and the objective reaches \(1\).

I expect this problem to be tractable: compute PF prices using the Eisenberg–Gale market-equilibrium formulation, then find the mass assignment on the maximum-bang-per-buck graph by a rational flow computation. Both steps use the listed support, so the natural parameters are \(m,\tau,L\), and the bit length of \(N\). The finite theorem points toward the same answer: with fixed positive type proportions, active PF prices grow with \(N\), and its rounding guarantee tends to one. In the mass model, the integer rounding disappears and PF utility is attainable exactly.

This is a population continuization. The goods remain divisible, as they already are in the paper; the new continuous object is the population distribution. The mirror covers Theorem 3’s truthful PF-approximation result. It does not attempt to mirror the paper’s two-agent social-welfare guarantees or its special two-item mechanisms.

The weakest point is the change in incentives. A single atomistic agent cannot move prices, while the paper’s finite bidders can. The direct menu remains meaningfully truthful—misreports can change the item received—but it models the price-taking limit of their strategic problem. Whether the finite SDM mechanism converges to this exact truthfulness notion, and what approximation error finite profiles incur as they approach the mass assignment, are further questions. I think the authors would recognize this as their problem’s large-population limit, especially given their market-equilibrium foundation, while a referee could reasonably insist that the limiting incentive notion be justified explicitly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a real computational result, so the negative case cannot dismiss it for lack of an algorithmic anchor. Its weakness as a continuization anchor is that the theorem’s hard part is strategic: a finite bidder’s report can change the PF prices and allocation. In the proposed atomless model, an individual has zero mass and cannot change either. Once prices are fixed by the reported population distribution, assigning each type only to its maximum-bang-per-buck goods is truthful automatically. The allocation is the PF market equilibrium itself, so the approximation ratio is \(1\). That removes the incentive problem Theorem 3 solves; it does not reveal a new continuous version of that problem.

The remaining computation—finding PF prices and assigning type mass over the maximum-bang-per-buck graph—is the Eisenberg–Gale market-equilibrium calculation the paper already invokes, followed by a flow computation. Its running time may benefit from grouping repeated valuation vectors, but that is type compression of a familiar equilibrium computation. SDM’s integer capacities explain the finite guarantee; removing them gives the PF benchmark back. The continuous question is therefore easy to state and solve, but its answer mostly restates the paper’s existing PF machinery.

There are ways to retain strategic force, but they change the object. If an agent keeps mass \(1/N\), unilateral reports can still affect outcomes at finite \(N\); then one has the high-multiplicity discrete mechanism problem, and Theorem 3’s own price-based guarantee already approaches \(1\) as prices grow. If instead a positive-mass group can coordinate a misreport, the question becomes coalition manipulation or group strategyproofness. That may be worthwhile on its own, but the strategic players are now blocs, not the paper’s individual bidders. Scaling supplies with population can remove artificial per-capita effects, but it does not restore an individual’s influence over market prices.

So the best case against this anchor is a degeneracy, not a claim that continuous allocation is implausible: the natural continuum limit makes individual truthfulness vacuous, while preserving nontrivial manipulation requires changing the players or returning to finite agents. The negative case is not airtight—an atomless market implementation may still interest the authors—but the proposed mirror does not seem to earn much of ChoCo’s effort beyond a type-compressed restatement of the PF equilibrium already used in the paper.
