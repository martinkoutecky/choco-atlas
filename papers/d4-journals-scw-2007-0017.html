The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is limited: the paper suggests a computational question that could be asked about its redistribution mechanisms, but it contains no named computational result to anchor that mirror. Propositions 1–6 make claims about Lorenz or poverty dominance; none classifies a problem as tractable or hard, gives an algorithm, or analyzes a computational encoding. For example, Proposition 6, proved here, says Lorenz dominance between an egalitarian equivalent and a conditionally egalitarian mechanism cannot be established without assumptions about the marginal distributions. That is an analytical result, not a complexity result.

The closest candidate question would be: given \(\tau\) rationally weighted types \((e,w)\), with \(\varepsilon=1\) and reference values \(\tilde w,\tilde e\), determine whether the resulting income distribution under \(F^{EE}_{\tilde w}\) Lorenz dominates the one under \(F^{CE}_{\tilde e}\). A solution would be a yes/no answer, or a cutoff witnessing failure. A population of many people grouped into a modest number of skill and preference types is plausible. But this is a proposed algorithmic extension, not an anchor from the paper; and Section 4 already models a continuous population using densities over \(e\) and \(w\). For finite support, comparing the distributions also appears to reduce to sorting at most \(\tau\) income values. So I would not present this as a qualifying positive case under your anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against rests on the missing computational anchor. Propositions 1–6 establish Lorenz and poverty dominance claims; none states the complexity of a computational problem, gives an algorithm, or studies an encoded input. Proposition 6 is the closest candidate, but it says that dominance between an egalitarian equivalent and a conditionally egalitarian mechanism depends on the marginal distributions. The proposed question—given rationally weighted types \((e,w)\), decide whether one mechanism’s income distribution Lorenz dominates the other’s—turns that analytical claim into a new finite-instance decision problem. It is not a computational result of this paper.

A stronger proposal might ask for all reference-parameter pairs that yield dominance, or for a choice of mechanism under a specified poverty or inequality criterion. Those are coherent questions, and a population grouped by skill and preference types is sensible. But the first adds a computational task the paper never studies; the second also needs a new objective, since the paper leaves the choice of reference parameters to normative judgment and empirical information. Neither supplies an anchor in the paper for a computational complexity landscape.

This is a limited negative case. It would be wrong to argue that the population cannot be continuized: Section 4 already uses distributions over skill and preference, and grouping people with the same characteristics is natural here. Nor does the analytical treatment count as an existing computational answer. The objection is that the proponent’s candidate is a plausible new algorithmic question, but not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper. If the programme accepts such extensions as mirrors, this objection is weak.
