What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, along with the corollaries, give asymptotic learning and consensus guarantees; none supplies a computational result eligible for mirroring.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that a population continuum is natural for this subject. A plausible regime is a very large online community whose members fall into a modest number of recurring roles. A type would need to capture everything the model uses: the member’s signal likelihoods and prior, and their influence role in the network. Mass would be the fraction of members in each type. In such a regime, \(n\) could be enormous while the number of types \(\tau\) stayed modest.

There is a modeling obstacle even to that plausibility case: members with the same signal model can observe different signals and develop different beliefs. A continuum version would therefore need to track a distribution of beliefs within each type, as well as a mass-compatible influence kernel. Replacing every type with one representative belief would generally change the paper’s dynamics. Arbitrary network positions could also make almost every member a distinct type, eliminating the high-multiplicity regime.

But I find no eligible computational anchor in the paper. The nearest candidate, Theorem 1, is proved here and gives almost-sure convergence guarantees: under its stated prior, connectivity, \(\mathcal P^*\), and independence assumptions, strong feedback or recurring \(\gamma\)-epochs suffice for weak merging; with recurring \(\gamma\)-epochs and an identifiable true state, they suffice for asymptotic learning. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that deterministic uniform strong connectivity and a positive prior imply almost-sure belief consensus, even when the true state is not identifiable. These are analytic results about an update process, not named results about computational complexity, algorithms, or hardness. The corollaries likewise give learning guarantees.

So I can make a case that a continuous-population *learning model* is worth formulating, but I cannot honestly present a prover-ready computational mirror for a named result in this paper or predict whether such a problem would be tractable, transfer hardness, or be continuum-specifically hard. Any such problem would be a new computational question, rather than a mirror anchored in the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that neither anchor is a computational result. Theorem 1 proves almost-sure merging and learning under assumptions on an infinite stochastic process. Theorem 2 proves almost-sure consensus for a deterministic uniformly strongly connected chain. The corollaries extend those guarantees. The update rule is an algorithm in the distributed-learning sense, but the paper gives no complexity, approximation, or parameterized result about computing an intervention or solving a finite decision problem. Under ChoCo’s gate, a plausible large population is not enough: there must be a named computational problem here to mirror.

The best version of a Theorem 1 mirror would use a large community with a few roles, then track a distribution of beliefs within each role. One could ask how much mass needs informative signals or altered links to ensure learning by a deadline. That might be a worthwhile new problem, but this paper has no intervention budget, finite-horizon objective, or algorithmic input for it. Asking whether the original infinite process converges in an exact continuum model remains an analytic learning question. Adding an optimization objective would create a new computational problem, rather than mirror Theorem 1.

The same gap defeats Theorem 2. A population-scale consensus model could ask for an approximate consensus limit or the smallest mass of altered links that prevents consensus. Neither question is in the theorem: it proves convergence, without a costed change or a finite-input computational task. Moreover, the theorem’s proof relies on finite-network mixing conditions. In Theorem 1’s \(\mathcal P^*\) setting, a normalized absolute probability vector with every coordinate at least \(p^*\) must satisfy \(p^*\leq 1/n\). That bound cannot stay positive as the population grows. Replacing it with a lower bound on a continuum influence density may be sensible, but it requires a new analytic model; it does not supply the missing computational anchor.

The proponent is right that a role-based population is conceivable. But exact aggregation is difficult: agents with the same signal model can see different histories and hold different beliefs, and the Bayesian update is nonlinear in those beliefs. A faithful model may need to track a distribution of beliefs and its dependence on the network and signal field, rather than one average belief per role. That could be valuable social-learning theory. It still does not turn either theorem into a computational result.

So the strongest case against this paper as a ChoCo mirror is firm: neither named theorem survives as an eligible anchor, and improving the proposed population model does not repair that. The literal universal claim that no continuous model inspired by the paper could ever be worth the programme’s time is weaker than the evidence allows. A new computational problem about interventions in a population network might be worthwhile; the paper’s own results do not anchor it.
