The continuous mirror question

Given registered candidates \(C\), unregistered candidates \(D\), target \(p\in C\), an agenda on \(C\cup D\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(C\cup D\), and a budget \(k\), does there exist \(D'\subseteq D\) with \(|D'|\leq k\) such that, after restricting each ranking and the agenda to \(C\cup D'\), \(p\) is the earliest candidate in the agenda whose mass of types ranking it above all its successors exceeds \(1/2\)?

The model it lives in

High-Multiplicity CCAC-Successive: the society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) complete ranking types, with total mass \(1\); the controller chooses an integral set \(D'\) of at most \(k\) candidates to add, and asks whether \(p\) becomes the successive winner. The computational input is measured by candidate count \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 26 through rational distributions over ranking types; the fractional voter-addition question based on Theorem 1 is also plausible. It leaves the paper's other amendment and successive control results, including candidate deletion and voter deletion, unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the parameterized complexity of High-Multiplicity CCAC-Successive in support size \(\tau\) alone, with \(m\) and \(k\) unrestricted?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is voter addition under the \(h\)-amendment procedure, because the population mass is the controller’s decision variable. A second, more technically secure mirror is successive control by adding candidates: there, the hardness transfers directly.

For the first, Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that for every fixed \(h\), CCAV-\(h\)-Amendment is \(\mathrm{W[2]}\)-hard when parameterized by the number of registered votes plus the allowed number of added votes, even when the target candidate is last in the agenda. The paper’s preliminaries imply the corresponding discrete problem is NP-complete.

A continuous version would be Mass-CCAV-\(h\)-Amendment. Fix \(h\), a finite candidate set \(C\), an agenda with target \(p\) last, and \(T\), the set of possible complete rankings of \(C\). The registered society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with total mass \(1\). An eligible pool of abstainers is a finite measure \(\nu\) over \(T\); its masses are measured relative to the registered population. Given a rational budget \(\beta\), choose real masses \(x_t\) with \(0\le x_t\le\nu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\beta\). The question is whether \(p\) wins the \(h\)-amendment procedure on the resulting society \(\mu+x\). A solution is such a vector \(x\); equivalently, one could ask for the minimum mass needed to make \(p\) win. The computational parameters are \(m=|C|\), the positive support size \(\tau\) of \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), and their total binary bit length \(L\).

A plausible regime is a large organization or electorate where members and eligible abstainers share a relatively small catalogue of preference rankings. Each type must include everything the problem uses: its ranking and whether its members are eligible to be recruited. The number of people can greatly exceed \(\tau\). The paper’s parliamentary example—persuading planned abstainers to participate—already gives a recognizable setting, and recruiting a fraction of each type expresses it in population shares.

I expect this mirror to be tractable, provisionally. Its masses change every pairwise majority margin linearly, while Theorem 1’s reduction selects whole voters and relies on an integral covering choice. Fractional recruitment may erase that part of the hardness. That does not itself give an LP: the remaining challenge is to handle the different amendment trajectories without enumerating exponentially many possibilities. The research question is whether a compact LP, separation method, or other polynomial algorithm exists in \(m,\tau,L\) for fixed \(h\), and what rounding or additive guarantee then follows for large finite electorates.

Theorem 26 supplies the strongest *hardness-transfer* anchor. Proved here by reduction from Red-Blue Dominating Set, it says that CCAC-Successive is \(\mathrm{W[2]}\)-hard parameterized by the number of added candidates, even when there are only two registered candidates and the distinguished candidate is not first in the agenda.

Its mirror, High-Multiplicity CCAC-Successive, has registered candidates \(C\), unregistered candidates \(D\), target \(p\in C\), an agenda on \(C\cup D\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(C\cup D\). For \(D'\subseteq D\) with \(|D'|\le k\), restrict the rankings and agenda to \(C\cup D'\). Candidate \(c\) majority-dominates its successors when the mass of types ranking \(c\) above every successor is greater than \(1/2\). The question is whether some \(D'\) makes \(p\) the first candidate in the restricted agenda who majority-dominates all of their successors.

This fits a large legislative body or membership organization with many people sharing preference rankings, where the controller can add proposals or motions to the agenda. The procedure and discrete candidate choices remain exactly those of the paper; only the electorate becomes a distribution. I expect hardness to transfer (Class B). The reduction’s vote profile becomes an empirical distribution with rational masses and preserves every strict-majority comparison. Replicating each constructed vote \(M\) times leaves the distribution and winner comparisons unchanged while making the population large relative to its support. The parameter \(k\) is unchanged, so the \(\mathrm{W[2]}\)-hardness carries over.

These mirrors cover voter addition for fixed-\(h\) amendment and candidate addition for successive winners; they do not claim to continuize the paper’s entire landscape. They keep the society as the continuous object and leave outcome spaces, time, and probabilistic voting out of the model.

The soft spot is that the two mirrors have different strengths. Mass-CCAV is the more population-native question, but fractional selection changes the discrete feasible set, and tractability is only my prediction. High-Multiplicity CCAC-Successive has an exact hardness transfer, but the candidate choices remain discrete and the continuous masses are input data. It is a strong, author-recognizable high-multiplicity mirror, though the result may mainly show that agenda and candidate combinatorics survive continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against starts with Theorem 1’s proposed Mass-CCAV mirror. Its input needs more than a society of voters: it needs a pool of eligible abstainers, a rule for how much mass can be recruited, and a normalization for the resulting population. One can make that model cleaner by giving each ranking type an eligibility label and choosing a mass of eligible agents, but then the central question is still whether the amendment winner can be changed by moving mass across majority thresholds. The paper already shows voter-control problems are fixed-parameter tractable in the number of candidates, and a continuous version can at least enumerate possible majority patterns and test each with linear constraints. That makes a major algorithmic payoff less certain.

This is only a weak objection. Parliamentary abstention is a natural source of an eligible pool; the type can include eligibility; and a budget measured as a fraction of the registered population is coherent. The paper’s fixed-parameter result does not settle the continuous problem’s complexity in \(m,\tau,L\). Most importantly, fractional recruitment changes the feasible set, which is precisely the kind of change that could make the mirror informative. The tractable outcome the proponent anticipates cannot itself count against asking the question.

Theorem 26’s High-Multiplicity CCAC-Successive mirror is harder to dismiss. Given any discrete profile in the reduction, represent it by its empirical distribution over rankings. For every candidate subset, the mass-majority conditions then reproduce the discrete winner conditions, including their strict thresholds. Replicating the votes gives a large population without changing those conditions. The reduction’s choices remain discrete candidate additions, so the continuum does not relax the combinatorial part of the problem. But that is not a scope objection: the society is still a distribution over types, and the paper’s own agenda-control scenario makes candidate addition plausible.

The most I can say against this second mirror is that the reduction’s hardness survives through an exact discrete-to-distribution correspondence. It establishes that continuization does not automatically remove hardness; it does not show that the broader rational-distribution model has no useful algorithms or structural results. The continuum is not already the paper’s model, so this is not a novelty collision under the programme’s criteria.

Taken together, these are cautions about payoff, not a case that no worthwhile mirror exists. I cannot honestly defeat either anchor under the stated rules: the first is a plausible fractional-control question, and the second is a well-posed high-multiplicity question with a valid hardness transfer. The universal negative case is weak.
