The continuous mirror question

Input: a finite firm set \(F\), worker types \(T\), a finite support \(\mathcal R\subseteq\Delta(T)\) of rational cohort profiles with rational probabilities \(\pi_r\), rational per-cohort capacities \(\kappa_f\), a discount factor \(\delta\), and rational piecewise-linear aggregate revenue and worker-utility functions. Each period a unit-mass cohort with profile \(r\sim\pi\) arrives; an assignment uses masses \(x_{ft}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_f x_{ft}\le r_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ft}\le\kappa_f\), alongside type-specific wages. Can one compute a threshold \(\delta_0<1\) and a finite-state self-enforcing matching process for every \(\delta>\delta_0\), with uniform random serial dictatorship on path, when a firm may deviate by recruiting any type-mass vector whose workers strictly prefer the deviation?

The model it lives in

A repeated matching market with a finite set of persistent firms and a unit-mass worker cohort each period. Workers are distributed over finitely many complete types; assignment variables \(x_{ft}\) and type-specific wages determine matches, while firms’ revenues and workers’ peer-sensitive utilities depend on aggregate type compositions. Firms follow random serial dictatorship on path and may deviate by recruiting masses of willing workers.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Proposition 2, proved here, is the closest result: “When firms are sufficiently patient, there exists a self-enforcing matching process in which firms play RSD in every period on path.” It is an existence theorem, not a complexity result or an algorithm. Lemma 1 characterizes firms’ minmax payoffs, and Proposition 1 characterizes supported payoffs; neither gives computational complexity either. So none is an eligible anchor, and I can’t honestly assign a continuous problem a tractability, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hardness prediction on the basis of this paper’s results.

The strongest positive *intuition* is a recurring, high-volume entry-level hiring market: many short-lived workers arrive each season, with relatively few complete types describing their skills and preferences, while a smaller set of firms persists across seasons. A continuous version could represent the cohort by type masses \(\rho_t\), scale firms’ capacities with cohort size, and use assignment masses \(x_{ft}\) and wages as the stage decisions. A solution would be a history-dependent matching process satisfying worker participation and deterring firms’ feasible, profitable deviation plans. Its target would be a continuum analogue of Proposition 2: whether random serial dictatorship can be sustained on path when firms are sufficiently patient.

That is a plausible population-continuization target, but it is an extension question rather than a mirror anchored by a computational result. The paper’s distribution \(\pi\) over worker type profiles and its public randomization device are uncertainty and randomization, not a continuous population. And the extension needs additional structure: the paper allows revenue and peer preferences to depend on named workers and whole profiles, whereas a finite-type continuum model needs those payoffs to be represented through type-level masses or other aggregate data. Whether the authors would accept that change as preserving their problem is the weakest point. The possible mirror covers only the RSD sustainment result, not the paper’s full characterization or its arbitrary finite-market primitives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Proposition 2 is an existence theorem: sufficiently patient firms can sustain an RSD process. Proposition 1 characterizes supported payoffs, and Lemma 1 characterizes minmax payoffs. None formulates a complexity question or supplies an algorithmic task. A continuum version of Proposition 2 would therefore extend an economic existence result; by itself, it would not fill ChoCo’s gap in computational complexity.

The proponent’s hiring-cohort scenario is plausible. Many short-lived workers could be grouped by complete payoff-relevant type, with capacities scaled to the cohort. That makes a continuous population a sensible object; existing high-multiplicity matching work supports that judgment. But the proposed target—whether RSD can be sustained for patient firms—is still an existence question. To make it computational, one might ask for the minimum discount factor that sustains RSD, or for the set of sustainable payoffs. Those are possible research questions, but the paper does not specify either as a problem with an input, output, or finite encoding.

That gap remains in the strongest version of the mirror. The paper permits firm revenues and worker utilities to depend on whole worker sets and type profiles, with complementarities and peer effects. A mass model can preserve such behavior only if those payoff functions are represented on distributions or type compositions. The paper gives no finite representation for those functions. If one supplies a succinct, structured representation, one has added restrictions that may erase the generality behind its result; if one encodes arbitrary functions explicitly, population aggregation does not itself make the relevant optimization tractable. RSD still asks firms to choose worker groups to maximize surplus, while the dynamic result also depends on incentives after deviations across histories. The continuum does not identify a canonical computational question or provide a reason to expect continuous-optimization machinery to apply.

So I would reject Proposition 2 as a ChoCo anchor, including its better type-mass version: it is a credible continuous *economic* extension, but making it a computational mirror requires inventing a separate task and its input model. The negative case is strongest on programme fit, not on the claim that no useful continuum model of repeated matching could ever be built. A repeated entry-level market with a few worker types is a genuine counterexample to any blanket claim that multiplicity is implausible here.
