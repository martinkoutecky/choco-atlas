The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), integral bundle domains \(X_t\), and feasible rational per-capita supply \(q\), use a weighted aggregate-demand oracle returning \(d(p)\in\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\operatorname{conv}\{x:x\in D_t(p)\}\), where \(D_t(p)=\arg\max_{x\in X_t}(v_t(x)-p\cdot x)\), to find an exact rational price \(p\) whenever there exists a type-level allocation \(\lambda_{t,x}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{x\in X_t}\lambda_{t,x}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,x}\lambda_{t,x}x=q\), and positive mass assigned only to demanded bundles.

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete valuation types. Each type's agents receive integral bundles, while \(\lambda_{t,x}\) records the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(x\); prices support only demanded bundles, and aggregate per-capita supply is \(q\). The associated potential is \(F_\mu(p)=\sum_t\mu_t\max_{x\in X_t}(v_t(x)-p\cdot x)+p\cdot q\); the computational task is to find an exact minimizing Walrasian price using weighted aggregate demand.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3.12 already has running time independent of the number of buyers, so the rational-mass version may offer less marginal research value than a mirror whose discrete algorithm depends on population size.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.12's price computation and, under gross substitutes, Theorem 6.9's price-and-allocation computation. It leaves the robust-price characterization and separate combinatorial algorithms untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the exact price algorithm achieve running time polynomial in \(n\), \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and the weighted demand-oracle cost?
  • Can the algorithm also return a sparse rational type-bundle allocation certificate within comparable compressed-input bounds?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a market whose society is itself a measure over complete buyer types. Let \(T\) be a support of \(\tau\) valuation types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). A type includes everything that affects its choices: its valuation \(v_t\) over a finite set \(X_t\) of integral bundles, and any bundle constraints. Let \(q\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^n\) be the supply per unit of population mass, and assume \(q\) is feasible.

An allocation is a collection of masses \(\lambda_{t,x}\ge0\): \(\lambda_{t,x}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(x\). Thus \(\sum_x\lambda_{t,x}=\mu_t\), while \(\sum_{t,x}\lambda_{t,x}x=q\). At prices \(p\), the allocation must use only demanded bundles, \(x\in\arg\max_{y\in X_t}(v_t(y)-p\cdot y)\). Each agent receives an integral bundle; only the mass of identical agents can split across bundles.

This fits a regime with \(N\gg\tau\): for example, a large marketplace with many buyers sharing a relatively small set of complete valuation and capacity profiles. Population growth scales supplies per capita. The type definition is strict: two buyers share a type only if all the preferences and constraints used by the problem agree.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.12, proved in this paper. It states that, for a market with \(n\) goods, integer supplies and integer-valued buyer valuations, an exact Walrasian price vector can be computed whenever one exists using only an aggregate demand oracle, in time
\[ O\!\left(n^2T_{\mathrm{AD}}\log(SMn)+n^5\log^{O(1)}(SMn)\right), \]
where \(T_{\mathrm{AD}}\) is the oracle query time, \(S\) is the maximum supply and \(M\) is the maximum absolute valuation. This is an algorithmic anchor; the paper gives no comparable hardness result.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Rational-Measure Walrasian Price. Its input is \(n\), the type support and binary-rational masses, feasible per-capita supply \(q\), integral bundle domains and valuation oracles, plus an aggregate demand oracle. At price \(p\), that oracle may return any
\[ d(p)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{x\in D_t(p)}\pi_{t,x}x, \qquad \sum_{x\in D_t(p)}\pi_{t,x}=1, \]
where \(D_t(p)\) is type \(t\)’s demand set. The task is to output an exact rational price \(p\) for which some such demand allocation clears \(q\).

The corresponding market potential is
\[ F_\mu(p)=\sum_t\mu_t\max_{x\in X_t}\bigl(v_t(x)-p\cdot x\bigr)+p\cdot q. \]
For every aggregate demand returned by the oracle, \(q-d(p)\) is a subgradient. A minimizer therefore certifies a price supported by a market-clearing allocation. The expected classification is Class A: the potential and its pricing oracle have the same form as in Theorem 3.12, with rational type weights. Clearing denominators gives a high-multiplicity finite counterpart; a further rational refinement realizes a rational type allocation as a finite replica. The expected running time should be polynomial in \(n,\tau,L\), and the demand-oracle cost, where \(L\) includes the bit lengths of masses and supplies. The bundle columns remain potentially exponential, so the LP alone is not an algorithm; exact oracle optimization is the computational question.

A second, distinct mirror concerns recovering the allocation as well as prices. Theorem 6.9, also proved here, states that for gross-substitute valuations the paper computes a Walrasian equilibrium—prices and an allocation—in time
\[ O\!\left((mn+n^3)T_V\log(nmM)+n^3\log^{O(1)}(mnM)\right), \]
where \(m\) is the number of buyers and \(T_V\) is the value-oracle query time. Its continuous counterpart, Type-Mass Gross-Substitutes Equilibrium, has the same type masses and integral bundles, with gross-substitute \(v_t\). It asks for both \(p\) and \(\lambda\) satisfying demand and market clearing. I expect Class A here too: gross-substitute demand can be priced efficiently, and the type-level welfare LP has only \(\tau+n\) equality constraints, so a basic feasible allocation can be returned with at most \(\tau+n\) positive type-bundle masses. This extends the paper’s named-buyer algorithm to weighted types and supplies that scale with the population.

The authors should recognize both questions as about their Walrasian equilibrium problem: they preserve integral individual bundles, utility maximization at prices, market clearing and the same dual potential. The continuous object is the population distribution, with fractional type-level allocation forced by that population model. The mirror covers Theorem 3.12’s price computation and, under gross substitutes, Theorem 6.9’s price-and-allocation computation. It does not claim to cover the paper’s robust-price characterization or its separate combinatorial algorithms.

The soft spot is that neither theorem states this rational-mass version. Theorem 3.12 uses integral aggregate-demand answers and exact rounding; the mirror needs a proof that its weighted, convexified demand answers preserve the exact bit-complexity guarantees. Theorem 6.9 also uses a finite named-buyer setup. Those extensions need real proofs. The case survives because both mirrors retain the paper’s pricing problem and demand structure, while replacing buyer multiplicity with \(\tau\) rational type masses. The central further questions are whether the exact bounds remain polynomial in \(L\), and whether the price computation can return a sparse type-level allocation certificate alongside the price.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about marginal research value, not whether the mirrors are well-posed. Theorem 3.12 already makes the market algorithmically macroscopic: it uses aggregate demand and its running time does not depend on the number of buyers. For rational masses \(\mu_t\) and per-capita supplies \(q\), one can clear denominators and form a finite replica with \(D\mu_t\) buyers of each type and supply \(Dq\). Refining \(D\) to realize a rational type-bundle allocation gives the same prices and clearing conditions; the potential is just scaled by \(D\). Theorem 3.12 therefore looks unusually close to an answer already: the proposed mirror mainly asks for its aggregate-demand method to be stated and analyzed on compressed rational weights. That suggests a smaller payoff than a mirror whose discrete algorithm depends on population size or individual structure.

This argument has a limit: under the programme’s stated standard, the finite-replica dictionary is a deliverable, and high-multiplicity work supports the mirror rather than colliding with it. A cheap-looking extension can still be worthwhile if it establishes the precise \((n,\tau,L)\) bounds. So this is a concern about priority, not a reason to reject Theorem 3.12’s continuous question.

Theorem 6.9 is a weaker independent anchor. Its stated algorithm has a dependence on \(m\), and its allocation extraction is designed for named buyers; its perturbation does not directly isolate a fractional allocation among members of one type. Expanding a type-mass market into replicas would not itself establish the proposed compressed running time. But the better mirror asks for type-bundle masses, not identities. A basic feasible allocation can be sparse, and gross-substitute demand offers a plausible route to computing it. The gap is a proof obligation, not a fundamental obstruction.

So the negative case does not defeat either anchor. It can say that the pricing mirror may be a low-marginal extension of the paper’s already aggregate algorithm, and that the allocation mirror needs real compressed-input analysis. It cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
