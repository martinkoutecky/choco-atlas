The continuous mirror question

Given a population of \(N\) agents represented by a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over reported locations in \([0,1]\), and two predicted facility locations, characterize the achievable consistency–robustness trade-off for anonymous, Pareto-efficient, strategy-proof mechanisms. Each individual deviation moves mass \(1/N\); evaluate minimum utility as \(1-\sup_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\min_{j\in\{1,2\}}|x-y_j|\) for chosen facilities \(y_1,y_2\), with consistency measured when the predictions are optimal and robustness over arbitrary predictions.

The model it lives in

Types are reported locations \(x\in[0,1]\); the rational mass \(\mu_x\) is the population fraction of each type, with individual mass \(1/N\). The input includes two predicted facility locations. A mechanism chooses two facilities, and its performance is measured by approximation ratios for the population's minimum utility under accurate and arbitrary predictions.

The objection that survived

The guarantees depend on support extremes rather than mass values, and strategy-proofness requires a finite individual mass; this limits the role of the distribution but does not make the population model incoherent.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 7's deterministic two-facility consistency and robustness guarantees for minimum utility. It leaves the single-facility, randomized-mechanism, maximum-distance, and characterization results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the achievable consistency–robustness frontier for strategy-proof mechanisms on these distributional reports?
  • Does allowing mechanisms to use mass values beyond their effect on the support improve that frontier?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper has a natural population-distribution extension. But it has no anchor of the kind this task requires: none of its numbered results classifies an underlying problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Its theorems establish incentive and approximation guarantees for mechanisms. So the qualifying anchor set is empty; I would not present this paper as evidence for ChoCo’s complexity landscape.

The nearest result is Theorem 7, proved here. For two facilities and two location predictions, it proves that, for \(\lambda\in[0,1/4]\), the strategy-proof \(\mathrm{MINMAX2P}_\lambda\) mechanism is \(\frac{2-\lambda}{2-2\lambda}\)-consistent and \(\frac{3+2\lambda}{2(1+2\lambda)}\)-robust for minimum utility; at \(\lambda=1/4\), both ratios are \(7/6\). For maximum distance, it is 1-consistent with unbounded robustness at \(\lambda=0\), and has unbounded consistency and robustness when \(\lambda>0\). That is a mechanism-performance theorem, not a complexity result.

A plausible unanchored extension would take a society to be a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over locations \(x\in[0,1]\), where each type is a reported location and \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of residents at \(x\). This fits a large service population with many residents but relatively few shared location types. Given two predicted facility locations, the mechanism outputs an ordered pair \((y_1,y_2)\); its minimum utility is
\[ 1-\max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\min\{|x-y_1|,|x-y_2|\}. \]
The corresponding research question would be to characterize the consistency–robustness trade-off among anonymous, Pareto-efficient, strategy-proof mechanisms on these distributional reports, returning a mechanism and proofs of its guarantees. Theorem 7 is a natural point of comparison for that question, but it does not establish its computational complexity.

The soft spot is strategy-proofness. In a genuinely nonatomic population, a single agent’s report changes no mass, so individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. Preserving the paper’s incentive question requires specifying finite-agent deviations—say, moving mass \(1/n\) between reported locations for a population of size \(n\). That makes the extension more precise, but also means it is a high-multiplicity version with an explicit individual mass, not a clean nonatomic limit.

Thus there is a credible population mirror for the paper’s mechanism-design guarantees, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as their problem. Under the requested anchor rule, however, there is no named result to mirror as a computational-complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s concession settles the strict anchor test: the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 7 is the closest candidate, but it proves approximation guarantees for a strategy-proof mechanism; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem.

The proposed city-resident model is sensible: let \(\mu\) describe the population’s locations and ask for prediction-aware mechanisms over that distribution. But preserving the paper’s individual strategy-proofness is awkward in a genuinely nonatomic model: one resident has zero mass, so changing their report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. Giving each resident mass \(1/n\) restores the incentive question, while returning to a sequence of finite games. And the paper’s minimum-utility objective,
\[ 1-\max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\min_{j\in\{1,2\}}|x-y_j|, \]
depends on which locations occur, not how much population holds each location. The same is true of the maximum-distance objective. Multiplicities therefore add no weight-sensitive computational structure to these guarantees. For two facilities on an interval, the mechanisms use simple location statistics, and the finite-support benchmark can be computed from the ordered support points.

A mass-sensitive objective or richer facility problem could create a worthwhile computational question. But changing to such a problem would leave Theorem 7 as its anchor only in name: the paper’s result concerns egalitarian distance guarantees and individual incentives. That would be an adjacent research direction rather than a continuous mirror of this result for ChoCo’s complexity programme.

So the strongest negative case is programme-relative, not universal. The paper offers no qualifying computational anchor, and its closest distributional extension appears to add little population-complexity content. Still, a continuous-population mechanism-design question about facility location is plausible and could be worthwhile outside that remit; I cannot honestly rule out every useful scenario.
