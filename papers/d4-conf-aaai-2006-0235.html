What the mirror covers

The proposed welfare extension draws on Proposition 11 and the unnumbered optimization algorithms in Sections 6.1–6.2; it does not mirror a named computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest affirmative angle is a continuous population of procurement stakeholders. It fits the paper’s software-package example: many users or business units must choose one package, and only a moderate number \(\tau\) of preference types occur. Each type has a complete multiattribute utility model; its mass \(\mu_t\) is its share of the user population. If utilities are measured in a common monetary scale, the decision is to choose one feature assignment \(x\) maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t U_t(x)\). Money-based measurable value functions in Section 5.2 give this aggregation a plausible interpretation.

The only arguable named anchor is Proposition 11, proved in this paper. It says that if \(G\) is a CUI network for \(U(S)\), then \(U(S)\) can be represented by conditional utility functions whose attribute sets correspond to undirected cliques in \(G\). It is a compact-representation result, not a complexity classification. The optimization algorithms and their running-time claims appear in Sections 6.1 and 6.2, but are not stated as numbered results.

A precise candidate problem is Continuous CUI Welfare Maximization. The instance has finite feature domains \(D_i\), a common CUI tree \(G\), and a finite support \(T\) of stakeholder types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\). Each type has a rational-valued utility function represented on \(G\). To preserve the tree structure under aggregation, require the types to share the same positive transformation functions \(g_i\), while allowing their local functions \(f_{t,i}\) to differ. The feasible outcomes are all assignments \(x\in\prod_i D_i\). The task is to return an assignment maximizing \(W_\mu(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t U_t(x)\). With shared \(g_i\), the weighted sum retains the same decomposition, so the paper’s CUI-tree optimization procedure suggests tractability, with input size also depending on \(\tau\) and the local tables. Further questions include whether tractability survives when types have different \(g_i\), or when feasibility constraints couple attributes.

This is the best positive case I can defend, but it is not a firm anchor-compliant case under the stated rule. Proposition 11 gives a representation, and the paper computes with CUI representations, so one could argue it qualifies under the formulation exception. Still, it does not itself formulate the welfare-maximization problem or assert its complexity. More seriously, the paper optimizes one decision maker’s utility; welfare over many people adds interpersonal aggregation. A shared money scale helps, but the common \(g_i\) condition is restrictive and is what lets the tree algorithm carry over. So I would present this as a narrow, plausible extension of the paper’s optimization model, not as a continuous mirror established by one of its numbered complexity results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 11 is the only named result the proposed mirror can anchor to, and it does not establish a computational problem or its complexity. It says that one decision maker’s utility function can be represented using conditional utility functions on cliques of a CUI network. The paper’s tree optimization algorithm is also for one utility function over attribute assignments. Neither result models a population. Real-valued or continuous attributes such as money and time do not supply the missing population axis.

The strongest repair is Continuous CUI Welfare Maximization: give each stakeholder type a mass and a utility over the same feature assignments, then maximize \(W_\mu(x)=\sum_t \mu_t U_t(x)\). That is a coherent collective-choice problem, but it adds a social welfare objective the paper does not specify. A utility function represents one decision maker’s preferences and is unique up to a positive affine transformation; summing people’s utilities therefore needs interpersonal normalization and an aggregation rule. Monetary value functions could supply a common unit, but then maximizing total willingness to pay is an added modeling choice, not a population version of Proposition 11. Under the proponent’s shared-\(g_i\) restriction, the weighted sum can be absorbed into the local functions and the paper’s tree procedure applied. Allowing different \(g_i\) may create a harder optimization question, but it can also destroy the shared CUI structure; that question would concern the new welfare model, not the proposition’s representation claim.

So the anchor fails: the paper contains no named computational result about a continuous society, and the best replacement changes the object from individual utility optimization to social welfare aggregation. The negative case is weakest at that boundary. A population of business units or stakeholders with comparable monetary valuations is plausible, and studying optimization over such a population could be worthwhile. I cannot rule out that research direction; I can only say it is an extension prompted by the paper, not a continuous mirror of its named result.
