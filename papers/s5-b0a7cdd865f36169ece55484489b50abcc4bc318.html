The continuous mirror question

Given finite candidates \(Q\cup S\), a candidate-arrival order, a current candidate, an addition budget \(k\), a chair order \(\sigma\), a distinguished candidate \(d\), and a rational mass distribution over partial ranking types of the already revealed candidates, determine whether the chair has a nonanticipating policy for adding at most \(k\) spoiler candidates such that, for every consistent future refinement of each partial mass into complete ranking types, the final plurality winner set contains some \(a\in Q\cup S\) with \(a\succeq_\sigma d\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity online plurality-control game: complete ranking types carry population mass \(\mu\), partial types may split into consistent refinements, the chair's decisions are binary and irrevocable for each arriving candidate, and the objective is a worst-case guarantee about mass-weighted plurality winners.

The objection that survived

The integer score thresholds \(B\) and \(B-1\), ceiling arguments, and adversarial balancing proof do not automatically extend to rational masses, so the claimed polynomial-time algorithm remains to be established.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the online-plurality-CCAC result of Theorem 4.2 and naturally suggests the other plurality variants, but it leaves the artificial \(E\)/\(E'\) \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\)-completeness results of Theorem 4.1 without a faithful mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can Theorem 4.2's adversarial balancing argument be reformulated for rational mass thresholds and yield a polynomial-time policy in the input bit length?
  • What precise representation and rounding or approximation guarantee relates this mass-refinement game to finite \(N\)-voter instances?

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper does admit a credible continuous mirror, although the two anchors have different strengths. My lead is a Class B mirror of Theorem 4.1: its hardness is driven by the sequential candidate agenda and the chair–adversary quantifier structure, so it should survive continuization. Theorem 4.2 gives a more natural Class A companion for plurality.

The common population model is this. The candidate set \(C\) and its presentation order remain finite and discrete. A voter type is a complete ranking of \(C\), and a society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over such types. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the electorate in cohort \(t\), not a fractional candidate or fractional winner. At any moment, voters reveal only their rankings restricted to the candidates seen so far. Thus a revealed partial type may later split into several complete ranking types, with the same total mass, as the adversary reveals how different population cohorts rank the next candidate. A chair’s decision is still an indivisible, irrevocable decision about a candidate.

A plausible regime is a large electorate—say, the members or viewers of a national competition, professional association, or large hiring process—with millions of voters but only tens or hundreds of recurring preference trajectories. The relevant comparison is \(N\gg \tau\), where \(N\) is the number of people and \(\tau\) the number of distinct complete or currently distinguishable preference types. The chair controls which candidates enter or remain in the competition, while voter preferences are exposed as candidates arrive.

The lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. Its first part states that, whenever the underlying election system has a polynomial-time winner problem, online constructive control by deleting candidates, online constructive control by adding candidates, online destructive control by deleting candidates in both the non-hand-tied and hand-tied models, and online destructive control by adding candidates all belong to \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\). Its second part states that there are election systems \(E\) and \(E'\), each with a polynomial-time winner problem, for which the corresponding online control problems are \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\)-complete. The proof gives the hardness reduction from QBF and notes that the hardness holds even with one voter. I use specifically the entry that online-\(E\)-CCAC is \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\)-complete.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\[ \textit{Online-}E^\infty\textit{-CCAC}. \]

An instance consists of a finite candidate set \(C=Q\mathbin{\dot\cup}S\), where \(Q\) are qualified candidates and \(S\) are potential additions; a presentation order; the current candidate; an addition budget \(k\); a chair preference order \(\sigma\); a distinguished candidate \(d\); and a rational mass distribution over the currently revealed partial voter types. At each future stage, the adversary may refine or split each partial-type mass among all insertion positions for the newly presented candidate, consistently with the rankings already revealed. After seeing that revelation, the chair irrevocably chooses whether to add the current spoiler, subject to the budget. A solution is a nonanticipating chair policy that guarantees that, at the end, at least one winner belongs to

\[ G=\{a\in C:a\succeq_\sigma d\} \]

against every admissible future revelation.

The election rule \(E^\infty\) is the mass version of the particular \(E\) constructed in the proof. Candidates retain labels \((F,i)\). If the labels are malformed, use different formulas, have an odd number of variables, or omit a required even-indexed candidate, there are no winners. For every odd \(i\), \(v_i\) is true exactly when \((F,i)\) is present. For every even \(i\), \(v_i\) is true exactly when mass \(1\) prefers \((F,i)\) to \((F,0)\). All selected candidates win if \(F(v_1,\ldots,v_\ell)\) is true; otherwise there are no winners. Winner determination remains polynomial. The original “there are two or more voters” condition is replaced by unanimity of mass, which is the natural high-multiplicity interpretation of the one-voter construction.

I expect \(\textit{Online-}E^\infty\textit{-CCAC}\) to be hard by transfer, not continuum-specifically hard. The same QBF construction survives: chair choices encode existential variables, future preference revelations encode universal variables, and the candidate sequence carries the alternation. Splitting mass does not create a fundamentally new Boolean value under the unanimity test: a split makes the relevant variable false, while uniform mass can realize either Boolean value when needed. Thus the hardness lives in the candidate agenda, not in the number of named voters. The continuous problem may require a separate upper-bound analysis because unrestricted mass refinements introduce rational or real-valued game states, but the expected classification is at least \(\mathrm{PSPACE}\)-hard and conceptually Class B.

This is a genuine mirror rather than a novelty collision. The paper studies finite voter lists and adversarially revealed rankings; it does not formulate a mass distribution over voter types or a mass-refinement game. The continuous version preserves the paper’s actual question—online candidate control—and continuizes only the population.

The second anchor is Theorem 4.2, also proved here. It states that online plurality-CCDC, online plurality-CCAC, online plurality-DCDC in both chair models, and online plurality-DCAC are all in \(\mathrm{P}\). I use the online-plurality-CCAC entry.

The corresponding problem is:

\[ \textit{Continuous Online Plurality-CCAC}. \]

The instance has the same candidate-arrival data, addition budget, chair order, distinguished candidate, and current partial profile, but the profile is represented by rational masses of voter types. At the end, if \(A\subseteq S\) is the set of spoilers added by the chair, plurality is computed on \(Q\cup A\) using mass-weighted scores

\[ \operatorname{pl}_\mu(a) = \mu\bigl(\{t:a\text{ is top-ranked in }t\text{ after masking to }Q\cup A\}\bigr). \]

The chair seeks a policy guaranteeing that some \(a\succeq_\sigma d\) is a plurality winner against every admissible future refinement of the population’s rankings. The solution is again the policy, not a fractional choice of candidates.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. The proof of Theorem 4.2 relies on score comparisons, the number of future good and bad candidates, the remaining budget, and the adversary’s ability to redistribute first-place support. In the mass model, integer vote counts become rational masses and the proof’s ceiling and averaging arguments become rational threshold inequalities. Candidate inclusion remains discrete, but plurality does not require enumerating all \(m!\) ranking types: only the currently exposed cohorts and their aggregate first-place masses matter. The expected classification is therefore Class A, with running time polynomial in the number of represented cohorts, candidates, and mass bit length. This would be a new theorem about the mirror, not something already proved by Theorem 4.2.

This plurality problem also generates useful follow-ups: continuous online control by deletion, destructive control, finite-population approximation bounds, and whether other rules—especially Borda or Condorcet rules—turn the adversarial mass-refinement problem into a genuinely continuum-specific hard problem.

The weakest point is the lead anchor’s election system. The \(E\) used in Theorem 4.1 is deliberately artificial and its hardness already holds with one voter, so its continuous version demonstrates that continuization cannot dissolve agenda-driven hardness more than it demonstrates a practical benefit from mass modelling. The plurality mirror is more natural, but its polynomial-time classification is a conjectured extension rather than a theorem in the paper. Even so, the paper clearly supplies two acceptable computational anchors, and the lead question is a precise population-continuized form of the authors’ central online candidate-control problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is against the Theorem 4.1 anchor, not against the paper as a whole.

The election system \(E\) used for the PSPACE-hardness result is intrinsically hostile to continuization. Its definition explicitly makes the one-voter case special: if there are two or more voters, everyone loses. Thus the exact high-multiplicity version collapses immediately. A society with one effective type and mass \(1\) merely reproduces the one-voter construction; it does not create a meaningful population continuum.

The proposed \(E^\infty\) repairs this by replacing “there is one voter” with a unanimity condition such as “mass \(1\) prefers \((F,i)\) to \((F,0)\).” But that is a new election rule, not a relaxation of the paper’s \(E\). For a heterogeneous population, one could instead use majority, a threshold, positive mass, or some other statistic, and these produce different games. Nothing in the paper selects unanimity. Moreover, arbitrary mass-splitting gives the chair additional observable states that did not exist in the one-voter QBF encoding. If splitting is disallowed, the model reduces to one effective ballot; if it is allowed, the claimed transfer is no longer immediate. The PSPACE result therefore supplies no convincing high-multiplicity mirror: preserving the original rule degenerates, while preserving the reduction requires inventing a new rule.

The plurality anchor is much harder to defeat. A large election, competition, or hiring process can plausibly have many voters but relatively few recurring preference types. Plurality is anonymous, the objective depends only on winners rather than identities, and the online history can naturally be represented by masses of partial ranking types that later split. Candidate arrivals and chair decisions remain discrete, which is permitted by the programme. The resulting weighted adversarial game is a legitimate continuous-population question.

The proponent has not proved that the polynomial algorithm survives fractional masses—the integer \(B\) and \(B-1\) arguments in Theorem 4.2 require real work—but that is not an objection to asking the question. Nor can one dismiss it as a mere encoding change: exactly such high-multiplicity reformulations are the programme’s intended objects.

So the honest negative verdict is limited. Theorem 4.1 is a poor, essentially degenerate anchor, but Theorem 4.2 still supplies a credible continuous mirror. The universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists therefore cannot be sustained.
