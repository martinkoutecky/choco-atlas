The continuous mirror question

Given a comparison graph \(G=(C,E)\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete rankings of \(C\), let \(q_t(e)\in\{-1,+1\}\) encode type \(t\)'s preference on oriented edge \(e\), and set \(\omega_e=\sum_t\mu_tq_t(e)\). Compute the normalized score vector \(\alpha\) minimizing \(\sum_{e=(u,v)\in E}(\omega_e-(\alpha_v-\alpha_u))^2\), together with the canonical orthogonal decomposition \(\omega=\partial_1^T\alpha+\partial_2\beta+h\), where \(h\in\ker\Delta_1\) and \(\beta\) is chosen with minimum norm.

The model it lives in

The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types. Its pairwise means \(\omega\) are the edge data; the score vector \(\alpha\), triangle component \(\beta\), and harmonic residual \(h\) are the computed outputs. Normalize \(\alpha\) on each connected component and choose the minimum-norm \(\beta\) for a canonical decomposition.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible mirror is Continuous Population Least-Squares Ranking with Hodge Residuals. Its types are complete rankings of \(m\) alternatives. An instance gives a comparison graph \(G=(C,E)\), an oriented edge set, and \(\tau\) supported ranking types with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to one. Each ranking gives a report \(q_t(e)\in\{-1,+1\}\) on every compared pair, according to which endpoint is preferred. The population’s mean comparison on edge \(e\) is \(\omega_e=\sum_t\mu_tq_t(e)\).

The decision variable is a score \(\alpha_v\) for each alternative. Ask for scores minimizing
\[ \sum_{e=(u,v)\in E}\bigl(\omega_e-(\alpha_v-\alpha_u)\bigr)^2. \]
Equivalently, minimize the population’s mean squared prediction loss, \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{e=(u,v)}(q_t(e)-(\alpha_v-\alpha_u))^2\): the two objectives differ only by a term independent of \(\alpha\). Normalize scores to sum to zero on each connected component to fix the additive freedom. The richer output mirrors the paper’s second computation too: a triangle component \(\beta\) and a harmonic residual \(h\), with \(\omega=\partial_1^T\alpha+\partial_2\beta+h\). Choose the minimum-norm \(\beta\) if a canonical output is wanted.

My single anchor is Theorem 6.2 (Basic Fact), proved in this paper. In its nondegenerate case, it says that the Hodge decomposition \(\omega=\partial_1^T\alpha+\partial_2\beta+h\), with \(h\in\ker\Delta_1\), is equivalent to four ways of specifying the same computation: the decomposition itself; sequential least squares, first fitting \(\partial_1^T a\) to \(\omega\), then fitting \(\partial_2 b\) to the first residual; the normal equations \(\partial_1\partial_1^T a=\partial_1\omega\) and \(\partial_2^T\partial_2 b=\partial_2 r^*\); and the corresponding KKT saddle systems. Here \(r^*\) is the first least-squares residual. The theorem assumes \(\alpha\notin\ker\partial_1^T\) and \(\beta\notin\ker\partial_2\); Remark 6.3 says to omit the corresponding equation when a component vanishes. The printed second KKT block appears to have a dimensional typo: its lower-left block should be \(\partial_2^T\).

This counts as a formulation anchor because the paper computes with these least-squares systems in its solver experiments. It does not claim a complexity class for them. I expect the mirror to be Class A, tractable: forming \(\omega\) takes \(O(\tau|E|)\) operations, and exact rational linear algebra solves the stated systems in polynomial time. The graph has at most \(m\) vertices, \(O(m^2)\) edges, and \(O(m^3)\) triangles. These are consequences for the mirror problem, not a complexity theorem proved by the authors. The anchor is strongest for the first least-squares ranking and covers the second residual decomposition as well.

The regime is a large panel rating a fixed set of products, teams, or proposals through pairwise comparisons. If users fall into a modest number of recurring complete ballot types, then \(N\), the number of users, can be very large while \(\tau\ll N\). This is especially plausible when the survey uses a fixed comparison design and users share the same ranking; the paper itself discusses social-choice voters’ complete preferences as producing edge orientations. The high-multiplicity input records the fractions of users with each ballot, rather than enumerating them. The authors could reasonably recognize the mirror: their underlying computation is already ranking alternatives from pairwise comparison values, and the population simply supplies those values as weighted pairwise margins.

The mirror is deliberately narrow. It covers the ranking and inconsistency-decomposition computations, not the paper’s experiments on random clique-complex topology or its comparisons of particular numerical solvers. It also raises a useful dictionary question: for finite counts \(n_t\), \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), does the weighted-margin instance preserve the paper’s output exactly? Here it does, by construction. Other questions include the bit complexity of canonical exact outputs and how the residual components vary as the ballot masses change.

The weakest point is that this population layer may look like an input interpretation rather than a new computational problem. The paper already takes real-valued edge comparisons as data, and the mirror compresses a crowd into those same edge averages; it does not introduce a new optimization over agents. Nor does Theorem 6.2 establish a complexity classification. Still, the population is a plausible source of the paper’s data, and the theorem’s computed formulation gives a precise underlying problem to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole anchor, Theorem 6.2 (“Basic Fact”), is an equivalence among Hodge decomposition, least-squares problems, normal equations, and KKT systems for an already-given comparison vector \(\omega\). It establishes no complexity result and models no society, voters, or types. The paper’s solver experiments are numerical comparisons, not a computational complexity classification. So the theorem does not itself supply a population problem to continuize.

The strongest proposed mirror is coherent: let each judge or voter have a complete ranking, take a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, and form each edge’s mean comparison \(\omega_e=\sum_t\mu_tq_t(e)\). But the paper’s computation then receives only \(\omega\). The scores and, with a canonical choice, the Hodge components are determined by that vector; the distribution matters only as one way to produce it. Many different populations can give the same \(\omega\), and the paper asks no question about changing the population, targeting its members, or retaining individual-level outcomes. A finite electorate mapped to its edge averages already fits the paper’s real-valued input model. Thus the proposed finite-to-continuous dictionary would be for a newly added “voters generate comparisons” wrapper, not for a discrete society problem stated in this paper. Making population composition computationally active would require adding a new task, such as intervention or robustness.

That is the best case against this anchor: the mirror is a plausible application, but it does not make the society the object of the paper’s computation. I would not call the negative case airtight. The authors explicitly discuss social-choice voters with complete preferences, so they might recognize the population-sourced version as their ranking computation. If that recognition suffices, then \(\mu\mapsto\omega\mapsto\) least-squares ranking is a well-posed continuous mirror, even though the paper contains no complexity theorem about it.
