The continuous mirror question

Given a supplier network with \(\tau\) occupied service locations, nonnegative edge costs, a rational mass \(\mu_t>0\) at each location type \(t\), and rational weights \(a_t\), compute an optimal nonnegative per-resident charge \(p_t\). Require that every proper measurable coalition \(Q\), with mass \(q_t=\mu(Q\cap t)\) and occupied locations \(U(q)=\{t:q_t>0\}\), satisfies \(\sum_t q_t p_t\le c(U(q))\), where \(c(U)\) is the supplier's minimum spanning tree cost to locations in \(U\). Equivalently, optimize \(\sum_t a_t y_t\) subject to \(y_t=\mu_t p_t\ge0\) and \(\sum_{t\in U}y_t\le c(U)\) for every \(U\subseteq T\).

The model it lives in

An atomless cost-sharing society with a finite support of service-location types carrying masses \(\mu_t\). The decision variables are per-resident charges \(p_t\), or total type charges \(y_t=\mu_t p_t\); the objective is a rational weighted sum, and coalitional stability imposes the MST cost bound for every set of occupied types.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 2's hardness for general linear optimization in the MST setting, transferred to weighted optimization over \(P(N,c)\). It does not preserve Theorem 5's hardness for maximizing total shareable costs, nor the paper's approximation algorithm for that objective.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which structural restrictions on the supplier network, such as bounded treewidth, permit polynomial-time or fixed-parameter weighted optimization over the location coalition constraints?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is for the paper’s general linear optimization over stable cost shares, rather than its headline problem of maximizing the total shareable cost.

Consider a utility network with a supplier and a finite set \(T\) of service locations. A type is a resident’s location and relevant service profile; residents of type \(t\) are indistinguishable for the problem. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with \(\tau=|T|\) occupied locations but potentially millions of residents. A coalition’s outside option is to connect the supplier to every location where the coalition has positive mass. Let \(c(U)\) be the minimum spanning tree cost for the supplier and locations \(U\).

Here is the continuous problem, Weighted Continuous Stable-Share Optimization. Given the network, \(\mu\), and rational weights \(a_t\), find nonnegative per-resident charges \(p_t\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t p_t \]
subject to stability for every proper measurable coalition \(Q\):
\[ \sum_{t\in T} q_t p_t \le c(U(q)), \qquad q_t=\mu(Q\cap t),\quad U(q)=\{t:q_t>0\}. \]
A solution is an optimal charge schedule \(p\) and its objective value. Nonnegative charges are a natural cost-sharing restriction, and the paper explicitly studies that restriction.

Writing \(y_t=\mu_t p_t\), these continuous constraints become exactly
\[ y\ge 0,\qquad \sum_{t\in U}y_t\le c(U)\quad\text{for every }U\subseteq T. \]
For proper \(U\), take the whole mass at each location in \(U\). For \(U=T\), take a coalition containing a \(1-\varepsilon\) fraction of every type and let \(\varepsilon\) tend to zero. Conversely, these inequalities bound every coalition’s charges. Thus the atomless model naturally yields the paper’s \(P(N,c)\cap\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^T\) polyhedron. The optimization is over an exponentially constrained set of location coalitions; the computational question is to find an optimum, equivalently to provide a separation procedure for violated coalition constraints.

My anchor is Corollary 2, proved in this paper: for minimum cost spanning tree games, a polynomial-time algorithm for linear optimization over the almost core would imply \(P=NP\). The paper derives this using its general equivalence between optimization over the almost core and over \(P(N,c)\), together with the coNP-hard core-membership result it cites from Faigle et al. [18]. The continuous problem above lands directly on the \(P\) side of that equivalence. Its expected complexity is therefore hardness that transfers: the hard choices are among subsets of the \(\tau\) locations, and distributing residents continuously across those locations does not remove that combinatorial structure. The paper’s Corollary 2 is the named anchor; the cited membership result and the paper’s equivalence explain why this particular continuous formulation inherits the difficulty.

This is a plausible regime for the authors’ model: large cohorts of residents share a location, network access, and service profile, while the number of locations is much smaller than the population. Fixed connection costs also make it credible that serving a positive mass at a location incurs the same link cost regardless of that mass. The model continuizes the population; the fractional charge schedule is simply the natural way to assign costs to that population.

The limitation is substantial and specific. This does not preserve Theorem 5’s NP-hardness for maximizing total shareable costs. In the continuous model, proper coalitions can approach the whole population, so their stability constraints imply \(y(T)\le c(T)\); since MST games have a nonempty core, maximizing total charges reaches \(c(T)\). That objective is easy here. My case is therefore for a mirror of the paper’s general weighted optimization result in its MST setting, not for a continuous version of its headline total-share maximization result. A natural follow-up is whether useful approximation or parameterized algorithms exist when the number or structure of service locations is restricted.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is against the paper’s headline objective, Theorem 5’s maximization of total shareable cost. In an atomless society, a coalition can take a \(1-\varepsilon\) fraction of every type while remaining proper. If type \(t\) pays a finite per-capita charge \(p_t\), stability gives
\[ (1-\varepsilon)\sum_t \mu_t p_t \le c(T). \]
Taking \(\varepsilon\) to zero restores the grand-coalition constraint. Since MST games have a nonempty core, maximizing total charges then yields \(c(T)\). The almost-core relaxation that drives Theorem 5 has disappeared in the continuum. That is a genuine degeneration of this natural mirror.

It does not, however, defeat the proponent’s anchor, Corollary 2. With arbitrary type weights, the change of variables \(y_t=\mu_t p_t\) turns the continuous problem into optimization over \(P(N,c)\). Theorem 2 and Corollary 2 supply the relevant complexity result. A hard MST instance can be represented with one location type per graph vertex and any positive population masses; the number of residents can be much larger than the support. The hardness in \(\tau\) is not, under the programme’s stated standard, a reason to reject the mirror.

One could object that the masses disappear from this weighted problem after the change of variables, so it adds no new computation beyond the finite location game. But that is not a sound defeat here: it is a well-posed population model, and a known or equivalent answer does not make the question unworthy. The honest negative case is therefore weak. I can show why the headline shareable-cost result collapses, but I cannot responsibly dismiss the weighted continuous mirror.
