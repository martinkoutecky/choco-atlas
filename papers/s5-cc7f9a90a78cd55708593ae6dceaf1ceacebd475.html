What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result that qualifies as an anchor. Its introductory claim that minimax-regret optimization is NP-hard is a broad literature statement, not a numbered result of this paper. The numbered results are geometric propositions. For example, Proposition 6, stated here as a straightforward observation, says that if one basis is minimum-weight at every extreme point of the current uncertainty region, then it is uniformly optimal throughout that region and the minimax regret is zero. The algorithm uses this as a stopping test, but the proposition does not establish a complexity classification. The paper also gives no formal bound on the algorithm’s running time or query count.

A population interpretation of its type vectors and weights may be conceivable, but it would not give the anchor-based case requested here. I would not present Proposition 6 or the experimental results as a tractability theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no surviving named anchor in the proponent’s case. Proposition 6 is a geometric stopping criterion: if one basis is optimal at every vertex of the current uncertainty region, it is uniformly optimal there. It gives no complexity or query bound. The experiments report performance, not a complexity result. The paper does define an algorithmic problem, but its main claims about faster convergence and fewer queries are not formal algorithmic bounds to translate.

The deeper obstacle is that the paper’s continuous object is the *uncertainty set*, not a society. Its ground set contains jobs, edges, or other items from which a matroid base is selected. The weight vector describes one decision-maker’s uncertain trade-offs across those items; \(\lambda\) indexes possible weights. A preference query asks that decision-maker to compare two items. Neither \(\lambda\) nor the uncertainty polytope is a distribution over agents, and the query answers do not aggregate voters’ preferences.

The strongest plausible repair is to use many repeated jobs or items, group interchangeable ones into types, and let capacities or selected quantities scale with type mass. That could produce a meaningful high-multiplicity matroid-optimization problem. But the repeated units are still decision objects, not a population whose preferences determine the social choice. The elicitation algorithm also reasons about individual item pairs and matroid exchanges; compressing items into masses changes that query problem. It may be worthwhile high-multiplicity optimization, but it does not mirror this paper’s preference-elicitation result as a continuous society.

One could instead make the users into the population and aggregate their valuations over bases. Then the model has a genuine society distribution, but it replaces the paper’s single decision-maker’s minimax-regret elicitation with a social aggregation objective. With identical users, the added mass has no substantive effect; with heterogeneous users, the objective and elicitation task have changed. That is a possible new research direction, not a continuous mirror the paper itself supplies. The negative case is strongest on this model mismatch and the absence of a named computational result; the universal claim remains hard to prove, since the broader aggregate-valuation variant could still be interesting on its own.
