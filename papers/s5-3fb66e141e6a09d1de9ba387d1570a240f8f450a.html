The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is qualified: this paper has a very natural continuous-population analogue, but it does not contain a qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s strict rule. Its numbered results are structural characterisations of maximum-likelihood voting, not results asserting membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), NP-hardness, FPT, or a comparable complexity classification.

The best candidate is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It states, in full, that every scoring rule is both an MLEWIV rule and an MLERIV rule. For the winner interpretation, the paper constructs an i.i.d. noise model in which, conditional on correct winner \(w\), a vote \(t\) has probability proportional to \(2^{s(t,w)}\); maximizing likelihood therefore maximizes the scoring-rule score of \(w\). For the ranking interpretation, if the correct ranking is \(c_1\succ\cdots\succ c_m\), a vote \(t\) has probability proportional to

\[ \prod_{i=1}^m (m+1-i)^{s(t,c_i)}, \]

so maximum likelihood orders candidates by their scoring-rule scores. This theorem is proved here, not cited from elsewhere.

A plausible continuous mirror would be:

Continuous i.i.d.-MLE Evaluation. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), a scoring vector \(s\), and a rational population distribution \( \mu \) over complete rankings \(T\). The mass \( \mu_t \) is the fraction of a large population whose observed vote is ranking \(t\). The latent outcome is either a winner \(w\in C\) or a complete ranking \( \rho \) of \(C\). Using the noise model constructed in Theorem 1, output the maximum-likelihood latent outcome under the normalized population log-likelihood

\[ L_o(\mu)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log p_o(t). \]

For the winner version, the solution is

\[ \arg\max_{w\in C}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t s(t,w). \]

For the ranking version, it is the candidates sorted by those aggregate scores. Ties are retained, as in the paper.

This is a genuine population continuization. A type is a complete observed ranking; mass is the fraction of users or query sessions producing that ranking; the decision variable is the common latent “correct” winner or ranking; and the objective is per-capita log likelihood. The independent-noise assumption is interpreted through the high-multiplicity limit: for rational \( \mu \), multiplying all masses by a common denominator recovers an ordinary finite profile, and the normalized log likelihood is the finite log likelihood divided by the electorate size.

The most convincing regime is document or recommendation selection: millions of user sessions evaluate the same fixed set of documents, products, or alternatives, with many sessions sharing the same ranking behaviour. The number of agents can be vastly larger than the number of ranking types actually observed. The paper itself motivates voting through document selection, so the authors should recognise this as their model rather than as an unrelated relaxation. The “correct outcome” remains discrete; only the population is continuous.

The expected classification is Class A, but modestly so. The problem is solvable by weighted score aggregation in time polynomial in \(m\), the support size of \( \mu \), and the encoding length. The result does not show discrete hardness dissolving: winner and ranking evaluation are already easy in the finite model. Its value would instead be as a clean high-multiplicity formulation and as a starting point for harder follow-up problems, such as:

  • learning or choosing the noise model from population data;
  • finding the smallest population perturbation that changes the MLE;
  • robust MLE under uncertainty in \( \mu \);
  • continuous inverse voting, where one changes minimal population mass to force a desired inferred outcome.

A second, weaker structural mirror comes from Theorem 3, also proved here. It states that Bucklin is neither MLEWIV nor MLERIV. The proof uses Lemma 1: two vote populations can produce the same Bucklin outcome separately but a different outcome when combined. Concretely, \(V_1\) contains two copies of \(a\succ b\succ c\succ d\succ e\) and one copy of \(b\succ a\succ c\succ d\succ e\); \(V_2\) contains two copies of \(b\succ d\succ a\succ c\succ e\), one copy of \(c\succ e\succ a\succ b\succ d\), and one copy of \(c\succ a\succ b\succ d\succ e\). Bucklin returns \(a\succ b\succ c\succ d\succ e\) on both populations, but \(b\succ a\succ c\succ d\succ e\) on \(V_1+V_2\).

Its continuous counterpart would ask whether there exists one i.i.d. noise family \(p_o(t)\) such that, for every rational population distribution \( \mu \), the MLE outcome equals Bucklin’s outcome. The answer is immediately negative: normalize \(V_1\) and \(V_2\) into rational distributions \( \mu^1,\mu^2 \), and their appropriately weighted mixture gives the same contradiction. Thus the obstruction transfers exactly; it is not removed by continuization. This is a sensible continuous question, but again it is a structural impossibility result, not a computational complexity result.

That is the decisive weakness. The paper has no named computational anchor. Proposition 1, Lemma 1, and Theorems 2–7 are all proved structural statements about whether voting rules can be represented as i.i.d. maximum-likelihood estimators. The discussion of computational voting results in the introduction cites other work; it is not a result of this paper. A referee could therefore accept the continuous-population MLE formulation as natural, but under the ChoCo grading rule could still reject it as a compliant mirror because the paper never makes the computational claim that the mirror is supposed to continue.

So the strongest positive verdict is: the paper supplies a highly plausible continuous population object, especially for Theorem 1, and its finite-to-continuous correspondence is exact. But it is a weak ChoCo candidate because the main contribution is not a computational result and the leading mirror largely replaces normalized vote counts by masses without creating a new complexity phenomenon.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor at all. Its numbered results—Proposition 1, Lemma 1, and Theorems 1–7—are structural statements about whether a voting rule can be represented as an i.i.d. maximum-likelihood estimator. They contain no complexity classification, algorithm, hardness result, approximation guarantee, or parameterized theorem. The introduction mentions computational voting work only as background. Under ChoCo’s gate, no choice of population scenario can repair that mismatch without changing what is being mirrored.

Theorem 1 is nevertheless a legitimate continuous-population construction, and I would not attack it by denying that. A population of document-ranking or recommendation sessions is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. For a distribution \(\mu\), the normalized log likelihood is indeed

\[ \sum_t \mu_t \log p_w(t) = \text{constant} + (\log 2)\sum_t \mu_t s(t,w), \]

so the maximum-likelihood winner is the scoring winner; the ranking version works analogously. This is a valid continuous formulation.

But it is a formulation of the theorem’s algebra, not a computational mirror of a computational result. The finite theorem already quantifies over arbitrary vote vectors, and dividing their log likelihood by the electorate size gives precisely the population expression. There is no computational problem in the paper whose complexity is being continued. The proposed “continuous MLE evaluation” is a new evaluation problem imposed on a structural existence theorem. The suggested follow-ups—robust MLE, inverse voting, minimal population perturbation, or learning the noise model—could certainly become worthwhile ChoCo problems, but they are new problems, not mirrors of Theorem 1. Their value cannot be credited to this paper’s result.

Theorem 3 has the same problem. Its \(V_1,V_2\) witness can be normalized into distributions \(\mu^1,\mu^2\), and the contradiction survives under an appropriate mixture. In fact, this is a perfectly natural population restatement of Lemma 1: additive log likelihood cannot represent a rule whose common outcome on two populations changes on their aggregate.

That observation establishes that Bucklin remains outside the i.i.d.-MLE class over continuous profiles. It does not establish a computational contribution. The theorem proves one fixed structural impossibility, not the complexity of deciding representability for a rule family. A stronger proposed problem—given a succinct voting rule, decide whether it admits an i.i.d. MLE representation, or compute the smallest violation—would require choices absent from the paper: an encoding of rules, a representation of admissible noise models, a finite or symbolic description of the type space, and a precise input-size measure. Those choices might support a new research programme, but the resulting problem would no longer be the continuous mirror of Theorem 3.

Thus the proponent has shown that this paper admits sensible continuous population semantics. That is not enough here. The continuous object is well posed, the high-multiplicity story is plausible, and neither identity dependence nor degeneration is a valid objection. The failure is narrower and more fundamental for ChoCo: the paper contributes no computational statement for continuization to preserve or transform. Any genuinely computational mirror must be supplied by adding a new problem around the paper, rather than extracted from one of its results.

If “worthwhile” is broadened to include statistical interpretation or structural characterization, the negative case is weak: Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 do have respectable continuous analogues. Under the programme’s stated computational gate, however, the correct verdict is that no qualifying continuous mirror exists in any scenario without changing the paper’s contribution.
