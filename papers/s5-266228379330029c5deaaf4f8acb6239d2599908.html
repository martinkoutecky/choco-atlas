The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent gives no computational anchor, so there is no proposed mirror to defeat. That is the decisive problem: the paper’s numbered results are axiomatic characterizations, equivalences, and constructions. None classifies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. Theorems 2 and 3, for example, translate between cost functions and CFMM potentials; they do not ask for an algorithm on a society. Continuous quantities in the paper are asset reserves, trades, and probabilities—not a continuous population.

The strongest plausible population model would group traders by their complete type: beliefs, valuations, budgets, and perhaps arrival behavior. One could then ask how a mass distribution of such traders affects equilibrium trades, prices, or the cost of moving the market to a target state. That could be a sensible adjacent research project. But the paper specifies no population objective or computational question of that kind. Its participants arrive sequentially, and a distribution over types alone does not specify their order or state-dependent behavior. Supplying those assumptions would create a new model, rather than mirror one of the paper’s results.

The most theorem-adjacent alternative—computing the conversion between a cost function and a CFMM potential—does not rescue the mirror. The paper gives those transformations directly, and trader masses do not enter them. A complexity study of their implementation would concern function representations, not continuization of the population.

So the negative case is strong for the paper as written: it offers no named computational result for ChoCo to mirror. The broader claim that no continuous-population computational study of markets could ever be worthwhile is weaker; the paper does not establish that. But such a study would need a new question and model, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
