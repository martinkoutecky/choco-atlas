The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying affirmative case from this paper under your anchor requirement. Its named results are axiomatic equivalences: for example, Theorem 3.1 proves that on a UCNR domain, strategy-proofness is equivalent to UCSP plus elementary monotonicity; Theorems 3.2, 4.1, and 5.1 give related equivalences. None asserts a computational complexity result, and the paper does not analyze the complexity of checking strategy-proofness. The introduction’s claim that UCSP offers a simpler way to check it is motivation, not a named algorithmic result.

The nearest imaginable mirror would take a large society with many people sharing complete preference rankings, represent it by a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, and map \(\mu\) to a lottery over alternatives. But a unilateral report by an infinitesimal agent leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, making the paper’s incentive question vacuous unless the model adds positive-mass deviations or coalitions. Those would change the problem being studied. More fundamentally, the paper’s contribution is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational question about a continuously represented population.

So there is no continuous problem I can honestly present as an anchored mirror of this paper’s results. Its lottery-valued outcomes are continuous, but that is outcome-space continuity; the society in the paper remains finite.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorems 3.1, 3.2, 4.1, and 5.1 are axiomatic equivalences between strategy-proofness, UCSP, and monotonicity conditions. The paper’s claim that UCSP can make SP easier to check, and its reference to a declining ratio of constraints, do not give an algorithm or a complexity bound. To turn that motivation into a computational problem, one would first have to choose an encoding for the RSCF and the domain. The paper does not supply one.

The natural population model makes the incentive question collapse. Represent an anonymous RSCF as a map from a distribution \(\mu\) over preference types to a lottery. A single agent’s misreport changes no mass in an atomless population, so the lottery stays the same and every unilateral incentive condition holds automatically. Unanimity can still constrain the map, but studying those constraints would be an axiomatic continuum problem—the kind ChoCo excludes.

The obvious repairs change the question. Keep finite individual deviations of size \(1/n\), and the proposed problem is a high-multiplicity version of checking incentives; it may be worth studying, but the paper gives no computational result or representation to anchor it. Replace individual deviations with positive-mass deviations, and the agents become blocs or coalitions. A differential version would likewise impose new incentive conditions. None is the paper’s equivalence theorem. Adding identity or role to each type can preserve the paper’s unrestricted dependence on named agents, but then the type space may have support as large as the population, defeating the intended aggregation.

The lotteries in the paper are already continuous outcomes; they do not make the society continuous. And the paper’s discussion of continuous alternatives concerns axiomatic results, not a computational treatment of a population distribution.

So I see no worthwhile anchored continuous mirror here. The limit of that conclusion is worth stating plainly: an independently motivated computational study of anonymous, high-multiplicity mechanism verification could be worthwhile. That possibility does not rescue this paper as a source of a continuous computational result.
