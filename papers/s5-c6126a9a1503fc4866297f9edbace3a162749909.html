The continuous mirror question

Given finite resident types \(R\) and hospital types \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_r\) and \(\nu_h\), strict orders over types, per-hospital capacities \(c_h\), and an expansion budget \(\beta\), let \(q_h=\nu_hc_h\). Is there \(\delta_h\ge0\) with \(\sum_h\delta_h\le\beta\) such that canonical resident-proposing deferred acceptance on type mass, with hospital-type capacities \(q_h+\delta_h\) and only boundary types split, returns a stable mass matching \(x\) satisfying \(\sum_{r,h}\operatorname{rank}_r(h)x_{rh}\le\kappa\)?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity typed many-to-one market where \(x_{rh}\) transports resident mass \(\mu_r\) to hospital-type capacity \(q_h+\delta_h\), \(\delta\) is the expansion decision, and the objective is \(\operatorname{AvgRank}_\infty(x)=\sum_{r,h}\operatorname{rank}_r(h)x_{rh}\), with stability defined by resident-proposing mass deferred acceptance and hospital priority cutoffs.

The objection that survived

Strict rankings of named copies do not automatically collapse to type orders: fractional cutoffs replace identity-level tie-breaking, and the village reduction may disappear, so the proposed model is not a theorem-preserving two-way dictionary.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the average-rank capacity-expansion and capacity-reduction problems represented by Theorems 3.1 and 4.1. It leaves the partition-budget variants in Theorems \(5.2\)–\(5.5\), the incomplete-list cardinality variants, and the auxiliary tied stable-marriage result in Corollary 3.2 unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does mass deferred acceptance with capacity variables admit a polynomial-size LP, separation oracle, or equivalent algorithm in \(\tau_R,\tau_H\) and the input bit length?
  • Can the village reduction be represented with finitely many repeated types and fractional expansion mass, or does divisibility destroy its hardness gap?
  • What rounding theorem maps rational mass solutions back to finite markets, and what additive error results after clearing denominators?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s two central capacity-variation problems. The paper itself is not continuous, but its objects are unusually well suited to continuization: residents are assigned whole hospitals, capacities are additive, stability is defined by preference comparisons, and the objective is an aggregate rank sum.

Consider a large annual residency or school-allocation market. There are \(N\) residents but only \(\tau_R\) resident types: a type records the complete preference order, acceptability information, and any other feature used by the matching rule. There are also \(\tau_H\) hospital types, each representing many interchangeable hospitals with the same priority order over resident types, the same baseline capacity \(c_h\), and the same expansion or reduction eligibility. A type is therefore a genuine complete type, not merely a preference label.

Let \(\mu_r\) be the mass of resident type \(r\), with \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\), and let \(\nu_h\) be the mass of hospitals of type \(h\). The aggregate baseline capacity of \(h\) is \(q_h=\nu_hc_h\). A matching is a transport plan \(x_{rh}\), where \(x_{rh}\) is the mass of residents of type \(r\) assigned to hospital type \(h\). Each infinitesimal resident still receives one integral hospital assignment; \(x\) is fractional only because it aggregates many interchangeable residents.

For a given capacity vector, stability is defined by resident-proposing deferred acceptance on mass: resident mass proposes down its preference lists, each hospital type retains its highest-priority proposed mass up to its aggregate capacity, and a type may be split only at a capacity cutoff. Equivalently, there is no positive mass of residents and hospital capacity that mutually prefer one another to their current assignments. The objective is the per-capita version of the paper’s rank objective,
\[ \operatorname{AvgRank}_\infty(x) = \sum_{r,h}\operatorname{rank}_r(h)x_{rh}. \]
If one wants an exact blow-up interpretation, the ranks can be normalized by the number of hospitals; multiplying the normalized objective by \(N\) recovers the paper’s unnormalized rank sum.

The natural regime is \(N\gg \tau_R+\tau_H\): for example, hundreds of thousands of applicants and many repeated preference cohorts, but only hundreds or thousands of distinct resident and hospital profiles. Extra capacity is also scaled: if the finite market has budget \(B\), the continuum instance has seat-mass budget \(\beta=B/N\). This is the necessary co-modification, not a change from population continuity to outcome continuity.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.1. It is proved in this paper, rather than merely cited. It states that \(\text{Min-Avgexp HR}\) is \(NP\)-complete. Its optimization version, \(\text{Min-Avgexp HR}^{\mathrm{opt}}\), cannot be approximated within a factor of
\[ \left(\frac{\bar n}{2}\right)^{(1-\varepsilon)/2} \]
for any \(\varepsilon>0\), unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), where \(\bar n\) is the number of residents. The result concerns choosing where to allocate a total expansion budget and then finding a stable matching of minimum average hospital rank. The paper proves it under the usual complete, strict-preference HR model; the reduction in fact uses very restrictive capacities, including capacities at most one. The proof encodes the hard tied stable-marriage problem of Corollary 3.2 into strict-preference HR through the paper’s “village” construction.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is:

\[ \textsc{Min-Avgexp HR}_{\infty} \]

An instance consists of finite resident and hospital type sets, rational masses \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), strict complete type-level preference orders, rational baseline capacities \(q_h\), an expansion budget \(\beta\), and a rational target \(\kappa\). The question is whether there exists a vector of extra capacity masses \(\delta_h\ge 0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_h\delta_h\le \beta \]
such that the resident-optimal stable mass matching under capacities \(q_h+\delta_h\) has
\[ \operatorname{AvgRank}_\infty(x)\le \kappa. \]
A solution is the expansion vector \(\delta\) together with the resulting stable mass matching \(x\).

This is recognizably the same problem: choose hospitals to expand, run stable matching, and optimize residents’ average hospital rank. The only difference is that indistinguishable hospitals and residents are represented by mass. The paper’s integer budget \(B\), integer matching, and integer rank sum become their normalized high-multiplicity counterparts.

I expect this version to be Class A, although proving that is itself a worthwhile result. For a fixed \(\delta\), mass deferred acceptance is finite and type-level. The optimization variables \(\delta_h\) and \(x_{rh}\) are linear-flow quantities, and the objective and budget are linear. A natural route is a parametric deferred-acceptance or configuration-LP formulation, with complexity measured in \(\tau_R,\tau_H\), and the input bit length rather than in \(N\). The paper’s \(NP\)-hardness may then dissolve because its village construction relies on allocating indivisible extra spots to particular gadgets; continuous seat mass can be divided among repeated type cohorts.

The strongest technical question is whether exact mass stability admits a polynomial-size separation or column-generation formulation. If it does, this is a clean example in which discrete capacity-expansion hardness is population-multiplicity hardness. If fractional type flows introduce a genuinely nonconvex blocking condition, the problem could instead become Class C; that boundary is precisely what the mirror should investigate.

A second, independent anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved in this paper. It states that \(\text{Min-Avgred HR}\), the capacity-reduction decision problem, is \(NP\)-complete. Its optimization version cannot be approximated within
\[ \left(\frac{\bar n}{2}\right)^{(1-\varepsilon)/2} \]
for any \(\varepsilon>0\), unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). The result holds even in the restricted construction where hospital capacities are all one. The proof again reduces from the hard tied stable-marriage problem, but now removes capacity rather than adding it.

Its continuous counterpart is:

\[ \textsc{Min-Avgred HR}_{\infty} \]

Given the same typed market, a rational removal budget \(\beta\), and target \(\kappa\), choose capacity-removal masses \(\rho_h\) satisfying
\[ 0\le \rho_h\le q_h,\qquad \sum_h\rho_h\ge \beta,\qquad \sum_h(q_h-\rho_h)\ge 1. \]
The question is whether the stable mass matching under residual capacities \(q_h-\rho_h\) has
\[ \operatorname{AvgRank}_\infty(x)\le\kappa. \]
A solution consists of \(\rho\) and the resulting stable mass matching \(x\).

I again expect Class A for the mass-DA formulation: the action is now a continuous capacity-removal vector, and the matching objective remains additive. The interesting issue is whether the paper’s reduction survives when capacity can be removed fractionally from repeated hospital types. If it does, this would be a Class B hardness-transfer result; if not, it would show that the apparent hardness is tied to indivisible capacity changes rather than to matching structure itself.

I would deliberately stop at these two anchors. Theorem 5.3 gives another possible mirror for partitioned expansion and maximum-cardinality matching, but adding it would make the case broader rather than stronger. The two selected theorems already cover both directions of the paper’s main contribution.

The weakest point is the hospital-side type assumption. In some real markets, priorities are highly individualized, so \(\tau_R\) and \(\tau_H\) may grow almost as fast as the number of agents. In that regime the mirror gives little compression. There is also a genuine semantic choice between exact positive-mass blocking and an ex-post stable mass plan; an arbitrary fractional matching must not be treated as stable merely because its marginals look feasible. Those are real vulnerabilities.

They do not, however, make the mirror artificial. Large school and residency markets do contain repeated preference and priority cohorts, and the proposed problem preserves the paper’s defining ingredients: two-sided preferences, capacities, stable matching, expansion or reduction budgets, and resident rank welfare. A finite instance with rational masses can be recovered by clearing denominators, while a large repeated-type market collapses back to the continuous instance. The central follow-up questions are whether the mass problem is polynomial-time solvable, whether the village reduction survives with repeated types, and how accurately its solutions round back to finite markets.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is that the proposed mass model is not actually a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s stable-matching problem. It replaces the identity-level information on which both the matching and the capacity intervention operate.

In the paper, a hospital has a strict order over named residents and a resident has a strict order over named hospitals. The quantity being optimized is \(\sum_{(i,j)\in M}\operatorname{rank}_i(j)\), where those identities matter. In the proposed model this becomes \(\sum_{r,h}\operatorname{rank}_r(h)x_{rh}\). That expression is meaningful only after replacing strict rankings of individual agents by rankings of types or blocks. Copies of a hospital type are then tied from the residents’ perspective, and copies of a resident type are tied from hospitals’ perspectives. The model therefore needs a new stability notion, a tie-breaking convention, or a cutoff rule for partially accepted types.

There is no harmless way around this. If ties are allowed and a type may be split fractionally, one obtains an anonymous, typed-priority matching market. That may be interesting, but it is not the strict HR object studied in the paper. If strictness is restored by specifying the order of all copies within each type, that order is additional identity-level input of size proportional to the finite population. The supposed compression disappears. Clearing denominators does not solve this: it creates copies, but does not specify the individual priority orders needed to recover the paper’s deferred-acceptance outcome.

This defeats the proposed reading of Theorem 3.1 in particular. Its reduction uses the “village” gadgets precisely to give distinct residents and hospitals different roles, ranks, capacities, and blocking behaviour. The useful object is not merely a population histogram. The budget allocates one extra spot to particular gadget hospitals, and the proof depends on exactly which \(v_{\ell,h}^s\) receives it. Merging those agents into types destroys the village. Keeping each role as a separate type leaves \(\tau\) growing with the whole constructed instance, so there is no high-multiplicity compression. The proposed mass problem may still be a new capacity-planning problem, but the theorem no longer anchors it.

The same objection is even sharper for Theorem 4.1. Its hard instances have \(c_j=1\), and the reduction removes individual spots from particular hospitals. A continuous variable \(\rho_h\in(0,1)\) is not a softened version of removing a spot from that hospital; it gives the hospital fractional capacity that the original model forbids. Enforcing \(\rho_h\in\{0,1\}\) restores the discrete intervention and removes the relevant continuity. Replicating hospitals and removing capacity from a type instead changes the problem to one about divisible capacity pools, again losing the identity-specific village structure.

The strongest repair is to keep a finite set of named hospitals, make only the residents nonatomic, and give hospitals priorities over resident types. That is coherent, but it necessarily introduces priority blocks or a continuous priority coordinate. It is best described as a new continuum matching model, not as the high-multiplicity relaxation of the strict capacity-variation problems proved in this paper. The alternative repair—replicating both residents and hospitals—has the same problem: either the replicas are genuinely interchangeable, in which case the strict individual model has been replaced, or they are not, in which case the type representation does not compress the instance.

This is a serious weakness in the positive case’s claim of a two-way dictionary and of “the same problem with mass.” It shows that neither Theorem 3.1 nor Theorem 4.1 automatically supplies a faithful continuous mirror. At most, they motivate a separate study of fractional-capacity intervention in anonymous matching markets.

The negative case is nevertheless not decisive. Large school-choice and residency markets genuinely contain repeated resident cohorts, and a typed mass version with hospital priority blocks is mathematically natural. Its stability notion can be made canonical, and its capacity budget can be scaled sensibly. Thus I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. What can be defended is narrower: the proponent has not shown that their two anchors are continuizations of the paper’s strict identity-level problems; they are promising new continuum matching questions whose relationship to the paper remains to be justified.
