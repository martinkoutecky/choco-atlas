The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of shareholder types \(T\), each specifying ownership density \(q_t\) and value \(v_t\), and a rational population measure \(\mu\) with \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t=1\), can one construct in time polynomial in the input size a finite-description anonymous direct mechanism? For every aggregate report measure, the mechanism assigns each reported type an ownership density \(z_t\) and scaled payment \(p_t\), conserves the asset and money, is dominant-strategy truthful and ex-post individually rational relative to initial ownership, and achieves expected aggregate welfare at least \(1-\frac{1}{e}\) times the full-information optimum.

The model it lives in

A continuum of shareholders with complete types \(t=(q_t,v_t)\) carrying mass \(\mu_t\); an anonymous direct mechanism assigns per-capita ownership densities \(z_t\) and scaled transfers \(p_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_tz_t=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_tp_t=0\). The objective is expected aggregate welfare relative to the full-information allocation, subject to dominant-strategy truthfulness and ex-post individual rationality.

The objection that survived

The paper specifies priors over privately reported values, while the proposed mirror treats realized values as population types; the direct-mechanism and DSIC semantics for that continuum still need precise definition.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the partnership-dissolving guarantee in Corollary 5.2; it leaves the bilateral-trade results and the reductions for two-player divisible-good markets aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite-owner mechanism be extended to an anonymous continuum direct mechanism that preserves dominant-strategy truthfulness and the \(1-\frac{1}{e}\) welfare guarantee?
  • How should the mechanism’s input and truthfulness constraints relate the common value priors to the population measure of realized reports?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best foothold is partnership dissolving, where the paper already has many owners and a divisible asset. Corollary 5.2, proved in this paper, says that there is a dominant-strategy incentive-compatible, individually rational, budget-balanced mechanism for partnership dissolving that achieves a \(1-\frac{1}{e}\) approximation to optimal efficiency. The result applies to \(n\) players who initially own shares of one asset and have linear valuations. It is the strongest anchor for a population mirror because the underlying problem already concerns a group of shareholders.

A plausible regime is a large cooperative or employee-shareholder buyout: many small owners fall into a modest number of share and valuation classes. For a precise mirror, let the input list finitely many classes \(t\), their population masses \(\mu_t\), and their private-value distributions \(F_t\), all with rational finite support. Let \(q_t\) be the class’s normalized ownership density, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t=1\). In a finite population of \(N\) owners, each member of class \(t\) initially holds \(q_t/N\) of the asset. The population measure is over complete types: share class and realized value.

Call the proposed problem Mass Partnership Dissolving\(_\infty\). Given that input, construct a finite description of a symmetric direct mechanism. On reported values it assigns each owner a post-trade ownership density \(z_i\) and a scaled payment \(p_i\), conserving the asset and money: \(\int z_i\,d\nu=1\) and \(\int p_i\,d\nu=0\), where \(\nu\) is the population measure over types. Require dominant-strategy truthfulness and ex-post individual rationality relative to initial ownership; an owner of value \(v_i\) has scaled utility \(v_i z_i-p_i\), with initial utility \(v_iq_t\). The objective is expected aggregate welfare, compared with the full-information allocation. The question is whether a polynomial-time construction in the number of types and input bit length can guarantee welfare at least \(1-\frac{1}{e}\) times optimum.

I would expect the fixed-support version to be tractable: the paper’s posted-price guarantee and its reduction to partnership dissolving suggest a type-level construction. The questions that follow are whether the finite-population mechanisms converge to this one, whether the best achievable ratio changes in the limit, and how the answer depends on the number of distinct types. This mirror covers the partnership-dissolving result only; it does not continuize the paper’s bilateral-trade value distributions as a substitute for the population.

The weak point is substantial: Corollary 5.2 gives an approximation guarantee, not a running-time bound or a complexity classification. Its mechanism also sells individual owners’ shares using finite-player auctions; when an owner becomes infinitesimal, the effect of a unilateral report and the distribution of the highest other value can change. A proof of the mass version would need to handle that limit directly.

So this is a credible positive *research question*, but there is a formal anchor limitation: the paper contains no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result. If the required anchors must assert one of those classifications, Corollary 5.2 does not qualify, and the paper supplies no qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Corollary 5.2 gives a welfare guarantee for a mechanism; Theorems 4.1 and 5.1 do the same for bilateral trade and its reduction to partnership dissolving. None classifies a problem by running time or gives an algorithmic complexity bound. The distributional inputs are also arbitrary, with no finite encoding or bit length. Asking for a polynomial-time construction from rational finite-support inputs is sensible, but it adds a computational problem the paper never posed.

Corollary 5.2 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, and a large employee-shareholder buyout is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The obstacle is that the proposed version changes what the mechanism knows and acts on. The paper models each owner’s value as private information and sells that owner’s share using a second-price auction against the highest-valued other owner. The proposed input instead gives value distributions for classes, then asks for a continuum-agent direct mechanism. If class members share only an ownership class and a prior, their realized values still differ; if realized values define the types, their population masses are private outcomes rather than the distributional input. Resolving that distinction, and defining the strategy and allocation model, is substantial new mechanism-design work. It does not follow from the corollary’s guarantee.

There is also a limit issue: as the number of owners grows, the highest value approaches the top of its support, while the efficient finite-population allocation concentrates the asset with a highest-valued owner. In an atomless continuum that owner has zero mass. A formulation using allocation densities may therefore have only a supremum for its full-information benchmark, requiring another co-modification. Finite-support values can avoid this particular issue, so it does not rule out the proposed mirror.

That leaves a real weakness in the negative case: the shareholder scenario is credible, and the proposed continuum mechanism-design question could be worthwhile in its own right. I would argue against counting it as a mirror of this paper’s computational results, because there are none. I cannot honestly argue that no useful continuous question can be built from the partnership-dissolving result.
