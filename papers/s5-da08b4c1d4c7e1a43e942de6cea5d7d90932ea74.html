I can’t make a qualifying positive case under your anchor rule: the paper has no named computational result about an algorithm, complexity class, or computing a matching or manipulation. Its numbered propositions are strategic impossibility results.

For example, Proposition 1, proved in this paper, says that for any marriage market with \(|M||W|\ge 2\) and externalities, no core mechanism is strategy-proof. Proposition 3, also proved here, says that when both sides have at least two members, some coalition can change its reports so that every member prefers a matching in the new core to every matching in the original core. These are meaningful formal results, but neither establishes a computational property of a problem. They therefore cannot serve as the required anchors.

The nearest possible mirror would be a continuum matching market where types have preferences over aggregate matching outcomes, mass is the share of each type, and a matching is a measure describing how that mass is paired. One could ask whether a core-selecting mechanism is strategy-proof against reports by positive-mass groups. But that changes the strategic question: an individual in an atomless population has zero mass, while Proposition 3’s construction even uses a singleton manipulating coalition. The paper also gives rankings over all matchings of a finite society, which do not directly supply a fixed type space as the population grows. So this is an adjacent research question, not a defensible continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. No expected complexity class follows from the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–4 concern axiomatic properties of mechanisms and strategic manipulation: they establish when core or stable mechanisms can be strategy-proof, and when coalitions can manipulate them. The paper gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or computational problem whose complexity it determines. Its discussion of algorithms in the surrounding literature does not change that. Under ChoCo’s stated remit, there is no qualifying anchor here.

The proponent’s nearest candidates, Propositions 1 and 3, do not survive that test. A continuous version of Proposition 1 could ask whether a core-selecting mechanism is strategy-proof when agents report preferences over aggregate matching outcomes. A continuous version of Proposition 3 could ask whether a positive-mass coalition can manipulate such a mechanism. Both could be interesting incentive-theory questions, but neither asks the computational question this paper answers: the propositions make no claims about how hard it is to find a core matching, select among core matchings, or find a profitable report.

There is also a real modeling change beneath those proposed mirrors. In an atomless society, a single agent has zero mass, so individual strategy-proofness can become vacuous for mechanisms whose outcome depends on aggregate reports. Replacing a singleton manipulator with a positive-mass bloc makes manipulation meaningful, but changes the strategic object: a bloc of agents sharing a type coordinates its report. Proposition 3 even exhibits a singleton coalition. To define a continuum matching with externalities, one could represent pairings as flows between types and make preferences depend on the resulting aggregate flow. That is a plausible new model, but aggregate flows generally discard distinctions between complete matchings that the paper’s preferences can rank differently. Restricting preferences so flows suffice may rescue the model, at the cost of changing the paper’s unrestricted externalities.

So the strongest defensible negative verdict is that this paper offers no mirror for ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme. That is not proof that no worthwhile continuous research could grow from it: the positive-mass manipulation question could be worth pursuing as incentive theory. But that possibility does not turn these propositions into computational anchors, and it does not establish a continuous computational mirror of this paper.
