The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive lead is a continuous version of the *extremal seeding* question mentioned in Future Work: on a fixed torus \(\mathbb{T}_L^d\), put unit mass at each site and let \(x_v\in[0,1]\) be the initially black mass at site \(v\). At each round, make all the mass at \(v\) black exactly when the total black mass at its neighboring sites is at least \(r\). Ask for a minimum-mass vector \(x\) that eventually makes every site black. Here the types are sites, mass is the fraction of a large cohort at each site, and the objective is \(\min\sum_v x_v\). This co-modifies the dynamics to accommodate fractional initial bundles, and the paper itself points to minimum initial seeding as a natural extremal question.

But it has no qualifying named anchor in this paper. The first main theorem (Theorem 1 in the text’s ordering) says that for \(p\ll L^{-d/2^{r-1}}\), two-way \(r\)-BP on \(\mathbb{T}_L^d\) becomes fully white a.a.s. The other displayed theorems concern asymptotic survival or takeover thresholds, including results cited from or derived from prior work. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. So I cannot honestly present the seeding question as a mirror of a named result here, or assign it a tractability class based on this paper.

The high-multiplicity story is also weak for the paper’s actual results. Sites have distinct neighborhood positions, so treating each site as a type gives \(L^d\) types and no large multiplicity per type. Merging sites by symmetry loses the spatial arrangements of rare black clusters on which the threshold proofs rely. The proposed seeding problem may be a sensible follow-up to the paper’s Future Work, but it does not supply the required computational anchor for an affirmative continuous mirror of this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s main results are probabilistic threshold statements: as the torus grows, they identify ranges of the initial black probability \(p\) in which the process has different outcomes a.a.s. None gives the complexity of a computational problem. The proponent’s candidate anchor—the first phase-transition theorem—therefore cannot support a complexity mirror. The proponent effectively concedes this; making the theorem an anchor would require inventing a new computational question.

The best repair is the paper’s future-work question about minimizing the number of initially black nodes needed for takeover. One can formulate a fractional version by assigning each site \(v\) an initial black mass \(x_v\in[0,1]\) and minimizing \(\sum_v x_v\). A cohort of interchangeable agents at each site is a possible interpretation. But this is a new fractional seeding problem, not a continuous version of a computational result established in the paper. The paper offers no algorithmic or complexity result for the discrete seeding question to mirror.

That formulation also leaves the paper’s central structure at the lattice sites. If agents at each site sample their initial colors independently, large cohorts average toward mass \(p\) at every site; if each cohort shares one random color, the original site-level process remains, with one binary state per site. If instead neighbor masses drive the update, that defines a new mass-threshold dynamic. In each case, retaining the geometry and correlations requires preserving which sites are adjacent. Merging sites by their local symmetry loses that information; keeping them site-indexed leaves \(L^d\) spatial types and does not expose the kind of population-compression opportunity that motivates ChoCo.

A continuous spatial model could still be interesting, and fractional seeding could merit a separate study. Those possibilities do not establish a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper’s results. The negative case is strongest on that narrower point: the paper supplies no computational anchor, and its closest candidate is an adjacent new problem whose continuum population interpretation does not simplify the spatial object. I cannot prove that no future site-mass formulation would be worth studying; that universal claim is the weak edge of the case.
