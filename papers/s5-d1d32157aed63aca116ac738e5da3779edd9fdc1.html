What the mirror covers

The attempted anchors, Proposition 1 and Corollary 1, are economic characterizations; no result in the paper supplies a computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is that this paper has a natural high-multiplicity regime: a very large organization can delegate a decision to many interchangeable experts, with only three relevant types after information acquisition—uninformed, signal \(h\), and signal \(\ell\). The number of experts can be large while the number of types stays fixed. That makes the population a plausible continuous object.

But the paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its lemmas, propositions, and corollary establish economic characterizations and comparative statics; none classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, FPT, or similar, and none presents a formulation that the paper computes with. The closest result is Proposition 1, proved here: for a fixed committee size, the cost of inducing information acquisition falls as the voting threshold approaches simple majority; when the size is even, the two nearest thresholds minimize that cost. Corollary 1, also proved here, says that when the principal is ex-ante indifferent between the alternatives, the optimal reward scheme induces majority rule. These are useful results, but they are not computational anchors.

The closest continuous problem I can formulate is: take an atomless unit-mass committee with types \(\{\emptyset,h,\ell\}\), and let the principal choose nonnegative state-and-decision-contingent rewards and a threshold on the masses of \(h\) and \(\ell\) reports. Ask for the reward scheme and threshold maximizing expected decision benefit minus transfers, subject to information acquisition being an equilibrium. The authors would recognize the economic model this is based on, and the type structure is a good high-multiplicity fit.

Its weakness is also fundamental. In an atomless committee, one agent’s signal cannot change the aggregate decision. With information cost \(c>0\), acquiring information therefore gives an agent no private benefit, even when a positive mass of informed agents would make the aggregate decision accurate. The central pivotality incentive in this paper disappears. Preserving it requires retaining positive-sized agents or changing the incentive mechanism, so this candidate does not cleanly mirror the paper’s main results as a continuous-population problem.

So the regime supplies a plausible starting point, but I cannot give the requested per-anchor continuous questions honestly: there are no qualifying computational anchors, and the nearest proposed mirror loses the paper’s key mechanism in the atomless limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that the paper supplies no computational anchor. Its numbered results characterize an economic model; they do not classify an algorithmic problem. Proposition 1, Corollary 1, and Proposition 3 derive properties of the optimal contract and committee size from binomial pivotality calculations. One could newly pose a problem about computing optimal contracts from encoded inputs, but that would add a computational question the paper neither states nor studies.

The committee is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, so the negative case should not rest on claiming that experts cannot recur in large numbers. The stronger problem is that the paper’s central incentive—the chance that one agent is pivotal—does not survive an atomless version. If a fraction of the continuum acquires information, its signal masses are deterministic conditional on the state. One agent’s effort or signal changes none of those masses, so it cannot change the collective choice or the reward they expect. With information cost \(c>0\), acquiring information is strictly worse. Thus the positive mass of informed agents needed for the paper’s majority-rule and committee-size results is not an equilibrium of the continuous model.

That defeats the best version of the proposed mirror, as well as continuous versions of the paper’s main results: the agency-cost ranking of thresholds, the majority-rule conclusion, and the inverse-U committee-size result all depend on finite pivotal events. Treating the mass of informed agents as a control variable would restore an optimization problem, but it would replace agents’ individual information-acquisition incentives with a planner’s choice of effort. Keeping positive-sized agents or finite committees preserves pivotality, but then the relevant decision unit remains finite; putting a continuum of such committees in the model adds a population of institutions rather than continuizing the agents in a committee. Rewards scaled to offset vanishing pivotality also change the fixed-stakes economics, and cannot give an atomless agent influence under any finite reward.

This is a strong objection to a continuous mirror of the paper’s contribution, not proof that no worthwhile adjacent research is possible. A heterogeneous extension might motivate new algorithmic questions, but it is future work, and it faces the same incentive collapse unless it retains finite pivotal units or changes the mechanism.
