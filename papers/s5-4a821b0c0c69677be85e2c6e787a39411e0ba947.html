The continuous mirror question

Given horizon \(H\), contexts \(\mathcal C\) of size \(g\), and supplier types \(t\) in a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of size \(\tau\), each with mass \(\lambda_t\), known per-unit cost \(k_t\), and unknown context-dependent mean value \(\mu_{t,c}\), choose each round a procurement vector \(x_t\) with \(0\le x_t\le\lambda_t\) and total mass at most \(q\). Observe one bounded reward from a sampled procured unit. Determine the achievable expected aggregate welfare regret against the per-context full-information linear allocation optimum, and whether a policy with per-round computation polynomial in \(\tau\), \(g\), and the input bit length achieves \(\widetilde O(q\sqrt{\tau gH})\).

The model it lives in

A continuous supplier population is a distribution \(\lambda\) over complete types \(t\), each specifying its per-unit cost and context-dependent reward distributions. The platform chooses feasible procurement masses each round and learns from one audited unit reward; its benchmark maximizes total surplus \(\sum_t x_t(\mu_{t,c}-k_t)\) subject to the mass and capacity constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 2's UCB welfare-regret guarantee under truthful costs. It does not establish continuous analogues of the ETC truthfulness results, the frozen-payment incentive guarantees, or Corollary 3's tradeoff lower bound.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is \(\widetilde O(q\sqrt{\tau gH})\) regret attainable with one audited reward per round under the stated mass constraints, and what lower bound matches it?
  • Under what additional cohort bidding assumptions, if any, does the paper's frozen-payment regret–incentive frontier extend to type-level mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case for a mirror is narrow: this paper has a named lower bound on a learning–incentive tradeoff, but no named result placing a problem in P, proving NP-hardness, or giving a parameterized complexity classification. Its algorithmic results concern regret rates. So it offers a plausible continuous-population online learning question, rather than a clean instance of ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme.

A concrete mirror is procurement from a large fleet of interchangeable demand-response units. Let the society be a distribution \(\lambda\) over a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of supplier types, where \(\lambda_t\) is the fraction of units of type \(t\). A type completely specifies its per-unit cost \(k_t\) and its bounded, context-dependent reward distributions \(P_{t,c}\), with means \(\mu_{t,c}\). Suppose the operator procures a fixed fraction \(q\) of the fleet per round, subject to \(0\le x_t\le\lambda_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le q\). It observes one audited reward from the selected supply each round. Full-information welfare in context \(c\) is the maximum of \(\sum_t x_t(\mu_{t,c}-k_t)\) over those feasible allocations.

The continuous cohort frozen-payment frontier asks: for horizon \(H\), after \(M\) bid-independent exploration observations, can a mechanism freeze its per-unit critical prices, continue adapting its allocations, and beat the paper’s regret–incentive tradeoff? Define \(R_H^{\mathrm{tot}}\) as worst-case expected aggregate welfare regret against the full-information allocation. Define \(\epsilon_H^{\mathrm{post}}\) as the worst-case expected aggregate utility gain available to a type cohort from changing its common ask—or withdrawing—after seeing the exploration history. The question is whether, for fixed \(q>0\), every mechanism in this class must satisfy
\[ \epsilon_H^{\mathrm{post}}+C R_H^{\mathrm{tot}} \ge c q\left(M+\frac{H-M}{\sqrt M}\right) \]
for universal constants \(c,C>0\), and whether matching upper bounds are possible.

My lead anchor is Corollary 3 (Frozen-payment regret–incentive tradeoff), proved in this paper from Theorem 6 and Lemma 5. It says that for any frozen-payment mechanism using \(M\) bid-independent payment-learning samples,
\[ \epsilon_T^{\mathrm{post}}+C R_T^{\mathrm{tot}} \ge c\left(M+\frac{T-M}{\sqrt M}\right). \]
For \(M\le T/2\), this is \(\Omega(M+T/\sqrt M)\), minimized at \(M\asymp T^{2/3}\) with value \(\Omega(T^{2/3})\). In particular, a mechanism with \(R_T^{\mathrm{tot}}=\widetilde O(T^{1/2})\) must have \(\epsilon_T^{\mathrm{post}}=\widetilde\Omega(T^{3/4})\). The paper proves this result; it is not cited from elsewhere. In the proposed mirror, the intuition is that estimating a frozen price from \(M\) observations still leaves error of order \(M^{-1/2}\), which accumulates over the remaining rounds when the cohort has positive supply.

I expect that statistical frontier to persist when the population is continuous, provided the feedback remains one audited reward per round. The anchor already isolates the one-context, one-producer case, so a continuous cohort version is a natural boundary case. This is a statistical sample-complexity prediction, though—not a Class A/B/C computational-hardness classification. One further question is how the rates depend on support size \(\tau\) and context count \(g\), and whether UCB-style allocation can retain regret scaling with \(\tau\).

The weakest point is that the paper’s strategic agent is an individual producer, while this mirror makes a cohort submit a common ask and introduces fractional procurement capacity. That is plausible for an aggregator representing many standardized units, but it is a real model change. If identical suppliers bid independently, one agent in an atomless cohort has vanishing influence, and the paper’s incentive lower bound need not survive. The paper’s Proposition 2 supplies a named UCB regret guarantee, but neither it nor Corollary 3 establishes a classical computational-complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 3 is the strongest anchor, but its frontier is a statistical and incentive lower bound: \(M\) observations leave price error of order \(M^{-1/2}\), which accumulates over later rounds. Proposition 2 and Theorems 1 and 3 likewise bound regret for UCB and explore-then-commit. None classifies the complexity of computing an allocation or mechanism, or gives an algorithm for a computationally difficult problem. A numbered theorem is not, by itself, a computational-complexity result.

The fleet mirror is plausible: let each of \(\tau\) supplier types have mass \(\lambda_t\), and procure a fraction \(q\) per round. For the paper’s allocation structure, full-information procurement is a linear optimization over those types; with explicit finite support, it reduces to sorting types by surplus and filling capacity. UCB adds estimates and comparisons over the same support. The mirror could yield regret rates in \(\tau\) and \(g\), and a cohort-level version of the frozen-price tradeoff. Those may be useful results, but they do not bring the paper’s problem within reach of the continuous-optimization technology ChoCo aims to explore. Adding network constraints or coupled sourcing decisions could create harder optimization, but that structure would come from an expanded procurement model, not from the results this paper establishes.

There is also a tension around incentives. If suppliers bid independently in an atomless population, one supplier’s report has zero mass, so its effect on aggregate allocation—and its individual deviation gain—vanishes. A common cohort bid preserves a nonzero effect, but makes the cohort or its aggregator the strategic player. That is a plausible procurement arrangement, though it changes the unit of agency. Theorem 6’s one-supplier statistical lower bound could still be lifted to such a cohort, with welfare and cohort utility scaled by its mass. That would be a valid continuous-population statement; its content would remain about information and incentives rather than computation.

So the strongest case against is a scope objection: the paper’s central results offer no computational-complexity anchor, and its natural finite-support mass version introduces little computational structure. The case is weaker on whether any sensible continuous model exists. A large fleet of standardized suppliers is convincing, so I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror can be formulated in any scenario; I would argue that this paper is a poor anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme.
