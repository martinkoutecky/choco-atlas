What the mirror covers

The proposed population extension covers award allocation across claim-size types; it leaves the paper's axiomatic characterization and other structural results without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind ChoCo requires as an anchor. Its main result, Theorem 2.2 (proved here), says that a division rule satisfies equal treatment of equals, bilateral consistency, composition down and composition up if and only if it is a member of the log-exponential family \(r^\theta\), including CEA and CEL. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a result about an algorithm, decision problem or computational complexity. So I cannot honestly turn it into a qualifying anchor.

The closest population mirror would be a tax or bankruptcy setting with many claimants sharing a small number of claim sizes. A type would be a claim amount \(c_t\), and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of claimants of that type. The decision would be a per-claimant award \(x_t\), with \(0\le x_t\le c_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=E\). This is a plausible continuous-population division problem, but it asks for an axiomatic extension of the paper’s framework, not a continuous computational version of one of its results. The paper’s real-valued claims and divisible awards are continuity in the quantities being divided; its population remains finite in each problem.

Thus there is a conceivable population-continuum research direction here, but no honest computational anchor on which to build the requested positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is decisive for ChoCo: Theorem 2.2 is not a computational result. It characterizes which division rules satisfy four axioms. The Lorenz ranking, implied continuity, homogeneity, and merging/splitting results are also structural or axiomatic; the paper gives no decision problem, algorithm, or complexity bound to mirror.

The proponent’s best population version is coherent. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of claimants with claim \(c_t\), and let \(E\) be the per-capita endowment. A mass version of \(r^\theta\) would choose \(\lambda\) so that
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\,\varphi_\theta^{-1}\!\bigl(\lambda\varphi_\theta(c_t)\bigr)=E, \]
then give each claimant of type \(t\) the corresponding award. Many creditors with repeated claim amounts, or taxpayers grouped by identical claims, make a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Classifying such mass rules under continuum versions of the paper’s axioms could be worthwhile mathematics—but it remains an axiomatic extension, outside the programme’s computational scope.

Trying to turn it into a computational mirror exposes the gap. For a given \(\theta\) and finite support, calculating awards reduces to finding a root of a one-dimensional monotone budget function. A harder task—choosing \(\theta\) to optimize progressivity or risk, for example—would require an objective the paper does not define. That would be a new problem inspired by its applications, not a computational question raised or answered by its results.

So the negative case is strong against a ChoCo mirror of this paper’s results, but weak as the universal claim that no continuous-population version could be worthwhile. The claims distribution is a plausible continuous society; what is missing is a computational anchor.
