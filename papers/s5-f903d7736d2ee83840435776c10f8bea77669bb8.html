What the mirror covers

No named result supplies a computational anchor; the proposed limit would not preserve the pivotal-voting results in Propositions 1–4.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result of the required kind: it gives no complexity classification or algorithmic result for a problem. Its numbered propositions concern economic equilibrium and comparative statics. For example, Proposition 3 characterizes when a suboptimal time-consistent equilibrium exists under the hazard-rate condition; it does not assert that finding one is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. So there is no qualifying anchor for a continuous-computational mirror.

A possible extension would ask for the time-consistent voting threshold in a large committee whose members’ signal types have specified masses. But the continuum limit removes the feature that drives the paper’s key strategic-voting results: an individual voter has zero influence on the outcome and is never pivotal. With iid signals, the population’s empirical signal distribution also converges to the known distribution. Preserving the pivotal incentives would require an additional finite-agent or scaling structure, which the paper does not study. Its continuous signal distribution is uncertainty about individual signals, not a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest candidate is Proposition 3: given the signal distribution and \((\alpha,n)\), find the suboptimal time-consistent majority rule. But this is not a computational result in the programme’s sense. The proposition already reduces the task to a single root: \(\mathbb E[\theta\mid\theta\ge x]-x=n(1-\alpha)x/\alpha\), followed by \(k=\lceil n(1-F(x))\rceil\). The paper gives equilibrium and comparative-static characterizations; it gives no complexity classification or algorithmic result for a computational problem.

The natural continuous mirror would treat signal values as voter types and their proportions as a society. In the paper’s i.i.d. model, the law of large numbers then fixes those proportions at the prior distribution, whose mean is zero. Since utilitarian project welfare is the average signal, it collapses to zero. More fundamentally, an individual has zero influence over the outcome in a nonatomic population, so the pivotal event that selects voters’ cutoffs disappears. The strategic responses behind Propositions 1–4 are no longer pinned down: a voter is indifferent between voting either way, and the planner’s welfare no longer selects the paper’s optimal or suboptimal equilibrium. Preserving finite pivotal incentives requires keeping a finite electorate; retaining aggregate uncertainty requires adding structure such as a common shock. Either move changes the central strategic problem rather than continuizing it.

Large juries, faculty panels, and advisory committees can be imagined, but the paper gives no regime where many repeated voter types coexist with the pivotal incentives its results require. One could study a new continuum-agent voting model, or compute the paper’s finite-\(n\) equilibria more efficiently. Neither supplies a worthwhile continuous-computational mirror of a named result here. That is a strong negative case, though not a proof that no author could devise a related model they would want to study.
