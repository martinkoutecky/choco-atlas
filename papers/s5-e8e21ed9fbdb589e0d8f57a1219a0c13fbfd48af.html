The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of item classes \(K\), buyer and seller types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and binary-rational package values, budgets, endowments, and reservation values, return a welfare-maximizing core outcome or determine that none exists. Each unit of mass receives or supplies one whole package, while a type's mass may be divided across packages; per-capita supplies scale with the population. Assignments and payments must respect type masses, item availability, individual budget and reservation constraints, and aggregate budget balance. Core stability requires that no measurable coalition can reallocate its available endowments with feasible payments so that every member strictly improves. What is the exact computational complexity in \(|K|\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A replicated combinatorial exchange with interchangeable buyer and seller cohorts, rational mass by complete type, indivisible packages for each agent, and fractional aggregate assignments and type-level package payments. Maximize aggregate gains from trade subject to budget, reservation, supply, budget-balance, and measurable-coalition core constraints.

The objection that survived

The paper's motivating examples and CATS experiments do not establish that complete package-value, budget, and endowment profiles recur at low support; the proposed cohort regime remains a plausible modeling assumption.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror targets Theorem 2's welfare-maximizing core computation. It leaves the empty-core example, coalition-reduction and decomposition methods, restricted cases, solver techniques, and finite-instance experiments unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the QSAT2 reduction force integral type-mass assignments, or does allowing fractional mass break its gadgets?
  • For fixed or small \(\tau\), can core stability be handled by an efficient formulation or separation algorithm?
  • What formal relationship connects outcomes and core existence in finite replicas to the mass model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Mass-Core Exchange\(_\infty\): the same welfare-maximizing, budget-constrained core problem, with large cohorts of interchangeable buyers and sellers represented by mass.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that computing a welfare-maximizing core outcome in a combinatorial exchange with budget-constrained buyers is \(\Sigma_p^2\)-hard. Here, an outcome must specify both an allocation and payments: the allocation maximizes gains from trade, buyer payments respect budgets, payments balance, and no coalition can profitably deviate. The paper proves this hardness by a reduction from QSAT2 in its appendix; this is the authors’ result, not a complexity result they cite from elsewhere.

For the mirror, take a finite set \(K\) of item classes and a finite support \(T\) of participant types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). Each buyer type \(t\) has a per-person package valuation \(v_t(S)\) and budget \(B_t\). Each seller type has a per-person endowment and a reservation value for each package it can supply. A type is complete: its members have the same side of the market, package values, budget, and endowment. Mass means the fraction of agents of that type. In an instance with \(N\) participants, a type occurring \(n_t\) times has mass \(n_t/N\); available supply scales with seller mass and per-person endowments.

Call the problem Mass-Core Exchange\(_\infty\). Given those type masses and market data, find an allocation and payment schedule that maximizes aggregate gains from trade and is core-stable. The allocation variables \(x_{t,S}\) and \(y_{t,Z}\) give the mass of buyer type \(t\) receiving package \(S\) and seller type \(t\) supplying package \(Z\). For each type, the assigned mass cannot exceed \(\mu_t\); aggregate demand for each item class cannot exceed aggregate supply. Each unit of mass receives or supplies a whole, integral package, while the mass assigned to any package may be fractional. Per-type, per-package payments obey buyers’ budgets, sellers’ reservation values, and aggregate budget balance. A solution is a finite rational description of these mass assignments and payments that maximizes
\[ \sum_{t\text{ buyer},S} x_{t,S}v_t(S) - \sum_{t\text{ seller},Z} y_{t,Z}r_t(Z). \]
It must also resist blocking by every measurable coalition: no subpopulation can re-trade its available endowments, respect individual budgets and budget balance, and make every member strictly better off. The computational question is the exact complexity of finding this outcome in \(|K|\), support size \(\tau\), and the bit length \(L\) of the rational input.

A plausible regime is a repeated regional quota exchange with many small fishing operations but relatively few standardized vessel, gear, endowment, financing, and package-bid profiles—for example, thousands of operations represented by tens or hundreds of recurring profiles. This is a scenario for the mirror, not a claim that every real fishery already has such a small support. It makes the scaling natural: the market grows by adding operators of existing profiles, while each operator still trades whole quota packages. It also preserves the paper’s central question: can the market achieve the greatest gains from trade while remaining stable under budget constraints? The type-level payments are part of this high-multiplicity regime, since interchangeable participants receive the same package quote.

My first expectation is Class B, hardness transfers, because the paper’s reduction appears to put its combinatorics in package choices, item structure, and participant types—not merely in repeated copies of identical agents. Those dimensions remain part of the continuous input. That is a hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 2.

The weakest point is precisely that caveat. The finite reduction does not prove hardness for Mass-Core Exchange\(_\infty\): splitting a type’s mass among packages may undo the assignment gadgets that encode QSAT2. Establishing that the continuous problem is the authors’ problem is relatively direct; establishing that their hardness survives is not. The useful next questions are whether a QSAT2 reduction can force the relevant masses to act integrally, whether fixed \(\tau\) admits an LP or separation algorithm for core stability, and how the mass problem relates to large finite replicas in optimum welfare and core existence. If fractionalization breaks the reduction, that would still be an informative result: it would locate exactly where continuization changes this problem’s complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is not that the paper lacks a computational result: Theorem 2 is an explicit \(\Sigma_2^p\)-hardness result, and the paper’s main examples are large markets. The question is whether that result has a worthwhile population mirror.

Its hardness construction appears to depend on participants being distinct. The QSAT2 gadgets assign buyers and sellers different roles through their valuations, budgets, and package interests. Because a type must match on all of those features, preserving the construction gives roughly one type per gadget participant, with mass about \(1/N\) each. That encodes the finite instance in a continuous input; it does not exploit high multiplicity. Merging those types to reduce support would erase distinctions the reduction uses.

A better proposal is the cohort exchange in the other analyst’s case. But its plausible types must share their *entire* package valuation, budget, and endowment—not just broad characteristics such as vessel class or business sector. In the paper’s motivating markets, package interests and budgets can distinguish participants sharply. And if many traders are genuinely interchangeable, a mass model lets each type be split among packages and lets measurable fractions deviate. That is a coherent atomless market, but its coalitions and aggregate resource accounting differ from those of the paper’s finite exchange. Holding scarce quota or slots fixed as the population grows also makes the per-capita endowment vanish; scaling supply instead describes a replicated market.

This makes the bridge from Theorem 2 to the proposed mirror unestablished. Yet it does not defeat the mirror itself: a replicated exchange with standardized cohorts and supply that scales with the population is plausible, and the paper’s authors might recognize its core computation as their problem. So the negative case is weak against the user’s universal claim. It can question whether this paper’s hardness result motivates the continuous problem, but it cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous-population mirror.
