The continuous mirror question

Given a finite horizon \(A\), a recency factor \(0<\rho<1\), and rational masses \(\mu_{a,\sigma}\) over age-ranking types \((a,\sigma)\in\{0,\ldots,A\}\times S_n\), compute the ranking obtained by sorting \(B_\rho(i)=\sum_{a=0}^{A}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\rho^a\mu_{a,\sigma}\sigma(i)\). When each age cohort follows one of two Mallows distributions with equal concentration and modes differing by one adjacent swap, determine when this ranking recovers the current mode, including for unequal cohort masses.

The model it lives in

Treat each cohort age and complete ranking as a type carrying mass μ. Compute each candidate's recency-weighted average rank from the supported types, then sort; for the one-swap Mallows drift, compare the recent and stale weighted cohort masses.

The objection that survived

The continuous version replaces the theorem's random sample stream with exact cohort masses, so its deterministic recovery condition does not preserve the theorem's finite-sample uncertainty or sample-complexity guarantee.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's one-swap drift case and Theorem 1's stationary Borda recovery. It leaves the high-probability sample bound in Theorem 5, parameter selection in Theorem 6, weighted tournament generalizations, and empirical results outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • What exact necessary and sufficient mass-balance condition characterizes recovery for a finite horizon with unequal cohort masses?
  • Can the recovery condition be extended to multiple drifts or changing concentration parameters?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s recency-weighted Borda result. It has a natural high-multiplicity setting: a large platform collects full rankings of the same shortlist in hourly or daily cohorts. Millions of people may contribute, while many share the same ranking and cohort. A type is \((a,\sigma)\), where \(a\) is the cohort’s age in update periods and \(\sigma\in S_n\) is the person’s complete ranking. Its mass \(\mu_{a,\sigma}\) is the fraction of the population with that type. Age belongs in the type because it changes how much a ranking counts. This keeps the population continuous while leaving time discrete.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in the paper. It considers two Mallows distributions with the same concentration parameter whose modal rankings differ by one adjacent swap. With \(t=0\) the most recent ranking, the recent rankings \(t<m\) come from the current mode \(\pi_0=\pi\tau\), while older rankings \(t\ge m\) come from \(\pi\). The theorem states that uBorda returns \(\pi_0\) in expectation when \(m>\log_\rho(0.5)\).

Its continuous counterpart is Continuous Recency-Weighted Population Rank Aggregation. An instance gives \(n\), \(0<\rho<1\), a finite age horizon \(A\), and binary-rational masses \(\mu_{a,\sigma}\ge 0\) over \(\{0,\ldots,A\}\times S_n\), summing to one. Define

\[ B_\rho(i)=\sum_{a=0}^{A}\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\rho^a\mu_{a,\sigma}\sigma(i). \]

The solution is the ranking \(\hat\pi\) that minimizes \(\sum_i B_\rho(i)\hat\pi(i)\), equivalently sorting candidates by increasing weighted average rank. Under the theorem’s Mallows promise, the age cohorts with \(a<m\) have current mode \(\pi_0\), and those with \(a\ge m\) have the old mode \(\pi\). If \(q_a=\sum_\sigma\mu_{a,\sigma}\), the relevant mass balance for the swapped pair is

\[ \sum_{a=0}^{m-1}\rho^a q_a > \sum_{a=m}^{A}\rho^a q_a. \]

With equal cohort masses, this becomes the theorem’s threshold as \(A\) grows. The continuous algorithm computes the scores from the \(\tau\) supported types and sorts them, in \(O(n\tau+n\log n)\) arithmetic operations. I expect this mirror to be Class A.

There is a small proof issue to fix before relying on Theorem 3: the comparison printed as Equation (7) in its proof has the inequality reversed. The stated threshold follows from the recent mass outweighing the stale mass, as the score calculation requires. That direct mass-balance statement is the safe form of the continuous result.

A second, stationary anchor is Theorem 1, attributed to Fligner and Verducci (1988), with a proof sketch in this paper: for i.i.d. rankings from \(\mathrm{MM}(\pi,\theta)\), Borda is a consistent estimator of \(\pi\). Its mirror, Continuous Mallows–Borda Center, takes a rational-mass society \(\mu\) over \(S_n\), computes \(b_\mu(i)=\sum_\sigma\mu_\sigma\sigma(i)\), and returns the ranking minimizing \(\sum_i b_\mu(i)\hat\pi(i)\). When \(\mu\) is exactly a Mallows distribution with positive concentration, the expected-rank ordering recovers its mode. This is also Class A, with the same score-and-sort computation. It gives a stationary baseline for the recency-weighted problem, rather than another version of the drift claim.

These two mirrors preserve what the paper actually computes: aggregate complete rankings into one ranking using average positions, with recency weights in the lead problem. A plausible regime is a feedback platform with many responses per collection window, where the number of people greatly exceeds the number of supported \((\text{age},\text{ranking})\) types. Neither mirror makes outcomes or time continuous; only the population is represented by masses. The age tag is a discrete part of each type.

The main limitation is that the paper’s guarantees concern a stream of sampled rankings, while these mirrors take the population’s mass distribution as input. With exact masses, sampling uncertainty disappears: Theorem 3’s expected-score comparison becomes an exact population-score comparison. Recovering finite-sample, high-probability guarantees would require a separate sampling model. Also, an exact Mallows distribution has support over all \(n!\) rankings, so an explicit mass-list input can have enormous \(\tau\); the efficient regime needs compact or empirically limited support. This makes the recency-weighted mirror plausible for cohort-based high-multiplicity data, but not a claim that every Mallows stream becomes efficient just by writing it as a continuum.

The scope here is deliberately limited to Theorems 1 and 3: stationary Borda recovery and its recency-weighted, one-swap drift case. The paper’s introduction cites Kemeny NP-hardness from Dwork et al. (2001), but it has no numbered theorem establishing a worst-case complexity classification, so I do not use that citation as an anchor. The next useful questions are whether the mass-balance guarantee extends to unequal cohort masses, multiple drifts, or changing concentration, and how finite-population sampling affects recovery.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central problem is that the paper’s theorems are statistical, not complexity results. Theorem 1 is a consistency claim; Theorems 3 and 5 give recovery and sample bounds under an evolving Mallows model; Theorem 6 chooses a forgetting parameter. The paper gives update costs for its algorithms, but it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. A continuous analogue can be posed, but that is a weaker fit to ChoCo’s aim of mapping computational complexity and algorithms.

For Theorem 3, the age-and-ranking population is a plausible mirror: a large platform could have many people in each cohort with the same ranking. But with exact cohort masses, the theorem’s sampling question disappears. For the promised one-swap drift, recovery reduces to comparing the recent and stale weighted masses; for arbitrary masses, the task is to compute weighted average ranks and sort. This is a valid deterministic calculation, but it does not retain the theorem’s sample-complexity result. To retain that result, one must restore finite random samples and their sizes, which puts the problem back in the statistical stream setting rather than making population size the computational object. The proposed mirror therefore has a dilemma: retain a mass-balance calculation, or retain the finite-sample uncertainty that made Theorem 3 interesting.

The same applies to Theorem 1. With the exact Mallows distribution as input, sorting expected ranks is a direct computation, and consistency as the sample grows is no longer the question. With finite samples, it is the original estimation problem again. A better encoding does not obviously rescue a computational question: an explicit Mallows distribution has support on all \(n!\) rankings, while a compact Mallows description includes the mode the estimator is meant to recover. This is a limitation of the proposed task, not a claim that a simple Class A result could never be worthwhile.

The strongest alternative is to mirror the paper’s weighted tournament rules, perhaps asking for a mass-weighted Kemeny ranking. But the paper already defines those rules for arbitrary positive real voter weights. Given masses \(\mu_{a,\sigma}\), one can set each ranking’s weight to \(\mu_{a,\sigma}\), or to \(\rho^a\mu_{a,\sigma}\) for recency weighting. The resulting frequency and margin matrices are exactly the paper’s weighted matrices. This is not an objection that high-multiplicity work has already been done, nor an objection based on what the complexity answer would be; it is that this paper’s own weighted formulation already accommodates the proposed inputs algebraically. The mass notation adds a population interpretation without yielding a distinct computational problem.

I would not claim that no convincing high-multiplicity scenario exists. The cohort example is credible. The negative case is weaker if ChoCo counts any clean continuous reformulation and easy algorithm as worthwhile. But against these particular anchors, the case is that continuization either strips away the statistical guarantee or restates computations already supplied by weighted Borda and weighted tournament rules.
