The continuous mirror question

Given a unit-mass, atomless society of delegates and budget \(B\), choose measurable efforts \(x(t)\in[0,1/2]\) satisfying \(\int x(t)^\beta\,d\mu(t)\le B\), with each delegate’s voting power proportional to effort. What is the maximum limiting probability of electing the correct outcome when individual voting errors are independent and no delegate retains positive voting weight?

The model it lives in

Let \(\mu\) be an atomless distribution over delegate types, with the single-type case \(c(x)=x^\beta\) matching Theorem 8. For effort \(x(t)\), delegation is weighted by \(x(t)\); when \(\int x\,d\mu>0\), the limiting correct-vote share is \(\frac12+\frac{\int x(t)^2\,d\mu(t)}{\int x(t)\,d\mu(t)}\). Thus the limiting reliability is \(1\) for any positive effort mass and \(1/2\) when all effort is zero.

The objection that survived

In a genuine atomless limit, independent voting noise vanishes, so the finite-\(N\) sampling fluctuations and Theorem 8’s one-versus-three threshold do not transfer; the proponent’s normal approximation retains those fluctuations rather than deriving the atomless objective.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the budgeted reliability optimization for delegates represented by a mass distribution, including the objective underlying Theorem 8. It does not preserve that theorem’s finite-committee threshold or cover the reward-scheme equilibrium results in Sections 3 and 4.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. The paper proves no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar, so it has no anchor that qualifies under that strict complexity criterion. It does, however, contain named results about the budgeted optimization problem. The strongest one to build on is Theorem 8, proved here in Section 5.4 and Appendix A.10.

Theorem 8 considers the symmetric optimization benchmark with convex costs \(c(x)=x^\beta\), where \(\beta>1\), and compares a single DRep with three equal-effort DReps. With \(\beta^\star=\ln(3)/(\ln(3)-\ln(2))\approx2.7095\), it proves: if \(\beta<\beta^\star\), one DRep always gives a higher success probability than three, regardless of budget \(B\); if \(\beta>\beta^\star\), three equal-effort DReps give a higher success probability than one exactly when
\[ B\le B^\star=3^{3/2}\left(\frac{3^{1-1/\beta}-2}{4}\right)^{\beta/2}. \]
The theorem does not classify the boundary case \(\beta=\beta^\star\). This is an exact optimization result, but it is not a complexity result.

My lead candidate is Mass-Committee Reliability Optimization. Its society is a pool of eligible DReps with one type \(t\), of mass \(\mu_t=1\). The type completely specifies the delegate’s cost and voting behavior: effort \(x\in[0,1/2]\) costs \(c(x)=x^\beta\), and produces probability \(1/2+x\) of voting for the correct outcome. There are \(N\) effective delegate slots. Choose an active mass \(q\in[0,1]\) and a common effort \(x\) for that mass, subject to \(Nq x^\beta\le B\). Thus \(Nq\) is the committee’s effective size, and each active delegate’s normalized voting weight is equal. The input is \(N,B,\beta\), and a requested additive accuracy \(\varepsilon\); a solution is a feasible pair \((q,x)\) whose reliability is within \(\varepsilon\) of the optimum.

To retain the paper’s uncertain voting outcome in a population-mass model, define reliability by the normal-limit approximation to the finite weighted vote:
\[ R_N(q,x)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{2\sqrt{Nq}\,x}{\sqrt{1-4x^2}}\right), \]
where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Set \(R_N(0,x)=1/2\), and \(R_N(q,1/2)=1\). The binary outcome stays binary; the population of delegates becomes the continuous object. The scale \(N\) preserves finite-vote uncertainty—the unscaled atomless limit would make the success probability collapse to a deterministic outcome.

A plausible regime is a large governance programme with many eligible delegates in a few standardized expertise and cost bands, so \(N\) is large relative to the number of types. The single-type restriction is the sharpest match to Theorem 8’s homogeneous setting. The optimization question is the paper’s: how much of the delegate pool should exert effort, and at what effort level, to maximize the chance of a correct weighted vote under a budget? I expect this restricted mirror to be Class A for additive approximation: the budget binds for any fixed \(q\), leaving a one-dimensional optimization over \(q\) with an explicit objective. That is a prediction about the proposed mirror, not a result proved in this paper. It covers Section 5’s optimization benchmark; it does not mirror the reward-scheme equilibrium results in Sections 3 and 4.

The weakest point is also substantial: Theorem 8’s headline comparison is between committees of sizes one and three, while the normal-limit objective is credible when the active committee is large. The mirror therefore preserves the optimization problem’s ingredients and direction of study, but it does not preserve that theorem’s specific finite-committee threshold. And in the paper, the strategic agents are DReps; the much larger stakeholder population is represented only through delegation weights. A referee could reasonably say that a large, homogeneous pool of eligible DReps is possible but not the natural population in the paper’s setting.

The next questions are whether the mass optimum approximates the exact finite-\(N\) binomial objective with useful additive guarantees; whether heterogeneous delegate types with support size \(\tau\) remain tractable; and whether the paper’s threshold-reward equilibria have a meaningful type-mass analogue. There is no hardness anchor here, so the usual question of discrete hardness dissolving in the continuum does not arise from this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 8 is a named optimization result, but it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. It gives a closed-form comparison between one and three equal-effort delegates in a symmetric benchmark. That makes it a weak ChoCo anchor, though not enough by itself to dismiss a mirror: a broader question about optimizing committee size could still be worth asking.

The most charitable population mirror would give delegates types \(t\) with mass \(\mu\), and let each type exert effort \(x(t)\). Delegation is then proportional to effort. If \(X=\int x(t)\,d\mu>0\), the expected share of delegated weight voting correctly is
\[ \frac{\int x(t)(1/2+x(t))\,d\mu}{X} =\frac12+\frac{\int x(t)^2\,d\mu}{X}>\frac12. \]
As each delegate’s individual weight vanishes, independent voting errors average out. For finite approximations, the variance of the weighted vote is at most \(\frac14\sum_i w_i^2\), which tends to zero when no individual retains positive weight. The continuum therefore selects the correct outcome with probability one for any positive effort mass. At zero effort, the paper’s convention gives probability \(1/2\). Delegate heterogeneity in costs does not restore the reliability tradeoff: it can change which efforts are affordable, but not this limit.

That is the fundamental problem with the proposed \(R_N(q,x)\) mirror. Its \(N\) and normal approximation preserve finite-delegate sampling noise. For fixed positive active mass \(q\) and effort \(x\), that noise disappears as \(N\) grows. Keeping a finite committee preserves the paper’s one-versus-three comparison, but then the active mass tends to zero and the committee remains a finite set of delegates. Keeping positive active mass makes the committee grow with \(N\), and the theorem’s finite-committee comparison no longer describes the limit. A special budget or noise scaling could support a finite-size asymptotic study, but that would be a different question from continuizing the society in Theorem 8.

The stakeholders do not offer another population axis: the paper does not model their types or choices. A continuum of potential delegates with a finite selected committee would leave the outcome calculation unchanged when those candidates share one type. A continuum of delegates who retain correlated voting errors could avoid the collapse, but the shared uncertainty would be a new feature, not a mirror of the paper’s independent competence model.

So the negative case is strongest for the paper’s central reliability objective: its uncertainty comes from finitely many delegates, and that uncertainty vanishes in a genuine population limit. I cannot rule out a worthwhile neighboring project on finite-size fluctuations or shared uncertainty. Those projects would add or preserve structure the continuous mirror itself removes; they do not rescue Theorem 8 as a useful continuous-population question.
