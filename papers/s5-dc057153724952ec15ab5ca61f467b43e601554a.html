Its numbered propositions characterize strategic communication and welfare-optimal authority assignments; they do not classify an algorithmic problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. The appendix’s simulations do not supply such a result either.

The closest formal results are Proposition 1 (proved here): with a common state and private communication, for generic ideologies, every Pareto-optimal assignment gives all decisions to one leader; Proposition 2 (proved here): with a common state, the optimal assignment under cabinet communication Pareto-dominates any assignment under private-only communication; and Proposition 5 (proved here): with policy-specific information, full decentralization is generically never optimal, and the most moderate politician receives decisions for sufficiently moderate and sufficiently extreme experts, but not necessarily intermediate ones. These are plausible anchors for further economic analysis, but none meets the requested computational criterion.

The nearest imagined population regime would be a very large parliamentary party with many politicians sharing a small set of bias and information types. But turning that into a continuous problem means deciding how masses of politicians communicate and how authority is assigned to types. The paper instead makes communication strategic between individual politicians and gives each policy to one individual decision-maker. A nonatomic population could change those incentives; assigning authority to type masses would change the decision structure. A continuum over policies alone would not continuize the population.

So there is no paper-grounded continuous problem I would hand to a prover per the stated requirements. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The affirmative case supplies no positive anchor: the paper has no named result about computational complexity or algorithms. Propositions 1, 2, and 5 characterize equilibria and welfare-optimal authority assignments. One could pose a new problem—compute an optimal assignment from a distribution of politician types—but that would not mirror a computational result in this paper. Under the programme’s stated gate, this is the strongest objection.

The best version of Proposition 1’s mirror would use a large parliament with many politicians of each bias and information type. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s own large-parliament analysis supports it. The problem is what happens to the strategic communication at the continuous limit. With a finite number of politicians, a sender’s report can affect a minister’s decision, and the paper’s truth-telling condition tightens as the minister’s information \(d\) grows, requiring a bias gap no greater than \(1/[2(d+2)]\). In an atomless population, an individual report has no effect at all. Truth-telling then becomes payoff-neutral, so the paper’s strategic constraint disappears; under its welfare-maximizing equilibrium selection, truthful reports can be selected without the incentives that drove Proposition 1. Preserving those incentives requires retaining influential individual senders, which leaves the continuous model’s central object behind.

The same issue defeats the strongest version of Proposition 2’s mirror. Its cabinet advantage comes from how the prospect of shared information changes individual incentives to report truthfully. When each sender is negligible, a message to one minister rather than several does not change the sender’s payoff. Private and public communication can both support truthful reporting, so the comparison that gives cabinet meetings their force collapses under the continuum limit. Giving a positive mass of politicians collective control over reports could restore strategic influence, but it makes a type group the actor. The paper instead models individual politicians communicating simultaneously.

Proposition 5 fares no better as a computational mirror. In its policy-specific setting, each politician supplies information about one policy, with \(K=I\). If both politicians and policies grow into continua, the resulting model distributes a collection of independent, one-signal decisions across policy types. The paper already shows that each decision can be assigned independently by comparing ideological loss with a fixed information gain. A continuum version would aggregate those decisions into an integral; it would not expose a new population-level computational problem. Holding the number of policies fixed leaves only finitely many relevant experts.

The proposed scenario of many repeated jurisdictions also fails to repair these anchors: it averages outcomes across separate executive games, while each game retains the paper’s finite strategic structure. A distribution over governments is not a continuous society within a government.

The negative case is strongest as a claim about this paper’s fit with ChoCo, not as proof that no related continuous model could ever be worthwhile. A high-multiplicity finite analysis of politicians with repeated types might be sensible, and a broader model of strategic communication in a large population could be interesting. But each would need a new computational question, and the atomless limit weakens the paper’s defining strategic incentives. I would therefore reject all three anchors as ChoCo mirrors while conceding that the universal claim—no scenario could be worthwhile—is stronger than the evidence permits.
