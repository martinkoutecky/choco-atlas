The continuous mirror question

Given finite supports of circular preference types in categories \(A,B,C\), with rational masses \(\mu^A,\mu^B,\mu^C\) each summing to \(1\), does there exist a nonnegative coupling \(x_{abc}\) with those marginals such that no triple \((a,b,c)\) has positive mass of type \(a\) assigned to less-preferred \(B\)-types than \(b\), positive mass of type \(b\) assigned to less-preferred \(C\)-types than \(c\), and positive mass of type \(c\) assigned to less-preferred \(A\)-types than \(a\)? Find such a stable coupling or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

Use three finite supports of complete circular preference types, one for each category, with rational type masses totaling \(1\) per category. The decision variables \(x_{abc}\ge 0\) give the mass assigned to each triple and must reproduce all three mass distributions as marginals. A triple blocks when each of its types has positive assigned mass with a less-preferred partner in the category it ranks; stability requires that no such triple exists.

The objection that survived

Theorem 8 covers only markets with four agents per category, while the proposed problem asks about arbitrary rational type masses and computation; the paper supplies no transfer from its small-case construction.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends the circular ordinary-stability existence question in Theorem 8 to type-mass couplings. It leaves aside Theorem 7’s selected-individual guarantee, the strongest-link and weakest-link cases, strong stability, and the balanced-game argument.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every rational distribution over circular preference types admit a stable coupling?
  • Can a stable coupling be found efficiently in the support sizes and input bit length?
  • How does stability of a mass coupling relate to stability in finite replications of the same type distributions?

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a fully compliant case anchored in a named computational-complexity result: the paper has no numbered theorem or proposition classifying a problem as tractable or hard. The closest result is Theorem 8, proved here: under the circular preference rule, every market with four agents in each of the three categories has a stable matching. Theorem 7 proves the corresponding existence claim for three agents per category, with an additional guarantee about one chosen agent’s match. These are combinatorial existence results, not complexity classifications. The introduction’s NP-completeness result is attributed to Ng and Hirschberg, but it is unnumbered here and concerns strong stability in general 3GSM, not the paper’s circular, ordinary-stability question.

The strongest positive lead is therefore a direct extension of Theorem 8, supported by the paper’s broader Conjecture 3: Circular Stable Mass Matching. For each category \(A,B,C\), an instance gives a finite support of preference types and a rational mass for each type, with total mass \(1\) in each category. An \(A\)-type ranks \(B\)-types, a \(B\)-type ranks \(C\)-types, and a \(C\)-type ranks \(A\)-types, as in the circular rule. A solution is a nonnegative array \(x_{abc}\) whose marginals equal the given masses. Each unit of mass is assigned a triple; fractional \(x\) records the proportions assigned to each triple.

Define \(E_A(a,b)\) as the mass of type \(a\) currently matched to \(B\)-types it likes less than \(b\), summing over its current \(C\)-partners. Define \(E_B(b,c)\) and \(E_C(c,a)\) analogously. A triple \((a,b,c)\) blocks if all three eligible masses are positive: then some positive mass from each type can leave its current assignment and form that triple. The problem is to find such a stable \(x\), or report that none exists. This is an exact search question with a rational input, parameterized by the support sizes and input bit length. Matching remains integral for each infinitesimal agent; only the society’s distribution over integral triples is continuous.

A plausible regime is a large cyclic clearinghouse—say, workers ranking jobs, jobs ranking shifts, and shifts ranking workers—with many agents but far fewer distinct relevant ranking profiles. A type is the complete ranking used by the problem; its mass is a fraction of the population. This keeps the paper’s circular preference structure and makes the continuous population the object of study. The restriction that each category ranks only one other category is strong, but it is the paper’s own model, not a simplification introduced for the mirror.

The authors would plausibly recognize this as their question continued to mass distributions: it asks whether stable triple assignments exist under exactly their circular preferences. Theorem 8 itself covers only the four-agent case; it does not establish the mass result. The natural scope is just circular ordinary stability—not strongest-link or weakest-link preferences, strong stability, or the paper’s balanced-game argument.

For the mass search problem, my tentative positive-side guess is Class A: allowing types to split across triples may remove discrete feasibility obstacles, and the paper’s small-case results and Conjecture 3 give some reason to hope for universal existence. But stability is still a disjunctive condition across triples, so this is a research hypothesis, not an algorithm suggested by the paper. Useful follow-ups are whether stable mass assignments always exist, whether one can find them in time polynomial in support size and bit length, and how the answer relates to stable matchings in finite replications.

The weak point is substantial: the only numbered anchor proves existence for fixed \(N=4\), while the proposed problem has arbitrary support and asks for computation. The circular instance regime is also stylized. The paper’s unproved conjecture makes the extension natural, but neither it nor Theorem 8 supplies the missing computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative starts with a mismatch in what the paper proves. Its numbered results, Theorems 7 and 8, are finite existence theorems; Theorem 7 also guarantees a particular match for a chosen individual. The paper’s conjectures are existence claims too. Its computer search reports experiments, and the cited NP-completeness result concerns strong stability in general 3GSM, not the paper’s circular ordinary-stability problem. There is no proved computational-complexity result here to continuize.

The proposed mass-matching question adds a computational task: search over allocations of arbitrary rational type masses, and decide whether a stable one exists. That is a plausible research question, but it is a new problem built from Theorem 8’s subject matter, rather than a continuous counterpart of a computational result in the paper. Theorem 7 adds little to that case: its guarantee singles out one person, whose mass is zero in a continuum. Replacing that guarantee with one about a positive-mass type would be another new statement, and its existence part would still lead back to the same broader question.

There is a further concern about whether the paper itself supplies a compact type model. Its agents rank named individuals. As the market grows, those rankings can make agents distinct, so the support need not stay small. The proposed cyclic clearinghouse of worker, job, and shift types repairs this by having agents rank partner types. That is coherent, but it is a modeling extension beyond the paper’s named-agent markets.

That concern does not defeat the best version of the mirror. A large cyclic clearinghouse with repeated preference types is plausible, and a mass coupling can preserve which triples are formed. Blocking by positive mass is also a sensible continuous stability notion. High multiplicity supports this regime; it is no collision. So the negative case can question whether this paper is a strong source for ChoCo’s computational programme, but it cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario.

My case against is therefore weak where it matters most: the circular stable mass-matching problem survives as a credible new question. The firmer objection is about the connection to the paper’s proved results, not the value of studying that question.
