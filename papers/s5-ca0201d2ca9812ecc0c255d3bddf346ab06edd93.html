What the mirror covers

The proposed extension concerns only Split Cycle’s independence-of-clones theorem; it leaves the other axioms, the equivalence of Split Cycle definitions, and the general framework results alone. None of these supplies a computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best population mirror has a natural model: types are complete voter preference relations over a fixed candidate set, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of a large electorate with type \(t\). For a profile with few distinct rankings, Split Cycle could use weighted margins
\[ M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a]\bigr). \]
That is a plausible extension of the paper’s profile framework to a high-multiplicity electorate.

The closest result is the Lean theorem non_clone_choice_ind_clones_split_cycle, proved in this paper. It says that if \(D\) is a nonempty set of clones of candidate \(c\), then deleting \(c\) preserves whether each candidate outside \(D\cup\{c\}\) is a Split Cycle winner. A corresponding statement for rational masses is conceivable, but it would be an axiomatic theorem about Split Cycle.

That is the limit of the positive case: the paper has no numbered result asserting a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar. Its formal verification results are not computational-complexity anchors under the requested criteria. So there is no qualifying anchor—and therefore no continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover. The weakness is the paper’s subject itself: it formalizes and verifies voting-theoretic properties rather than studying the complexity of problems over electorates.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole anchor the proponent raises is non_clone_choice_ind_clones_split_cycle. Its mass version is quite natural: give each ranking type a rational weight, compute weighted majority margins, and ask whether removing one candidate from a clone set preserves the winners outside that set. But that remains an axiomatic theorem, exactly the kind of question the programme excludes.

For this theorem, the move to rational masses also adds little mathematical substance. With finitely many supported types, clear the denominators of their weights to obtain an ordinary finite profile. Split Cycle’s margins all scale by the same positive factor, so its defeat relation and winners are preserved. The same translation applies to the paper’s other finite-profile axioms, with suitable definitions for voter addition and removal. That makes a weighted formalization plausible, but it yields no algorithmic objective, complexity claim, or optimization problem to investigate.

A stronger proposal might ask for the minimum mass of voters who must change rankings to make a specified candidate win under Split Cycle. That is a legitimate population-level computational question, and it could be worth studying independently. But the paper does not formulate or analyze such interventions; adding costs and a search objective would create a new problem, not mirror the clone-independence result. The same is true of asking about winner robustness.

So the proponent’s anchor does not survive as a ChoCo mirror. The negative case is weaker if read as claiming that no worthwhile continuous question could ever be asked about Split Cycle: the natural electorate model exists, and a new intervention problem might be valuable. What the paper supplies is no named computational result for that question. Its contribution is formal verification of voting axioms, and a continuous restatement of those axioms does not fill the programme’s computational gap.
