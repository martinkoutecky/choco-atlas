What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation would cover equilibrium participation, turnout, candidate winning probabilities, voter welfare, and quorum choice, but none is anchored by a named computational result; atomlessness also removes pivotal turnout.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case has a plausible setting: a large referendum, with a fraction \(\alpha\) of voters supporting the status quo and \(1-\alpha\) supporting the challenger, and many voters sharing each type and the same voting cost. A candidate mirror would take type-specific participation rates \(p_A,p_B\) as its variables and ask for stable turnout equilibria and the quorum that maximizes expected voter welfare.

But I find no eligible anchor in this paper. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its results are finite-electorate equilibrium analysis: an existence argument in Section 2.2, formulas for the three-voter case, and numerical solutions for larger electorates. The paper’s findings about turnout, winning probabilities, and welfare are not complexity classifications. So there is no named computational result here to restate as an anchor, and no per-anchor problem or expected complexity class I can responsibly offer.

The proposed mirror also has a substantive difficulty. In an atomless population, an individual voter has zero influence on whether the quorum or majority threshold is met. With positive voting costs, the pivotal incentive at the heart of the paper disappears. Preserving it requires a finite-electorate or finite-size approximation, which leaves the central population-continuization question unresolved. Thus large referenda make the regime credible, but do not by themselves supply a convincing continuous mirror of the paper’s pivotal-voting results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no computational result to mirror. Its contributions are equilibrium existence, analytical formulas for three voters, and numerical solutions for larger electorates. It does not ask for the complexity of computing an equilibrium or an optimal quorum. A question such as “compute the stable equilibrium from \(N,\alpha,c,Q\)” would add a computational layer to the paper; it would not continuize one of its computational results.

The referendum setting does pass the high-multiplicity test: many voters can share one of two preference types and a common cost. That is a sensible regime, and I would not use high multiplicity as an objection. The trouble is that the paper’s central mechanism depends on an individual vote having a chance of changing the outcome. In an atomless society, each voter has zero mass. For any quorum expressed as a share \(q\), changing one voter’s action changes neither aggregate turnout nor the result. With \(c>0\), voting then costs \(c\) and brings no instrumental benefit, so abstention is optimal for both types. If \(q>0\), the status quo wins for lack of quorum; if \(q=0\), the result follows the tie rule. The turnout response, pivotal incentives, and welfare tradeoff the paper studies have disappeared.

Changing how the quorum scales does not restore that mechanism. A fixed \(Q\) becomes a vanishing share as \(N\) grows; setting \(Q=qN\) preserves a meaningful threshold, but an individual still cannot move it. A common shock to \(\alpha\) could preserve uncertainty about which candidate has more support, but it would not make one voter pivotal. Preserving pivotality would require a finite-size model or a singular scaling that retains finite-population fluctuations, for example by tuning costs to the pivotal-probability scale near a threshold. That could be an interesting asymptotic analysis, but it keeps the finite-size mechanism in the model and is not a continuous-population computational mirror of this paper.

The negative case is strongest against the proposed equilibrium-and-welfare mirror: the natural atomless version removes the behavior that gives those questions their substance, while the paper supplies no computational result to anchor a ChoCo complexity question. The universal claim is less airtight. One could formulate a new computational project around large finite electorates or their finite-size limits, but that would be an adjacent project, not a mirror of a result in this paper.
