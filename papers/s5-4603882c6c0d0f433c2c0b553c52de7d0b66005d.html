The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s scenario-core problem, but this paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor it to. Theorems 1–5 give PAC stability bounds; they do not establish that a problem is in P, hard, or belongs to another complexity class. Algorithm 1 computes a compression set, but the paper gives no named complexity result for that computation. So I cannot honestly present any of these as a qualifying anchor.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here using a cited compression bound. It says that, with confidence at least \(1-\beta\) over the agents’ independent private samples, the probability that some allocation in the private-sample scenario core becomes unstable under a fresh uncertainty realization is at most \(\sum_i \epsilon_i(s_{i,K})\). Here \(s_{i,K}\) is the observed compression size for agent \(i\), and each \(\epsilon_i\) is determined by that agent’s sample count and confidence share. This is a substantive result about core stability and data compression, but it is a statistical guarantee, not a computational-complexity claim.

A concrete continuous problem suggested by it is Private-Sample Mass-Core Feasibility. Consider a large cooperative pool with finitely many complete agent types \(T\), where a type includes coalition eligibility, payoff-relevant attributes, and the agent’s private sample history \(H_t\). Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s population fraction, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Give the instance a per-capita grand-coalition value \(\bar u\) and a succinct, type-invariant coalition-worth function \(v(q,\xi)\), where \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) is the fraction of the whole population of type \(t\) in a deviation. The decision is to find per-capita payoffs \(x_t\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t=\bar u \]
and, for every nonzero coalition mass vector \(q\),
\[ \sum_{t\in T}q_t x_t \;\geq\; \max_{t:q_t>0}\ \max_{\xi\in H_t} v(q,\xi). \]
The solution is a stable type-level allocation; if desired, a specified convex tie-break objective can select one. This preserves the paper’s central question—whether sampled coalition values leave an allocation stable—while making the society, rather than the allocation or uncertainty, the continuous object.

A plausible regime is a large federation of standardized providers or producers that repeatedly form coalitions to deliver services. Many agents share a small number of contractual and operational types, so population size can greatly exceed the number of types. The authors could recognize this as their scenario-core question in a mass-allocation regime, provided coalition values really depend on coalition composition and type attributes. That proviso matters: their paper permits arbitrary values for labeled coalitions, which need not have a well-defined composition-based mass limit.

The soft spot is private sampling. Because sample histories affect the core constraints, they must be included in a complete type. With finite uncertainty support and a fixed sample count, there can still be finitely many histories independent of population size. With continuously valued samples, however, histories may be nearly all distinct, leaving no useful small-support regime unless the model admits a sufficient-statistic type description. I would not predict tractability, hardness transfer, or continuum-specific hardness without a more explicit representation of \(v\) and those histories. This is a credible candidate problem, but it is not a mirror anchored by a qualifying named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no qualifying computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–5 establish PAC stability bounds; they do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Algorithm 1 computes a compression set, but the paper gives no named complexity result for that computation. Theorem 1 is the closest anchor, yet it is a statistical guarantee about generalization from private samples, not a result about algorithmic tractability or hardness. So it cannot support the paper-anchored ChoCo case the proponent was asked to make.

The proposed mass-core problem is more vulnerable when private sample histories are treated as types. If uncertainty is continuously valued, independent finite histories will generally be distinct across agents. Including each history in the complete type then makes the support grow with the population, removing the proposed small-support high-multiplicity regime. Replacing histories with a finite summary is possible only when that summary preserves every coalition’s sample-based constraint; the paper gives no such representation. The proposed model also needs anonymous, composition-based coalition values, whereas the paper permits values for labeled coalitions. Those choices can make a coherent new game, but they are extra structure, not a continuization supplied by the paper.

There is a stronger version that survives those objections: standardized providers with finitely many uncertainty outcomes and fixed sample counts could have finitely many history types, and coalition worths could depend succinctly on type composition. That is a plausible high-multiplicity core problem, and the authors might recognize it as related to their subject. I cannot honestly rule it out as worthwhile. But the paper’s PAC theorem does not identify a computational problem for that model. Choosing a feasibility or optimization task, and specifying the coalition-value representation and its pricing problem, would be new work rather than a mirror of a named computational result here.

So I would argue against treating this paper as a paper-anchored continuous-complexity mirror: it offers no qualifying result to anchor one, and its most direct private-history model usually lacks moderate support. The broader claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile related problem is weak. The finite-history, anonymous-game scenario is credible; what is missing is a computational result in this paper that the scenario could mirror.
