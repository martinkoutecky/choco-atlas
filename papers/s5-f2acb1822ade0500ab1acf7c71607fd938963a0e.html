The continuous mirror question

Given a finite bipartite type graph \(H=(U,V;E)\), worker and firm type masses \(p_i,q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) summing to \(1\) on each side, strict rankings of acceptable counterpart types, and a rational budget \(\kappa\), can the type masses be split among post-intervention rankings and assigned by a matching flow so that all mass is matched, the resulting type-level matching is stable, and the total mass-weighted adjacent-swap cost is at most \(\kappa\)? Stability requires that no worker-ranking variant and firm-ranking variant both have positive mass assigned to less-preferred counterpart types while preferring each other.

The model it lives in

A two-sided market with finitely many worker and firm types, rational mass for each type, shared rankings over acceptable counterpart types, unit capacities, and mass-splittable adjacent-swap interventions. A matching flow assigns the mass, stability excludes mutually blocking type variants, and cost is the mass-weighted number of adjacent swaps.

What the mirror covers

The primary mirror covers Theorem 4.4's swap-budget question for obtaining a perfect stable matching over types. The secondary proposal covers Theorem 3.2's fixed-allocation stabilization; neither establishes the paper's ETH lower bounds for the mass model.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of the type-mass decision problem as a function of the number of types, ranking support, and input bit length?
  • Does hardness persist when the number of types is small relative to the population, or do fractional cohort interventions make the problem tractable?
  • Under what conditions can a fractional witness be converted to a discrete witness with a corresponding swap-budget bound?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a labor market with large cohorts of interchangeable workers and firms. A type specifies an agent’s side, capacity, acceptable counterpart types, and ranking of those types. A type has rational mass, and the number of types \(\tau\) can be far smaller than the number of people. For example, a hiring market might have thousands of graduates across a few qualification and location profiles, and vacancies across a few role profiles.

This makes one modeling choice explicit: members of a type are indifferent among individual counterparts of the same type. A swap exchanges adjacent counterpart types in one agent’s ranking. The intervention may apply to any fraction of a cohort, creating modified-ranking subtypes; its cost is the mass changed times the number of swaps. That is a natural population version of the paper’s policy intervention, though the type-level indifference is a real co-modification of its strict rankings over named agents.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper by reduction from the almost-stable-matching result it cites as Theorem 4.3. Theorem 4.4 says that \(\Delta\)-PSM—deciding whether at most \(k\) adjacent swaps can produce an instance with a perfect stable matching—is NP-complete and W[1]-hard when parameterized by \(k\).

A precise continuous analogue is Type-Mass Swap Distance to Perfect Stability. Its input is a finite bipartite graph \(H=(U,V;E)\) of worker and firm types; positive rational type masses \(p_i\) and \(q_j\) on the two sides, each summing to \(1\); a strict ranking of acceptable counterpart types for each type; and a rational budget \(\kappa\). Every individual has capacity one. Split each type’s mass among possible post-swap rankings: write \(z_{i,r}\) for worker mass of type \(i\) with final ranking \(r\), and \(w_{j,s}\) for firm mass of type \(j\) with final ranking \(s\). The splits must preserve the original masses. Choose a matching mass \(x_{ir,js}\) on each acceptable edge, with row sums \(z_{i,r}\) and column sums \(w_{j,s}\). It must match all mass.

The swap cost is
\[ \sum_{i,r} z_{i,r}d_K(r,r_i)+\sum_{j,s}w_{j,s}d_K(s,s_j), \]
where \(d_K\) is the number of adjacent swaps between rankings. Ask whether there is such a perfect matching and set of ranking splits with cost at most \(\kappa\), stable in the following type-level sense: for every worker and firm variant, the mass of workers assigned to less-preferred firm types and the mass of firms assigned to less-preferred worker types cannot both be positive. The solution is the rational mass split and matching witnessing yes. With \(n\) individuals on each side and mass \(1/n\) per individual, \(k\) swaps correspond to budget \(\kappa=k/n\).

For unrestricted type support, I would tentatively expect hardness to remain: the matching graph and choices of rankings retain combinatorial structure. But Theorem 4.4 does not prove that for this model. In particular, whether the problem becomes tractable when the population is large relative to \(\tau\), with binary-rational masses, is an open question. Fractional campaigns may remove the discrete choices that drive the paper’s reduction.

A second, cleaner population mirror comes from Theorem 3.2, also proved in this paper. It says that for a capacitated SMP instance and a given feasible subgraph \(S\), a polynomial-time algorithm finds a minimum-length swap sequence making \(S\) stable. The continuous analogue is Type-Mass Fixed-Allocation Stabilization. Its input has rational masses of worker and firm types, integer capacities, rankings over counterpart types, and a prescribed allocation law: for each type, a rational mass for each feasible per-agent bundle of counterpart types. The bundle distributions must agree on the mass of contracts across each type pair. The action splits each type-and-bundle cohort among modified rankings, preserving its assigned bundle. Ask for the minimum mass-weighted number of adjacent swaps that makes the prescribed allocation stable. Stability means no worker and firm cohorts both have positive mass that would accept a mutually blocking contract, given their capacities and current bundles. Its solution is the ranking split attaining that minimum.

I expect this second problem may be Class A: Theorem 3.2’s proof reduces stabilization to submodular minimization, and rational cohort weights suggest a weighted version. That is a plausible extension, not a consequence of the theorem. The main questions are whether the submodularity survives cohort splitting and whether the minimum can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the bundle support, and the mass bit-length \(L\).

These mirrors cover the paper’s swap-to-perfect-stability hardness and fixed-allocation stabilization result; they do not claim to cover every result in the paper. The authors have a reason to recognize them: their motivation is policy changes to matching markets, and their tractable result already treats capacitated, many-to-many matching.

The weakest point is that the paper’s reductions use individual vertices and strict rankings over named agents, while these mirrors rank counterpart types and allow fractional parts of cohorts to receive different swaps. In particular, the \(\Delta\)-PSM reduction does not establish hardness when the number of types is small compared with the population. That limits what can be claimed about hardness transfer, but it does not erase the well-defined computational questions about the type-mass versions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that the paper’s primitive is an identity-indexed graph: each agent ranks named neighbors, and the graph’s edges and rankings jointly determine feasibility and blocking. In Theorem 4.4’s reduction, those local identities carry the construction. Preserving them in a complete type makes the type include its neighborhood and its order over named counterparts, so the number of types can grow with the number of agents. Collapsing named counterparts into employer or employee classes keeps the type count small, but changes strict rankings over individuals into rankings over classes and allows fractional matching. That is a plausible new market model; it is not a high-multiplicity encoding of the instances that establish Theorem 4.4.

The swap budget also has no single continuum scale. With \(n\) people, \(k\) individual swaps correspond to mass cost \(k/n\), which vanishes for fixed \(k\) as \(n\) grows. A fixed per-capita mass budget instead allows \(\Theta(n)\) swaps, outside the theorem’s parameterized claim. So Theorem 4.4 supplies no complexity transfer to the proposed Type-Mass problem. This weakens the case that it is a *mirror of that result*. It does not make the mass-budget problem uninteresting: it is a coherent computational question, and the reduction does not answer it.

Theorem 3.2 has a similar gap. Its input fixes a matching of named agents. To aggregate it, the proposed mirror must replace that matching with a distribution over bundles and let cohorts split across revised rankings. Those are consequential modeling choices, not details recovered from the theorem. The theorem’s submodular-minimization proof therefore gives no result for the proposed version. Still, fixed-allocation stabilization for cohorts with shared rankings is well-posed, and whether the relevant submodularity survives is a real question.

That is about as strong as the negative case gets. The paper’s capacitated extension and policy-intervention motivation make class-level labor-market matching a plausible scenario the authors could recognize. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists. The case against is that neither cited theorem currently supports the proposed continuous model’s complexity claims; it is especially weak against the fixed-allocation stabilization mirror, which looks like a natural new problem in its own right.
