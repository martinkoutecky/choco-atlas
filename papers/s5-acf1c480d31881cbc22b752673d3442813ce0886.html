The continuous mirror question

Fix \(m\) items, rational per-capita supplies \(q\in[0,1]^m\), and a domain \(\mathcal V\) of capped-additive valuations with rational parameters. An instance is a supported distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete valuation types, with rational masses summing to \(1\). Each type receives integral bundles, but its mass may be split among bundles: \(x_{t,S}=\mu_t\pi_{\mu,t}(S)\), where \(\pi_{\mu,t}\) is a lottery over \(S\subseteq[m]\), \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}=q_j\) for every item \(j\). Does there exist, for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), a polynomial-time truthful maximal-in-range direct mechanism, with a report-independent family of allocation rules and per-agent payments, whose allocation is feasible and has welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\infty}/(2-\varepsilon)\)? Truthfulness means that, holding \(\mu\) fixed under a unilateral zero-mass deviation, every true type \(t\) weakly prefers its truthful report to every report \(r\): \(\sum_S\pi_{\mu,t}(S)v_t(S)-p_{\mu,t}\geq\sum_S\pi_{\mu,r}(S)v_t(S)-p_{\mu,r}\). The running time is measured in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length.

The model it lives in

A large market of bidders with repeated capped-additive valuation types and item supplies scaled with population size. The allocation assigns each individual an integral bundle while type mass can be split among bundles; a direct mechanism specifies each report’s allocation lottery and payment, with a fixed report-independent range.

The objection that survived

The theorem concerns two named bidders and partitions of unique items, while aggregate mass assignments do not specify what an individual receives after a unilateral misreport; the continuous mechanism’s report menus and incentive constraints must therefore be defined separately.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the allocate-all-items, two-bidder capped-additive maximal-in-range hardness question of Theorem 3.5 in a repeated-type market. It leaves the paper’s other auction settings, VC-dimension results, multi-bidder bounds, and double-capped-additive result unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(2-\varepsilon\) inapproximability barrier persist when type mass can be split fractionally among integral bundles?
  • Can a report-independent range of feasible allocation rules support the stated continuous individual truthfulness constraints and be optimized in polynomial time?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper. It says that when all items must be allocated, no polynomial-time maximal-in-range mechanism achieves an approximation factor below \(2-\varepsilon\) for two-bidder combinatorial auctions with capped additive valuations, unless \(\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{P/poly}\). Here “factor” means the mechanism’s welfare is at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/(2-\varepsilon)\); the theorem rules out guarantees better than roughly one half.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large auction platform with many similar organizations bidding on a catalog of distinct goods or slots. Organizations with the same complete valuation parameters are one type. If there are \(N\) bidders but only \(\tau\) distinct types, with \(N\gg\tau\), their reports can be represented by a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over types. This is a property of that market regime, not of auctions generally.

My lead question is the *Continuous Allocate-All Capped-Additive Auction Mechanism Problem*. An instance has \(m\) items, rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and a supported list \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type \(t\) is its full capped-additive valuation, \(v_t(S)=\min\{\sum_{j\in S}a_{tj},B_t\}\). Each member of the population receives an integral bundle, while the mass assigned to a type and bundle may be fractional: \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(S\). Feasibility requires
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq[m]}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:\,j\in S}x_{t,S}=q_j. \]
The objective is total welfare, \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\).

The computational question is whether, for each fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), there is a polynomial-time truthful maximal-in-range direct mechanism that, on every supported report distribution, returns such an allocation with welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}_\infty/(2-\varepsilon)\). A solution is the mechanism: its allocation rule must use a report-independent range, its per-agent payments must make truthful reporting optimal, and its running time must be polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. The range condition matters because Theorem 3.5 is about maximal-in-range mechanisms, not all possible truthful mechanisms.

This keeps the paper’s underlying question—computational limits on truthful welfare approximation—while making the population the continuous object. Fractional \(x_{t,S}\) records how a continuum of same-type agents can receive different integral bundles; it does not make the individual bundles divisible. Repeated large markets with many bidders per valuation type are a plausible use case, and the paper’s focus on social welfare and truthful mechanisms makes the authors’ recognition of the question credible. I would expect hardness to persist because the item-bundle combinatorics remain, but that is a conjecture about this mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 3.5.

The weakest point is also the key technical question: Theorem 3.5 has only two bidders, and its hardness is in the item universe. A continuum of bidders permits splitting a type’s mass among bundles, which may convexify the allocation problem and break the paper’s reduction. So the theorem motivates the continuous question but does not establish its \(2-\varepsilon\) barrier. The first follow-up is whether that barrier survives fractional mass assignment; then one can ask whether the pricing problem for the continuous allocation formulation is tractable, and how complexity depends on \(m\), \(\tau\), and bit length.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 3.5, so there is a real computational result to consider: it rules out a polynomial-time maximal-in-range mechanism with an approximation guarantee better than \(2-\varepsilon\) for a two-bidder, allocate-all-items auction. I can’t dismiss it on the ground that the paper has no complexity theorem.

The strongest objection is that the theorem’s object is a fixed range of partitions of a finite set of unique items, evaluated for two named bidders. The proposed \(x_{t,S}\) records aggregate mass receiving each bundle. That aggregate plan does not specify what happens to an individual who misreports as a type with zero mass, so it does not by itself define the individual truthfulness constraints the theorem is about. The mechanism also needs a report-independent range of individual allocation rules, including what a deviator receives and pays.

There is a substantive scaling choice behind that gap. Keep the paper’s \(m\) unique items as the population grows, and per-capita supply tends to zero: with \(N\) bidders, each item supplies only \(1/N\) per capita. If instead \(q_j\) stays positive, the market has a population-scaled number of copies of each item. That is a coherent large-market model, but its outcomes are mass assignments of bundles across replicated goods, rather than partitions of the paper’s item set. The partition-shattering argument in Theorem 3.5 does not itself supply the continuous mechanism question.

A stronger mirror can repair these issues: define a nonatomic market with per-capita supplies, specify each agent’s allocation and payment for every possible report, and require a fixed range of measurable allocation rules. Or define finite populations with repeated valuation types, then study a limit. Either would be a meaningful research problem. In the nonatomic version, however, a unilateral report changes no aggregate mass, so the link between individual truthfulness and the aggregate allocation must be built into the model; in the finite version, the limit must preserve incentive constraints as each agent’s mass vanishes.

That is a serious reason to doubt that Theorem 3.5 itself yields a clean continuous mirror. It does not establish that no worthwhile scenario exists. Repeated bidder types and population-scaled supplies make a plausible large-market setting, and the repaired question could interest the authors even though it changes the outcome space. So the negative case is weak against the universal claim: I would not claim this anchor is defeated.
