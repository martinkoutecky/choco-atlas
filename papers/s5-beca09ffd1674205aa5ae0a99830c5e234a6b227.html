The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a finite support \(\mathcal A\subseteq 2^C\) of approval types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_A\) summing to \(1\), compute \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|\le k\), such that for every nonempty \(X\subseteq C\) and every \(\beta\in[k]\), \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal A:\,|A\cap X|\ge\beta,\,|A\cap W|<\beta}\mu_A < |X|/k\).

The model it lives in

Approval sets are the types, each carrying mass \(\mu_A\); the decision is a committee \(W\). A type-aggregated FJR-GJCR tracks one virtual payment state per supported type, uses mass-weighted affordability sums, and must output a committee of size at most \(k\) satisfying the mass FJR inequalities.

The objection that survived

The paper's polynomial-time bound is stated in the explicit voter count \(n\); polynomial dependence on support size \(\tau\) and mass bit length \(L\) requires analyzing the type-aggregated rounded updates and proving the FJR potential invariant for fractional masses.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the FJR-GJCR committee-construction guarantee and its size bound, relying on Theorem 1, Lemma 1, and Theorem 2. It leaves the EJR+ corollary, the MES-extension result in Proposition 2, and the paper's axiomatic and participatory-budgeting questions unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the type-aggregated FJR-GJCR preserve the FJR potential invariant and run in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\)?
  • Does the mass version also satisfy an appropriate continuous analogue of EJR+?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Rational-Mass FJR Committee. The paper’s computational anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: FJR-GJCR is polynomial-time computable on an explicitly listed election. Lemma 1, also proved here, shows it selects at most \(k\) candidates, and Theorem 1, proved here, shows its output satisfies FJR. Together, these give a polynomial-time algorithm for finding an FJR committee. The paper supplies no named computational hardness result, so this is the natural anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large union or professional association electing a fixed-size member council. Many members may choose from a small set of published approval slates, producing hundreds of thousands of voters but only dozens or hundreds of distinct ballots. A type is an approval set \(A\subseteq C\); its mass \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of members using that ballot. This fits the paper’s model directly: approval sets fully describe the preferences relevant to the rule, and the committee remains a subset of candidates of size at most \(k\).

Precisely, an instance consists of candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a support \(\mathcal A\subseteq 2^C\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_A\) summing to \(1\). The task is to find \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|\le k\), satisfying the following condition for every nonempty candidate set \(X\subseteq C\) and every \(\beta\in[k]\):
\[ \sum_{\substack{A\in\mathcal A:\\ |A\cap X|\ge\beta,\ |A\cap W|<\beta}}\mu_A < \frac{|X|}{k}. \]
The left side is the mass of voters who could form a weakly cohesive group over \(X\), yet each approves fewer than \(\beta\) members of the committee. Requiring it to be below \(|X|/k\) is exactly the continuous coalition reading of FJR: no such group can have enough mass to meet its quota. Call this problem Rational-Mass FJR Committee.

I expect it to be tractable, in Class A. The paper’s algorithm already tracks each voter’s approval count in \(W\) and virtual payment. In the mass version, those values are shared by every voter of a type, so the state can be kept for the \(\tau=|\mathcal A|\) types. Candidate affordability becomes a weighted sum over types; the payment increments can use the paper’s denominator \(k(k+1)\). The proof’s sums over voters then become mass-weighted sums. This gives a credible route to running time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the bit length \(L\) of the masses. The mirror covers committee construction with FJR, including the size and representation guarantees; it does not claim a result about the paper’s separate MES-extension proposition. Natural follow-ups are whether the mass version also preserves EJR+ and whether the approach extends to the participatory-budgeting question raised in the conclusion.

The weakest point is a real proof obligation: Theorem 2’s stated running time is polynomial in the explicit voter count \(n\), not in the support size and binary mass encoding. Clearing denominators in the masses could require exponentially many voters. A mirror therefore needs a new bit-complexity analysis of the type-aggregated algorithm, and a careful proof that the fractional-coalition formulation preserves FJR’s quantifiers. The algorithm and potential argument in the paper give good reasons to expect that extension, but the paper does not already establish it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is that this mirror may be too easy to earn much research attention. The natural continuous question is to find a committee \(W\), \(|W|\le k\), such that for every nonempty \(X\subseteq C\) and \(\beta\in[k]\),
\[ \sum_{\substack{A:\ |A\cap X|\ge\beta\\ |A\cap W|<\beta}}\mu_A < \frac{|X|}{k}. \]
That is exactly FJR with voter counts replaced by mass. The paper’s algorithm already treats voters with the same ballot identically: they have the same approval count in \(W\) and the same virtual payment. A mass version can keep one state per supported ballot and replace sums over voters with mass-weighted sums. The rounding error also scales with total mass, so normalizing the electorate to mass \(1\) appears to preserve the committee-size argument. The potential proof seems to admit the same substitution.

If that lift goes through, the main contribution would be a bit-complexity analysis for the type-aggregated algorithm, polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\). That is useful, but it may be a short extension of Theorem 2 rather than a new optimization problem or a demanding continuization result. The case against is strongest as a question of research priority: the mirror appears well-posed, but its likely technical yield looks modest.

That does not defeat the anchor. Theorem 2 is a named computational result, and Theorem 1 plus Lemma 1 establish what the algorithm computes. Approval ballots are complete types for this problem, and a large association with many members but relatively few ballot patterns is a credible high-multiplicity regime. The strict threshold in the mass condition is exact for rational masses; it is not a reason to reject the model. Nor does the separate MES-extension proposition supply a stronger objection: the proponent did not claim a continuous mirror of it, and the task permits one emphatic mirror.

So the negative case is weak on the merits. I can argue that the likely proof is routine and the payoff limited, but I cannot honestly show that the continuous-population question fails or that no plausible scenario supports it.
