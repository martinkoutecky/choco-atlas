The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational complexity result. Its numbered results concern how exclusions change the \(\phi^E\) payoffs. For example, Theorem 2 (proved here) says that in an asymmetric glove game with \(|R|<|L|\), adding an excluded pair across the two sides reduces the scarce-side player’s payoff more than the other player’s. Theorem 1 (also proved here) says that in an apex game, excluding a coalition between two minor players affects all minor players’ payoffs equally. Neither states that a problem is tractable or hard.

Theorem 2 is the closest thematic candidate: one could imagine a market with large cohorts of standardized glove owners and ask how exclusion patterns affect their aggregate value. But that would require defining a continuum version of the \(\phi^E\) value, which the paper does not provide, and it would still not mirror a named computational result. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its Theorems 1 and 2 and Corollaries 1–6 are comparative claims about the \(\phi^E\) payoffs: how exclusions affect them under particular game assumptions. The paper asks no complexity question and gives no algorithmic classification. The fact that its definition averages over \(n!\) orders suggests a possible computation problem, but does not itself state one.

Theorem 2 is the strongest candidate for a more ambitious mirror: model a large glove market with repeated left- and right-glove owners, then ask how exclusions affect their aggregate value. That makes high multiplicity quite natural for the market setting. The obstacle is the theorem’s specific perturbation: adding an exclusion between two named players. With arbitrary partner-specific exclusions, a player’s complete type includes their partner set, so compressing the society to a few types loses the relation the theorem studies. If instead each type excludes every member of another type, that is a coherent mass model, but it replaces one excluded pair with a block-wide incompatibility; Theorem 2 does not establish a result about that change. Keeping just two marked individuals preserves the theorem’s perturbation, but then the proposed object is a continuum with named exceptions, and a computational question about it would be new work rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 1 has the same gap. Its apex-game setting can plausibly have many interchangeable minor players, but the claim concerns the effect of excluding a particular pair of minors on every minor player’s payoff. Collapsing the minors to one type erases which pair is excluded. A block-level exclusion would change the model; retaining the pair’s identities requires extending the type description and defining how their finite-player ordering calculation becomes a population value. One could choose a random-priority limit or a derivative-based value, but that choice defines a new value and raises new questions. It does not supply the missing computational anchor.

So neither theorem survives as an anchored ChoCo mirror, even after improving the scenarios. That is a case against treating this paper as a direct continuation target. It is not proof that no worthwhile neighboring project exists: computing a suitably defined mass-based excluded-coalition value for succinct games could be interesting, and the glove market gives a sensible setting. But that project would first need to define its population value and its computational problem; it would not be a continuous mirror of a computational result this paper reports.
