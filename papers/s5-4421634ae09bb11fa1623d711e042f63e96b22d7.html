The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of additive valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a finite set \(J\) of item classes with per-person supplies \(s_j\), and integral bundles \(S\subseteq J\) for each person, let \(y_{t,S}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(S\). Subject to \(\sum_S y_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}y_{t,S}\le s_j\), compute or approximate the maximum population Nash welfare \(\exp(\sum_{t,S}y_{t,S}\log v_t(S))\), where \(v_t(S)=\sum_{j\in S}v_{tj}\); define the optimum as zero if no feasible assignment gives positive value to all positive-mass types.

The model it lives in

A continuous population with type masses \(\mu_t\), additive values \(v_{tj}\), and per-capita item supplies \(s_j\). Each person receives an integral bundle, while the population mass can be split among bundles. The optimization is \(\max_y \sum_{t,S:v_t(S)>0} y_{t,S}\log v_t(S)\), subject to type-mass and item-supply constraints; exponentiating the optimum gives population Nash welfare.

What the mirror covers

The welfare mirror covers Theorem 4.3’s additive-valuations objective; it does not establish that its inapproximability threshold transfers. It leaves the paper’s budget-additive rounding results and its other equilibrium results outside this anchor, although Theorem 3.4 has a separate fixed-support analogue.

Open questions for a prover

  • For type \(t\) and item prices \(p_j\), can the bundle-pricing problem \(\max_{S\subseteq J}\left(\log v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\right)\) be solved in polynomial time for arbitrary additive values?
  • Does Theorem 4.3’s \(\sqrt{8/7}\) inapproximability threshold, or any nontrivial hardness bound, hold for rational-mass continuous populations?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper: for every constant \(\delta>0\), unless \(P=NP\), no \((\sqrt{8/7}-\delta)\)-approximation algorithm exists for maximizing Nash social welfare with additive valuations.

A natural continuous mirror is a large allocation market with recurring agent profiles. Let \(T\) be the finite set of complete additive valuation types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). Let \(J\) be a finite set of item classes, and let \(v_{tj}\) be type \(t\)’s value for class \(j\). Class \(j\) has supply \(s_j\) per unit of population. Each person receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq J\); the variable \(y_{t,S}\) records how much mass of type \(t\) receives that bundle. A feasible allocation satisfies
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq J}y_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}y_{t,S}\le s_j. \]
Its Nash welfare is
\[ \exp\!\left(\sum_{t,S}y_{t,S}\log\!\left(\sum_{j\in S}v_{tj}\right)\right), \]
with zero welfare if positive mass receives zero value. The problem is to maximize this quantity and return the mass assignment \(y\). The supplies are normalized per person, so they scale with population size; allocations to bundles remain integral for each person.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large platform allocates placements to many advertisers, where millions of accounts fall into a modest number of valuation profiles and the placement inventory grows with the advertiser population. A type includes the full valuation vector, so agents grouped together really are interchangeable for the problem. The paper’s indivisible allocation question remains recognizable: it still asks how to distribute goods to agents to maximize Nash welfare. The continuous change is that a fraction of a type’s mass can receive each bundle.

I would provisionally expect this mirror to be Class C, through difficulty in choosing bundles against item prices. The configuration formulation has exponentially many bundle choices, and its natural pricing question is to maximize \(\log(\sum_{j\in S}v_{tj})-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\) over bundles \(S\). That looks like a substantive combinatorial problem even though type mass is divisible. This is a research expectation, not a hardness result: the concrete questions are whether that pricing problem has an efficient algorithm, and whether the paper’s inapproximability threshold persists for this continuous-population allocation problem.

A second, particularly direct mirror comes from Theorem 3.4, also proved here: Algorithm 4 computes a thrifty and modest equilibrium in polynomial time for money-clearing markets with a constant number of buyers or a constant number of sellers. Here the type is a buyer’s complete market description—its utility vector, budget, and utility cap—and \(\mu_t\) is the population fraction with that description. For each good \(j\), retain the paper’s unit supply and earning cap \(d_j\); type \(t\) has per-person budget \(b_t\), utility cap \(c_t\), and utility coefficients \(a_{tj}\). The money-clearing condition becomes
\[ \sum_{t\in U}\mu_t b_t\le \sum_{j\in N(U)}d_j \quad\text{for every }U\subseteq T, \]
where \(N(U)\) contains goods valued positively by at least one type in \(U\). The task is to find prices and per-person type demands that are thrifty and modest, respect the goods’ capped supplies, and satisfy Walras’ law.

This is a strong match to the paper’s market question. An instance with many buyers but only \(\tau=|T|\) distinct profiles can be represented by \(\tau\) aggregate buyers: scale each type’s budget, utilities, and cap by \(\mu_t\). Thus the theorem gives a polynomial-time result when the number of types is constant (or the number of goods is constant), even if the population itself is large. I expect Class A in that fixed-support regime. Further questions are whether exact equilibrium is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), and what complexity applies when \(\tau\) grows.

The Nash-welfare mirror is the more ambitious anchor; the equilibrium mirror is the more secure one. My weakest point is that Theorem 4.3’s reduction does not automatically survive the mass model. Its construction may rely on many distinct valuation profiles and on assignment choices that cease to be all-or-nothing when type mass can split among bundles. So I would not claim that its \(\sqrt{8/7}\) hardness transfers. The positive case is that the continuous welfare problem is still a concrete, author-recognizable high-multiplicity version of their allocation problem, while Theorem 3.4 independently establishes a clean continuous-population mirror for the paper’s market computation results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the two anchors offer different amounts of new work, but neither supports the claim that the paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror.

Theorem 3.4 is the weaker anchor for a new project. For a type \(t\) with mass \(\mu_t\), per-person budget \(b_t\), cap \(c_t\), and utilities \(a_{tj}\), aggregate its allocation as \(X_{tj}=\mu_t x_{tj}\). A single synthetic buyer with budget \(\mu_t b_t\) and cap \(\mu_t c_t\) then represents the whole type: its utility is \(\min\{\mu_t c_t,\sum_j a_{tj}X_{tj}\}\). With supplies and earning caps normalized consistently, the equilibrium conditions reduce to those of the paper’s finite market. Thus the fixed-\(\tau\) case of the proposed mirror follows directly from the paper’s constant-buyer result; for general \(\tau\), the paper’s other equilibrium results apply to the compressed market too. That makes this a clean high-multiplicity dictionary, but a thin standalone research contribution unless it yields a new encoding-sensitive bound or a result beyond the finite-market algorithms.

Theorem 4.3 is harder to dismiss. Its reduction depends on indivisible assignments: switch items encode variable choices, and equation items reward particular combinations. In the continuous model, mass of a type can split among bundles. Keeping every reduction role as a separate type preserves the gadgets but makes support grow with the instance; duplicating roles to create genuine high multiplicity lets mass split across the choices the reduction meant to enforce. So the paper’s approximation-hardness proof does not itself establish the continuous hardness claim.

That failure does not defeat the better mirror. With finite item classes, rational type masses, per-capita supplies, and integral bundles for each infinitesimal agent, maximizing population Nash welfare is the linear optimization problem
\[ \max \sum_{t,S} y_{t,S}\log v_t(S) \]
subject to \(\sum_S y_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and the item-supply constraints. It has exponentially many bundle variables, and its pricing question is to maximize \(\log v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\). This is a concrete computational question about the same allocation objective. Whether its pricing problem is tractable, hard, or yields a useful approximation is not settled by the failed transfer of the discrete reduction.

So the honest negative case is limited: the equilibrium anchor looks largely like exact aggregation of a model the paper already solves, and the welfare hardness anchor cannot be carried over by the paper’s proof. But the reworked welfare mirror remains well-posed, recognizably about the paper’s problem, and plausibly worth studying. I cannot defend the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror.
