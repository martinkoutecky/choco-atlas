The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) item classes, rational per-agent supplies \(q_j\in[0,1]\), three additive valuation types \(R,C,U\) each of mass \(1/3\), and exact high-multiplicity MMS benchmarks \(\sigma_R(q),\sigma_C(q)\), find a mass assignment \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq[m]\) maximizing \(r\), subject to \(\sum_Bx_{t,B}=1/3\), \(\sum_t\sum_{B\ni j}x_{t,B}=q_j\), \(x_{R,B}=0\) when \(v_R(B)<r\sigma_R(q)\), \(x_{C,B}=0\) when \(v_C(B)<r\sigma_C(q)\), and \(x_{U,B}=0\) when \(v_U(B)<\pi_U(q)\), where \(\pi_U(q)=\sum_jq_jv_U(j)\) and \(\sigma_t(q)=\max_{p\in\mathcal P(q)}\min_{B:p_B>0}v_t(B)\) for \(\mathcal P(q)=\{p\ge0:\sum_Bp_B=1,\sum_{B\ni j}p_B=q_j\}\).

The model it lives in

A three-type configuration LP over mass \(x_{t,B}\) of integral bundles, with per-agent item-class supplies \(q_j\), MMS thresholds \(r\sigma_t(q)\) for \(R,C\), and a full-PS threshold \(\pi_U(q)\) for \(U\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers high-multiplicity analogues of Theorems 14 and 15 at the level of their fair-allocation tasks and conditional algorithmic guarantees. It does not establish the \(11/12\) or \(19/18\) constants in the continuum, nor does it cover Theorem 10, Theorem 17, or the finite-instance lower-bound propositions as transferred results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the fixed-\(r\) configuration LP be solved exactly in polynomial time, or is its bundle-pricing problem NP-hard?
  • Can the high-multiplicity MMS benchmarks \(\sigma_R(q)\) and \(\sigma_C(q)\) be computed or certified efficiently rather than supplied?
  • Do the finite-instance \(11/12\) and \(19/18\) obstructions have exact mass-allocation analogues?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper does admit a credible continuous mirror, but the honest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the allocation problem, not a claim that the paper’s \(11/12\) or \(19/18\) constants automatically survive unchanged.

The paper contains no numbered NP-hardness theorem of its own. Its statement that computing MMS is NP-hard is background, not a numbered result proved here. My strongest anchors are therefore the two main algorithmic theorems.

The natural regime is a large population of \(3N\) recipients with only three complete valuation types, \(R,C,U\), each occurring with mass \(1/3\). Think of three recurring cohorts competing for standardized indivisible item classes: \(Nq_j\) copies of class \(j\), where \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\) is the per-capita supply. All \(R\)-agents have the same additive valuation vector, and likewise for \(C\) and \(U\). Thus \(N\) grows while the number of types remains \(3\).

To preserve the paper’s indivisibility, every individual receives an integral subset \(B\subseteq [m]\); only the population-level allocation is fractional. Let \(x_{t,B}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(B\). Then

\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\frac13 \]

for every \(t\), and

\[ \sum_t\sum_{B\ni j}x_{t,B}=q_j \]

for every item class \(j\). No item is divided between agents.

The corresponding high-multiplicity MMS benchmark for type \(t\) is obtained from distributions over integral bundles. Let \(\mathcal P(q)\) be the set of distributions \(p\) over \(2^{[m]}\) satisfying

\[ \sum_B p_B=1,\qquad \sum_{B\ni j}p_B=q_j. \]

Then define

\[ \sigma_t(q)= \max_{p\in\mathcal P(q)} \min_{B:p_B>0} v_t(B), \qquad \pi_t(q)=\sum_j q_jv_t(j). \]

Here \(\sigma_t(q)\) is the limiting MMS value and \(\pi_t(q)\) is the per-capita proportional share. For rational data, these distributions can be implemented exactly by sufficiently large finite populations, so this is genuinely the high-multiplicity limit rather than an unrelated fractional-allocation model.

My lead anchor is Theorem 14, proved in this paper in Section 3.4 using the computer-assisted MILP9G. It states:

For three agents with additive valuations over goods, ambitious coarse atomic exhaustive search generates a \(\rho\)-\(P^+MM\) allocation with \(\rho\ge 11/12\), and, given the MMS partitions of agents \(R\) and \(C\), the allocation algorithm runs in polynomial time.

Here \(R\) and \(C\) receive at least \(\rho\) times their MMS, while the prespecified agent \(U\) receives her full proportional share.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuous \(P^+MM\)-Goods. Given \(m\), \(q\), the three additive valuation types \(R,C,U\), a target \(r\) (in particular \(r=11/12\)), and exact high-multiplicity MMS certificates for \(R\) and \(C\), decide whether there exists a mass allocation \(x\) satisfying the supply constraints above and

\[ x_{R,B}=0 \quad\text{whenever}\quad v_R(B)<r\sigma_R(q), \]

\[ x_{C,B}=0 \quad\text{whenever}\quad v_C(B)<r\sigma_C(q), \]

and

\[ x_{U,B}=0 \quad\text{whenever}\quad v_U(B)<\pi_U(q). \]

The optimization version maximizes \(r\). A solution is the mass vector \(x\); each infinitesimal recipient still receives one integral bundle \(B\).

This is recognizably the same question. It preserves additive valuations, indivisible bundles, MMS, PS, the prespecified \(P\)-agent, and the distinction between the \(R,C\) and \(U\) roles. The only change is exactly the high-multiplicity change: one named agent of each type becomes a large mass of indistinguishable agents, and item supply scales with population.

I would initially expect the certificate-given version to be a Class A candidate: it is a linear optimization problem over masses of integral bundles, and the finite-agent assignment explosion has been replaced by a column-generation problem. The crucial unresolved issue is separation over the exponentially many bundle columns. If additive valuations yield a suitable pricing oracle, this is a clean continuous-optimization result. If bundle pricing is knapsack-like when \(m\) is part of the input, the same mirror may instead become Class C. That uncertainty is a research question about the mirror, not a reason to reject it.

The second anchor is Theorem 15, also proved here, in Section 3.6 through MILP9C. It states:

For three agents with additive valuations over chores, ambitious coarse atomic exhaustive search generates a \(\rho\)-\(P^+mm\) allocation with \(\rho\le 19/18\), and, given the minimax-share partitions of agents \(R\) and \(C\), the allocation algorithm runs in polynomial time.

For chores, \(R\) and \(C\) receive bundles costing at most \(\rho\) times their minimax shares, while \(U\) receives cost at most her proportional share. Proposition 5 in the paper establishes the goods/chores sign-reversal equivalence.

The continuous chores problem is therefore:

Continuous \(P^+mm\)-Chores. Given nonnegative additive costs \(w_t(j)\), per-capita supplies \(q\), and minimax certificates, define

\[ \eta_t(q)= \min_{p\in\mathcal P(q)} \max_{B:p_B>0} w_t(B), \qquad \pi_t(q)=\sum_jq_jw_t(j). \]

Find a mass allocation \(x\) minimizing \(r\) such that

\[ x_{R,B}=0 \quad\text{if}\quad w_R(B)>r\eta_R(q), \]

\[ x_{C,B}=0 \quad\text{if}\quad w_C(B)>r\eta_C(q), \]

and

\[ x_{U,B}=0 \quad\text{if}\quad w_U(B)>\pi_U(q). \]

The target corresponding to the theorem is \(r=19/18\). I expect the same classification as for goods: an LP-based Class A result in explicitly represented or suitably structured bundle spaces, with a possible Class C boundary for compact instances whose pricing problem is hard.

The paper’s negative examples make these questions substantive rather than vacuous. Proposition 13, proved here, gives a nine-item goods instance in which every allocation leaves \(R\), \(C\), or \(U\) at most \(11/12\) of the relevant MMS or PS. Proposition 16 gives the analogous \(19/18\) obstruction for chores. A natural continuous follow-up is whether these obstructions survive high-multiplicity pooling, or whether mass mixing improves the attainable ratio.

The weakest point is that Theorem 14 is genuinely a three-agent theorem. In the mirror, MMS is an \(N\)-way high-multiplicity benchmark rather than a three-way partition, so the constants \(11/12\) and \(19/18\) cannot simply be quoted as inherited facts. The paper also gives polynomial running time only after the relevant MMS partitions are supplied. Computing the continuum MMS certificates may itself be difficult. Thus the defensible claim is not “the theorem already proves the continuous result”; it is that the same underlying fairness problem has a natural, precise, high-multiplicity formulation whose central computational object is exactly the kind of mass-allocation LP and pricing problem that the continuization programme studies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper’s substance is tied to exactly three agents and a finite set of individually scarce items. Its named runtime results do not survive the population limit as computational results.

Theorem 14 is conditional on being given MMS partitions for \(R\) and \(C\). The paper explicitly says that computing MMS is NP-hard; the theorem therefore gives a polynomial search only after the central hard benchmark has already been supplied. In the proposed mirror, the correct MMS benchmark for \(3N\) agents is a \(3N\)-way partition, not a three-way partition. The nine “atomic bundles” and the \(3\times3\) geometry disappear. If one retains three-way certificates, one is no longer using the cloned population’s MMS. If one uses the correct \(3N\)-way benchmark, one has introduced a new configuration-assignment problem whose columns are bundles satisfying value thresholds, with pricing essentially a constrained knapsack problem. That may be a worthwhile new problem, but it is not a continuous version of the algorithm proved in Theorem 14.

The only way to preserve the theorem’s structure is to require all \(R\)-mass to receive one common bundle, all \(C\)-mass another, and all \(U\)-mass a third. Then the population is ornamental: the problem has collapsed back to the original three-agent allocation. Allowing \(x_{t,B}\) to split each type across bundles avoids that collapse, but precisely by replacing the paper’s integral three-agent allocation with a new convexified allocation model. The paper supplies no algorithmic result for that model.

Theorem 15 does not repair this defect. By Proposition 5, the chores result is just the sign-reversed goods result. Its continuous analogue therefore faces exactly the same mismatch between three-agent minimax partitions and \(3N\)-agent high-multiplicity benchmarks. It is not an independent computational anchor.

The natural scaling choices are also damaging. If the item set is kept fixed while the number of agents grows, eventually there are too few positive-valued items to give every agent a nontrivial bundle, so the MMS-type benchmark becomes zero or otherwise uninformative. If item supplies are scaled with \(N\), then the model must decide whether agents may receive multiple copies of an item class. Allowing this produces a repeated-item packing problem rather than the paper’s finite indivisibility problem. Forbidding it, as in the proposed \(B\subseteq[m]\) formulation, adds an item-class capacity constraint that is not present in the paper. That is a coherent new model, but it is a modelling invention, not a limit already latent in the stated theorems.

Propositions 13 and 16 are weaker anchors still. They say that every one-shot integral allocation of the nine-item instance has some losing role. They do not provide a linear obstruction valid for the high-multiplicity configuration polytope. Once \(R\)-, \(C\)-, and \(U\)-agents may be distributed over different bundles, the losing role can vary across copies, and the case analysis in the propositions gives no reason that the resulting mass LP is infeasible. Proving that the obstruction survives would require a new dual certificate or a new combinatorial argument. Conversely, if it disappears, that is precisely because the continuous model has changed the allocation problem rather than mirrored the proposition.

So the best negative conclusion is that this paper is a poor source for a ChoCo mirror: its principal results are conditional finite-\(n=3\) approximation guarantees, and every nondegenerate population formulation either loses the theorem’s geometry or becomes a new configuration LP whose main complexity is in bundle pricing and benchmark computation.

But the universal claim cannot honestly be sustained. The proponent’s \(x_{t,B}\) model is a sensible high-multiplicity fair-division problem, and asking whether its configuration LP is tractable is genuinely computational. The negative case shows that the paper does not itself establish that mirror; it does not show that the mirror is not worth studying.
