What the mirror covers

The paper's named results characterize pairing rules and their geographic extensions; it does not analyze the computation of matching outcomes.

The case FOR (proponent)

The most plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large residency market with many couples. A type could be a complete pair of hospital rankings, together with the couple’s compromise and togetherness parameters; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of couples of that type. Applying a pairing rule to each type would give a distribution over couples’ joint rankings.

That gives the setting a plausible population story, but it does not yield a computational problem from this paper. The rule still aggregates the preferences of exactly two doctors. Computing the distribution of joint rankings would just apply the rule type by type; choosing parameters to optimize a matching outcome would add a new problem the paper does not study. Fractionalizing hospital positions or couple assignments would continuize outcomes, not the population.

There is also no qualifying anchor. The closest is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for each \(k\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}\), a pairing rule satisfies Strong Pareto, \(k\)-Compromise, and \(k\)-Threshold-Consistency if and only if it is the \(k\)-Lexi-Pairing rule. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a result about a problem being tractable, hard, or parameterized. The paper contains no named computational result of that kind, so it supplies no anchor for the required continuous-complexity question.

The weak point is decisive: many couples may make a high-multiplicity regime sensible for a *new* matching problem, but the paper’s own object is preference aggregation within one couple. I can’t honestly state a continuous problem per anchor when the paper has no computational anchor to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 is the closest named anchor, but it characterizes a pairing rule by axioms; it says nothing about the complexity of deciding, optimizing, or approximating a computational problem. Theorem 1 and Proposition 4 have the same limitation. A continuous analogue of those characterizations would continuize an axiomatic question, which the programme excludes.

The most plausible population model is a distribution over couples, with each type recording both partners’ rankings, the parameters \(k,t\), and any relevant geography. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: couples of the same type are interchangeable. But applying the paper’s rule to each type is pointwise. The population distribution \(\mu\) does not enter the rule: each type produces its ranking over hospital pairs independently. Listing the resulting rankings and their masses is straightforward computation, not a complexity question about the paper’s result.

A stronger proposal would put that population into a matching market and ask how to assign hospital positions to the mass of couples. This respects the population axis, and the paper itself suggests that its rules could inform matching mechanisms. But the computational problem would then come from the added market: hospital capacities, assignment or stability requirements, and an objective or performance measure. The paper neither defines nor analyzes those elements. Its pairing rules would supply preferences to a new problem; they would not be the object whose computational complexity the continuous mirror charts.

There is a further mismatch in trying to continuize Theorem 2 itself. The theorem compares hospital pairs for one couple, using that couple’s two rankings. Averaging rankings across many couples would create a collective preference over private assignments, changing the aggregation problem rather than extending it to a larger population. Treating each couple as a type avoids that mistake, but leaves Theorem 2 unchanged and applied separately to each type.

So I would reject a continuous-complexity mirror of this paper’s results. The honest limit of that case is important: a continuous matching problem for a large population of couples could be a worthwhile project, and the authors’ discussion makes such an application plausible. What the paper does not provide is a named computational result anchoring that project as its mirror.
