The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that historical claims have a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Imagine recurring water-shortage or permit allocations among many firms. A type would specify a firm’s present claim and its full sequence of past claims and awards; mass would be the fraction of claimants with that exact profile. The regime is plausible when standardized claimants are numerous and the number \(\tau\) of distinct profiles is much smaller than the number of firms. Exact shared profiles preserve the paper’s fixed-agent history structure.

The paper itself, however, has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuization. Theorem 1, proved here, says the historical operator preserves several axioms, including anonymity, continuity, order preservation, scale invariance, resource monotonicity, and securement. Theorem 2, also proved here, lists axioms it does not preserve. Theorem 3 characterizes the historical operator by balanced treatment and non-arbitrariness. These are named results, but none asserts that a problem is tractable, hard, or complete. Proposition 1 establishes that the adjustment parameter exists and describes when it is unique; it gives no complexity bound. So these results cannot honestly serve as the computational anchors your criteria require.

A concrete, but unanchored, computational question suggested by the paper is Type-Mass Historical Allocation for the Proportional Rule. An instance consists of a finite support \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to \(1\), current per-claimant claims \(c_\theta\), a rational per-claimant endowment \(e\leq\sum_\theta\mu_\theta c_\theta\), and rational past claims and awards \((c_\theta^s,x_\theta^s)\), with \(0\leq x_\theta^s\leq c_\theta^s\). Each type’s adjusted claim is
\[ \widehat c_\theta=c_\theta+\sum_s(c_\theta^s-x_\theta^s). \]
The question is to compute the current per-claimant awards \(x_\theta\) under the paper’s historical operator applied to the proportional rule. In particular, find \(\lambda\geq0\) and \(x_\theta\) satisfying
\[ x_\theta=\min\!\left\{c_\theta,\; e\,\frac{\widehat c_\theta}{\sum_{\eta\in\Theta}\mu_\eta\widehat c_\eta}+\lambda\right\}, \qquad \sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta x_\theta=e. \]
A solution is the exact rational award for every supported type, with a \(\lambda\) witnessing these conditions. If the denominator is zero, set the proportional tentative awards to zero.

This is a recognizable extension of the paper’s rule: it applies the same history adjustment and final balance-and-boundedness correction to a distribution of claimant types. For this specific proportional-rule problem, I would expect tractability: weighted threshold search over the \(\tau\) types computes the awards, with rational arithmetic polynomial in the input length and number of types. It covers only the computation of this one induced rule. Further questions include whether similarly efficient algorithms exist for other base rules, and whether optimizing over rules subject to historical fairness constraints has a richer complexity landscape.

The soft spot is also the central one: the paper studies the ethical properties and characterization of rules, not the complexity of computing them. This candidate question is plausible as a population-continuous extension, but the paper’s named results do not establish it as a mirror of a computational result, and its proportional instance looks straightforward. Under the stated anchor requirement, the positive case is therefore limited.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 concern which axioms the historical operator preserves; Theorem 3 characterizes the operator axiomatically. Proposition 1 establishes that the adjustment parameter exists and describes uniqueness. None states a complexity claim about an algorithmic problem.

The strongest direct mirror would put complete claim histories into a distribution of types and restate Theorem 3’s characterization using mass-weighted balance. That is a coherent continuous-population model, but its result would still be axiomatic. Continuizing the population does not turn the characterization into a computational question, and the programme explicitly excludes axiomatic continuization as its goal. The same applies to continuous versions of the preservation and non-preservation claims in Theorems 1 and 2.

The proposed proportional-rule problem is also well formed: exact claim-and-history profiles can serve as types, and their masses can represent many identical claimants. Its limitation is its connection to the paper’s results. Computing awards for that fixed rule is an added evaluation task; none of the named results makes a computational claim that the task could mirror. Extending the question to arbitrary standard rules runs into a representation problem: the paper treats a rule as an abstract mapping, with no finite encoding for an algorithm to receive. Restricting to the named classical rules supplies representations, but then it selects a new, specific computational question rather than extracting a complexity result from the paper.

So the positive case establishes a plausible high-multiplicity regime and a clean type-mass formulation. It does not establish a computational mirror of this paper’s contribution. The universal claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile adjacent computational work is harder to defend: the proportional allocation question could be useful on its own. The negative case is strongest—and, under the programme’s stated scope, decisive—when the claim is that this paper offers no worthwhile continuous *mirror*.
