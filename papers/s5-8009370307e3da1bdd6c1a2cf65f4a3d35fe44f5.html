What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror: Proposition 2 and the other numbered propositions state existence, uniqueness, or impossibility claims without computational guarantees or complexity classifications.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that this paper’s finite-population model has a natural continuous-population counterpart. A type is one of the \(m!\) strict rankings of \(m\) alternatives; its mass \(\mu_\pi\) is the fraction of citizens holding ranking \(\pi\). A deliberation step transfers mass between rankings according to the paper’s persuasion groups and truth-resolving update rule. The resulting masses determine scores under the chosen scoring rule. This fits large consultations or deliberative polls where citizens are numerous but their rankings fall into relatively few types.

The nearest result to anchor this in is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It says that with three alternatives and intermediate persuasion cost, a truth-revealing democracy exists and is unique: first form persuasion groups whose members agree on the best alternative, then groups whose members agree on the worst, and finally use antiplurality. The resulting rule always selects the objectively best alternative, whatever the original profile and finite electorate size. This is meaningful evidence that the authors’ model treats people through type counts and procedures over those types, so replacing counts with masses is plausible.

But Proposition 2 is not a computational result: it establishes an existence and uniqueness characterization, not that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. The same is true of the paper’s other numbered propositions. So there are no qualifying computational anchors in this paper, and I would not present Proposition 2 as one.

A precise computational question suggested by it would be: given \(m\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, a correct ranking \(\theta\), and a persuasion distance \(\delta\), find a permitted sequence of maximal \(\delta\)-consistent persuasion partitions and a scoring rule whose final winner is the top alternative in \(\theta\); report failure if none exists. A solution is the sequence and scoring parameter, or a certificate that no such pair works. The expected complexity is open. For \(m=3\), there are only six ranking types, so the problem is small; a substantive complexity question would need \(m\) to vary. That extension would need a careful definition of the update rule and allowed deliberation paths for arbitrary \(m\).

The weak point is that this is a new computational question motivated by the paper, not a continuous version of a computational problem the paper studies. The model is built to prove a social-choice characterization, and its original results already quantify over finite electorates of any size. The continuous formulation may be a sensible new object, but this paper supplies no computational result for it to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the only plausible anchor, and it establishes a characterization, not a computational result. The paper asks whether a deliberation path paired with a scoring rule is truth-revealing; it gives no algorithmic input/output problem, running-time question, or complexity claim. The other propositions likewise concern existence or impossibility. By ChoCo’s stated criterion, that leaves no computational result here to mirror.

A continuous society over ranking types is still a natural representation of a large electorate. But for Proposition 2, replacing counts with rational masses does not create computational work: the claim already ranges over every finite profile and electorate size. The path and scoring rule must work uniformly across those profiles. The masses are not inputs to an optimization the paper asks someone to solve.

The proposed search for a path and scoring rule given \(\mu\) and the objectively correct ranking \(\theta\) changes the question. It tailors a procedure to one known society and known truth; Proposition 2 asks whether a democracy works regardless of the original profile. That proposed search is closer to a new truth-implementation design problem—the paper itself distinguishes truth-implementing from truth-revealing. One could instead ask for an algorithm that finds or classifies universally truth-revealing procedures as the number of alternatives varies, but then the task is a new computational classification of deliberation procedures, with no society distribution as its input. It may be worthwhile follow-on work; it is not a computational mirror supplied by this paper.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s purpose: there is no named computational result to continuize. It does not show that no useful mass-based design problem could ever be built from the model. It shows that doing so would be new research rather than a continuous computational counterpart of the paper’s results.
