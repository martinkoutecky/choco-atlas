The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\), and types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\) and listed Lottery supports \(\mathcal B_t\), does there exist a mass assignment \(x_{t,A}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal B_t}x_{t,A}=\mu_t\) such that, for every \(c\in C\setminus W\), \(\sum_{t,A:\,c\in A,\,A\cap W=\emptyset}x_{t,A}<1/k\)?

The model it lives in

Use one variable \(x_{t,A}\) for the mass of type \(t\) receiving supported ballot \(A\). Maximize a common slack \(\delta\geq 0\) subject to \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal B_t}x_{t,A}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,A:\,c\in A,\,A\cap W=\emptyset}x_{t,A}+\delta\leq 1/k\) for every \(c\in C\setminus W\); answer yes exactly when the optimum is positive.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers fixed-committee IsPossJR under the Lottery model. It leaves aside MaxJR, probability computation, Candidate-Probability, PJR, and EJR.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the support-feasibility formulation remain tractable when each type's ballot support is given compactly rather than listed?
  • What continuum formulation captures a probability-sensitive or typical-outcome version of the question?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror I see is a continuous version of IsPossJR under the Lottery model. A convincing regime is a large civic platform where users approve comments or proposals. Many users can share the same uncertainty type: the same distribution over a small list of possible approval sets, estimated from their engagement patterns or predictions. With millions of users but hundreds or thousands of such types, representing cohorts by population mass is natural. The paper itself motivates uncertainty by noting that a voter can represent a group and that an approval probability can encode the fraction of that group who approves. ([paper, introduction](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19391))

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It establishes that IsPossJR is NP-complete under the Lottery model, even when each voter assigns positive probability to at most three approval sets and the committee size is \(k=n/2\). The problem asks whether a given committee satisfies JR with nonzero probability. ([Theorem 4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19391))

Call the mirror Continuous Lottery-Possible JR. Its input is a candidate set \(C\), a committee size \(k\), a fixed committee \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|=k\), and \(\tau\) types. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a Lottery distribution with positive-probability support \(\mathcal B_t\subseteq 2^C\). The question is whether there is a possible aggregate society in which \(W\) satisfies JR. A certificate assigns mass \(x_{t,A}\ge0\) to each \(A\in\mathcal B_t\), subject to \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal B_t}x_{t,A}=\mu_t\). Each person still has an integral approval set; \(x_{t,A}\) is the share of type \(t\) with ballot \(A\).

For the resulting ballot masses, JR holds exactly when, for every \(c\in C\setminus W\), the mass of unrepresented voters who approve \(c\) is less than \(1/k\). In the certificate variables this is \(\sum_{t,A:\ c\in A,\ A\cap W=\varnothing}x_{t,A}<1/k\). Thus the decision is a linear program: maximize a common slack \(\delta\ge0\), subject to those sums plus \(\delta\) being at most \(1/k\). Answer yes exactly when the optimum is positive. With explicitly listed ballot supports, this takes time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), the total number of listed type-ballot pairs, and the rational input bit length.

This is a meaningful high-multiplicity relaxation of the anchor’s support question. In any finite clone population, assigning each voter a ballot from their type’s positive-probability support gives a profile with nonzero probability, however small that probability is. Conversely, a rational mass assignment with positive JR slack can be scaled by a common denominator to a finite clone population with the same JR outcome. So the mirror preserves the anchor’s “possible” quantifier while allowing cohort mass to divide among its possible ballots. I expect Class A: the continuous problem is in P by the LP above, while the NP-hardness in Theorem 4.1 comes from choosing one complete ballot for each named voter.

The weakest point is that the LP captures support feasibility, not the typical outcome of independent draws: in a large iid population, observed shares concentrate around the lottery probabilities, while IsPossJR counts even extremely unlikely profiles as possible. The paper’s nonzero-probability question supports the support-based interpretation, but an author who wants typical or noticeably likely outcomes would regard that as a different mirror. The claim is also deliberately narrow: it covers fixed-committee IsPossJR under the Lottery model, not MaxJR, Candidate-Probability counting, PJR, or EJR. Natural next questions are whether the stronger axioms admit comparable mass formulations, and how complexity changes when the task asks for substantial probability rather than mere possibility.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchor—Theorem 4.1’s NP-completeness of IsPossJR under the Lottery model—is that its “nonzero probability” condition has fragile meaning at the continuum limit. For any finite number of voters, every profile assembled from positive-probability ballots has positive probability, even if that probability shrinks exponentially with population size. In a continuum of independently uncertain voters, those atypical aggregate splits may have probability zero; the almost-sure aggregate is instead fixed by the lottery probabilities. So the proposed LP answers a support-feasibility question, not whether JR holds with positive probability in a limiting stochastic society.

That objection does not defeat the best mirror, though. The paper itself motivates approval probabilities as fractions of a represented group, and a civic platform could plausibly have large cohorts with identical ballot supports. Under the paper’s existential “possible” quantifier, the LP’s arbitrary mass splits are exactly the limits of finite profiles assembled from those supports. The rare-event issue means this mirror does not preserve probability magnitudes or typical outcomes, but it does preserve the anchor’s yes/no support question. Whether authors accept that as the intended continuous question is the real uncertainty.

So the negative case is weak: there is a coherent high-multiplicity regime, a named computational result, and a tractable mass formulation. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists for this anchor.
