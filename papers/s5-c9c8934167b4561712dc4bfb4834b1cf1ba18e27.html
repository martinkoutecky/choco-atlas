The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(C\), a finite set of complete-ranking voter types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a fixed rule for resolving pairwise-majority ties, let \(T_\mu\) be the induced tournament. Starting from any positive integer urn \(n_0\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{C}\), repeatedly draw three alternatives independently from the current urn distribution, compare them sequentially under \(T_\mu\), and add a ball for the resulting winner. Does the urn distribution \(p_\tau\) converge almost surely, and what is its limit? Theorem 4 says it converges to the unique \(p^*_\mu\) satisfying \(g_\mu(p^*_\mu,q)\geq 0\) for every distribution \(q\in\Delta(C)\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, represented by its positive-mass support and rational type masses. These masses induce pairwise-majority comparisons; the urn state is a distribution over alternatives, and the update reinforces the winner of three sequential comparisons. The target is almost-sure convergence of that urn distribution to the induced tournament game’s optimal strategy.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s three-alternative convergence result and Theorem 7’s two-alternative support and non-convergence results for majority tournaments induced by the electorate; it leaves the auxiliary tournament and Markov-chain results outside this population formulation.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can finite-time bounds for reaching an \(\varepsilon\)-neighborhood of \(p^*_\mu\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\) be given in terms of the number of alternatives, the support size, the mass encoding length, and the minimum nonzero majority margin?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is for a large electorate whose preferences determine the tournament. Let \(C\) be the alternatives and let \(T\) be a finite set of complete rankings of \(C\). The electorate is a distribution \(\mu\) over those rankings, with rational masses \(\mu_t\); a type is one complete ranking, and its mass is the fraction of the electorate holding it. The regime is many voters but relatively few distinct rankings, so the support size \(\tau\) is small compared with the electorate size. Assume every pair has a strict majority, or resolve majority ties by a fixed rule. Those pairwise majorities induce a tournament \(T_\mu\).

This is a natural voting scenario for the paper: its introduction explicitly motivates tournaments with majority politics. The urn is then an adaptive procedure for deciding which alternatives to sample. Its state \(n_\tau\) records the number of balls of each alternative, and the sampling distribution is \(p_\tau(x)=n_\tau(x)/\sum_y n_\tau(y)\). The electorate supplies the comparisons; the urn updates the probability of considering each alternative. Continuity is in the electorate, not in the alternatives or in the urn’s update rule.

The paper has no named computational-complexity result such as a P, NP-hardness, or FPT theorem. Its closest computational anchors are results about the behavior of reinforcement algorithms. Those are still worth mirroring, but the distinction limits what the positive case can claim.

My lead is Continuous-Profile Three-Alternative Reinforcement Convergence, anchored in Theorem 4, proved in this paper. Given \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings that induces \(T_\mu\), and any initial urn \(n_0\in\mathbb{N}_{+}^{C}\), draw three alternatives independently from the current urn distribution, compare them sequentially under \(T_\mu\), and add one ball for the resulting winner. The question is whether the urn’s sampling distribution converges almost surely, and to what. A solution is a proof that it converges to \(p^*_\mu\), the unique tournament-game strategy satisfying \(g_\mu(p^*_\mu,q)\geq 0\) for every distribution \(q\). Theorem 4 states exactly that, for every initial urn and every tournament, three-alternative reinforcement converges almost surely to \(p^*\).

This is the strongest mirror because it preserves the paper’s full learning process and its target, while replacing the primitive tournament input with a continuous high-multiplicity electorate that generates it. The authors should recognize that connection: their result applies unchanged once the electorate’s majority relation is formed. I would expect the convergence question to have the affirmative, tractable outcome in the algorithmic sense established by the theorem. That does not establish polynomial running time or a finite-time approximation guarantee.

A useful second anchor is Theorem 7, also proved here. For the same continuous electorate and initial urn, change the procedure to sample and compare only two alternatives per round. The theorem states that alternatives outside \(\operatorname{supp}(p^*_\mu)\) receive probability tending to zero almost surely; with positive probability the urn distribution has no limit; and if \(\operatorname{supp}(p^*_\mu)=C\), it has no limit almost surely. The corresponding continuous problem asks for those same asymptotic guarantees under two-alternative reinforcement. Its answer is the stated support concentration and nonconvergence behavior. This is a worthwhile companion anchor because it preserves the paper’s central three-versus-two comparison rather than presenting the positive result in isolation. It is a negative convergence result, not a hardness result; the paper’s A/B/C complexity trichotomy does not strictly classify it.

The scope is deliberately narrow: these mirrors cover the paper’s reinforcement results, not its general tournament framework or every result about the optimal strategy. Further questions include finite-time bounds for reaching an \(\varepsilon\)-neighborhood of \(p^*_\mu\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\), and how such bounds depend on \(m\), \(\tau\), and the bit length of \(\mu\). The paper’s theorems do not answer those questions.

The weakest point is that the continuous population matters only through the signs of its pairwise majority margins. Once \(T_\mu\) is constructed, the masses and support size disappear from the dynamics, and the paper’s result already holds for every tournament. So this is a plausible continuous-population formulation of the authors’ voting motivation, but it does not expose a new complexity landscape or show that continuization creates a computational advantage. The positive case rests on a legitimate high-multiplicity electorate and faithful algorithmic mirrors—not on a claim that this paper fills ChoCo’s central complexity gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that the paper proves no computational result in ChoCo’s sense. Theorem 4 and Theorem 7 are theorems about almost-sure behavior of a stochastic learning process. They give no running time, approximation guarantee, or algorithm for a population-level task. The urn may be viewed as an algorithm, but its asymptotic convergence is the object of the analysis; there is no computational problem over societies for the programme to continuize.

The electorate proposed for Theorem 4 is a sensible high-multiplicity model: voter types can be complete rankings, and their masses induce pairwise margins
\[ M_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t-\sum_{t:y\succ_t x}\mu_t. \]
But after those margins determine the tournament, the electorate leaves the process. Its state is a distribution over alternatives, and its updates depend on the tournament alone. Any two profiles inducing the same tournament give exactly the same transition law. Thus the proposed continuous-profile convergence statement is Theorem 4 with \(T\) replaced by \(T_\mu\), preceded by pairwise-majority aggregation. That is a valid restatement, but it introduces no population-level computational question. A finite-time bound depending on majority margins could be interesting; it would be a new stochastic-process analysis, rather than a mirror of Theorem 4’s asymptotic claim.

Theorem 7 has the same problem. Its “support” is the support of \(p^*\) over alternatives, not the support of the electorate over voter types. Substituting \(T_\mu\) gives the same concentration and nonconvergence claims for each induced tournament. The strongest population-sensitive extension would be a control or robustness problem: find the least mass or cost of voter changes that makes a target alternative a Condorcet winner, or changes the support of \(p^*\). That adds an intervention, a cost model, and a target absent from the paper. Theorem 7 also does not classify convergence across all tournaments: it proves almost-sure nonconvergence when \(p^*\) has full support and only positive-probability nonconvergence otherwise. A control problem promising convergence would need a sharper behavioral characterization. If one instead asks only to create a Condorcet winner, the main task is continuous Condorcet control; the urn theorem supplies motivation, not the computational problem.

The negative case is strongest against the two submitted anchors: both carry an electorate only as an upstream encoding of a fixed tournament, and neither is a complexity result. It is weaker against the universal claim. A continuous voter-control problem tied to the learning process could be worthwhile if the programme accepts derivative questions. I would treat that as a new project inspired by this paper, rather than evidence that either theorem has a worthwhile continuous mirror.
