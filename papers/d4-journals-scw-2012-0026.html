What the mirror covers

The range bounds in Theorems 1 and 2 and Proposition 2, and the binary-range characterization in Theorem 3, are structural incentive results. None supplies a computational result to continuize.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible population story exists: households affected by a public bad could be grouped by their complete single-dipped preference order over facility locations. A society would then be a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\mu_t\) the share of households of type \(t\). That is a natural high-multiplicity regime, and it continuizes the population rather than the locations.

But the paper has no eligible computational anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says that any strategy-proof rule on the full single-dipped domain has a range of at most two alternatives. Theorem 2 gives a structural restriction on which alternatives can lie in the range; Proposition 2 bounds the range by four on a restricted domain; and Theorem 3, cited from earlier work, characterizes strategy-proof rules with binary range. These are results about incentives and the structure of rules. None asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithmic result of the kind the requested anchors require.

One could invent a problem about maximizing the range of a strategy-proof rule given \(\mu\), but that would turn the paper’s axiomatic question into a new computational one. It also faces a modeling problem: when each agent has zero mass, an individual’s report cannot change \(\mu\), so ordinary individual strategy-proofness risks becoming vacuous. Preserving the paper’s incentive question would require an additional convention about how an individual deviation changes the population.

So the positive case is limited to the naturalness of a continuous population model for repeated household types. There is no named result here to anchor the required continuous computational problem, and presenting one as the paper’s mirror would overstate the fit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The household story is credible: many residents can share the same complete preference order, so this paper does have a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The objection is not that its agents are inherently individuated. It is that the paper’s named results do not give that population a computational role.

The strongest candidate is Theorem 1. It bounds the range of every strategy-proof rule on the full single-dipped domain, regardless of how often each preference occurs. Theorem 2 likewise restricts the range from the available preference types; Proposition 2 bounds it for a restricted domain. Theorem 3 characterizes binary-range rules through responsiveness and the \(xy\)-based condition. These are results about which rules satisfy an incentive axiom, not algorithmic claims about an input society. Replacing a finite profile with its distribution of rankings leaves the questions they answer unchanged. Asking instead to optimize over rules given \(\mu\), or to maximize welfare under a rule, would add an objective and a computational problem that the paper does not supply.

There is a deeper obstacle to making the incentive results depend on a genuinely continuous population. If a rule depends only on the population distribution, one agent has measure zero: changing that agent’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. Individual strategy-proofness then loses its bite. The natural rescue is to test deviations by coalitions of positive mass, but that changes the paper’s notion of group strategy-proofness, which includes finite coalitions, and supplies no computational task or cost model from this paper. Keeping a finite agent’s influence instead requires retaining an atomic mass such as \(1/n\); that preserves the finite population’s incentive question rather than making society itself the continuous object.

So the strongest negative case is not that no plausible continuum society exists. It is that none of the paper’s results identifies a computational question for that society, and the paper’s central incentive property becomes vacuous under the most direct atomless interpretation. The case is not airtight: a follow-up could develop a positive-mass coalition model and new algorithmic questions. But that would be a new research programme built from the setting, not a worthwhile continuous computational mirror of these results.
