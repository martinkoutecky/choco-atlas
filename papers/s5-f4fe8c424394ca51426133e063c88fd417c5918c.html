What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation concerns MIV's profile-specific metric distortion; the paper's global upper bound and tightness result already use quantities expressed as voter fractions. Computing the profile-specific supremum is additional work not claimed by the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is real but narrow. This paper has no named result classifying a problem as in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its named results are distortion guarantees. I would use those as anchors, while being clear that the computational question below is a natural extension of the paper’s analysis, not a complexity theorem it proves.

Take a large election with many voters but relatively few recurring ballot types. A type is a ranking, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with ranking \(t\); the support size \(\tau\) can be much smaller than the number of voters. For a concrete high-multiplicity metric regime, assume each ballot cohort shares a location and therefore the same candidate-distance profile. The rule sees the ranking masses, while the metric remains hidden and is quantified over when measuring distortion. The lottery over candidates stays as in the paper; the continuum is the population measure \(\mu\).

For an admissible metric \(d\), write
\[ \mathrm{SC}_\mu(c;d)=\sum_t\mu_t d(t,c). \]
The rule is the paper’s MIV applied to \(\mu\): plurality scores are top-choice masses, veto rates are masses ranking a candidate last among those active, and the maximal lottery uses pairwise margins computed from \(\mu\).

Lead anchor — Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper. It states that MIV has metric distortion at most \(5/2\). Its continuous-population form is: for every finite candidate set, every distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, and every consistent metric on the type representatives and candidates, MIV’s expected social cost is at most \(5/2\) times the least candidate social cost.

The question I would hand to a prover is High-Multiplicity MIV Robust Distortion. Given \(C\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and a fixed valid maximal-lottery selection, compute
\[ D_\mu(p)=\sup_{d\models\mu} \frac{\sum_{c\in C}p(c)\,\mathrm{SC}_\mu(c;d)} {\min_{a\in C}\mathrm{SC}_\mu(a;d)}, \qquad p=\tfrac12\bigl(\mathrm{IV}(\mu)+\mathrm{ML}(\mu)\bigr), \]
and return a compatible metric attaining the supremum. This keeps the paper’s rule, ordinal input, metric uncertainty, and social-cost objective intact.

I expect this profile-specific problem to be Class A in the stated homogeneous-cohort regime. MIV can be computed from the \(\tau\) masses; Integrated Veto has at most \(m\) phases, and a maximal lottery is obtained from the pairwise-margin game. For the metric optimization, enumerate a candidate \(a\) that minimizes social cost, scale \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(a;d)=1\), impose \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(a;d)\le \mathrm{SC}_\mu(b;d)\) for every \(b\), and maximize MIV’s expected cost. The metric and ranking-consistency conditions are linear constraints on distances among the \(\tau\) type points and \(m\) candidates. This gives \(m\) rational LPs of polynomial size. That tractability is a deduction for this mirror question, not a result stated in Theorem 5.1; the theorem supplies the bound \(D_\mu(p)\le 5/2\).

Second anchor — Theorem 5.2, also proved in this paper. It states that MIV’s metric distortion is at least \(5/2\). The paper’s witness family has candidates \(a,b,c_1,\ldots,c_r\). For each \(k\), masses \((1/2+\varepsilon)/r\) and \((1/2-\varepsilon)/r\) rank \(c_k\) first, with the former ranking \(b\succ a\) and the latter \(a\succ b\). Under the paper’s compatible metric, the distortion is at least \(5/2-1/r-\varepsilon\), which approaches \(5/2\).

The corresponding Global High-Multiplicity MIV Distortion question is to determine the supremum of \(D_\mu(p)\) over all finite candidate sets, ranking distributions, and compatible metrics. Theorems 5.1 and 5.2 together give its answer: \(5/2\). The lower-bound distributions have finite support and can be rational, so they are directly realizable by large finite electorates as well as by a nonatomic population with the same type masses. This is an exact extremal answer, rather than a hardness-transfer claim.

The authors would recognize these mirrors: they preserve MIV, rankings, compatible metrics, and the social-cost ratio. The paper itself supports the population interpretation by defining its probabilities as fractions of voters and observing that its arguments use those fractions rather than voter counts. Prior high-multiplicity election work also supports the regime’s plausibility. A natural further question is whether a profile-specific optimal lottery, chosen using \(\mu\), can beat MIV’s robust distortion; the paper’s tight bound establishes MIV’s performance, not its optimality among randomized rules.

The weak point is novelty and scope. Every rational \(\mu\) is already a finite profile after scaling, and the paper’s fraction-based proof extends directly to real masses. So the \(5/2\) guarantee is essentially inherited, not a new continuous theorem. Also, the exact LP above uses cohorts with homogeneous metric costs; the paper permits voters with the same ranking to occupy different metric positions. This is a sensible high-multiplicity instance regime, but it does not settle the richer version with heterogeneous hidden costs. The strongest case is therefore a tractable, precise population mirror of this rule’s distortion analysis—not evidence that the paper contains a new complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sharpest objection is that neither anchor is a computational result. Theorem 5.1 proves a distortion guarantee; Theorem 5.2 proves that the guarantee is tight. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem or gives its complexity. The proposed task of computing the exact distortion on a given profile is a new problem, not the continuous form of either theorem.

More fundamentally, these results already work with a distribution over rankings. The paper defines plurality, veto rates, pairwise comparisons, and social cost using fractions of voters, and says its arguments depend on those fractions rather than voter counts. A finite profile maps directly to its empirical distribution \(\mu\). Even the paper’s allowance for voters with the same ranking to occupy different points in the hidden metric does not seem to obstruct this compression: average their distances to each candidate, and average distances between distinct ranking classes. Triangle inequalities and ranking consistency survive averaging, while candidate social costs are preserved. Conversely, a metric on one representative per ranking can be replicated across the voters of that type. So the theorem’s metric-distortion analysis already has the substance of the proposed continuous-population model.

Theorem 5.2 does not supply a separate population-limit result. Its witness uses finitely many ranking types for each fixed \(r\); the limit \(5/2\) comes from increasing the number of candidates \(r\), not from taking the population to a continuum. Its rational masses can be realized by finite electorates.

The strongest better version of the proponent’s case is the profile-specific quantity \(D_\mu\). The proposed LP appears to capture the full metric uncertainty, including heterogeneous metric positions, and could compute that quantity in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length. But that is a new algorithmic question around these theorems, not a mirror of them. It also has the same formulation for a finite profile, whose empirical distribution is \(\mu\). That leaves a plausible high-multiplicity LP problem, but no distinctive continuous question supplied by the paper’s results.

So the negative case is strongest against these two anchors as *continuous mirrors*: their population distribution is already implicit in the paper’s analysis, and the exact per-profile LP is an added question. It is weak as a universal claim. Computing \(D_\mu\) could still be worth studying as a high-multiplicity problem, especially for large elections with few recurring rankings. I cannot honestly rule out that scenario.
