The continuous mirror question

Given a unary Tight Bin Packing instance \(S=\{s_1,\dots,s_m\}\) with \(\sum_i s_i=kB\) and \(s_i\le B\), let \(W\) be the distinct source-item values together with \(B\), and set \(q_w=(|\{i:s_i=w\}|+2\mathbb{1}[w=B])/(k+1)\). Consider two recipient types of masses \(\mu_0=1/(k+1)\) and \(\mu_1=k/(k+1)\), with cost factors \(0\) and \(1\), common utility \(u(b)=\sum_{w\in W}wb_w\), and per-capita supply \(q_w\) of each good type. Does there exist a mass assignment \(\lambda_t(b)\ge0\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{W}\) such that \(\sum_b\lambda_t(b)=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}\lambda_t(b)b_w=q_w\), every pair of bundles in its positive-mass support satisfies \(u(b)\ge u(b'-e_w)\) whenever \(b'_w>0\), and \(\sum_{t,b}\alpha_t\lambda_t(b)u(b)\le kB/(k+1)\)?

The model it lives in

The population lift of Theorem 12's reduction: two cost-factor types with masses \(\mu_0=1/(k+1)\) and \(\mu_1=k/(k+1)\), unary-valued replenishable good types with per-capita supplies \(q_w\), integral bundles, and mass assignments \(\lambda_t(b)\). The objective is average factor-weighted utility, subject to exact supplies and EFx across all bundles in the support.

The objection that survived

The stated LP treats each source item as usable only once, while the population lift permits repeated copies of a commodity in a bundle; multiset configurations appear to repair the gap while retaining unary pricing.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 12's unary restricted-cost hardness family via its Tight Bin Packing reduction. It leaves the other results, including general costs, the full restricted-cost problem, and the approximation bounds, outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one prove the full equivalence between the unary population lift and fractional packing by integral multiset configurations of value \(B\), then solve the resulting configuration LP using exact unbounded-knapsack pricing?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s restricted-cost model in its disaster-relief interpretation. Take a large population of recipient regions, each with the paper’s common additive valuation for supplies. A region’s type records its complete cost profile; in the paper’s factor model, the type is determined by \(\alpha_t\), where receiving bundle \(b\) costs \(\alpha_t u(b)\). Regions of the same type are interchangeable for the purposes of the problem.

Let the population have type masses \(\mu_t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\), summing to \(1\). There are \(\beta\) replenishable good types, with positive integer values \(w_r\), and per-capita supplies \(q_r\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\). A person still receives an integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{\beta}\), with utility \(u(b)=\sum_r w_rb_r\); the mass assigned to different bundles may be fractional.

Call the resulting decision problem Mass-MinCost-EFx. Given \((\mu_t,\alpha_t)\), the supplies \(q_r\), the values \(w_r\), and a budget \(K\), decide whether there are nonnegative masses \(\lambda_t(b)\) such that
\[ \sum_b\lambda_t(b)=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,b}\lambda_t(b)b_r=q_r\quad\text{for every }r, \]
and the total cost
\[ \sum_{t,b}\alpha_t\lambda_t(b)u(b) \]
is at most \(K\). The support of the allocation must be EFx: for any two bundles \(b,b'\) used by positive mass and any \(r\) with \(b'_r>0\),
\[ u(b)\ge u(b'-e_r). \]
Thus every person gets an integral bundle and satisfies the paper’s fairness condition; only the distribution of bundle outcomes within a type is continuous. The objective is the same cost objective, averaged per capita. Positive good values make the relevant bundle set finite: EFx bounds the gap between any two supported bundle values by the largest item value, while their average value is fixed by the supplies.

A plausible regime is many relief zones or communities with standardized valuations and a small number of delivery-cost classes. The number \(N\) of recipients can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of recipient types—indeed, the reduction below uses just two cost types. Supplies scale with the population, as they would in a recurring or large-scale distribution operation. In particular, the type-level average bundle can be fractional even though every recipient’s bundle remains integral.

My anchor is Theorem 12, proved in this paper. It says that \(\minEfx\) under restricted cost functions is NP-hard even when all numbers in the input are encoded in unary. The proof reduces from Tight Bin Packing, which the paper identifies as strongly NP-hard: for \(k\) bins of capacity \(B\), with item sizes summing to \(kB\), it creates \(k+1\) agents, goods of values equal to the item sizes plus two goods of value \(B\), and cost factors \(0\) for one agent and \(1\) for the other \(k\). The budget threshold is \(kB\). A feasible allocation at that threshold exists exactly when the items pack into \(k\) bins.

For the continuous version of this reduction, use masses
\[ \mu_0=\frac1{k+1},\qquad \mu_1=\frac{k}{k+1}, \]
for the zero-cost and unit-cost types. Give each source item per-capita supply \(1/(k+1)\), the two additional \(B\)-valued goods supply \(2/(k+1)\), and set the budget to \(kB/(k+1)\). This is a two-type population with high multiplicity: for any large replication factor \(R\), there are \(R\) low-cost recipients and \(kR\) regular recipients, alongside \(R\) copies of each source item. The source instance’s single named recipients have become cohorts.

I expect the unary hardness to dissolve on this anchored family. The budget forces the regular cohort’s average bundle value to be at most \(B\), while the low-cost cohort’s average must be at least \(2B\). So some low-cost bundle has value at least \(2B\). Since no good is worth more than \(B\), EFx then requires every regular bundle to be worth at least \(B\). Their average is at most \(B\), so every regular bundle in the support must be worth exactly \(B\). The reverse EFx comparisons force the low-cost bundles to have value \(2B\), using two \(B\)-valued goods. What remains is a fractional exact bin packing question: can the source goods be distributed as a mass of integral \(B\)-valued bundle configurations?

Write \(P\) for an integral configuration of source items summing to \(B\), and \(x_P\) for its mass after rescaling by \(k+1\). For distinct source items, the resulting feasibility system is
\[ \sum_{P:\,i\in P}x_P=1\quad\text{for every item }i,\qquad \sum_Px_P=k,\qquad x_P\ge0. \]
This has exponentially many configurations, but its pricing problem is a knapsack dynamic program: find a subset of items of exact total value \(B\) maximizing given dual weights. With unary item values, that pricing problem is polynomial-time solvable; the ellipsoid and separation machinery then gives a polynomial-time algorithm for this continuous anchor family. The fractional configuration masses need not correspond to a partition into \(k\) whole bins, which is precisely why the paper’s discrete reduction does not carry over automatically. I therefore expect Class A for the continuous version of Theorem 12’s unary hardness family. This is a proposed mirror and algorithmic route, not a claim that the paper’s hardness proof establishes the continuous result.

The scope here is deliberately narrow: Theorem 12’s restricted-cost hardness family. The next questions are whether the same configuration approach handles all unary Mass-MinCost-EFx instances, whether binary values introduce weak hardness through pricing, and how the optimum of finite replicated instances relates to the mass optimum.

The weakest point is the scaling story. The reduction’s low-cost type has mass \(1/(k+1)\), and its one-off goods become replenishable supplies when lifted to a population. That is a real co-modification of the allocation setting. A large relief operation with recurring supplies makes it plausible, and the support remains only two recipient types even when the population is large. The other serious limitation is technical: the LP argument above uses the exact structure forced by this reduction. It does not by itself settle the complexity of the full continuous MinCost-EFx problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I don’t think an honest negative case defeats the Theorem 12 anchor. Its strongest vulnerability is a mismatch in the proposed configuration LP: the paper allocates a set of distinct items, each usable once, but the proposed mass model allows \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\beta}\). Scaling each item’s supply does not stop one recipient from receiving multiple copies of the same item type. The regular bundles therefore need not correspond to subsets \(P\), as the argument assumes.

That is a real flaw in the stated derivation, but it has a natural repair. Add a per-recipient cap of one copy of each source item type. Then subset configurations are valid, and unary values give a polynomial-time \(0\)-\(1\) knapsack pricing algorithm. Or keep the uncapped commodity model and use multiset configurations; unary values still permit polynomial-time exact-value pricing. Either version leaves a coherent continuous question about whether the reduction’s discrete hardness survives.

The broader mirror also has a plausible setting: many recipients with common valuations and a small number of delivery-cost profiles, with supplies scaling alongside the population. The theorem is genuinely computational, and the co-scaling of supplies is explicitly allowed by the programme. The necessary bundle cap is a modeling choice the authors could recognize, not a fundamental barrier.

So the negative case can challenge the proponent’s exact LP argument, but not the worthwhile mirror. I would report that flaw and still regard this anchor as surviving.
