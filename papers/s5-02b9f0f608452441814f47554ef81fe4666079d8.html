The case FOR (proponent)

On the anchor rule you set, this paper has no qualifying computational result. Its numbered results are about equilibrium outcomes: Proposition 1 characterizes the unique outcome without redistribution; Corollary 1 gives conditions under which that outcome misses the optimal stopping rule; Proposition 2 says full redistribution makes every renegotiation-proof equilibrium efficient for sufficiently small \(\Delta\); Proposition 3 says that, under any non-collegial voting rule and any \(\hat{\tau}>0\), such an equilibrium can sustain the optimal stopping rule for sufficiently small \(\Delta\); and Proposition 4 gives conditions under which limited redistribution and veto players prevent that outcome. These are proved equilibrium results, not complexity classifications, algorithms, or hardness reductions. Treating any of them as a result “in P” would overstate the paper. So there are zero eligible anchors, and no per-anchor computational mirror to hand to a prover.

There is a plausible population-level setting behind the analysis: a large legislature or organization with many interchangeable members. A type could record a member’s reform payoff \(r_t\), safe payoff \(s_t\), and other bargaining-relevant attributes; \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s population share. A type-level transfer schedule could satisfy \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t=1\), with votes aggregated by mass. Proposition 3 is the closest motivation for asking whether a positive redistribution cap can sustain efficient experimentation in that setting.

The difficulty is that the paper’s random sequential proposal order and individual pivotal votes are central to its equilibrium results. In a continuum population, an individual has zero voting mass, and the paper does not specify how proposal order or pivotality should scale. Defining those rules would be part of a new model, not a direct continuization. The paper’s limit \(\Delta\to0\) concerns time, while its continuous tax and transfer choices concern policy; neither makes the society continuous. Thus the strongest honest positive claim is that the paper motivates a new high-multiplicity bargaining project. It does not supply a named computational result for the requested mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Under ChoCo’s computational anchor rule, this paper supplies no eligible result. Propositions 1–4 and Corollary 1 characterize equilibrium outcomes or establish when an equilibrium can sustain the planner’s stopping rule. They do not give a complexity classification, algorithm, or hardness reduction. The planner’s stopping-rule calculation is an analytic benchmark, not a computational result. That is the strongest case against a mirror tied to this paper: no named computational result has been identified for one.

Proposition 3 is the best candidate for a stronger case. A large legislature with a distribution \(\mu\) over members’ payoff types \((r_t,s_t,\ldots)\), a mass-based voting quota, and type-level transfers could motivate asking whether a positive redistribution cap \(\hat{\tau}\) supports efficient experimentation—or what the minimum such cap is. A high-multiplicity legislature is a plausible setting; I would not reject it just because members have different payoffs.

But that question would require a new bargaining model. Proposition 3’s construction uses finite decisive coalitions that rotate, an individually identified proposer order, and the threat that a particular member loses access to future redistributed revenue. A population distribution alone does not specify how those strategic roles and coalition changes work in the continuum. One could supply mass-level rules, but then computing the minimum \(\hat{\tau}\) would be a new computational question about a newly specified equilibrium model. The paper neither poses that problem nor offers the LP or pricing structure that would make it a natural ChoCo mirror. Its limit \(\Delta\to0\) concerns time, not population.

That makes the negative case strong under the programme’s anchor criterion, but weaker as the universal claim requested. A computational study of high-multiplicity bargaining might be worthwhile on its own. I cannot honestly conclude from this paper that no continuous scenario could be worthwhile; I can conclude that none of its results, including Proposition 3, currently anchors a computational continuous mirror.
