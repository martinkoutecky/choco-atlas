The continuous mirror question

Given types \(T\) with rational capacities \(\mu_t \ge 0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), nonnegative rational attribute vectors \(q_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^d\), and rational \(A,b\), choose masses \(x_t\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) satisfying \(A\sum_{t\in T}x_tq_t\ge b\), while minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T}x_t\); report infeasibility if no such choice exists.

The model it lives in

A continuum of interchangeable campaign contributors, represented by a finite support of types with mass \(\mu_t\) and shared attributes \(q_t\). The decision variable \(x_t\) is recruited mass, the objective is total recruited mass, and the winning requirements are linear inequalities on aggregate attributes.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers linear-threshold coalition search under a minimum-mass objective; it leaves the vertex characterization, the claimed enumeration method, and nonlinear or nonmonotone constraints aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What integrality or rounding guarantees relate the minimum-mass optimum to the discrete minimum coalition for a population with type counts \(n_t\)?
  • How does the problem’s complexity change when the attribute dimension \(d\) is part of the input?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is the paper’s search for a minimal winning coalition under linear constraints. Theorem 3, proved in this paper, claims that linear constraints make this search efficient: the winning region is navigable through its boundary; minimal winning coalitions correspond to vertices defined by tight constraints; with \(r\) constraints in fixed attribute dimension \(d\), there are at most \(\binom{r}{d}\) such vertices; and nonnegative attributes make winning monotone under adding players, so greedy removal can produce a minimal winner. The paper’s abstract and introduction mention NP-hardness for general simple games, but no numbered result here proves that hardness, so I would not use it as an anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large campaign drawing from a platform’s pool of opt-in creators. Each creator type has a fixed vector of reach across audience segments, and creators of that type share the same relevant terms. The pool could contain many creators but relatively few such profiles. A mass \(\mu_t\) then represents the fraction of the eligible pool with profile \(t\). This is a population mirror: the selected mass is fractional because the cohort is large, while reach requirements remain linear constraints on the selected population.

I would hand a prover this problem, which I’ll call Minimum-Mass Conditional Coalition. The input is a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), nonnegative rational attribute vectors \(q_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^d\), and rational \(A,b\) specifying the winning condition \(A\sum_{t\in T}x_tq_t\ge b\). The question is to find \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) satisfying that condition and minimizing \(\sum_t x_t\), or report that no feasible choice exists. The output is the selected mass of each type. With binary-encoded rational input, this is a linear program, solvable in time polynomial in the input size, including the support size \(\tau=|T|\), number of constraints, dimension, and bit length. I expect this mirror to be tractable.

This formulation has a direct foothold in the paper: Section 6 itself writes an LP minimizing \(\sum_i x_i\) subject to linear winning constraints and \(0\le x_i\le1\). In the continuous version, those variables have a natural meaning as recruited mass from each type. A mass-minimum winner is also minimal under coordinatewise deletion: any strictly smaller winning mass profile would have lower total mass. So the question preserves the paper’s coalition-formation objective while making fractional selection meaningful. For a discrete population with \(n_t\) agents of type \(t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and \(x_t=z_t/n\) translates selected counts \(z_t\) into mass; population-scaled thresholds then give the matching normalized constraints.

My weakest point is that this is closer to the paper’s LP-based search for a small coalition than to its formal claim about *inclusion-minimal* coalitions. Those concepts need not agree, and the paper’s geometric justification has serious gaps. In particular, a minimal coalition’s aggregate attribute vector need not be a vertex of the winning polyhedron: with vectors \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) and winning constraints requiring both coordinates to be at least \(1/2\), the coalition containing both players is inclusion-minimal, but its aggregate \((1,1)\) is not a vertex of the winning region. The paper’s enumeration bound also depends on \(d\) being fixed, and its fractional LP does not generally return an integral coalition. These issues weaken Theorem 3 as a discrete algorithmic result. The continuous LP mirror survives them because its decision variable is mass and its objective is explicitly minimum mass; its tractability follows from the LP formulation, not from the claimed vertex correspondence.

The scope here is just the paper’s monotone, linear-constraint coalition search and its LP formulation. Natural follow-up questions are whether the normalized discrete optimum converges to the continuous optimum, what integrality or rounding guarantees hold, and how complexity changes with nonlinear or nonmonotone constraints. This mirror does not continuize outcomes, time, or axioms; it makes the population itself continuous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the paper’s advertised geometric result does not support the proposed mirror. Its claim that minimal winning coalitions correspond to vertices is false: with players \(q_1=(1,0)\), \(q_2=(0,1)\) and constraints \(x_1\ge \tfrac12,\ x_2\ge \tfrac12\), the coalition containing both players is inclusion-minimal, but its aggregate \((1,1)\) is not a vertex of the winning region \([\tfrac12,\infty)^2\). The paper’s vertex count therefore does not justify its claimed search method.

That undermines the anchor, but not the better mirror. A minimum-mass coalition over type capacities is a coherent problem, and its optimum is inclusion-minimal under coordinatewise deletion. The paper’s Section 6 already writes a fractional inclusion LP, so the mirror closely tracks a relaxation the authors themselves proposed. That makes the paper a weaker source for a new computational result; it does not make the population question ill-posed. The finite-player LP and a population distribution over types remain distinct models.

Nor does the proposed creator-pool scenario fail the high-multiplicity test. If creators with the same audience vector and terms are interchangeable for the model, their available mass is a natural type capacity. The paper does not establish that such a regime is common, but the same additive-threshold problem could plausibly describe other large pools of interchangeable contributors. Requiring types to include every problem-relevant attribute does not defeat that construction.

So the negative case is a priority objection: this paper’s claimed vertex insight is unsound, and its strongest population mirror is close to its own fractional LP formulation. That may make it a lower-yield candidate than a paper whose continuous version exposes a new structural or pricing problem. But those points do not show that no worthwhile mirror exists. Under the programme’s criteria, this anchor survives the stronger challenge; I can’t honestly argue the universal negative.
