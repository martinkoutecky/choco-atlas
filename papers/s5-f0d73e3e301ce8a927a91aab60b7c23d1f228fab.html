The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), home categories \(X\) with rational supplies \(q_x\), and each type’s encoded general unit-demand preference over \(X\cup\{0\}\) and payments, compute an equilibrium price vector \(p\) and allocation masses \(a_{t,x}\) at the coordinatewise least equilibrium price vector. Require \(\sum_{x\in X}a_{t,x}\le \mu_t\), \(\sum_{t\in T}a_{t,x}\le q_x\), and \(a_{t,x}>0\) only when \(x\) is demanded by type \(t\) at prices \(p\); any unassigned mass must demand \(0\), and any unused supply must have price zero. For a finite input encoding, represent preferences by rational piecewise-linear utilities that are strictly decreasing in payment and need not be quasi-linear.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic assignment market with finitely many complete agent types carrying masses \(\mu_t\), category supplies \(q_x\) per capita, type-category allocation masses \(a_{t,x}\), and prices \(p_x\); the target is an equilibrium allocation at the coordinatewise least equilibrium price vector.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 1’s MPE-finding task; it leaves the paper’s structural characterizations and its strategy-proofness and stagewise and stepwise incentive results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of TM-MPE in the category count \(r\), support size \(\tau\), utility-description size, and binary input length \(L\)?
  • Can a type-level algorithm compute least equilibrium prices when a type’s mass splits across tied demands, and can the paper’s connectedness structure help?
  • What exact relationship does common-denominator replication give between the distributional solution and finite-market MPEs, including the effect of encoding size?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a large housing allocation market: tens of thousands of applicants and homes, but relatively few applicant types and housing categories. A type describes everything relevant about an applicant—preferences over categories, income effects, and budget-related preferences—so people in a type are interchangeable for the model. Homes in a category share the relevant features. The number of people and homes can be very large while the numbers of types and categories remain modest. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s assignment market, not a claim that every assignment market is large.

My anchor is Corollary 1, proved in this paper: the Serial Vickrey mechanism finds a minimum price equilibrium in finitely many steps, with agents reporting finite-dimensional prices finitely many times. This is an algorithmic result about the underlying problem of finding an MPE. The paper does not give a polynomial-time bound or a P/NP classification, so the corollary must not be presented as one.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Type-Mass Minimum-Price Equilibrium (TM-MPE). An instance has a finite set \(T\) of applicant types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a finite set \(X\) of home categories with rational per-capita supplies \(q_x\). Each type \(t\) has a complete unit-demand preference over \(X\cup\{0\}\) and payments. For a finite computational encoding, represent those preferences by rational piecewise-linear utilities \(u_t(x,p)\), strictly decreasing in payment \(p\), but not necessarily quasi-linear or sharing a common money effect. The support size \(\tau=|T|\), category count \(r=|X|\), representation size, and binary input length are the relevant parameters.

The decision variables are prices \(p_x\ge 0\) and assignment masses \(a_{t,x}\ge 0\). Each type’s assigned mass is at most \(\mu_t\); total mass assigned to category \(x\) is at most \(q_x\). Positive assignment from \(t\) to \(x\) requires that \(x\) be among type \(t\)’s most-preferred categories at the posted prices. Any unassigned mass must demand the outside option, and any unused supply must have price zero. A solution is an equilibrium price vector and a mass allocation satisfying those conditions, with \(p\) coordinatewise no greater than any other equilibrium price vector. There is no scalar objective: the target is the least equilibrium price vector, just as in the paper. The task is to return this solution, or report that the instance has no such equilibrium.

This is still the authors’ problem in its essentials: unit demand, heterogeneous goods, monetary prices, general preferences with income effects, and a minimum-price equilibrium. The fractional variables count how much of each type receives each whole home category; they do not give an individual a fractional home. With rational masses and supplies, a common-denominator replication also gives the direct bridge back to a finite market.

I would tentatively expect the computational question to be tractable (Class A), because aggregation removes the need to represent every applicant separately, and at fixed prices the assignment part is a mass-flow feasibility problem. The open work is finding the least supporting prices efficiently when preferences are general. Further questions include whether runtime can be polynomial in \(r,\tau\), the preference-description size, and \(L\); whether discrete MPE prices converge to the continuous answer under replication; and whether a serial, type-level price-report process can mirror the paper’s mechanism.

The mirror covers Corollary 1’s MPE-finding task. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s incentive results or establish continuous strategy-proofness.

My weakest point is the anchor: “finitely many steps” says nothing about polynomial runtime, and the paper’s mechanism uses individual agents and connectedness paths. Extending that construction to type masses may require new arguments, especially when mass splits among tied choices. The piecewise-linear input format is also a computational restriction on the paper’s broader preference domain. Those are substantial gaps, but they do not make the mirror artificial: a large housing cohort with repeated complete preference types is a sensible regime, and the proposed problem preserves the paper’s MPE question rather than replacing it with an easier welfare objective.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the sole anchor, Corollary 1, is that “in a finite number of steps” is not a complexity bound. The paper establishes that its Serial Vickrey mechanism finds an MPE, but gives no running time in the number of agents, objects, or preference-description bits. So the corollary should not be treated as a tractability result that the continuous version could inherit.

There is also a real gap between the mechanism and the proposed mirror. The mechanism elicits reports from named agents and uses paths of individually connected agents. In a type-mass market, one type’s mass may split across several demanded categories; designing a type-level process that handles those splits is new work. The rational piecewise-linear utility encoding also narrows the paper’s broad preference domain, and grouping homes into categories discards distinctions that matter in many housing markets.

Those points weaken the claim that the paper’s mechanism has already been continuized. They do not defeat the better version of the mirror: computing minimum-price equilibrium for a large market with repeated complete preference types and category supplies that scale with population. That remains a sensible assignment market, and rational masses and supplies permit an exact finite replication. The paper does not establish that the compressed instance can be solved efficiently, but asking whether it can is a meaningful computational question. Replication is the high-multiplicity bridge ChoCo is meant to study, not a collision that makes the question disappear.

So the negative case is weak at its central burden. I can argue against calling Corollary 1 a complexity classification, and against claiming the existing individual-level mechanism directly handles mass splits. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror of its MPE-finding problem.
