The case FOR (proponent)

There is no compliant anchor in this paper. Its only numbered result, Theorem 1, is proved here and says that PAV and SeqCCAV satisfy JR; it does not assert a computational complexity result. Section 4 does discuss coNP-completeness of checking EJR and PJR, but only in unnumbered prose, citing results from elsewhere ([1, 4]). So I cannot present those claims as the numbered anchors your instructions require.

The strongest substantive candidate is a continuous version of checking whether a given committee satisfies EJR. An instance consists of an alternative set \(A\), committee size \(k\), a committee \(W\subseteq A\) of size \(k\), and a list of \(\tau\) approval types \(S\subseteq A\), each with rational mass \(\mu_S\), where the masses sum to \(1\). A type is the complete approval ballot; its mass is the fraction of the electorate submitting it. The question is whether \(W\) satisfies continuous EJR. A violating coalition can be represented by submasses \(x_S\leq\mu_S\): for some \(\ell\in[k]\), the selected types must all approve a common set \(Q\subseteq A\) of size \(\ell\), all must approve fewer than \(\ell\) members of \(W\), and their total mass must be at least \(\ell/k\). Equivalently, a violation exists exactly when, for some \(\ell,Q\),

\[ \sum_{\substack{S\supseteq Q\\|S\cap W|<\ell}}\mu_S\ \geq\ \frac{\ell}{k}. \]

The answer is yes or no; a no-instance has a witness \((\ell,Q)\) and the eligible submass. This changes the population measure while leaving the committee as a size-\(k\) set, so it stays within the programme’s scope.

A plausible regime is a large professional association electing a committee from a fixed slate. Many members may submit the same approval ballot, giving \(n\) far larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct ballots. The mirror is especially natural here because EJR is already stated in terms of voter groups and the entitlement threshold \(n/k\); in the continuous form, that threshold becomes the mass \(\ell/k\). The authors should recognize it as their verification question expressed for a population distribution.

If the paper’s unnumbered, cited claim is admitted as a fallback anchor, the expected classification is Class B: hardness transfers. A discrete profile with \(n_S\) voters of type \(S\) maps to \(\mu_S=n_S/n\), and the continuous violation condition then agrees with the discrete one. The paper says EJR/PJR verification is coNP-complete and attributes that result to [1, 4]; from that claim, EJR verification in this mirror is coNP-hard. It is also in coNP: a violation certificate \((\ell,Q)\) can be checked by summing the listed rational masses. Thus the continuous problem would be coNP-complete. Further questions include whether hardness persists for bounded \(\tau\), and whether parameters such as \(k\), \(\tau\), or ballot size yield fixed-parameter algorithms.

The weakest point is also decisive: this is not a numbered computational anchor from the paper, so it cannot meet the stated anchor requirement. Substantively, the mirror is nearly an exact weighted restatement of EJR verification; it offers a clean high-multiplicity problem, but not an evident continuous-optimization gain.

Substantively, though, that is a narrow objection. With approval sets as types and rational masses \(\mu_S\), continuous EJR verification asks whether, for every \(\ell\) and every \(\ell\)-element set \(Q\),
\[ \sum_{\substack{S\supseteq Q\\ |S\cap W|<\ell}}\mu_S < \frac{\ell}{k}. \]
A violation has a concise certificate \((\ell,Q)\). This is a coherent high-multiplicity problem: it fits large electorates or associations with many members sharing ballots, and it tracks no individual identities.
