The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a valid computational anchor in this paper. Its named theorems establish price-of-anarchy bounds, not complexity or algorithmic results for a problem the paper studies.

The closest candidate is Theorem 1, proved here using Lemmas 6 and 11: for egalitarian profit sharing with weighted coverage utilities, the worst-case ratio of optimal social welfare to welfare at a pure Nash equilibrium is exactly \(1.6\). Theorem 2 bounds that ratio for marginal-gain sharing, and Theorems 3–5 give exact bounds for other sharing rules and utility settings. These are substantive results, but they do not assert that a problem is hard, tractable, or approximable. The paper’s reference to hardness of approximation in [11] helps establish a lower bound; it is not a named computational result about the utility game proved here.

A plausible population story would be a large workforce with a small number of recurring skill profiles, choosing among employers. Types would be skill sets, mass the fraction of workers with each profile, and decisions allocations of that mass across teams. One could then ask about equilibrium welfare in a continuum-agent version. But that question changes the paper’s atomic Nash equilibrium into a continuum equilibrium, with new issues around task coverage when a team has zero mass. More fundamentally, it asks for an equilibrium-efficiency bound—the analytic kind of question the ChoCo scope excludes.

So there is a plausible high-multiplicity story, but no qualifying named anchor for a ChoCo computational mirror. Theorem 1 is the nearest result, not a sound basis for claiming the kind of mirror requested.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a sound anchor, even though it is not itself a complexity theorem. It identifies a concrete computational object: the worst equilibrium welfare relative to the optimum in an egalitarian coverage game. A continuous mirror can ask how hard it is to compute that quantity—or to find a worst-welfare equilibrium—when the workforce is given as rational masses of skill types. The theorem supplies the objective and equilibrium concept to study; the mirror asks for their algorithmic complexity.

The high-multiplicity story fits the model closely. A type is a worker’s task-capability set, with any relevant costs or parameters included. Many workers can share that type, and the objective depends on which tasks each team covers, not on worker identities. For masses \(x_{r,t}\) assigned from type \(r\) to team \(t\), task \(s\) has load \(q_{s,t}=\sum_{r:s\in S_r}x_{r,t}\), and team welfare is \(v_s\) whenever \(q_{s,t}>0\). One can ask for the least welfare among stable mass allocations and compare it with the optimal coverage welfare.

Coverage at zero load makes the payoff definition delicate, but the paper itself supplies a natural scale: give each worker mass \(\epsilon=1/n\), where \(n\) is the population size. A worker’s equal share of a task with load \(q\) is \(v_s/q\); after joining an empty team, the load is \(\epsilon\). This is the finite game’s payoff multiplied by \(n\), so best responses are preserved. Studying the equilibrium computation as \(\epsilon\) shrinks gives a precise high-multiplicity bridge and makes the boundary behavior part of the question, rather than a reason to discard it.

The proponent is right that Theorem 1’s stated contribution is an efficiency bound, not an algorithm. But that distinction does not erase the mirror: the bound concerns an optimization over equilibria and allocations, and its continuous version yields a concrete search and decision problem over type masses. Whether that problem admits efficient algorithms, and how its answer relates to the finite-population theorem, is a worthwhile computational question.
