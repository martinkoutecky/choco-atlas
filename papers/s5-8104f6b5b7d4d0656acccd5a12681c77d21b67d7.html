What the mirror covers

The paper’s results concern enumerating preference functions and recovering leximin or leximax indifference classes; the proposed agenda-ranking task covers threshold aggregation over mass profiles but leaves the recovery algorithm’s canonical index problem aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a mass version of threshold ranking for a large population of evaluators. It is narrow: the paper gives a real algorithmic result, but no named theorem says its problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. The closest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It gives a recursive way to recover a vector’s grade multiplicities from its threshold ordinal number. More precisely, for \(0\le k\le m-2\), let \(n_0=n\ge n_1\ge\cdots\ge n_k\), and let
\[ L_k=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{n_j+m-j-1}{m-j},\qquad L_0=0. \]
If the first \(k\) grade multiplicities are \(v_j=n_{j-1}-n_j\), then for \(0\le i\le n_k\), the condition \(v_{k+1}=n_k-i\) is equivalent to
\[ \Phi(x)\in \left[ L_k+\binom{i+m-k-1}{m-k}+1,\; L_k+\binom{i+m-k-1}{m-k-1} \right]. \]
Once \(n_1,\ldots,n_{m-2}\) are known, the remaining multiplicities follow from
\[ v_m=\Phi(x)-L_{m-2}-1,\qquad v_{m-1}=n_{m-2}-v_m. \]
This is a constructive inversion result, not a stated complexity bound.

A continuous problem I would put to a prover is Mass Threshold Tier Retrieval. An instance consists of a finite agenda \(A\), \(m\) grades, a finite set \(T\subseteq[m]^A\) of evaluator types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and a tier number \(k\). Each type records an evaluator’s grades for every alternative. For \(a\in A\) and grade \(j\), set
\[ p_{a,j}=\sum_{t\in T:\,t(a)=j}\mu_t. \]
Rank alternatives by lexicographically *minimizing* \((p_{a,1},\ldots,p_{a,m-1})\); equal vectors are indifferent, and the last grade’s mass is determined by the others. Number the distinct tiers from worst to best. The task is to return the \(k\)th tier and its grade-mass vector, which compactly represents its indifference class.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many reviewers rate a fixed set of proposals using a shared rubric. Reviewers with the same complete rating vector are one type; the population may be enormous while the support \(\tau=|T|\) stays moderate. The decision is the tier to return, and the ordering objective is the paper’s threshold rule applied to grade shares. It is computable in polynomial time in \(|A|,\tau,m\), and the input bit length: aggregate the masses, compare the resulting vectors, and sort the alternatives. That tractability is a consequence of the proposed model, not a complexity result proved by the paper. The mirror covers threshold ranking and indifference, not the paper’s full recursive reconstruction theorem.

The authors have a good reason to recognize the connection: they explicitly allow coordinates to stand for agents or criteria, and the threshold rule depends only on grade multiplicities. A mass histogram is the direct high-multiplicity counterpart of those multiplicities. The regime is also about a real evaluator population, rather than treating fractional outcomes as if they continuized the society.

The weak point is substantial. The paper’s ordinal number indexes all grade histograms for fixed \(n,m\); the proposed tier number indexes only the alternatives on a supplied agenda. In the continuous simplex of mass profiles there is no corresponding canonical scalar ordinal number, so this does not preserve the paper’s central “recover the class from its ordinal number” task. The positive case is therefore for a continuous version of the underlying threshold ranking, not a faithful continuization of its main inversion problem. Further questions about continuous class recovery would first need a defensible replacement for that finite canonical ordinal index.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The evaluator-population scenario is credible. A large review panel using a shared \(m\)-grade rubric can be modeled by types that record each reviewer’s grades across the agenda, with rational mass for each type. Aggregating those masses gives the grade shares for every alternative.

That makes Mass Threshold Tier Retrieval a sensible continuous aggregation task, but it does not mirror Theorem 3’s central computation. Given the type masses, one sums them to obtain each alternative’s grade-share vector, compares those vectors, and sorts the agenda. This takes polynomial time in the agenda size, support size, grades, and input bit length. The task’s \(k\) indexes a tier among the supplied alternatives. In the paper, \(\Phi(x)\) indexes a grade histogram among all histograms for fixed \(n,m\), and Theorem 3 recovers that histogram from its index. The proposed task ranks alternatives; it does not invert the paper’s enumerating preference function.

The strongest replacement would preserve that inversion question. Replace each finite histogram by a mass vector \(q\) in the grade-share simplex, order those vectors by the continuous threshold rule, and ask for the vector at a given ordinal position. For \(m\ge 3\), this loses the paper’s key input: the continuous lexicographic order has no canonical ordinal position of the finite kind used by \(\Phi\). Its domain is not well-ordered, and the paper itself notes that leximin on a continuous multidimensional domain has no real-valued utility representation. Encoding the mass vector itself as a lexicographic tuple supplies the answer as the input. Choosing an external measure to define ranks would make the rank depend on that choice. Restricting masses to a finite denominator grid restores ordinal indices, but also restores the discrete histogram problem.

There is a special case at \(m=2\): the mass vector has one degree of freedom, so its share directly supplies a scalar rank. That makes recovery immediate. More elaborate computational questions could add constraints on feasible alternatives or an optimization objective over mass profiles, but those ingredients are absent from the paper’s reconstruction problem. They would motivate a related project rather than continue its main result.

So the negative case rests on fidelity, not on the population model: a continuous population of evaluators makes sense, while the paper’s distinctive computation relies on a finite canonical ranking of histograms. The case is not airtight—constrained threshold optimization may be worthwhile in its own right—but the proponent’s tier-sorting task does not establish a worthwhile continuous mirror of Theorem 3.
