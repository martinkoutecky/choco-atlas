The case FOR (proponent)

Under the prompt’s anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational result: it does not classify a computational problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, FPT, or similar. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 establish asymptotic properties of mechanisms; Theorem 3 bounds revenue and provision probability. They are important results, but they are not computational-complexity anchors.

The closest result for a continuous-population extension is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that the maximum total expected payment over incentive-compatible, individually rational mechanisms is \(O(\sqrt n)\): formally, \(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{(q_n,\{t_i^n\})}\mathbb E[\sum_i t_i^n(V)]/\sqrt n<\infty\). Consequently, if \(c_n/\sqrt n\to\infty\), every incentive-compatible, individually rational, eventually ex-ante budget-balanced mechanism has provision probability tending to zero, with \(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P(q_n(V)=1)/(\sqrt n/c_n)<\infty\). This is the strongest nearby anchor because it concerns the pivotality and revenue limit that a continuum version would change.

A concrete extension would be Atomless Public-Good Feasibility. The instance is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite set of valuation types \(T\subseteq[0,\bar v]\), with \(0\in T\), and a positive cost \(\kappa\), where \(\sum_{v\in T}\mu_vv>\kappa\). Think of a very large community whose residents fall into a modest number \(\tau=|T|\) of valuation types, with each type occupying mass \(\mu_v\). A direct mechanism observes the reported type distribution \(\nu\), chooses a provision probability \(q(\nu)\in[0,1]\), and charges a reporter \(r\) an expected transfer \(p(r,\nu)\). The question is whether there are such functions with positive provision at the truthful distribution, \(q(\mu)>0\), while satisfying dominant-strategy truthfulness, ex-post individual rationality, and budget balance:
\[ v q(\nu)-p(v,\nu)\ge v q(\nu)-p(r,\nu),\qquad v q(\mu)-p(v,\mu)\ge0,\qquad \sum_{v\in T}\mu_vp(v,\mu)-\kappa q(\mu)\ge0. \]
Here an individual has zero mass, so changing their report does not change the public-good probability. Truthfulness therefore forces \(p(v,\nu)\) to be the same for every report \(v\). Individual rationality for type \(0\) makes that transfer nonpositive; the budget constraint then forces \(q(\mu)=0\). So this mirror has a simple impossibility answer even though provision is socially efficient at \(\mu\). Its direction is a continuum-specific impossibility, not a computational-hardness result.

This is at least a plausible companion to Theorem 3: it asks what happens to the same public-good, incentive, and budget tension when the society is represented by type masses and individual pivotality vanishes. It covers the revenue and provision side of the paper, not its positive AMT mechanism results. The authors might recognize the connection, since the paper emphasizes pivotal probability; they also distinguish their finite-\(n\) asymptotic model from continuum-agent models, so they may regard this as an adjacent question rather than a mirror of their result.

That distinction is the weak point. Theorem 3’s \(O(\sqrt n)\) revenue comes from finite random samples of valuations; the atomless model removes that sampling variation altogether. And, under the requested anchor standard, Theorem 3 does not supply a computational-complexity result. So there is a concrete continuous-population question here, but the paper does not provide a valid named computational anchor for the requested case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the only serious anchor, but it is not a computational result: it bounds revenue over a class of mechanisms and derives a provision-probability limit. Theorems 1 and 2 likewise prove asymptotic properties, not the complexity of an algorithmic problem. None supplies the named computational problem a ChoCo mirror is meant to continue.

The proposed atomless feasibility question exposes the deeper problem. In a society described by a distribution of reports, one agent’s deviation changes no mass, so it cannot change the public-good probability. Dominant-strategy truthfulness then forces that agent’s expected payment to be the same for every report: with allocation unchanged, no type can prefer one report’s payment to another. Since the paper’s valuation domain includes zero, ex-post individual rationality makes this common payment non-positive. Total receipts are therefore non-positive, and with positive cost a balanced budget requires zero provision. Randomizing provision does not change the argument. If the type domain instead has a positive minimum valuation, the only new possibility is a uniform charge capped by that minimum; feasibility reduces to a direct comparison with cost.

That is a continuum degeneration in the relevant sense: the atomless model removes individual pivotality, and the incentive and participation conditions collapse the mechanism choice to a constant-payment check. The feasibility question has no pricing problem or algorithmic landscape to investigate. The exact sampling model that restores pivotality is the paper’s finite-\(n\) model; treating the valuation distribution \(F\) as the population itself confuses uncertainty about finite agents’ values with a population of type masses. Treating a positive-mass bloc as the strategic agent would change the strategic unit and the paper’s independent individual-incentive problem.

This is a strong case against the proposed mirror, not a proof that no nearby finite-type mechanism-design problem could be worthwhile. Such a problem might be interesting, but retaining finite pivotal agents or replacing them with strategic blocs would make it a different target, rather than a continuous-population computational mirror of Theorem 3.
