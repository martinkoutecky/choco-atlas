What the mirror covers

Theorem 1, Theorems 2–3, and Corollaries 2–4 are axiomatic characterizations; no computational result is covered by a mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, says that under citizen sovereignty and Condition 1, a social choice function is strategy-proof if and only if it is unanimous. Theorems 2 and 3 give related equivalences with Maskin monotonicity. Corollaries 2–4 apply those characterizations to Nash implementation. These are axiomatic results: none asserts that a computational problem is tractable, hard, or belongs to a complexity class.

The nearest possible mirror would take complete single-peaked preference relations as types, let \(\mu\) give each type’s population share, and ask whether the strategy-proofness–unanimity characterization still holds for allocation rules over that population. But that would be a new axiomatic question, not a continuous computational problem anchored in a named result of this paper. It would also require care: a single agent’s report has a nonzero effect in the paper’s finite society, while an individual in a nonatomic population has zero mass. And the paper’s infinitely divisible good is continuity of the outcome, not of the population.

So I can’t make a positive case that satisfies the requested anchor and prover-ready computational-question requirements. The central limitation is the paper’s subject matter, not the plausibility of high multiplicity for private-good economies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The population side of a continuous version is quite plausible. Let \(\mu\) be a finite-support distribution over complete single-peaked preference types, scale the resource supply to a per-capita amount \(\omega\), and assign each type \(t\) a per-capita allocation \(x_t(\mu)\), with \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t(\mu)=\omega\). Individual strategy-proofness need not become vacuous: even if one report does not change \(\mu\), it can change which type’s allocation that individual receives. So neither the economic setting nor the individual incentive question rules out a continuous model.

The obstacle is what the paper actually establishes. Theorem 1 characterizes strategy-proofness by unanimity under citizen sovereignty and Condition 1; Theorems 2 and 3 give related axiomatic equivalences. Corollaries 2–4 characterize implementation using those properties. None states a computational problem or gives a computational result to continue. Recasting any of them over \(\mu\) would still ask whether axioms are equivalent, not how hard it is to compute an outcome, manipulation, or implementation.

One could create a computational problem around this setting—for example, find the least mass that must change reports to alter an allocation. But that requires specifying a rule, a cost for changing types, and an encoding of the rule and preferences. The paper supplies none of these. Alternatively, one could study verification for a chosen class of piecewise-linear rules, but that class and its decision problem would be new modelling choices, not a computational mirror of the paper’s results.

So the proponent’s closest candidate—a continuous version of Theorem 1—is coherent but axiomatic. The implementation corollaries do not repair that gap: their characterization could support a later computational project once a rule representation is fixed, but it does not itself define one. The negative case is strongest on this boundary, not on whether high multiplicity makes sense. Private-good allocation is a natural high-multiplicity regime; this paper simply offers no named computational result for ChoCo to continuize.
