The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror I can make is a type-mass version of the paper’s positive-penalty delegation game. The anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved here using Kakutani’s fixed-point theorem: for the paper’s game \(\mathcal G(N,U,W,\epsilon)\), with \(W=\{w_i\}\subseteq\mathbb R^{n+1}\), a Nash equilibrium exists for every \(0<\epsilon<1\). The theorem also lists \(f\in\mathbb R^{n+1}\) among its data. This is an equilibrium-existence result, not an algorithm or a complexity classification.

A plausible regime is a large online organization whose members fall into a small number \(\tau\) of recurring delegation and preference profiles: for example, millions of members but a few dozen profile types. A type includes the member’s preferences over where their vote is ultimately consumed and the delegation options the model uses. Each type has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Suppose the organization applies one delegation policy to each type cohort. The policies and resulting weight flows are continuous; the population is represented by its distribution over types.

Call the resulting decision problem Type-Mass Delegation Equilibrium Existence. An instance is \((T,\mu,W,\epsilon)\), where \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), \(\mu\) is the type-mass distribution, each \(w_t\in\mathbb Q^{\tau+1}\) gives type \(t\)’s satisfaction for vote weight consumed by each type or lost to the paper’s null state, and \(0<\epsilon<1\) is the chain penalty. A policy profile is a column-stochastic matrix \(P\), with column \(t\) specifying how type \(t\)’s cohort retains or delegates its weight among types. Using the paper’s \(V^\epsilon\), define type \(t\)’s payoff as \(U_t(P)=w_t\cdot V^\epsilon(P,e_t)\), where \(e_t\) is a unit source at type \(t\). The aggregate weight distribution is \(\sum_t\mu_tV^\epsilon(P,e_t)=V^\epsilon(P,\mu)\). The question is whether there is a \(P\) such that, for every type \(t\) and every alternative column \(p'_t\), \(U_t(P)\ge U_t(P_{-t},p'_t)\). A witness is such a matrix \(P\); for the decision version, the answer is yes or no.

The mirror is recognizable as the paper’s equilibrium problem: it retains fractional delegation, chain penalties, utilities based on where a type’s own vote ends up, and unilateral policy changes. Theorem 4.1 transfers directly to this type-level game, since its strategic players are precisely the \(\tau\) cohorts. The expected direction for the existence decision is tractable: the theorem says every valid positive-penalty instance has answer yes. The more substantive follow-up—computing an equilibrium witness in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length, or finding an approximate one—remains unanswered by the paper.

The weakest point is the cohort assumption. The mirror treats each type as choosing one shared policy, so it studies type-synchronous equilibria rather than every equilibrium in which same-type individuals might choose different policies or target particular named agents. That makes the scenario plausible for cohorts governed by a common delegation policy, but it narrows the original game. Also, the paper has no named theorem classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time, hard, FPT, or similar. Under the programme’s strict anchor rule, Theorem 4.1 is the nearest anchor, but the paper contains no eligible computational-complexity anchor. This case therefore supports a population-continuum analogue of the paper’s existence result; it does not yet supply the desired complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named theorem classifying a computational problem by complexity. Its theorems establish properties of the voting measure and existence of Nash equilibria; Algorithm 1 computes the measure, but does not state a complexity result. That leaves no direct computational result to mirror.

The strongest proposed anchor, Theorem 4.1, is an analytic existence theorem for a finite-player game whose strategy spaces are already continuous. In the type-mass version, each type becomes one strategic player with one shared delegation policy. But with the proposed payoff \(U_t(P)=w_t\cdot V^\epsilon(P,e_t)\), the mass \(\mu_t\) never enters anyone’s incentives. Changing the population proportions while keeping the types fixed leaves the game unchanged. The mirror therefore represents the society by masses in its input, while its equilibrium question remains about a finite \(\tau\)-player game.

Making mass affect the incentives requires changing the model. Letting members of a type choose separately removes the shared type policy; treating them as atomless players changes the deviation and equilibrium notion. And if recipients are anonymized as types, the model no longer preserves the paper’s preferences over named agents. Those may be sensible research directions, but Theorem 4.1 does not settle their computational questions.

The negative case is not airtight: recurring cohorts governed by shared policies are plausible, and the authors might recognize that as their game. If so, Theorem 4.1 does support a continuous-population existence statement. My case against is narrower: that statement is not a computational mirror, and the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor the programme’s complexity questions.
