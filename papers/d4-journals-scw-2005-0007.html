What the mirror covers

The proposed test covers the strict-regime inconsistency conditions associated with Proposition 3. It leaves aside finite-\(n\) probabilities, boundary behavior such as impartial culture, and the truth-tracking comparisons.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Its propositions are probability and convergence results. So there is no anchor that meets the requested standard. The strongest nearby basis for a positive case is Proposition 3, proved in the paper: with independent, identically distributed judgments over \(TT,TF,FT,FF\), if
\[ p_{TT}+p_{TF}>\tfrac12,\quad p_{TT}+p_{FT}>\tfrac12,\quad p_{TT}<\tfrac12, \]
then the probability of a collective inconsistency under propositionwise majority voting tends to \(1\) as the odd electorate size \(n\) tends to infinity. If instead \(p_{TT}+p_{TF}<\tfrac12\), or \(p_{TT}+p_{FT}<\tfrac12\), or \(p_{TT}>\tfrac12\), that probability tends to \(0\). The paper proves this using the law of large numbers. This is a genuine named result, but it is not a complexity result.

The best continuous mirror I can make from it is Continuous Doctrinal-Inconsistency Testing. Given a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over the four consistent judgment types \(TT,TF,FT,FF\), where each type accepts the rule \(R\leftrightarrow(P\land Q)\), determine whether the mass-majority judgments on \(P,Q,R\), together with that unanimously accepted rule, are inconsistent. Use strict majority, so mass exactly \(\tfrac12\) does not count as a majority. The answer is yes exactly when
\[ \mu_{TT}+\mu_{TF}>\tfrac12,\quad \mu_{TT}+\mu_{FT}>\tfrac12,\quad \mu_{TT}<\tfrac12. \]
The input is the type support and its rational masses; the output is yes or no. There is no intervention variable or optimization objective in the paper’s problem. This is a decision problem, solved by checking three inequalities.

A plausible regime is a large referendum electorate deciding several logically connected propositions. Millions of voters can share one of four relevant judgment types, so the number of agents is large while the number of distinct types is fixed. The authors would have reason to recognize this mirror: in the strict-inequality regions above, Proposition 3 says finite electorates sampled from those type proportions become almost surely paradoxical or almost surely non-paradoxical as their size grows. The test takes the corresponding deterministic mass-majority question. It is tractable by direct arithmetic, but that is a feature of this particular mirror, not a complexity classification established in the paper.

The mirror covers the strict-regime limit behavior of Proposition 3. It does not preserve the finite-\(n\) probability question, the impartial anonymous culture result, or the paper’s truth-tracking comparisons. Its weakest point is that this deterministic test is exceptionally simple and loses important behavior at ties: under impartial culture, for example, equal masses produce no strict mass majority, while the paper finds that the finite-electorate paradox probability tends to \(\tfrac14\). Boundary cases would need a separate account of sampling fluctuations. So this is a plausible continuous-population analogue of one substantive result, but a weak foundation for a computational-complexity programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 3 classifies the limiting probability of an event in an i.i.d. finite-electorate model. It does not analyze the complexity of deciding, optimizing, or manipulating a social choice outcome. A mass-majority test may be a sensible description of an aggregate society, but that alone does not give this paper a computational problem for ChoCo’s programme.

The proposed test substitutes the type probabilities \(p_{TT},p_{TF},p_{FT},p_{FF}\) for population shares and checks three inequalities. That is the deterministic limit predicate behind Proposition 3’s strict regimes. It also loses the paper’s central probabilistic question at the boundary: under impartial culture, the four masses are each \(1/4\), so the continuum has no strict majority on either premise, while the paper finds that the finite-electorate paradox probability tends to \(1/4\). The missing behavior comes from finite-sample fluctuations.

The stronger version cannot keep both features. Computing the paradox probability from a distribution over types retains the finite electorate size \(n\) and the multinomial sampling that the paper studies; the society remains finite. Replacing that sampling with a random continuum would make uncertainty, rather than population, the continuous object. A mass-reallocation or robustness problem could be added, but the paper does not study interventions, and that would be a new problem rather than a mirror of Proposition 3.

There is a real weakness in this negative case: a referendum with many voters and four recurring judgment types is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and its deterministic mass-majority question is coherent. So I cannot honestly claim that no continuous population model can be made. The narrower case is that this anchor supplies no worthwhile *computational* mirror: its natural mass version is the deterministic limit criterion, while preserving the result’s substantive uncertainty keeps the population finite.
