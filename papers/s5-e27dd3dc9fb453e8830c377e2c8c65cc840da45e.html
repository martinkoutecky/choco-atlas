What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible named anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Its main finding—that mayor turnover and system-state instability increase as voters shift from goal-oriented to policy-oriented evaluation—is an unnumbered simulation finding in §§4.2 and 5.1. The Hotelling result is mentioned as a special case of the simulation, not proved as a numbered computational result here. I can’t honestly attach an anchor number to a continuous problem.

The strongest plausible mirror is a continuous-population version of that simulation. A type would be a voter cohort with a fixed ideal social-system state (and, if varied across voters, its evaluation parameters); its mass is its fraction of the city. A plausible regime is a large city whose residents fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of recurring cohorts, with \(\tau\) far smaller than the population. Given rational cohort masses, an initial state, the system-transition matrix, a finite sequence of candidate pledges, and the paper’s evaluation and abstention rules, each cohort assigns its mass to its preferred candidate; the plurality winner’s policy then updates the state. One precise question is to compute the exact partition of \(p\in[0,1]\) into regions with the same mayor sequence, reporting turnover and trajectory variation on each region.

That question tracks the paper’s central simulation closely, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as their population-level model. It covers only the reported relationship between preference structure, mayor changes, and state trajectories; it does not mirror a complexity theorem. Its weakest point is that the paper’s simulated voters have individually sampled ideal states, so a small set of shared cohorts is an additional high-multiplicity regime, not a result of the paper. Also, computing one fixed-\(p\) trajectory is just aggregation over the types at each round. The parameter-partition question may be more interesting, but the paper establishes no complexity claim about it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s opening identifies the strongest objection: the paper has no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its central finding comes from comparing simulation traces across six parameter settings. The Hotelling result is presented as a special case of the simulation, not as a computational result proved here. There is therefore no discrete complexity result for a continuous mirror to continue.

The population itself is a plausible candidate for continuization. A large city could be modeled by a distribution over residents’ ideal social states and evaluation parameters. That might remove sampling noise and make the paper’s stability finding more precise. But it would make the *model* continuous; it would not supply the missing computational question.

The proposed partition of \(p\) into regions with the same mayor sequence is a reasonable extension, but the paper does not define that output as a problem. The sequence depends on the candidate pledges drawn at each round, and “trajectory variation” needs a specified measure. Fix the draws and horizon, and the task is to propagate the model and aggregate vote shares each round. Average over the draws, and one must choose a distribution and a stability statistic. Both can be studied, but those choices create the computational task rather than recover one from the paper. Generalizing the horizon, state dimension, or distribution representation to obtain a broader complexity landscape would take the work further from its stated experiments.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a ChoCo complexity benchmark: there is no computational result to anchor the mirror, and the suggested parameter-partition problem is newly imposed. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous study could grow from the paper is weaker. A continuous population model could be useful for studying the dynamics, but that would be a new modeling and analysis project, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.
