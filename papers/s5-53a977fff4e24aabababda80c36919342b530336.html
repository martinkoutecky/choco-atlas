The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about one narrow point: this paper gives no named complexity bound or hardness theorem, so it would be inaccurate to present Theorem 1 or Theorem 2 as an algorithmic result. But that does not rule out a worthwhile continuous computational question unless “mirror” means translating a complexity result the original paper never made. Proposition 1 identifies deferred acceptance (DA) as a rule that can be sequentialized by PAO, and Theorem 2 links DA’s strategy-proofness to robust truthful implementation. Those results give a concrete rule and implementation setting to study computationally.

Consider a large school-admissions cohort, grouped by complete types \(t\): each type includes a preference ranking and priority class. Let \(\mu_t\) be the rational share of applicants of type \(t\), and let school capacities \(q_j\) scale with the population. Each applicant still receives one school; a type’s aggregate assignment can be fractional when capacity cuts through a mass of otherwise indistinguishable applicants.

One computational question is: given costs \(\gamma(t,t')\) for shifting applicants between preference types while keeping their priority class fixed, what is the least-cost mass transfer that makes DA assign at least \(r\) of the population to a designated school? The input has support size \(\tau\), \(m\) schools, and rational data of bit length \(L\). Exact, approximation, and parameterized algorithms would say whether this aggregate DA intervention admits a tractable optimization model or has a harder boundary. This is a population continuization: it aggregates applicants, scales capacities per capita, and asks a computational question about a rule named in the paper.

The gap is that the paper does not study such interventions, so this is a computational extension anchored in its DA results, not a theorem-for-theorem translation. The experiments themselves are a poor mirror: their eight-person markets are individually constructed. Still, one plausible large-market regime and a well-defined mass problem are enough to defeat the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
