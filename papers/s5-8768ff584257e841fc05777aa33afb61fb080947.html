The continuous mirror question

Given a finite item set \(I\), finite buyer-type set \(T\), rational valuations \(v_t(i)\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_i\), a piecewise-constant arrival stream \(\rho:[0,1]\to T\) with rational breakpoints, and a rational threshold \(R\), does there exist a measurable pricing and allocation policy with revenue at least \(R\)? At time \(s\), the arriving mass of type \(\rho(s)\) is allocated among currently available items at utility-maximizing prices, with seller tie-breaking; feasibility requires \(\sum_i x_i(s)\leq 1\) and \(\int_0^1 x_i(s)\,ds\leq q_i\). Every mass assigned item \(i\) at time \(s\) must weakly prefer that purchase to every item still available at every later time \(r\geq s\), at its price then; revenue is \(\int_0^1\sum_i p_i(s)x_i(s)\,ds\).

The model it lives in

A unit mass of buyers arrives along a finite rational-breakpoint stream of valuation types; item supplies scale per capita. Measurable item prices and allocation rates govern demand and inventory, and the objective is total revenue subject to ex-post envy across future prices.

The objection that survived

This bounds the computational formulation, though the threshold question over measurable policies remains statable.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the predetermined-order ex-post revenue-hardness branch of Theorem 20 and its Vertex Cover reduction; it leaves the ex-ante branch and the paper's other welfare and revenue results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the continuum version retain the Vertex Cover approximation gap when buyer mass can split across tied choices?
  • Can every rational instance be represented or approximated by policies with finitely many price changes?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a mirror of the ex-post branch of Theorem 20, proved in this paper. The theorem says that maximizing revenue in unit-demand ex-post envy-free dynamic pricing is APX-hard even when the arrival order is known in advance and ties are broken by the seller. Its reduction, from Vertex Cover on 3-regular graphs, gives each vertex four items and a vertex-agent who values each of them at \(2\); each edge gets an agent who values the four items at either endpoint at \(1\). All other values are \(0\). With edge-agents first, there is a pricing and allocation with revenue \(m+2n-k\) exactly when the graph has a vertex cover of size \(k\). The theorem also proves APX-hardness for the ex-ante variant with vertex-agents first; I rely only on the ex-post result here. The reduction’s source hardness is cited from elsewhere, but Theorem 20’s pricing result is proved in this paper.

This has a natural high-multiplicity regime: a large marketplace with many repeat buyers, a fixed catalogue, and predictable waves of customers with the same valuation vector. The complete type is a buyer’s valuation for every item together with their arrival cohort. Buyers in a cohort are indistinguishable for the model. A finite realization can have \(K\) copies of each cohort member, with \(K\) copies of each item; then the population is \(K(n+m)\), while the number of types remains \(n+m\). Normalizing by population size gives each buyer type mass \(1/(n+m)\) and each item supply \(1/(n+m)\). Scaling supply with population is part of this mirror: it keeps the market nontrivial as the number of buyers grows.

I would call the resulting problem Continuous Ex-Post Revenue Maximization. An instance has a finite item set \(I\), a finite set \(T\) of types with rational valuations \(v_t(i)\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_i\), and a known piecewise-constant arrival stream \(\rho:[0,1]\to T\). The mass of type \(t\) is \(\mu_t=|\{s:\rho(s)=t\}|\), and the stream records the predetermined order of cohorts. At time \(s\), infinitesimal buyers of type \(\rho(s)\) arrive. The seller chooses nonnegative prices \(p_i(s)\) for items with positive remaining supply and an allocation flow \(x_i(s)\in[0,1]\), where \(x_i(s)\) is the fraction of the arriving mass assigned to item \(i\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_i x_i(s)\leq1\) and \(\int_0^1 x_i(s)\,ds\leq q_i\). Assigned buyers must receive a current utility-maximizing available item, allowing seller tie-breaking. For ex-post envy-freeness, mass assigned to \(i\) at \(s\) must weakly prefer that purchase to every item still available at every later time \(r\geq s\), at its price \(p_j(r)\); buyers who take nothing must have no positive-utility future option. Revenue is \(\int_0^1\sum_i p_i(s)x_i(s)\,ds\). The computational question is to maximize this revenue, or in decision form, determine whether a feasible measurable policy reaches a given rational threshold.

The reduction’s market is a direct finite-support subfamily of this question: use one cohort for each vertex-agent type and edge-agent type, give each mass \(1/(n+m)\), and put edge cohorts before vertex cohorts. The authors should recognize the mirror: it keeps their unit-demand buyers, fixed order, changing prices, revenue objective, and ex-post comparison across future prices. The population and stock become mass quantities; the temporal fairness question stays intact. This is a computational question about the population, rather than a continuization of outcomes or uncertainty.

My working expectation is Class A, though it is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 20. At mass scale, assignments can split across tied choices and inventory becomes a flow. That may turn some of the discrete cover-selection structure into a fractional optimization problem, perhaps expressible with a polynomial-size program or an efficient separation oracle in \(|I|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. The next questions are whether such a formulation exists, whether an optimum can be represented by finitely many price changes, and whether the analogous ex-ante problem or the paper’s open fixed-order weak-envy problem behaves differently.

The weakest point is precisely the fractionalization: Theorem 20’s APX-hardness does not establish hardness for this mirror. Its four-copy construction and its revenue gap may depend on choices that a continuum of buyers can split. Conversely, dynamic prices and future-price envy constraints may retain enough combinatorial structure to keep the problem hard. That gap is a substantive question for the programme, and the proposed mirror makes it concrete.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 20 is a genuine computational anchor: it proves APX-hardness for a named problem, and the reduction is part of this paper. Neither the outside source of Vertex Cover hardness nor the possibility that the continuous answer differs weakens the case for asking the mirror. The ex-ante hardness result in the same theorem is a second version of the same anchor.

My strongest objection is that arrival position is part of what makes an agent’s type complete here. Two buyers with the same valuations but different arrival times face different remaining inventory and different future price paths; under ex-post envy-freeness, they also compare against different sets of times. Put exact arrival position into the type, and the proposed finite-support model no longer applies: the continuum stream has a continuum of zero-mass types. Group buyers into finitely many cohorts instead, and either arrivals within a cohort are simultaneous, changing the paper’s sequential repricing model, or prices can change within the cohort, so its members no longer share the same problem-relevant type.

The proponent’s fluid model handles this by indexing buyers by time and allowing a measurable price policy. That is coherent, but it needs a finite input and output representation before it has a standard computational-complexity question. Restricting policies to a finite number of price changes might provide one; showing that this restriction preserves the optimum is itself a substantial modeling result. Replicating buyers does not resolve the issue: if each arrival position becomes its own type, the support grows with the population.

This is a real weakness in the proposed finite-type mirror, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Continuous time may be a co-modification forced by the sequential population model, and a fluid formulation with a suitable finite representation could be exactly the question the authors would recognize. Batched arrivals also offer a plausible related regime, though they would change the within-cohort pricing opportunities. I cannot honestly defeat the anchor across those alternatives. The negative case is weak: the mirror needs sharper computational foundations, but the paper provides a credible starting point for one.
