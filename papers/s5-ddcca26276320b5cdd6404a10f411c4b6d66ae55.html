The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), \(K\) chore classes, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), entitlement coefficients \(e_t>0\), per-class supplies \(s_k\ge 0\), and positive costs \(c_{tk}\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t e_t=1\), let a worker of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^K\) incur cost \(v_t(B)=\sum_{k=1}^K c_{tk}B_k\). A mass assignment \(y\) has finite support, satisfies \(\sum_B y_{t,B}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,B}y_{t,B}B_k=s_k\) for every \(k\); write \(\mathcal Y(\mu,s)\) for these assignments and define \(W_t^\infty=\inf_{y\in\mathcal Y(\mu,s)}\max_{(u,B):\,y_{u,B}>0}(e_t/e_u)v_t(B)\). Is there an algorithm polynomial in \(K\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length \(L\) that outputs a rational finite-support mass assignment \(x\in\mathcal Y(\mu,s)\) such that \(x_{t,B}>0\) implies \(v_t(B)\le 20W_t^\infty\) for every type \(t\)?

The model it lives in

Types are complete worker profiles \((e_t,c_{t1},\ldots,c_{tK})\) with mass \(\mu_t\). The decision variables \(x_{t,B}\) specify the mass of type \(t\) receiving each integral bundle \(B\); they satisfy the type-mass and per-capita supply constraints. The fairness benchmark is each type's mass-WMMS value \(W_t^\infty\), and the target is a \(20\)-factor assignment computable from the compact type description.

What the mirror covers

Covers the general \(20\)-WMMS guarantee of Theorem 4.5 for repeated worker profiles and chore classes. It leaves the fixed-population bounds of Theorems 5.4 and 5.5 and compact-input running-time guarantees unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a \(20\)-WMMS mass assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(K\), \(|T|\), and \(L\), with polynomial-size output?
  • Can the entitlement-rounding loss be avoided in the mass model, and what is the best universal guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make a narrow positive case around Theorem 4.5. The paper has no numbered result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard. Its strongest broadly applicable computational anchor is a constructive approximation guarantee: Theorem 4.5, proved here, says that for any instance of asymmetric chore division with \(n\) agents, \(m\) indivisible chores, additive costs, and positive entitlements summing to one, there is a complete assignment in which every agent \(a_i\) receives chores costing at most \(20\,\mathrm{WMMS}_i\). The paper proves this with its layered moving knife algorithm. I would not use Theorems 5.4 or 5.5 as extra anchors: their guarantees concern fixed populations of three and four agents, so they carry less of the high-multiplicity case.

A plausible regime is a very large workforce repeatedly assigned unpleasant shifts or tasks. Workers fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of contract and cost profiles: everyone in a type has the same entitlement and the same additive costs for each task class. The total workforce \(N\) is large, while \(\tau\) and the number \(K\) of task classes stay relatively small. Task supply grows with the workforce, so each task class has a fixed per-worker supply. Each worker still receives an integral bundle of indivisible tasks; only the fraction of a type receiving each bundle is continuous.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover, named 20-WMMS\(_\infty\) Mass Assignment. An instance consists of \(K\) chore classes with rational per-worker supplies \(s_k\), and a finite set \(T\) of worker types. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), per-person entitlement coefficient \(e_t>0\), and additive costs \(c_{tk}>0\) for the chore classes. Require \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t e_t=1\). A bundle \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^K\) gives the integer number of chores of each class assigned to one worker, with cost \(v_t(B)=\sum_k c_{tk}B_k\).

A mass assignment is a finite-support distribution \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}B_k=s_k \quad\text{for every }k. \]
Thus a fraction \(x_{t,B}\) of the whole workforce consists of type-\(t\) workers who each receive the integral bundle \(B\). The benchmark for type \(t\) is
\[ W_t^\infty = \inf_{y\in\mathcal Y(\mu,s)} \max_{(u,B):\,y_{u,B}>0} \frac{e_t}{e_u}v_t(B), \]
where \(\mathcal Y(\mu,s)\) is the set of mass assignments satisfying the same supply and type-mass constraints. The question is: given the instance, find such an \(x\) for which \(x_{t,B}>0\) implies \(v_t(B)\le20W_t^\infty\) for every type \(t\). That is one continuous problem, not a request to make the chores divisible. I use positive costs in this first formulation so the relevant bundle choices are bounded and the benchmark has a finite configuration description.

The bridge to Theorem 4.5 is unusually direct. For rational data, scale by a common denominator \(N\): create \(N\mu_t\) workers of type \(t\), \(Ns_k\) chores of class \(k\), and give each type-\(t\) worker entitlement \(e_t/N\). A rational benchmark partition can likewise be scaled to integer counts. Its weighted ratios are exactly \(e_t/e_u\). Applying Theorem 4.5 to the scaled instance and dividing assignment counts by \(N\) gives the mass assignment. This makes the continuous guarantee credible and gives a clean discrete-to-continuous dictionary. It does not establish a polynomial-time algorithm in the binary input length: the scaling denominator can be very large.

For the stated search problem, I would expect tractability to be the lead conjecture. The theorem’s moving-knife structure may admit a type-level version that works with rational masses directly, avoiding expansion into \(N\) named workers. That is a conjecture for the continuous mirror, not a result of this paper. The paper’s factor of two comes from rounding entitlements to an entitlement-divisible instance; in a mass model, it is natural to ask whether the layered argument can use real type masses directly and recover the intermediate \(10\)-WMMS guarantee. Further questions are whether a compact assignment can be computed in time polynomial in \(K,\tau\), and the input bit length, and what the best universal continuous factor is. Computing the optimum could be harder than producing a \(20\)-guarantee: bundle configurations may create a pricing problem. The paper’s theorem does not settle that complexity.

This case covers Theorem 4.5’s general constant guarantee, not the paper’s small-\(n\) chore-oblivious bounds. Its weakest point is that the source paper proves existence through a constructive discrete algorithm but gives no polynomial-time complexity classification; its proof may simply expand an instance whose size is enormous compared with the rational mass input. Also, the regime requires repeated complete worker profiles and chore supply that scales with population size. Those are substantive assumptions, though they describe plausible large-cohort settings. The mirror’s value is the computational question that follows: can the assignment be produced from the compact type distribution, rather than from an expanded list of workers and chores?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.5 is the only strong anchor. I can’t honestly make a convincing case that no worthwhile mirror exists.

The best objection is that the theorem’s guarantee transfers almost automatically to the proposed mass model. With rational type masses and chore supplies, scale up to a finite instance, apply the \(20\)-WMMS guarantee, then scale the assignment back down. The mass benchmark is designed to match the finite WMMS benchmark under that scaling. So the existence of a \(20\)-WMMS mass assignment is not, by itself, a new result. The paper gives a constructive finite-instance procedure, but no running-time result for computing from a compact type description. Its loops depend on the numbers of agents, chores, and agent copies; scaling up may be enormous relative to the input’s bit length.

That leaves a sharper question: can one compute a good assignment in time polynomial in the number of types, chore classes, and input bit length, without expanding the population? This is a legitimate high-multiplicity question, but it is also the point where the negative case falters. The proposed setting—repeated task classes, supplies that grow with the workforce, and a modest number of complete worker profiles—is plausible. The bundles remain integral, while their frequencies are fractional, so the mirror preserves the central tension of the paper.

A stronger mirror might ask for the best achievable factor, or classify the compact-input complexity of finding an assignment within a specified factor. I see no fundamental reason those questions fail either. The paper does not pose them, and its theorem does not answer them in compact-input time; that limits how much the source result motivates the work, but does not make the questions unworthy. The fixed-\(n\) bounds in Theorems 5.4 and 5.5 are poor additional anchors for a population limit, but the general result in Theorem 4.5 survives.

So the honest negative case is weak: the \(20\)-factor itself is inherited by scaling, and the proposed scenario needs repeated profiles and task classes. But those are limitations on the contribution’s likely novelty and scope, not a convincing reason to reject every continuous mirror.
