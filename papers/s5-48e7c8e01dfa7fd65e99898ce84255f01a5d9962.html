The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant positive case: the supplied paper has no named computational-complexity result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class.

The closest hook is Theorem 1. Its proof, supplied here, says that under Assumptions 1 and 2 the proposed exchange policy achieves the maximum measure of matched pairs, while delaying exchange strictly reduces that measure. It is a proved optimality result for the paper’s dynamic continuum model, not a complexity classification; the theorem’s statement itself is not included in the excerpt. Theorem A-1 gives steady-state waiting-time formulas and an iterative pooling procedure, while Lemmas A-3–A-6 prove asymptotic matching feasibility under stated regularity assumptions. These are meaningful analytical results, but none supplies the required computational anchor.

The nearest unanchored computational question would be a mass version of kidney-exchange cycle packing. Let each type \(t\) describe a complete patient–donor pair signature, including its compatibility relations and any exchange-relevant attributes; let \(\mu_t\) be its rational mass. Given the compatibility digraph and a maximum cycle length \(K\), choose nonnegative mass \(x_C\) for each feasible exchange cycle \(C\), subject to
\[ \sum_C a_{tC}x_C\le \mu_t \quad\text{for every type }t, \]
and maximize
\[ \sum_C |C|x_C. \]
Here \(a_{tC}\) counts how many pairs of type \(t\) the cycle uses. A plausible regime would be a large exchange pool with many pairs but a moderate number of shared compatibility signatures. For fixed \(K\), this formulation appears tractable as an LP with polynomially many cycle variables in the support size. It covers a static exchange-matching slice related to Theorem 1’s maximum-matched-mass claim, not the paper’s full waiting-time and survival model.

The positive case is weak for two reasons. First, the paper already models the population continuously, so simply restating its mass-matching model would collide with the paper rather than provide a new mirror. Second, complete HLA compatibility signatures may be so varied that the number of types approaches the number of pairs, undermining the high-multiplicity regime. The unbounded-cycle and dynamic versions could still pose computational questions, but this excerpt provides no named complexity result to anchor them.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would vote against this paper as a ChoCo mirror candidate. The strongest reason is the lack of a computational anchor: Theorem 1 proves that a particular immediate-matching policy maximizes matched mass under the paper’s assumptions; Theorem A-1 derives waiting times; and Lemmas A-3–A-6 establish asymptotic matching feasibility. None states the complexity of a computational problem. The authors describe their contribution as an analytically tractable dynamic continuum model, with the population already represented by flows and limits over tissue types. A continuous-population question is already being studied here; a computational question would have to be added. ([paper](https://www.tayfunsonmez.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/IncentivizedKidneyExchange.pdf))

Theorem 1 is the closest anchor, but its result is unusually self-solving: match compatible reciprocal ABO types as soon as possible. Under the stated flow assumptions, that policy achieves the maximum matched measure. The model reduces exchange to a fixed collection of ABO pair categories, and the subsequent quantities are computed from their flows. That leaves no evident pricing problem or algorithmic landscape in the theorem for continuization to uncover. The waiting-time formulas and pooling procedure in Theorem A-1 do not provide a separate computational anchor.

The best repair is the proposed mass cycle-packing problem over complete pair types. It is a coherent model, but with a fixed maximum cycle length \(K\), it has only polynomially many cycle patterns in the support size \(\tau\) (on the order of \(\tau^K\)); the resulting mass-allocation problem is an LP. That makes a valid, likely routine optimization question, but it strips away the paper’s central interaction between exchange, survival, waiting, queue priority, and incentivized participation. Restoring those dynamics requires a substantially richer control problem. Allowing arbitrary compatibility graphs or unbounded cycles to create a harder computational question likewise moves away from the assumptions that yield the paper’s immediate ABO matching policy.

This is not a claim that kidney exchange lacks a sensible high-multiplicity regime—the paper itself makes that case. The negative case is weaker against the literal claim that *no* worthwhile mirror exists: richer type spaces and dynamic constraints could support a worthwhile new project. It is stronger against treating this paper as an anchored continuization candidate: the continuous population is already present, and the proposed computational problem is an additional problem one could choose to study, rather than the computational mirror of a named result here.
