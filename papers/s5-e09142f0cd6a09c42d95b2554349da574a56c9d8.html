The continuous mirror question

Input schools \(S\), ranking types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). For each type \(t\), let \(\phi_t(s)\) be the rank of school \(s\). Find an assignment flow \(x_{t,s}\ge 0\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{s\in S}x_{t,s}(\phi_t(s)-1)\), subject to \(\sum_{s\in S}x_{t,s}=\mu_t\) for each \(t\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,s}=\kappa_s\) for each \(s\).

The model it lives in

A mass of \(\mu_t\) represents students with ranking type \(t\); \(x_{t,s}\) is the mass of that type assigned to school \(s\). Minimize total rank cost subject to each type's mass being assigned and each school's per-capita capacity being filled.

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 3.2 and the balanced, complete-ranking minimum preference-index assignment at the core of the Hungarian mechanism. It leaves aside strategyproofness, stability, priority violations, and selection among tied minima.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best honest case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s cost minimizing assignment problem. There is no numbered complexity classification here, and no hardness result to dissolve. The closest computational anchor is Proposition 3.2: for a given preference profile, the matching \(M\) produced by the Hungarian algorithm has preference index \(\mu(M)\leq\mu(M')\) for every other matching \(M'\), where \(\mu(M')=\sum_i(\phi_i(M'_i)-1)\). The authors apply the Hungarian algorithm to their rank based costs; the algorithm and its polynomial time guarantee are imported from cited work, while this proposition is their application to school choice.

A continuous version can preserve that objective exactly. Call it Continuous Minimum Preference Index Assignment. An instance consists of a finite set \(S\) of \(m\) schools; a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) distinct complete student rankings; rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); and rational per capita school capacities \(\kappa_s\geq0\) with \(\sum_{s\in S}\kappa_s=1\). For each ranking \(t\), let \(\phi_t(s)\) be the position of school \(s\). Find a rational assignment flow \(x_{t,s}\geq0\) minimizing

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{s\in S}x_{t,s}\bigl(\phi_t(s)-1\bigr) \]

subject to \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}=\kappa_s\) for every school \(s\). Any flow attaining the minimum is a solution. Individuals still receive one whole school; \(x_{t,s}\) is the fraction of type \(t\) assigned to school \(s\). This is the paper’s balanced, complete ranking assignment problem, with seat capacities scaled to population mass.

A plausible high multiplicity regime is a large district’s entering cohort: for instance, tens of thousands of students applying to a finite set of programs, with many students submitting the same complete ranking because they draw on a limited set of school list templates. The relevant comparison is \(n\gg\tau\), not the total number of possible rankings. A type is exactly a submitted ranking, since that is all the objective uses about a student. The mirror fits the paper’s stated focus on student preferences and its Hungarian mechanism; it does not quietly add school priorities or stability constraints.

This problem is Class A. It is a min cost transportation problem with \(\tau m\) assignment variables, so it can be solved in polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and the binary input length \(L\). It also has an especially clean finite correspondence: scale rational masses and capacities by a common denominator to obtain integer student counts and seat counts. The assignment network is integral, so an optimal flow can be realized as a discrete matching in that scaled society. Thus the continuum captures the high multiplicity version of the same optimization question, with a compact input when many students share rankings.

The mirror covers Proposition 3.2’s minimum index assignment and the core optimization performed by the Hungarian mechanism. It does not cover the paper’s claims about strategyproofness, stability, or choosing among tied minima. It also raises useful follow up questions: can the finite to continuous correspondence be extended to the paper’s incomplete ranking and unequal capacity cases, and what happens to tractability when the objective includes priority violations or constraints that seek stability?

The weakest point is that the paper’s discrete assignment problem is already polynomial time, and its continuous version gains no fractional welfare improvement: every rational instance can be scaled to a finite assignment. The positive case is therefore for a sensible, authors recognizable high multiplicity formulation that supports algorithms in \(m,\tau,L\), rather than for a striking example of discrete hardness disappearing. The regime also depends on there actually being substantially fewer distinct rankings than students.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is about priority, not mathematical feasibility. Proposition 3.2 establishes that the Hungarian mechanism minimizes the summed rank cost for a fixed preference profile. The paper’s distinctively school-choice questions, however, concern how that preference objective sits alongside school priorities, stability, and strategic behavior. The proposed mirror drops all of those. It captures a preference-only assignment problem, but not the policy tradeoff that gives the paper much of its subject matter.

A better mirror would include priority categories in each student type and ask how much preference cost can be reduced while limiting priority violations, or while imposing a mass version of stability. That is closer to the paper’s conclusion. But strict priorities can distinguish nearly every student, making the type support as large as the population; the mirror then loses its high-multiplicity advantage. Coarse priority categories could restore repetition, but that is a narrower regime, and a type-level stability condition would need careful definition.

That case still does not defeat the anchor. The paper explicitly considers cities without well-defined or legally required priorities, and its Hungarian mechanism uses preferences alone. Its balanced, complete-ranking assignment problem is a legitimate subcase. A large cohort with many students submitting the same rankings is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the flow formulation preserves the paper’s objective. Unbalanced capacities and incomplete lists can be handled by natural extensions; their absence from the proposed formulation is not a fundamental objection.

The remaining argument is that this is a low-priority mirror: the result is an application of a standard polynomial assignment algorithm, not a new complexity theorem, and aggregating identical preference rows leads directly to a standard transportation problem. But the programme explicitly treats high-multiplicity formulations as worthwhile objects, and a familiar or easy answer does not make a well-posed mirror disappear. I cannot honestly make the universal negative case stick here. Proposition 3.2 has a clean, authors-recognizable continuous mirror; the strongest objection is that it adds little computational novelty, not that no worthwhile mirror exists.
