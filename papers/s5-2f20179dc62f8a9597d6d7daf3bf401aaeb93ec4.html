Why no mirror

The proposed fluid core-membership problem is a plausible extension of PI games, and both cases explain why its core may collapse to the Owen allocation density. However, the paper’s NP-completeness claim is unnumbered, while Theorem 3.5 gives a structural construction rather than a named computational result under the stated gate. Bit (a) therefore fails.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is conditional, because the paper has no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your rule. Section 3 says that testing whether an imputation belongs to a PI-game core is NP-complete, using an adaptation of Fang et al. (2002), but that claim is unnumbered. Theorem 3.5, proved here, is structural: with one essential player, every \(F_\sigma(o)\), for \(\sigma\in\mathbb{P}^{|\mathbb{P}|}\), is an extreme point of the core. It does not assert a complexity classification. So the paper fails the named-anchor gate; the following is a credible mirror of its unnumbered core-membership claim, not a compliant anchor-based case.

Call it Continuous PI Core Membership. A society has rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) over finitely many complete PI types \(t\), each specifying its per-unit demand vector \(d^t\) and production, inventory, and backlog cost vectors. A coalition may select any measurable portion of each type’s mass. Its demand is the sum of those masses times their demand vectors; its available unit costs are the componentwise minima among the types represented in the coalition. Production, inventory, and backlog quantities scale continuously with demand. Given a candidate cost-allocation density \(a_t(u)\) for agents of each type, ask whether it is efficient and whether every measurable coalition’s allocated cost is at most that coalition’s PI cost.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many standardized outlets or facilities with repeated demand schedules and cost parameters, cooperating to pool demand and use the cheapest available production and storage technologies. The number of agents can be large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct full types remains moderate.

For this direct fluid version, I expect Class A. Let \(y^*(A)\) be the PI dual shadow-price vector when the coalition contains positive mass of exactly the types in \(A\). Since \(y^*(A)\ge y^*(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\) componentwise, the core constraints force almost every agent of type \(t\) to bear at most \(d^t\cdot y^*(\operatorname{supp}\mu)\): test a coalition containing the agent’s type and arbitrarily small positive portions of every other type. Efficiency then forces equality almost everywhere. Thus the core collapses to the Owen allocation density, and membership reduces to computing that density and comparing it with the input allocation.

The weakest point is also what drives this tractability: any positive mass of a type makes its technology available to the whole coalition. Arbitrarily small coalitions therefore have full access to the cheapest technologies, while fractional production and inventory quantities modify the paper’s integer-quantity model. The authors might accept this as a fluid PI-game, but it is a meaningful model extension, and the easy membership result comes from that continuum coalition semantics. Questions about mass thresholds for technology access or retaining indivisible inventory would be separate extensions, not results established by this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper is that it fails the programme’s named-result gate. Its NP-completeness claim for core membership appears only in unnumbered prose in Section 3, described as an adaptation of Fang et al.’s reduction. Theorem 3.5 concerns which allocations are extreme points; Theorem 4.6 is an axiomatic characterization; Proposition 5.3 concerns when allocation rules coincide. None is a numbered computational result. So the proponent’s core-membership question is a plausible extension, but not a mirror anchored in a named result of this paper.

That candidate deserves a serious look, though. In the proposed fluid game, take a coalition containing arbitrarily small positive masses of every supported type. Its type support is then the grand society’s support, so it gets the grand coalition’s cost-minimizing technologies. If a type’s allocation density exceeds its demand cost at the grand shadow prices, a coalition can take some of that type and sufficiently small positive amounts of every other type and violate core membership. Efficiency then forces every type’s density to equal its Owen density, almost everywhere. Under these semantics, continuous core membership really does reduce to checking the Owen allocation.

The reservation is about the economic meaning of those semantics. In the paper, an essential player’s cost vector gives the whole coalition access to cheaper production, inventory, or backlog costs. In an atomless population, an arbitrarily small positive mass of that type still unlocks those costs for the coalition’s entire demand. Thus support, rather than the amount of participating mass, determines technology access. A capacity-scaled version could avoid that jump, but it would add a resource constraint absent from the paper’s game. The proposed mirror therefore relies on authors accepting that even an infinitesimal share of a technology type can provide nonrival access to its cost schedule.

That is a real modeling concern, but it does not defeat every scenario: repeated facilities sharing reusable technology could make that assumption sensible, and a collapsed core is still a legitimate computational result. The negative case is strong enough to exclude this paper from an anchor-based ChoCo study. It is not strong enough to support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
