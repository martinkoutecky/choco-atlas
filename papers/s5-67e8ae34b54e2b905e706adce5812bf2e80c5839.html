Why no mirror

Theorem 3.3 is a computational result, but it concerns exactly two strategic bidders. The proposed mass market replaces them with negligible agents whose reports do not change the aggregate distribution, changing the strategic problem rather than giving a high-multiplicity version of the theorem. That is a plausible follow-on, but the case does not establish a continuous analogue of this result that the authors would recognize as their problem.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror targets Theorem 3.3's deterministic truthful versus nontruthful approximation gap; it does not cover Lemma 3.1's characterization or Section 4's randomized mechanisms.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 3.3, which the paper states and proves as a synthesis of its arguments. For the extended multi-unit auction with two bidders, \(m\) identical items, and normalized almost-monotone valuation functions given by black boxes, it asserts a gap: a nontruthful FPTAS exists, but deterministic truthful polynomial-time mechanisms cannot guarantee any bounded approximation ratio. The relevant running-time comparison is polynomial in \(\log m\). The proof here builds on Lemma 3.1’s affine-maximizer characterization, which reduces to a result cited from Dobzinski and Sundararajan, and Lemma 3.2’s communication lower bound, which relies on cited work by Nisan and Segal and by Dobzinski and Nisan. The paper constructs the FPTAS itself. This is a mechanism-design lower bound, not an NP-hardness theorem. The paper’s universally truthful \(2\)-approximation is useful context, but it is not a numbered result, so I would not use it as an anchor.

A concrete continuous mirror is the Mass Extended Multi-Unit Truthfulness Gap. For a bundle bound \(K\), let \(D_K\) be the paper’s valuation domain on \(\{0,\ldots,K\}\): each type \(t\) has a normalized valuation \(v_t\), including its private exceptional index \(k_t\), and the same almost-monotonicity condition. The society is a unit mass with a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over \(D_K\), with rational masses and support size \(\tau\). There is a supply of \(\rho\) items per unit mass. Individuals still receive integral bundles; the continuum allows a type’s mass to be split among bundle sizes. A feasible allocation is described by \(x_{t,k}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,k}k x_{t,k}=\rho\). Its welfare is \(\sum_{t,k}x_{t,k}v_t(k)\), and \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu,\rho)\) is the maximum feasible welfare.

The question is whether this setting has the same approximation gap. A nontruthful algorithm receives the finite-support distribution and valuation oracles and returns a feasible allocation. A truthful mechanism receives individual reports; in the atomless limit, one person’s report does not change the aggregate distribution. Formally, it may use a public tie-breaking coordinate \(u\in[0,1]\) and return an integral bundle \(a_\mu(r,u)\) and payment \(p_\mu(r,u)\) for reported type \(r\). Truthfulness requires, for every true type \(t\), alternative report \(r\), and \(u\), that
\[ v_t(a_\mu(t,u))-p_\mu(t,u)\ \geq\ v_t(a_\mu(r,u))-p_\mu(r,u). \]
Feasibility and welfare are measured over the mass of agents and their integral assignments. The computational question is whether there is a nontruthful FPTAS with running time polynomial in \(\tau,\log K,L,1/\epsilon\), while every deterministic truthful mechanism with running time polynomial in \(\tau,\log K,L\) has an unbounded worst-case approximation ratio as \(K\) grows. A mechanism or a proof ruling out such a gap would answer the question. Here \(L\) includes the encoding lengths of the masses, supply, and valuation-oracle answers.

The regime is a large repeated market for identical capacity or standardized units: many customers have one of a small number of complete demand schedules, and supply scales with the population. A type includes the whole valuation function and its exceptional index, so agents grouped together are identical in every detail relevant to allocation and incentives. The almost-monotone domain is narrow, but it can represent customers with a target package and a structured residual value schedule. This is a genuine high-multiplicity question: \(N\) can be very large while \(\tau\ll N\). The per-capita supply and fractional type masses are co-modifications forced by taking the population limit; each person’s bundle remains integral.

I expect the truthful gap, if it survives, to be continuum-specifically hard rather than to follow automatically from Theorem 3.3. The bundle-size parameter \(K\) preserves the paper’s large-\(m\) computational axis, but its two-bidder characterization does not establish a lower bound for many agents or atomless incentive constraints. The mirror should therefore ask whether the gap persists, not claim that the theorem already proves it. Follow-on questions include whether a bounded truthful approximation becomes possible with a finite type support, what changes for truthful-in-expectation mechanisms, and how the result relates to finite markets as their type frequencies approach \(\mu\).

The weakest point is the move from two strategic bidders to a continuum of agents. The paper’s theorem depends on a characterization for its two-bidder setting; adding many bidders can change which truthful mechanisms exist. And in an atomless market, incentives are evaluated against a negligible agent who cannot affect aggregate reports. I have made that incentive condition explicit so it does not disappear into a vague “large market” story, but it remains a material change that the authors might regard as a related extension rather than the continuous form of their theorem. The case is strongest as a mirror of Theorem 3.3’s underlying problem—welfare allocation with private quantity valuations and computationally constrained truthfulness—not as a claim that its proof transfers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.3 is a real computational result, so the case against should not pretend the paper has no result to mirror. But it has one distinctive feature that the proposed mirror cannot retain: the theorem is about exactly two strategic bidders. Its parameter \(m\) counts items, and the lower bound concerns reporting black-box valuation functions over bundle sizes. It is not a population-size parameter.

The proposed market of many customers with demand schedules is a coherent high-multiplicity problem. But it changes the subject of Theorem 3.3. Lemma 3.1 characterizes truthful mechanisms in the two-bidder domain; Lemma 3.2 uses that characterization and a two-bidder communication lower bound. Neither establishes a constraint on mechanisms for a continuum of agents. In an atomless market, one person’s report leaves the aggregate distribution \(\mu\) unchanged. Truthfulness can still matter through the allocation and payment that person receives, but that is an individual menu-choice problem, not the two-bidder communication problem the theorem analyzes. Any approximation gap there would need a new argument about a new mechanism class.

Cloning bidders into many customers is the strongest way to motivate that market, but it does not repair the match. Replication changes who the strategic agents are and how their reports affect the allocation problem. Conversely, keeping the two bidders and interpreting each as a population of underlying customers leaves those customers outside the strategic mechanism: the population then changes the bidders’ valuation domain rather than becoming the society whose computational questions are being studied. Neither route continuizes the theorem’s population.

So the mirror as proposed does not survive as a mirror of Theorem 3.3. The honest limit of this negative case is that it cannot rule out a worthwhile *follow-on* about truthful approximation in large markets. Such a problem could be interesting, and the paper’s artificial two-bidder setting does not rule it out. The stronger claim—that no plausible population-continuous scenario could be worth the programme’s time—is not established by this paper. The case against is strongest on fidelity to the named result, and weaker on the universal claim the role asks for.
