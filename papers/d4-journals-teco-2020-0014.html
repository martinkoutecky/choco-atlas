What the mirror covers

The proposed interpretation concerns Theorems 3.1 and 4.1: support elicitation, incentive compatibility, and frugality. It leaves the paper's uniqueness, threshold-belief, general-utility, and experimental results without a continuous computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a population interpretation of Theorem 4.1, though it does not pass the paper’s required computational-anchor test.

A plausible regime is a large annotation campaign using repeated question templates and a modest set of recurring, calibrated worker profiles. Let \(t\) specify a worker’s complete belief vectors for the campaign’s questions, together with the utility assumptions used by the paper. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of worker-task assignments with profile \(t\), with \(n\) assignments and \(\tau\) recurring profiles where \(n\gg\tau\). A common payment rule \(f\) maps the evaluations on the \(G\) randomly selected gold questions to a payment. Each worker chooses an option subset for each question. The platform’s decision problem is to choose \(f\), with payments in the given range and \(f(1,\ldots,1)=\maxpay\), so that every type strictly prefers reporting its belief support, while minimizing aggregate payment to workers who select every option.

This is a recognizable population version of the paper’s elicitation problem. Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper, says that under the coarse-belief assumption, the stated mechanism is incentive-compatible and frugal. In this population interpretation its all-options payout is \(f(B,\ldots,B)\), so the aggregate payout is \(\sum_t\mu_t f(B,\ldots,B)=f(B,\ldots,B)\). The theorem’s mechanism gives that payout as
\[ \minpay+(\maxpay-\minpay)(1-\minbelief)^{G(B-1)}. \]
Theorem 3.1, also proved here, supplies a boundary: without the coarse-belief assumption, no mechanism elicits every worker’s support as required. These results concern individual incentives, however, rather than the computational complexity of a population problem.

Under the requested anchor rule, the paper has no qualifying named computational result. Theorem 3.1 is an impossibility about incentive compatibility; Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 concern incentive compatibility and frugality; the later theorems and proposition address threshold behavior, uniqueness, or utility. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise classifies its complexity. So the population problem above is only a candidate extension, not a problem I can honestly present as anchored to a qualifying result.

The authors could plausibly recognize the population interpretation: the paper explicitly treats payments to workers as independent and analyzes one worker without loss of generality. That same fact also limits the positive case. The masses do not affect the frugality objective, and the incentive constraints remain type-by-type. Theorem 4.1 already supplies the common payment rule; adding \(\mu\) creates no apparent continuous-optimization question or complexity landscape. Calling the candidate tractable would be an inference from its explicit formula, not a complexity result claimed by the paper.

The weakest point is the regime itself. Exact agreement on complete belief profiles is a demanding assumption for human workers, even in repeated annotation campaigns. A small number of standardized machine annotators or tightly calibrated worker profiles makes \(n\gg\tau\) more credible, though it shifts the scenario away from the paper’s human-crowdsourcing motivation. My case therefore supports a natural high-multiplicity *interpretation* of one result. It does not establish a continuous computational mirror anchored in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The candidate mirror of Theorem 4.1 has a structural problem: the population weights do no work in the mechanism design problem. With one shared payment rule \(f\), aggregate payment to workers who select every option is

\[ \sum_t \mu_t f(B,\ldots,B)=f(B,\ldots,B), \]

since \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Requiring strict truthfulness for every type also gives the same type-by-type incentive constraints regardless of \(\mu\). Thus the proposed continuous society adds a distribution to a problem whose constraints and objective are already individual. Theorem 4.1 gives the payment rule directly; there is no population optimization for a continuous algorithm to solve.

Theorem 3.1 does not supply a second opening. Its impossibility concerns a single worker’s incentives over the full belief domain. Adding independent workers with masses does not change that worker’s expected payment or remove the incompatible incentives. One could instead require truthfulness only for types in a chosen population distribution, or only for most of the population. But that changes the guarantee: it makes the task depend on a restricted domain or an aggregate tolerance, neither of which is the impossibility result in this paper.

The strongest version of the proposed crowdsourcing scenario still runs into this separation. If complete belief profiles are hidden, the platform cannot use them to assign profile-specific contracts; if they are known, eliciting them is no longer the task. A common contract preserves the paper’s setup, but then the population weights remain irrelevant. Making them matter would require a different objective, such as population-level label accuracy, cross-subsidies, or payments tied to aggregate behavior. Those could be worthwhile problems, but they are not mirrors of the paper’s incentive or frugality results.

The decisive point under the requested anchor rule is that the paper proves no computational complexity result. Its numbered results concern incentive compatibility, impossibility, frugality, uniqueness, and threshold behavior—not the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 4.1 is a natural population interpretation, but even its proponent concedes it is not a qualifying computational anchor.

I would not claim the universal assertion that no scenario could make this paper’s subject worthwhile to continuize. The discussion explicitly points toward aggregation of approval labels, which could motivate a separate computational problem over a population of worker types. But that would be a new anchor. The honest negative case is narrower: none of the named results here supports a worthwhile continuous computational mirror, and the proposed mirror of Theorem 4.1 loses its population variable before any computational question arises.
