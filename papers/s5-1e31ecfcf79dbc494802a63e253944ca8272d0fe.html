The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_r=\rho\) and \(\mu_o=1-\rho\), with \(\rho,\tau\in(0,1)\), consider the fixed-\(\beta\) limit of the paper's complete-graph WAL dynamics as population size grows. Initialize radicals with \((\theta_r(0),a_r(0))=(0,1)\) and ordinary agents with \((\theta_o(0),a_o(0))=(\tau,0)\); for each type \(s\), update \(\theta_s(t+1)=\sum_{u\in\{r,o\}}\mu_u\theta_u(t)\), \(p_s(t+1)=\sum_{u\in\{r,o\}}\mu_u a_u(t)\), and \(a_s(t+1)=1\) exactly when \(p_s(t+1)\geq\theta_s(t+1)\). Compute the limiting activity profile and total active mass.

The model it lives in

A complete-mixing society with two types, radicals and ordinary agents, carrying masses \(\rho\) and \(1-\rho\). Each type shares its initial threshold and activity; fixed individual self-weight \(\beta\) vanishes in the population limit. The output is the eventual type activity profile and total active mass.

The objection that survived

Theorem 1 analyzes one radical, while the mirror's positive-mass radical cohort is a separate initial-condition regime, so the theorem's phase boundaries do not establish that cohort's behavior.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns the complete-graph WAL dynamics of Theorem 1, extended from one radical to a radical type of positive mass. It leaves the star and ring results and the ego-network simulations aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a fixed number of types with rational masses and a rational row-stochastic block interaction matrix, what is the complexity of deciding whether the dynamics eventually reach full mobilization?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is narrow: Theorem 1 gives an exact finite-population answer to the same underlying question the mirror would ask—what activity pattern eventually results? The paper does not state a formal \(P\), NP-hardness, or parameterized-complexity classification. Theorem 1 is an analytic characterization; its explicit inequalities nevertheless give a polynomial-time procedure for predicting the outcome in that restricted setting. I would use it as an algorithmic anchor without claiming the authors themselves state a complexity result.

The mirror I find credible is Complete-Graph Continuum Mobilization. Its society has two types: radicals \(r\), of mass \(\mu_r=\rho\), and ordinary agents \(o\), of mass \(\mu_o=1-\rho\), where \(0<\rho<1\). Members of each type share their initial threshold and action; everyone interacts with the whole population. For a fixed self-confidence parameter \(\beta>0\), take the continuum limit of the paper’s complete-graph WAL updates. Each type’s threshold and activity level are then

\[ \theta_s(t+1)=\sum_{u\in\{r,o\}}\mu_u\theta_u(t), \qquad p_s(t+1)=\sum_{u\in\{r,o\}}\mu_u a_u(t), \]

for each \(s\in\{r,o\}\), with \(a_s(t+1)=1\) exactly when \(p_s(t+1)\geq\theta_s(t+1)\). The individual self-weight vanishes in this limit, so fixed \(\beta\) no longer appears in the equations. The instance is the rational masses \(\rho,1-\rho\) and a rational initial reluctance \(\tau\in(0,1)\), with \((\theta_r(0),a_r(0))=(0,1)\) and \((\theta_o(0),a_o(0))=(\tau,0)\). A solution is the limiting activity profile and its total active mass.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, is the anchor. For the complete graph in the WAL setting, with initial condition \(\theta(0)=[0,\tau,\ldots,\tau]'\) and \(a(0)=[1,0,\ldots,0]'\), it states:

  • If \(\beta>1\), then \(a_\infty=1\) when \(\tau<\gamma_1(\beta)\); \(a_\infty=0\) when \(\tau>\gamma_2(\beta)\); and \(a(t)=a(0)\) for every \(t\geq1\) when \(\gamma_1(\beta)\leq\tau\leq\gamma_2(\beta)\).
  • If \(\beta\leq1\), then \(a_\infty=1\) when \(\tau\leq\gamma_3(\beta)\), and \(a_\infty=0\) otherwise,

where
\[ \gamma_1(\beta)=\frac{n}{n-1}\frac{1}{\beta+n-1},\qquad \gamma_2(\beta)=\frac{n}{n-1}\frac{\beta}{\beta+n-1},\qquad \gamma_3(\beta)=\frac{1}{\beta+n-2}. \]

The continuous question has a direct answer. At the first update, every type has threshold \((1-\rho)\tau\), while the active mass is \(\rho\). Thus all agents become active if \(\rho\geq(1-\rho)\tau\); otherwise all become inactive. Each pattern persists. The requested output is therefore either the profile \((1,1)\) with active mass \(1\), or \((0,0)\) with active mass \(0\). Comparing the input rationals takes polynomial time in their bit length, so I expect this question to be tractable. The tie goes to activation, as in the paper’s update rule.

The regime is a large, well-mixed society with a positive fraction of radicals and a positive fraction of ordinary agents sharing each type. This preserves the population-mass interpretation as the number of agents grows while keeping the number of types fixed. It is a natural extension of the paper’s single-radical complete-graph analysis: the authors explicitly list multiple radical agents as future work. It is not the literal limit of one radical with fixed \(\tau>0\): that radical has mass \(1/n\), which vanishes, and the continuum outcome is no mobilization. The positive-mass radical cohort is what makes the continuum question nontrivial.

This case covers only Theorem 1’s complete-graph WAL setting. It does not mirror the star or ring analyses, or the ego-network simulations. The main weakness is substantial: the source paper studies analytic dynamics, and the continuum version collapses to one inequality. It shows that a population-continuous prediction problem is coherent and efficiently solvable; it does not show a rich new complexity landscape. Natural next questions are how prediction changes for a fixed number of types with nonuniform interaction weights, whether non-complete network structure has a meaningful population limit, and whether scaling \(\beta\) with population size preserves self-confidence effects.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the programme’s computational-result gate. Theorem 1 is a named result, but it analytically classifies trajectories for a prescribed finite-network setup. The paper defines no computational problem, input encoding, algorithm, or complexity bound. Turning its parameter inequalities into a decision procedure is legitimate, but that computational task is supplied by the analyst; it is not a result the paper states. The same applies to the other theorems: they characterize asymptotic dynamics rather than the complexity of a problem.

The proposed complete-graph mirror is coherent, and the calculation is sound. Its connection to Theorem 1 is weaker than it first appears, though. With one radical and fixed \(\beta\), the radical’s mass tends to zero and the self-weight vanishes; for fixed \(\tau>0\), the limiting society has no mobilization. Giving radicals positive mass avoids that degeneration, but changes the initial regime to a positive-density radical cohort—a direction the paper itself lists as future work. That may be a natural extension, but it does not continue Theorem 1’s one-radical result.

A stronger candidate would use a finite set of population types with rational masses and a block interaction matrix, asking whether the dynamics reach full mobilization or what eventual activity profile they produce. Scaling \(\beta\) with population size could also preserve self-confidence in the limit. These are plausible population models, and the authors might recognize them. They still introduce a computational problem and modeling choices that the paper does not analyze. I would therefore reject this paper as a direct ChoCo computational mirror, while treating those models as possible new research directions.

That negative case is strong on fit to the programme’s stated target, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The paper is fundamentally about population behavior, and a computational study of its continuous or high-multiplicity dynamics could be worthwhile. The defensible conclusion is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror—not that its model admits no valuable continuous computational question.
