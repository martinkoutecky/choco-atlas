The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is the scale of the DER setting: the paper projects hundreds of thousands of producers in one country and already models their production additively. In a regime with many similar installations, a type could specify technology, location or forecast class, capacity, and prediction-error distribution; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of DERs of type \(t\). The paper’s 16-farm experiment does not establish that such a regime has small support, but a population of similar rooftop solar installations could plausibly have \(N\gg\tau\).

A possible follow-up would be to compute a type-symmetric entry equilibrium: given type masses and production-outcome distributions, find the mass of each type that joins a single CVPP, with each type preferring its assigned option under the expected payments in Equations (2), (4), and (5). That keeps population mass central and stays close to the paper’s question about incentives to form CVPPs. But it adds a coalition-entry equilibrium that the paper does not formulate or compute, so it is a research lead, not an anchor-based mirror. Its complexity direction is not established here.

The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result such as hardness or an algorithmic classification. Its named CRPS property concerns continuous prediction outcomes, not a continuous population; its payment claims and experimental findings are not numbered computational results. So there are no eligible anchors, and I cannot honestly supply or classify the requested continuous problems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent offers no anchor to defeat: it identifies no result in this paper that classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s formal contribution is a payment mechanism built from CRPS and formulas for aggregating forecasts and distributing payments; its evaluation is experimental. Strict propriety is a property of a scoring rule over prediction outcomes, not a population-complexity result. The experiment’s finding that one mechanism gives stronger incentives for cooperation is not an algorithmic theorem either.

A continuous population is easy to motivate here. The paper itself discusses very large numbers of DERs, and a type could capture technology, location, capacity, and forecast characteristics. But replacing counts of similar DERs with type masses would not, by itself, create a computational question from the paper. The aggregation and payment formulas would then apply to those masses. Asking whether the scoring rule remains strictly proper would be an incentive-compatibility question about the mechanism, not a computational mirror of a named result.

The proposed entry-equilibrium question is the strongest possible extension, but it adds the missing problem rather than continuizing one the paper solves. The paper compares singleton participation with a CVPP containing all 16 farms; it does not specify agents choosing among coalitions or formulate an equilibrium to compute. That extension would need a formation protocol, participation costs or outside options, and a solution concept. Those may lead to worthwhile research, but they are new modeling commitments, not a continuous version of a result in this paper.
