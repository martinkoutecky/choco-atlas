The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\) and a rational baseline distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types, find a distribution \(\nu\) or certify none exists such that \(A_{\mathrm{WT}}\nu=A_{\mathrm{WT}}\mu\) but \(f_{\mathrm{Pl}}(\nu)\ne f_{\mathrm{Pl}}(\mu)\), where \(A_{\mathrm{WT}}\) maps ranking mass to pairwise preference fractions and \(f_{\mathrm{Pl}}\) returns the uniform lottery over candidates with maximum first-place mass.

The model it lives in

Types are strict rankings of \(m\) candidates, and the input society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) with support size \(\tau\). The decision variable is another distribution \(\nu\) over rankings; the task is to determine whether it can preserve all weighted pairwise fractions while changing the Plurality lottery, with running time measured in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers exact information preservation for weighted-tournament data and Plurality; it leaves the neural-network experiments, generalization results, and Participation fine-tuning outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of finding or ruling out such a \(\nu\) as a function of \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\)?
  • Can the ranking-space feasibility problem be solved through polynomial-time pricing, or does it admit a hardness reduction?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: the paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its closest named anchors are about whether an embedding preserves a voting rule. Those are useful computational results about what information a compressed society retains, but they do not establish a complexity classification.

A plausible regime is a large election or recommendation panel with a fixed slate of \(m\) candidates, millions of people, and comparatively few distinct complete rankings. A type is a strict ranking; its mass is the fraction of people with that ranking. For example, the society might have \(10^6\) people but only a few hundred supported rankings. That is a natural high-multiplicity regime, and it matches the paper’s focus on profile embeddings. The lotteries remain outputs of the rules; the continuization here is of the population.

Proposition 1, proved in the paper, is the general anchor: an embedding \(T\) preserves a PSCF \(f\) if and only if every pair of profiles \(X,\widehat X\) with \(T(X)=T(\widehat X)\) also has \(f(X)=f(\widehat X)\).

Its continuous analogue is the Continuous Exact-Decoder problem. An instance gives the candidates, ranking types, a rational linear embedding \(A\) of their masses, and a finite description of a PSCF \(f\). Decide whether there is a decoder \(g\) such that
\[ g(A\mu)=f(\mu) \]
for every population distribution \(\mu\) over rankings. A yes solution is such a decoder; a no solution is a pair \(\mu,\nu\) with \(A\mu=A\nu\) but \(f(\mu)\ne f(\nu)\). The decoder is the decision variable, and the objective is exact recovery of the rule’s lottery from the compressed population. I would expect the unrestricted problem to be hard, with tractable cases when the rule and embedding have exploitable structure. Proposition 1 supplies the right criterion, but does not classify that complexity.

The more concrete anchor—and my lead—is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: the weighted tournament embedding does not preserve Plurality. Its witness uses three rankings over \(a,b,c\). One society assigns mass \(1/3\) to each of \(a\succ b\succ c\), \(b\succ a\succ c\), and \(c\succ a\succ b\). A second assigns mass \(2/3\) to \(a\succ b\succ c\) and \(1/3\) to \(c\succ b\succ a\). Both have the same pairwise fractions, but their Plurality lotteries differ: the first is uniform over all three candidates, while the second selects \(a\).

That yields a per-instance Plurality Ambiguity problem: given a rational baseline society \(\mu\), is there another distribution \(\nu\) over rankings with the same weighted pairwise fractions but a different Plurality lottery? A solution is such a \(\nu\); a negative answer certifies that the pairwise data determine Plurality for that particular society. Theorem 1 gives an explicit yes instance. The continuous formulation is a linear-feasibility question over masses on rankings, with winner conditions added to force a different lottery. Its LP has a variable for each ranking, so its difficulty may turn on pricing over permutations. My provisional bet is continuum-specific hardness, but the paper proves no such hardness: the pricing problem, and whether it admits a polynomial method, remain open questions for this mirror.

These mirrors cover the paper’s exact embedding-preservation results in Section 4. They do not turn its neural-network experiments into complexity theorems, and they do not claim that the paper establishes algorithms for the continuous problems. The authors could plausibly recognize the questions: they directly extend their question of whether an embedding retains enough information to recover a rule, from finite profiles to arbitrary population masses.

The weakest point is that this extension may be thin. Every rational mass distribution is already representable by some finite electorate, and Theorem 1’s ambiguity witness carries over exactly. The paper also does not study a worst-case algorithm for finding such ambiguities. So the case for a continuous mirror rests on the new computational problem over arbitrary mass distributions and their ranking-space LPs—not on a complexity result that this paper already supplies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that DeepVoting does not state a computational problem whose complexity the continuous version could reclassify. Its named results concern information preservation: Proposition 1 characterizes when an embedding can recover a rule’s lottery, and Theorem 1 gives a finite counterexample for Plurality. The paper reports empirical learning accuracy and efficiency, but proves no algorithmic complexity bound. That makes this a poor vehicle for the programme’s central complexity question.

The proposed Continuous Exact-Decoder problem exposes the gap. Proposition 1 already says that a decoder exists exactly when the rule is constant on each embedding fiber. Asking whether this holds for all distributions adds a global decision problem, but the definition itself gives no computational bound, and an unrestricted decoder has no meaningful efficiency requirement. Requiring an efficient decoder could make the question sharper, especially for standard voting-rule families, but that would be a new algorithmic question rather than a continuous version of a result in the paper.

Theorem 1 presents a harder objection. Its counterexample already has rational masses: one society puts mass \(1/3\) on each of three rankings, and another puts mass \(2/3\) and \(1/3\) on two rankings. They have the same weighted pairwise data and different Plurality outcomes. So the global continuous question “does weighted pairwise data determine Plurality?” inherits its answer directly from the theorem. The proposed per-society Plurality Ambiguity problem is more interesting: given \(\mu\), find another distribution with the same pairwise fractions and a different winner. But that is a new high-multiplicity search problem, not a complexity result the paper makes or mirrors. Its exponentially large ranking-space LP could be worth studying; the paper supplies no basis for saying whether it is tractable or hard.

A broader learning mirror has a similar problem. The networks already consume fixed-size, normalized aggregate embeddings, independent of voter count. To turn the experiments into a continuous computational question, one must choose a new objective—such as worst-case error over the population simplex or robustness to mass perturbations. That might be useful, but it is not forced by the paper’s results. The natural continuation of its No Show experiments is weaker still: removing one infinitesimal agent has zero effect away from decision boundaries, while removing positive mass changes the individual Participation axiom into a coalition or perturbation question.

This is a skeptical case against DeepVoting as a source of an immediate continuization result: the paper offers properties and experiments, not a named complexity theorem, and its clearest preservation claim already has a rational-mass witness. But it does not establish the requested universal negative. The authors’ focus on embeddings makes exact recovery over mass distributions a plausible question they would recognize, and the ambiguity problem could be a worthwhile high-multiplicity optimization problem in its own right. I would call the case against weak at precisely that point: it shows that the mirror is not supplied by the paper, but it does not show that asking the mirror is not worth the programme’s time.
