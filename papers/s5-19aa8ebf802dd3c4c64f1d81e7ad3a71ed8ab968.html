The case FOR (proponent)

The best scenario for a population mirror is taxation or bankruptcy with many claimants whose claims fall into a relatively small number of categories. A type would be a per-capita claim level \(c_t\), and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of claimants with that level. With per-capita endowment \(e\), a continuous allocation is a schedule \(x_t\) satisfying \(0\le x_t\le c_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=e\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t c_t\ge e\). A rule maps the claim distribution and endowment to such a schedule. That is a plausible population continuization of one of the paper’s applications, and the authors could recognize the allocation model in it.

But the paper contains no named computational result to anchor that mirror to. Theorem 1 proves that contraction independence is equivalent to WARP; Theorem 2 relates contraction independence to SARP for two agents; Theorem 3 gives a sufficient condition for SARP; Theorem 4 establishes numerical representability under stated axioms and continuity; and Theorem 5 says every rule is weakly rational. These are axiomatic or representability results, not claims about computational complexity or algorithms. None qualifies as an anchor under your criteria.

So there is no honest way to state one continuous problem per qualifying anchor, or to classify those anchors as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard. The proposed allocation schedule could motivate a *new* computational question, but presenting it as a mirror of one of this paper’s named results would overstate the connection. The weak point is decisive: the paper’s agents remain a finite set, and its continuity concerns allocation outcomes and rules, not computational questions about a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case is already an argument against a mirror, despite being labeled the case for one. It proposes a plausible high-multiplicity setting—many claimants grouped by claim size—but names no computational result to anchor it.

That leaves Theorems 1–5 as the strongest candidates: they characterize when allocation rules satisfy WARP or SARP, when auxiliary conditions imply transitive rationality, when a rule has a numerical representation, and when every rule is weakly rational. Each is a statement about properties of an allocation rule, not the complexity of a computational problem. The rule \(F\) is an arbitrary function; the paper gives no finite encoding for it, no input model, and no algorithmic task. Turning one theorem into “decide whether an encoded rule satisfies WARP” would require choosing how rules are represented. Testing a finite record of observed choices would instead make the observations—not a continuous society—the input. Either could motivate new research, but neither is a computational mirror established by this paper.

The claimant scenario survives the modelling objection: repeated claim sizes and per-capita allocations make a continuous population sensible. But a distribution over claim types supplies no missing representation of \(F\), and does not turn these axiomatic results into complexity results. The paper’s continuity concerns allocations and rules over a finite set of agents; changing the population to a continuum would invite new analytic characterizations, while adding algorithms for manipulation or rule verification would introduce a new computational question.

So the case against a worthwhile mirror of this paper is strong: none of its named results supplies a computational anchor. That does not show that computational work on mass allocation could never be worthwhile; it shows that such work would need a different motivating problem or paper.
