The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of reviewer types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to one, a shared task distribution, and type-specific signal channels that are conditionally independent given ground truth, does the DMI payment rule—formed from two blocks of at least \(C\) tasks and averaged over peer-type masses—make truthful reporting dominant for each worker against arbitrary type-level reporting strategies, with strict preference over non-permutation strategies when informative truthful peers have positive mass?

The model it lives in

A continuum population over finite types \(\Theta\), represented by masses \(\mu_\theta\); each type has a signal channel over \([C]\), and each worker chooses a row-stochastic reporting matrix \(S_\theta\). The report-based payment is the mass-weighted average of pairwise DMI-squared terms across peers, and the objective is to preserve the paper's dominant-truthfulness guarantee.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorem 5.1's dominant-truthfulness guarantee and the expected-ranking part of Theorem 6.4. It leaves the concentration bound in the number of tasks and the paper's general properties of DMI outside the population mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the mass-averaged DMI payment preserve dominant truthfulness and strictness for every finite type support, including when the focal worker has zero population mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow mirror for peer evaluation by a large, typed crowd. There is an important limit, though: under your anchor rule, the paper has no qualifying named computational-complexity result. Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper, establishes that for \(n\ge 2\) and \(T\ge 2C\), the DMI mechanism is detail-free, minimal, individually rational, informed-truthful, and dominantly truthful; its normalized expected payment lies in \([0,(1/C)^C]\). Theorem 6.4, also proved here, relates expected payment differences to differences in information quality and gives concentration and pairwise misranking bounds. These are named mechanism and statistical guarantees, not results classifying a problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. The determinant formula is explicit and looks easy to compute, but the paper does not state a runtime theorem for it. So these can motivate a mirror, but they are not valid anchors under the stated criteria.

The best candidate is Continuum DMI Incentive Design. Let \(\Theta\) be a finite set of worker types, with rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to \(1\), and support size \(\tau=|\Theta|\). A type completely specifies a worker’s stable signal channel and any other relevant characteristics. On each task, a hidden answer \(G\in[C]\) is drawn from the same distribution; workers receive signals according to their type’s channel, independently conditional on \(G\). Tasks are independent. A type’s action is a row-stochastic reporting matrix \(S_\theta\), used consistently across tasks.

The instance consists of \(\Theta,\mu\), the signal model, and \(T\ge 2C\). The question is to produce a payment rule, based only on reports, that makes truthful reporting a dominant strategy for each worker against arbitrary strategies of the rest of the population, with strict preference over non-permutation strategies when a positive mass of informative peers reports truthfully. A solution is the payment rule and a proof of those guarantees. The direct candidate is the paper’s DMI payment: sample a peer from the population, split the tasks into two blocks, form the two \(C\times C\) report-count matrices, and pay the normalized product of their determinants. In expectation, the worker’s payoff averages the pairwise DMI-squared terms over peer types. Information monotonicity gives the incentive guarantee pair by pair, then integration over \(\mu\) gives it for the continuum.

The regime is plausible: a large crowdsourcing platform can have many reviewers but relatively few stable expertise or response-channel types. The motivating example is already close to the paper’s peer grading example; the continuum scenario is a platform with large cohorts of similar reviewers grading many similar essays. Here \(N\) grows while the number \(\tau\) of types stays moderate. The population’s mass distribution is the continuous object; the random signals and task sequence are part of the model, not the continuization.

This candidate is tractable: for rational type channels, pairwise expected payments can be computed from type-pair joint matrices and determinants, in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(C\), and the input bit length. Theorem 5.1 supports the incentive claim, while Theorem 6.4 supports a population-level quality-ranking extension when at least three informative types have positive mass. The mirror covers those guarantees, not the paper’s full mechanism-design setting.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper’s mechanism already works with two agents, so population multiplicity is not what makes its central result possible. The continuum mostly replaces a finite peer average with an integral, and the paper’s probabilistic signal model is not itself a continuous population. Thus this is a credible population-scale restatement of the peer-evaluation setting, but it does not expose a discrete hardness result that continuization could dissolve. Under the requested anchor standard, that keeps it from being a fully compliant positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational-complexity result to mirror. Theorem 5.1 proves incentive and payment properties; Theorem 6.4 proves an expected-score identity and concentration bounds. Neither classifies the complexity of a decision, search, or optimization problem. As the other analyst acknowledges, calling them “computational” because the determinant formula is easy to evaluate does not supply a runtime theorem. Under the stated anchor test, neither theorem is a qualifying anchor.

The best repair is a large peer-grading platform whose workers fall into a small number of stable signal-channel types. That is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario, and the continuous model is well posed. But it does not create a computational problem the paper was waiting to solve. For a worker of type \(\theta\), the expected DMI payment becomes an average of the existing pairwise payments,
\[ \bar p_\theta \propto \int DMI^2(\hat X_\theta;\hat X_{\theta'})\,d\mu(\theta'). \]
The incentive argument applies pair by pair, then averages over the population. Strictness requires positive mass of informative truthful peers. The paper already proves the pairwise guarantee with only two agents, so multiplicity is not what enables it. With finite type support, computing the average is just summing determinant terms; there is no pricing problem, separation oracle, or optimization bottleneck.

The same is true of Theorem 6.4. Under its ground-truth and conditional-independence assumptions, the continuum version averages the quality-ranking coefficients over peer types. The ranking identity carries through by integration. Its concentration result concerns the number of tasks \(T\), so adding population mass does not remove the individual task-sampling noise. Continuizing tasks to pursue that issue would change the population axis the programme says to hold fixed.

One could add a new objective, such as assigning reviewer types to overlapping task sets to minimize task use while preserving incentives. The paper’s discussion of overlap gives that idea a foothold, but it is an adjacent allocation problem, not a mirror of either theorem. Any complexity would come from the added assignment constraints, rather than from continuizing a computational result in this paper.

I would not claim that no plausible continuous model exists: the crowd-grading example is credible, and the authors might recognize its population-level version as their setting. The negative case is strongest on worth for ChoCo’s stated purpose. These results invite a straightforward population average, but the paper supplies no computational complexity result whose landscape that average could change. The universal claim is weaker than that conclusion: a broader mechanism-design programme could still find the extension worthwhile.
