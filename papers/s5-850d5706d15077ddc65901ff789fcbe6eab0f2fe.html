What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers Theorem 1’s majority-closure characterization; it leaves the structural results on maximal, normal, and symmetric CSDs untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor requirement exposes a real limit: this paper has no named theorem stating that a problem is NP-hard, in P, FPT, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity. The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved here, but it is a structural theorem rather than a complexity result. It says that a set \(\mathbf D\) of tilings is a Condorcet super-domain exactly when, for every three tilings \(T_1,T_2,T_3\in\mathbf D\), their coordinatewise majority aggregate \(sm(T_1;T_2;T_3)\) is itself a tiling. So this is the strongest positive foothold, though it does not strictly meet the requested complexity-anchor test.

The lead mirror is Continuous Majority Closure. An instance is an integer \(n\) and an explicit catalogue \(\mathbf D\subseteq\mathbf T_n\) of proposed voting designs, each represented by its inversion set in \(\Lambda=\binom{[n]}3\). A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those designs: \(\mu_T\) is the fraction of participating councils or local bodies proposing design \(T\). To avoid ambiguity at ties, consider distributions for which no subset of \(\mathbf D\) has mass exactly \(1/2\). The aggregate is the pseudo-tiling
\[ M_\mu=\left\{p\in\Lambda:\sum_{T:\,p\in Inv(T)}\mu_T>\tfrac12\right\}. \]
The question is whether every such society supported on \(\mathbf D\) produces a genuine tiling. A yes-instance returns yes; a no-instance can return three designs in \(\mathbf D\) and a four-color set witnessing failure of Ziegler’s criterion.

This is an exact continuous analogue of Theorem 1’s closure question. If any triple fails its test, putting mass \(1/3\) on each of the three designs gives a tie-free continuous society with the same failing majority aggregate. Conversely, when the triple test holds, Theorem 1 makes \(\mathbf D\) a CSD; the paper’s discussion of weighted majority systems then supports closure under the specified type-level weighted majorities. For an explicitly listed \(\mathbf D\), the resulting recognition problem is in P: check every triple of designs and test its aggregate on every four-color restriction. That polynomial-time consequence is derived from Theorem 1; the paper does not state it as a complexity classification.

A plausible regime is a large federation of local deliberative bodies choosing from a shared catalogue of admissible voting designs. The type is the complete proposed tiling, and mass is the fraction of bodies proposing it. With millions of participating bodies but only a small catalogue, \(\tau\) is far below the population size. The authors would likely recognize this mirror: it preserves their exact operation—majority over inversion triples—and their object—the validity of the resulting tiling. The paper’s weighted-majority discussion makes the connection especially natural, though it also means this is an extension of their existing framework rather than a major change in the question.

The mirror covers the paper’s majority aggregation and CSD closure result. It does not claim a continuous version of the paper’s constructions of maximal or symmetric CSDs. The expected direction is tractable, through the triple characterization. Further questions include how to handle exact ties while preserving closure, and whether recognition stays tractable when the catalogue of tilings is given succinctly rather than listed.

The weakest point is also decisive for the programme’s central aim: there is no named hardness result here whose fate in a continuum can be tested. This mirror is a credible high-multiplicity formulation of the paper’s aggregation problem, but its apparent payoff is a support-based polynomial recognition criterion, not a case where continuous modelling dissolves discrete hardness. The regime is most convincing when many bodies really do choose among a small set of shared designs; if almost every body proposes a unique tiling, the high-multiplicity benefit largely disappears.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only plausible anchor is Theorem 1, and it is a structural characterization, not a computational result: a collection \(\mathbf D\) is closed under majority aggregation exactly when every triple from \(\mathbf D\) has a tiling as its majority aggregate. The paper gives no complexity theorem about recognizing such collections, finding a maximal one, or computing an intervention. “Continuous Majority Closure” adds a recognition problem to the paper; it does not continuize a computational problem the paper states.

The federation-of-councils scenario is coherent: a type can be a proposed tiling, and \(\mu_T\) can be the fraction of councils proposing it. I would not reject it on grounds of multiplicity. But the proposed closure question largely restates the paper’s aggregation framework. A rational mass distribution with no ties defines a weighted majority system, which the authors explicitly discuss. Any triple that fails the test gives a failing society with mass \(1/3\) on each tiling; if every triple passes, Theorem 1 and the weighted-majority discussion give closure for the broader aggregation rule. For a supplied society, the question is whether this already-defined operator returns a tiling. For a supplied catalogue \(\mathbf D\), the society disappears from the input and the question becomes recognition by the triple criterion. Neither supplies the missing computational result.

The strongest repair would be a continuous design-bribery problem: move mass between proposed tilings, at some cost, to make a target tiling win or make the aggregate valid. That could produce a genuine optimization problem over a large type space. But it adds the intervention, target, and costs; the paper defines none of them. Flip distance is available mathematically, yet the paper gives no reason why changing one inversion triple should represent a council’s cost of changing its proposed voting design. This is a promising new extension, but it is not a mirror anchored in the paper’s results.

The remaining theorems concern the structure and construction of CSDs—such as maximal chains, compatibility, and symmetric domains. Their central objects are alternatives, triples, and collections of tilings. Giving the tiling proposals population masses changes the aggregation input, but does not reveal a computational question in those structural results.

So the negative case is strongest on the anchor requirement: there is no named computational result here to continuize, and the proposed closure mirror is already very close to the paper’s own weighted aggregation statement. It is weaker as a universal claim. A large federation choosing among shared designs is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a carefully motivated mass-transfer problem could be worthwhile. I cannot rule out such a scenario; I would say it needs a new problem and a defensible cost model, rather than treating Theorem 1 as the computational anchor.
