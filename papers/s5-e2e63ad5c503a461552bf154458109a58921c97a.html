What the mirror covers

Theorems 1–4 concern axiomatic impossibility or characterization, and none supplies a computational result for a population-continuous mirror. Section 13 discusses infinite populations normatively, not computationally.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case for is a model-level fit, not a qualifying paper result. A plausible society would be a large population of households grouped by their complete welfare-relevant type, with \(\mu_t\) the share of the population of type \(t\). That is especially natural here because §13 explicitly raises the problem of social welfare functions for infinite populations. It supports the claim that a continuous-population welfare model could make sense to this author.

But the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor a mirror. Theorem 1 (Arrow’s Theorem) says that, for finite \(n\ge2\) and \(|A|\ge3\), a social welfare function satisfying Social Ordering, Unrestricted Domain, Weak Pareto, and Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives is dictatorial. That is an axiomatic impossibility, not a complexity result. Theorem 2 characterizes welfarism through equivalent axioms; Theorem 3 characterizes utilitarianism using Weak Pareto, Anonymity, and \(\Phi_{\mathrm{CUC}}\)-invariance; Theorem 4 characterizes leximin using Strong Pareto, Anonymity, and Hammond Equity. None asserts that a computational problem is hard, tractable, or parameterized-tractable.

One could propose ranking alternatives from a distribution of welfare types, but that would be a new population-continuous axiomatic question. It would not mirror a computational problem established by this paper. So I cannot state a prover-ready continuous problem per anchor without inventing one. The population-continuum idea is plausible; the required computational anchor is absent.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this chapter contains no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is Arrow’s axiomatic impossibility; Theorem 2 is an equivalence between axioms for welfarism; Theorems 3 and 4 characterize utilitarianism and leximin. None studies an algorithmic problem or specifies an input, an output to compute, or a complexity measure. A distribution over welfare types could support new questions, but it cannot turn these results into computational results from this paper.

The closest mirror of Arrow’s Theorem would take a population distribution over complete preference types and ask which measurable social aggregators satisfy continuum versions of Pareto, independence, and nondictatorship. That is a coherent question, and the axioms would need careful restatement for a continuum. But its answer would be another axiomatic impossibility or characterization theorem. The same applies to Theorem 2: replacing utility profiles with distributions of utility types may produce a distributional welfarism theorem, but it still classifies rules by their properties rather than analyzing algorithms.

Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 offer the best chance of constructing a computational task. For example, given rational masses on finitely many complete utility types and an explicitly listed finite set of alternatives, one could compute utilitarian rankings by weighted sums. A leximin variant could compare the resulting utility distributions. With that input, the computations largely amount to summing or sorting the listed data. To get a substantial optimization problem, one would need to add an implicit or constrained alternative space, a representation of the utilities, and a task such as finding the best feasible alternative. That could be worthwhile work, but its computational substance would come from the added allocation model, not from either characterization in this chapter. For leximin in particular, a continuum also requires a choice of how to compare distributions of utility levels; the chapter supplies no such choice.

The proposed population of households with repeated welfare-relevant types is plausible under the programme’s definition of high multiplicity. So I would not reject it on the ground that individuals have distinct identities or that types cannot repeat. It simply does not supply the missing computational problem. Section 13’s discussion of infinite populations gives a reason to study welfare aggregation beyond finite societies, but it discusses normative issues such as Pareto rankings and impartiality, not computational complexity. An infinite sequence of generations is also not itself a distribution over types.

I therefore find a strong case against treating this paper as a source of a ChoCo computational mirror: every named result the proponent cites leads, at best, to a new axiomatic question. The negative case is weaker as a universal claim that no related continuous welfare-optimization problem could ever be valuable. Such a problem could be built, but it would need to be formulated from an additional computational model rather than mirrored from a result in this chapter.
