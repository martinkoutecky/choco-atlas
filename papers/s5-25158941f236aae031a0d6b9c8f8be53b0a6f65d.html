The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give a compliant numbered anchor: the paper has Definition 1 and Algorithms 1–2, but no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. The closest candidate is an unnumbered claim in §4.3: maximizing Expected Utility of Selection (EUS) over choice sets is NP-hard in general. The paper attributes this to prior work; it does not present it as a numbered result proved there. So the paper has no qualifying anchor under your rule.

That EUS claim is still the strongest lead for a narrow mirror. Consider a lender serving a very large bucket of applicants with the same initial profile, classifier, action set, and cost correlation structure. Suppose the bucket contains a small number \(\tau\) of recurring preference types, each sharing the same cost weights \(w_t\), with rational mass \(\mu_t\). A finite pool \(\mathcal I\) of action sequences is feasible and achieves recourse for everyone in the bucket. The lender must choose one menu \(O\subseteq\mathcal I\), of size \(k\), to show applicants. Under the paper’s noiseless response model, define the menu’s cohort value as \(\Phi(O)=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{I\in O}P_{\mathrm{NL}}(O\rightsquigarrow I\mid w_t)[-C(I\mid w_t)]\). The problem, Cohort EUS Recourse Menu, is to return a size-\(k\) menu maximizing \(\Phi(O)\). Every type represents otherwise indistinguishable users with the same preferences and costs; mass is their share of the applicant cohort. A plausible regime is millions of applicants in a standardized denial bucket and tens or hundreds of recurring cost schedules.

This mirrors the paper’s choice-set computation directly: it uses the same recourse plans, cost function, response model, and EUS objective, but averages utility over a population distribution rather than one user’s posterior over possible weights. For exact optimization, I would expect hardness to transfer if the finite-support EUS hardness applies to these recourse-feasible plans and CCS-induced costs. Under the noiseless model, the paper also says EUS is monotone submodular and gives a greedy \((1-e^{-1})\) approximation guarantee; the mass-weighted sum preserves submodularity, so the cohort objective has the same approximation route. Further questions are whether hardness survives the CCS restrictions, how the result depends on \(k\) and \(\tau\), and whether query feedback should update menus over multiple rounds.

The scope is just choice-set selection, not the full PEAR loop, its Bayesian preference updates, or W-FARE’s intervention search. My weakest point is that PEAR’s stated task personalizes queries to an individual, while this mirror imposes a shared menu on a cohort. The authors would likely recognize it as a batch version of their EUS problem, but they could reasonably regard the shared-menu requirement as a deployment variant rather than their central problem. The paper’s unnumbered hardness statement also does not establish the needed reduction for the more restricted cohort model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no qualifying anchor: it states no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing a computational result. Definition 1 formalizes personalized recourse, and Algorithms 1–2 describe PEAR and its greedy query procedure, but the paper’s experiments are empirical. Its unnumbered claim that EUS maximization is NP-hard is attributed to prior work; the approximation guarantee is also cited from prior work. That gives a useful lead, but not a computational result of this paper to mirror.

The proposed cohort menu is the strongest lead, and its best version is mathematically clear. For a shared starting profile and noiseless responses, its objective is
\[ \Phi(O)=\sum_t \mu_t\max_{I\in O}\bigl[-C(I\mid w_t)\bigr]. \]
But this is exactly the paper’s EUS objective under the distribution \(P(w)=\sum_t\mu_t\delta_{w_t}\). The paper already permits priors fitted to population-level preference data and models subgroups in its preference distribution. So, in this setting, replacing uncertainty about one user’s weights with population shares changes the interpretation of the distribution, not the optimization problem. This is a model identity, not an objection that an outside answer is already known: the proposed mirror adds no population-specific computational question.

For a broader applicant population, make a type include the full state, preferences, feasible actions, and action preconditions. If recourse can be tailored to each observed type, the expected objective separates across types; multiplying each type’s value by its mass does not couple their decisions. A common menu creates coupling, but that is the extra batch-recommendation constraint doing the computational work. It is a plausible deployment variant, yet the task is then average utility from a shared menu, not PEAR’s problem of eliciting one target user’s preferences. The paper’s EUS machinery already describes the menu objective in the homogeneous-profile case.

I cannot make the universal negative airtight. A lender serving a large, standardized applicant cohort could reasonably want one small recourse menu optimized for the cohort, and that may be a worthwhile problem in its own right. The stronger case against is narrower: it is not a computational result established by this paper, and the natural cohort formulation either reduces to its existing EUS objective or introduces a new shared-menu constraint. That leaves the “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” claim weak where it matters most: the batch-menu scenario is coherent, even if it does not supply the required paper anchor.
