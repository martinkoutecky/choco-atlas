The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\) with \(|C|=2^d\), a target \(\alpha^\star\), and a list of rational ranking distributions \(\mu^{(1)},\ldots,\mu^{(s)}\), form each scenario's pairwise-majority tournament, breaking ties by a fixed priority order. Is there one seeding of \(C\) into the leaves of the perfect binary bracket such that \(\alpha^\star\) wins in every scenario? For the hardness anchor, ask whether this remains NP-hard when either the shared feedback arc set size or the number of private arcs is fixed to a constant.

The model it lives in

Each electorate is a distribution \(\mu^{(r)}\) over complete rankings of \(C\), specified by its positive-mass support and binary rational masses. The mass of rankings preferring \(a\) to \(b\) determines their pairwise majority orientation, with ties resolved by a fixed priority order. The decision variable is a single seeding; the objective is whether the target wins in every induced tournament.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the STF hardness result in Lemma 6 and the joint-parameter algorithm in Theorem 5. It does not cover the probabilistic PTF result, which would need a separate model for the source of match uncertainty.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does bounded maximum ranking-support size \(\max_r \tau_r\) alone yield tractability when the induced shared feedback arc set size and private-arc count are unbounded?

The case FOR (proponent)

The credible mirror is simultaneous agenda control over large electorates. Candidates remain the players in the knockout bracket; the continuous population is the voters whose ranking distributions determine the pairwise election outcomes. That keeps the paper’s seeding decision intact while making the society itself a distribution over voter types.

Call the problem Continuous Simultaneous Agenda Control (CSAC). An instance has a candidate set \(C\), with \(|C|=n=2^d\), a target candidate \(\alpha^\star\), and a list of electorate scenarios \(r\in[s]\). Each scenario is a distribution \(\mu^{(r)}\) over strict rankings of \(C\), given by its positive-mass support and binary rational masses. A voter’s type is their complete ranking; \(\mu_t^{(r)}\) is the fraction of electorate \(r\) with ranking \(t\). For candidates \(a,b\), compute
\[ q_r(a,b)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t^{(r)}. \]
Orient their match according to the majority under \(\mu^{(r)}\), breaking exact ties by a fixed priority order. This produces a tournament \(D_r\). The question is whether there is one seeding \(\gamma\) of the candidates into the leaves of the perfect binary bracket such that \(\alpha^\star\) wins in every \(D_r\). A solution is that seeding.

The natural regime is a fixed agenda or bracket that must work across several large constituencies, consultation rounds, or possible electorate profiles. Each electorate may have many voters but relatively few distinct rankings: its population size can be much larger than its support size \(\tau_r\). The mass distribution describes those proportions directly, regardless of how many times each ranking is replicated. This is a recognizable election and agenda-control setting, and the ranking is a complete type for the majority rule used here.

My lead anchor is Lemma 6, proved in this paper. It states that STF parameterized by the shared feedback arc set size is para-NP-hard, and STF parameterized by the number of private arcs is also para-NP-hard. In other words, either parameter alone can be fixed to a constant without making the problem polynomial-time solvable, unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). The paper’s first case uses one tournament, so the private-arc count is zero. For the shared-FAS case, it reduces an arbitrary Tournament Fixing instance to two tournaments: the original tournament and a transitive one in which the target beats everyone. Their shared digraph is acyclic, so its shared FAS is zero.

That hardness carries to CSAC. Every tournament on \(C\) can be represented as the strict-majority relation of a rational distribution over rankings. One standard construction adds two ballots for each desired arc \(a\to b\): one ranks \(a\) above \(b\) and then the remaining candidates in some order; the other ranks the remaining candidates in reverse order and then puts \(a\) above \(b\). Together, these ballots give \(a\) a strict margin over \(b\), while their contributions cancel on every other pair. Applying this construction separately to each tournament in an STF instance gives electorate distributions with polynomial support and preserves every bracket outcome. The two zero-parameter hardness constructions therefore carry over as well. Each profile can also be represented by arbitrarily many replicated voters without changing its masses. The lead question is consequently whether CSAC remains hard with either shared FAS or private arcs alone fixed; the expected answer is yes, by hardness transfer. This is a Class B case: the agenda combinatorics in the candidate set survive the population continuation.

The complementary anchor is Theorem 5, also proved here. It states that STF is fixed-parameter tractable when parameterized jointly by the size of the shared FAS and the number of private arcs. The corresponding CSAC question is the same instance and seeding problem, now parameterized by \((\kappa,\pi)\), where \(\kappa\) and \(\pi\) are those two parameters of the majority tournaments induced by the electorate profiles. Compute the tournaments from the rational masses, then run the paper’s algorithm. This gives an FPT algorithm with running time \(f(\kappa,\pi)\operatorname{poly}(n,\text{input length})\). The expected classification is tractable in this joint-parameter regime, even though neither parameter suffices by itself. Lemma 6 is the stronger anchor for ChoCo’s central question about surviving hardness; Theorem 5 supplies a useful boundary on that hardness.

This scope covers the paper’s deterministic STF results, not its probabilistic PTF result. Modeling PTF through voter populations would require an additional account of where match uncertainty comes from—for example, independently sampled voter panels—and would be a different mirror question. Here voter mass induces deterministic majority tournaments.

The weakest point is that CSAC uses each voter distribution only through the pairwise majority orientations. Margins and most other information in the population disappear once those tournaments are computed, so this is a clean high-multiplicity electorate problem but not a showcase for continuous-optimization machinery. The simultaneous-society requirement is also more specific than a single election, though a fixed agenda robust across several constituencies is plausible. Those limitations do not break the mirror: it preserves the paper’s agenda-control question, its seeding action, and its target-winner condition, while the continuous object is genuinely the voter population. A useful next question is whether small support size \(\tau\), or uncertainty sets of nearby voter distributions, yields additional algorithms when \(\kappa\) and \(\pi\) are large.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat the proposed electorate mirror. The strongest objection is that it may be a low priority for ChoCo, not that it is ill-posed or unrecognized.

Treating the tournament’s players as a continuum fails more directly: each bracket leaf must hold one contestant, and the output is one named champion. A mass of contestants cannot be seeded into that bracket without changing the problem; if the target is instead a type, the identity-specific winner condition has changed. But the proponent’s voter-population model avoids this obstacle. Voters have complete ranking types, the society is a distribution over those types, and the paper itself connects tournament fixing to elections, agenda control, and sequential majority voting.

For each electorate, the masses can be summed into pairwise majorities and then discarded. Every rational profile can be scaled to an integer ballot profile with exactly the same majorities. Conversely, the proponent’s construction realizes any tournament using polynomially many ballots. So CSAC is polynomially equivalent to the paper’s STF problem: Theorem 5 and Lemma 6 carry over. That does not make the mirror a collision with existing continuous work, and the fact that hardness survives is not an objection. It does show, though, that the population contributes no new computational structure here. Replicating voters changes nothing; the support size, mass precision, and electorate size play no role after the majority tournaments are computed. The algorithms and hardness remain governed by the candidate agenda.

The probabilistic result is harder to mirror through an ordinary large electorate. If pairwise outcomes come from sampling voters, nonzero majority margins become deterministic as the panel grows. Keeping uncertainty requires adding a separate model of uncertain electorate scenarios, which moves back toward STF. That weakens a voter-based mirror of PTF, but it does not remove the deterministic STF mirror.

The most I can argue against is that this paper is a thin continuization: the continuous population acts as an input representation for a finite tournament problem, rather than creating fractional decisions or a population-sensitive algorithmic question. That may make it less valuable to prioritize. It does not defeat the anchors. Under the stated grading rule, both survive, and the negative case is weak.
