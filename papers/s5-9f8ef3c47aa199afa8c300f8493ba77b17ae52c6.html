The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_A)_{A\subseteq C}\) over approval types, \(J\subseteq C\), committee size \(k\), distance bound \(r\), and rational mass budget \(\beta\), decide whether transfers \(x_{A,B}\geq 0\) with \(B\supsetneq A\), \(\lvert B\setminus A\rvert\leq r\), \(\sum_B x_{A,B}\leq\mu_A\), and \(\sum_{A,B}x_{A,B}\leq\beta\) can produce \(\mu'\) in which no \(j\in J\) belongs to any winning \(k\)-committee under SAV.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity approval electorate has types \(A\subseteq C\) carrying mass \(\mu_A\). Variables \(x_{A,B}\) transfer mass to allowed supersets \(B\), with objective to minimize changed mass or test feasibility under \(\beta\), subject to SAV winner-avoidance inequalities.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is the SAV half of Theorem 19, used as a hardness anchor.

Theorem 19, proved in this paper, states that for every integer \(r\geq 1\), \(r\)-VoteAddChg destructive bribery under SAV and NSAV is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard even when \(k=1\). In the SAV part, the input has an approval election, distinguished candidates \(J\), a budget of at most \(\ell\) vote-level addition changes, and a Hamming-distance bound \(r\); the question is whether the changes can ensure that no candidate in \(J\) is a winning singleton. The proof is by reduction from RX3C. I use only the SAV branch of this theorem; the NSAV extension is not needed for the mirror.

Call the mirror Continuous Mass Vote-Addition Destructive SAV. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a finitely supported distribution \(\mu=(\mu_A)_{A\subseteq C}\) over approval types \(A\), a distinguished set \(J\subseteq C\), a committee size \(k\), a distance bound \(r\), and a rational mass budget \(\beta\). A type \(A\) means a voter approving exactly \(A\); all voters of that type have the same unit cost and the same allowed operation. The mass \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of society of that type.

A solution is a collection of transfers \(x_{A,B}\geq 0\), where \(B\supsetneq A\) and \(|B\setminus A|\leq r\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_{B}x_{A,B}\leq \mu_A \]

for every source type \(A\), and

\[ \sum_{A,B}x_{A,B}\leq \beta. \]

The transferred mass changes from approval type \(A\) to \(B\); all untransferred mass stays at \(A\). The resulting distribution is \(\mu'\). Under SAV, candidate \(c\) has score

\[ \operatorname{SAV}_{\mu'}(c) = \sum_{B\ni c}\frac{\mu'_B}{|B|}. \]

The decision question is whether there is such a transfer with no member of \(J\) contained in any winning \(k\)-committee. For the direct \(k=1\) mirror of Theorem 19, take \(J=C\setminus\{p\}\); then the requirement is exactly that \(p\) become the unique SAV winner. The optimization version minimizes the changed mass \(\beta\).

This is a genuine high-multiplicity scenario: imagine a very large electorate repeatedly expressing approval over a fixed slate of proposals, with perhaps millions of voters in each of a moderate number \(\tau\) of approval types. A campaign can reach a fraction of each type, and adding an approval to a reached voter is precisely the paper’s VoteAddChg operation. The candidates and approval sets remain discrete; only the population and the operation budget become continuous. Thus this is population continuization, not divisible committees, probabilistic voting, or a noise model.

The paper’s RX3C reduction also shows why this is an interesting mirror rather than a restatement. In its construction, every element belongs to exactly three three-candidate votes. In the continuous version, give each such type mass \(1\) and move mass \(1/3\) from every three-set type \(H\) to \(H\cup\{p\}\). The total changed mass is

\[ 3\kappa\cdot \frac13=\kappa, \]

exactly the discrete budget. Candidate \(p\) gains SAV score \(\kappa/4\). Each element candidate loses

\[ 3\cdot\frac13\left(\frac13-\frac14\right)=\frac1{12}, \]

so \(p\) becomes the unique winner regardless of whether an exact cover exists. The discrete \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hardness therefore dissolves because the reduction needs indivisible whole-vote choices. With \(q\) repeated voters of every type, the same construction is a legitimate large high-multiplicity electorate, and the fractional transfer can be implemented exactly whenever \(q\) is divisible by \(3\).

I would expect this mirror to be Class A. For \(k=1\), the winner inequalities are linear in the transfer variables. Although there may be exponentially many possible output approval sets \(B\), the pricing problem for SAV is simple: for a given source type \(A\), one considers each permitted cardinality \(|B|\) and adds the highest-weight candidates outside \(A\). This suggests a column-generation or separation-based LP algorithm polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. The natural follow-up questions are whether the result extends to arbitrary \(k\) and \(J\), whether NSAV retains tractable pricing, and how much rounding error is needed to recover a finite \(N\)-voter solution.

The weakest point is that Theorem 19’s own reduction is not high-multiplicity: its set types occur once. I therefore cannot claim that the theorem’s hardness transfers to the continuum; indeed, its exact-cover gadget fails under fractional mass. But that is precisely the meaningful ChoCo question. The authors’ underlying problem—destructive VoteAddChg under SAV with the same approval operations, distance bound, and winner-avoidance objective—survives intact, while the population regime exposes whether its discrete hardness was caused by population indivisibility.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative attack is that Theorem 19’s reduction does not survive continuization. Its hardness comes from selecting whole votes corresponding to sets. In the mirror, mass can be split across all set types.

Normalize the \(3\kappa\) set-vote types so that each has mass \(1/(3\kappa)\). Transfer one third of every type to the type obtained by adding \(p\). The changed mass is

\[ 3\kappa\cdot \frac{1}{9\kappa}=\frac13, \]

the normalized analogue of the discrete budget \(\ell=\kappa\). Candidate \(p\) gains SAV score \(1/12\). Each element candidate loses only

\[ 3\cdot\frac{1}{9\kappa}\left(\frac13-\frac14\right) =\frac{1}{36\kappa}, \]

so its final score is \(11/(36\kappa)\), below \(p\)'s score for the paper’s \(\kappa\ge 6\). Thus the continuous instance is feasible regardless of whether an exact cover exists. The reduction’s combinatorial core has disappeared.

That is the best available negative point—but it is not a valid objection under the programme’s standard. The purpose of the mirror is precisely to discover that discrete hardness was caused by indivisible voters. The resulting question remains perfectly well posed: given a distribution over approval types and a fractional campaign budget, can mass be moved by allowed approval additions so that the distinguished candidates cease to win?

Nor do the stronger versions repair the negative case. Approval ballots have no identity-dependent objective here: the rules, operation, and winner condition depend only on approval sets. If campaign prices differ, price can simply be included in the type, as the programme’s definition requires. A large electorate repeatedly voting over a fixed slate of proposals is a natural high-multiplicity regime, and a campaign reaching a fraction of each approval type is a direct interpretation of the mass transfer.

The continuum also does not degenerate. With normalized population mass, a fixed budget \(\beta>0\) changes scores by a fixed positive amount; nothing is being divided away. Restricting transfers to whole type blocks would avoid fractional behavior only by abandoning the intended high-multiplicity relaxation.

The proposed LP may need a more careful complexity analysis: its output types are exponentially numerous, and “simple pricing” should be proved rather than asserted. But that is an algorithmic question of exactly the kind ChoCo is meant to ask, not a defect in the mirror. Existing high-multiplicity work would support this modelling choice rather than collide with it, and the paper itself contains no continuous-population treatment.

So the honest negative case fails on its central burden. It can show that the paper’s RX3C hardness does not transfer, but that is a valuable Class A outcome, not evidence that the mirror is unworthwhile. Theorem 19 therefore remains a surviving anchor, and I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
