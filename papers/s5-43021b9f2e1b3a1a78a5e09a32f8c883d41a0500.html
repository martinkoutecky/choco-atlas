The case FOR (proponent)

I can identify a plausible population-continuous question, but I cannot honestly give it the required kind of anchor. The paper has no named result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Theorems 1–3 are proved here, but they concern properties of the weighted scoring function. For example, Theorem 1 says that the continuous weighted scoring function is the unique weighted scoring function based on \(f\) satisfying linearity and independence of zero-weight members. That is a formal result about the score construction, not the complexity of choosing a winning committee. Here “continuous” refers to the committee weight vector; the paper does not make the voter population continuous.

The strongest plausible mirror I can salvage is Continuous Weighted-Borda Committee Winner, but it is an extension of the paper’s model, not a mirror of a named computational result. An instance has \(m\) candidates, a committee size \(k\), an undirected compatibility graph \(G\) on the candidates, and a society \(\mu\) given by at most \(\tau\) complete rankings with binary-rational masses summing to one. Use the paper’s Borda committee score, averaged over \(\mu\):
\[ f_\mu(U)=\sum_t \mu_t\sum_{a\in U}\bigl(m-r_t(a)\bigr). \]
For each committee \(W\), assign candidate \(a\in W\) its degree in \(G[W]\), and use those degrees as the committee weight vector. The decision question is whether some size-\(k\) committee has continuous weighted score at least a given rational threshold \(Q\); a solution is such a committee. Equivalently, the optimization version asks for a maximum-score committee.

The regime is a large membership organization electing a small board: many members share a relatively small number of rankings, while compatibility between potential board members is represented separately by \(G\). In particular, the society can have \(N\) members, with each of \(m\) ranking types held by \(N/m\) members, for arbitrarily large \(N\). This is a natural high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s election model: it preserves its committee objective and replaces repeated individual ballots with their rational proportions.

The paper’s Theorem 3, proved here, says that the continuous weighted score is independent of the ordering of the weights if and only if the underlying scoring function is candidate-wise, meaning \(f(W)=\sum_{a\in W}f(\{a\})\). Borda is candidate-wise, so in this proposed mirror the weighted score simplifies to
\[ \sum_{a\in W}\deg_{G[W]}(a)\,f_\mu(\{a\}). \]
That theorem helps specify the mirror’s objective, but it is not a computational anchor.

I expect this proposed problem to be hard by transfer from discrete instances. In fact, there is a direct hardness argument for the proposed problem itself, though it is not in the paper: take \(m\) cyclic rankings, each with mass \(1/m\), so every candidate has Borda score \((m-1)/2\). Then committee \(W\)’s weighted score is \((m-1)|E(G[W])|\). Setting \(Q=(m-1)\binom{k}{2}\) asks whether \(G\) has a \(k\)-clique. Thus the decision problem is NP-hard even with \(\tau=m\), and its hardness comes from the candidate and compatibility structure, not population multiplicity. Further questions include whether useful approximation or parameterized algorithms exist for restricted compatibility graphs, and what happens as \(\tau\) varies.

The weak point is decisive for the requested case: this mirror has no qualifying anchor in the paper. The authors define the weighted rule as selecting score-maximizing committees, so winner determination is a recognizable computational question about their model; but they neither study its complexity nor prove the hardness above. This supports a plausible research extension, not a claim that the paper contains a computational result whose continuous mirror should be classified.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper contains no named computational result to continuize. Theorems 1–3 characterize properties of a weighted score: uniqueness under linearity and zero-weight independence, independence from null members, and when the score ignores the order of weights. None classifies the complexity of finding a winning committee.

Those theorems do extend to a continuous society, but only by replacing the voter sum with an expectation. For a ranking distribution \(\mu\), set \(f_\mu(W)=\sum_t\mu_t f(t,W)\); the characterizations then apply pointwise to \(f_\mu\). That gives a continuous-population version of the paper’s algebraic statements, not a computational question for the programme.

The strongest computational proposal is the continuous weighted-Borda winner problem with compatibility degrees from a graph. It is coherent, and the paper’s communication example makes that compatibility model plausible. But its optimization question is a new follow-up: the paper defines score-maximizing rules without studying the complexity of finding their winners. For Borda, the society enters this proposal through the \(m\) candidate scores \(b_a(\mu)\), and the objective becomes \(\sum_{a\in W}\deg_{G[W]}(a)b_a(\mu)\). The clique construction may establish hardness for that proposed problem; it does not supply a computational result from this paper to mirror.

So the negative case is strong on the requested anchoring criterion: no anchor survives as a computational result of the paper. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile continuous research question exists. The winner problem could be a sensible ChoCo extension, but the case for it comes from a newly posed algorithmic problem, not from continuizing one of this paper’s results.
