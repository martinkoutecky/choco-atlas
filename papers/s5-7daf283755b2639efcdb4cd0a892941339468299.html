What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror would cover the learning guarantees in Theorem 1 and Corollary 1, and potentially Theorem 2; it leaves out any computational complexity or optimization result, which the paper does not provide.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying case for a computational mirror from this paper: it has no numbered result classifying a problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized tractable. Theorem 1 gives the almost-sure exponential decay rate of each wrong-hypothesis belief when that hypothesis is transmitted with positive probability; Corollary 1 gives sufficient conditions for truth learning; Theorem 2 rules out total mislearning. These are convergence results for a finite network, not complexity results. The introduction’s NP-hardness statement concerns fully Bayesian group inference, cites reference [13], and is not a numbered result proved or analyzed here.

The most plausible population regime would be a large sensor network with many nodes but few repeated sensor and network-role types. A type would need to include both its likelihood model and its communication role; its mass would be the fraction of nodes of that type. One could then ask whether a continuum version of the paper’s bootstrapping process learns the truth under a given trending-topic distribution. That is a reasonable analytic limit to investigate, but the paper supplies no computational anchor for it, and deriving the continuum interaction and belief dynamics would be substantial new work. The communication structure may also make the number of distinct types large, weakening the high-multiplicity case. Under the requested anchor rule, there are zero continuous problems I can honestly submit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent submits no computational mirror to defend; they explicitly conclude that there are zero. Theorem 1, Corollary 1, and Theorem 2 are the strongest possible anchors, but each analyzes learning behavior in a finite network. None classifies the complexity of deciding or optimizing a computational problem. That is the central obstacle: continuizing this paper could yield new analysis, but the paper gives ChoCo no computational question to continuize.

The strongest candidate is a continuum analogue of Theorem 1’s convergence rate. One could model a large sensor network with repeated sensor and communication roles, then ask how wrong-hypothesis beliefs decay across the population. But the rate depends on both agents’ information and the network’s Perron vector. A mass distribution over sensor types alone does not determine that vector or the interactions: a complete type would also have to encode network role. In sparse networks, those roles can be highly individualized, so the support may grow with the population. In a dense block model or graphon model, a limit may be sensible, but it becomes an analytic limit of network dynamics. The theorem itself offers no complexity problem; optimizing network structure or topic schedules would add a design objective absent from the paper.

Corollary 1 has the same limitation. Its condition combines global identifiability with positive transmission probability for each wrong hypothesis. A continuum version could replace “some agent can distinguish this hypothesis” with a positive mass of informative agents, then prove a learning result. That is a coherent analytic question, but it does not arise as a computational question in the paper. Any algorithmic problem about choosing sensor types or transmission schedules would be a new design problem.

Theorem 2 is less suited to an aggregate mirror. It rules out total mislearning for every agent in the finite network. In an atomless population, an individual agent has zero mass, so the natural analogue is usually an almost-everywhere claim; preserving a guarantee for every agent would require extra uniform assumptions or explicit identity-level information. Such a reformulation may be mathematically worthwhile, but again it extends a martingale result rather than exposing a computational problem.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here: repeated sensors with the same likelihood model and network role. So the negative case is not that a continuous population model would be nonsensical. It is that every plausible mirror of the named results remains an analytic learning question. A computational study of network or communication design could be worthwhile, but it would introduce the very optimization problem the paper does not formulate. The case against a worthwhile *mirror* is strong; the broader claim that no scenario could ever motivate related computational work is harder to establish.
