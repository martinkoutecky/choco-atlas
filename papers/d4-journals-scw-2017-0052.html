What the mirror covers

The proposed anchor, Theorem 6, covers finite-range allocation rules and line implementability; the other theorems and Proposition 1 are also structural results, not computational anchors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest hook is Theorem 6, proved in this paper: for a convex type set \(T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^d\), continuous valuations \(v(a,\cdot)\), and a finite-range allocation rule \(f:T\to A\), \(f\) is implementable if and only if its restriction to every line segment in \(T\) is implementable. Here, implementability means there is a payment function making truthful reporting optimal against every alternative report. This is a useful structural characterization, but it is not a named complexity result, and the paper does not compute with a formulation in a way that supplies the required kind of anchor.

That distinction defeats the positive case under the programme’s rules. The paper’s continuum is one agent’s set of possible private types, not a population distribution over many agents. Adding a distribution \(\mu\) over those types would not make the theorem a population result: \(\mu\) does not enter its incentive constraints. Turning it into a population-weighted allocation or repair problem would add a new objective the paper does not study. Theorem 6 may suggest geometric tools for future algorithms, but it does not support a continuous-population problem per the required anchor standard. So I cannot responsibly give a mirror question or predict its complexity class. The paper offers a structural analogy, not a qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6 is the strongest possible anchor, and the better version of its mirror still fails the programme’s test. Its continuum is one agent’s set of possible private types \(T\), not a population of agents. “Finite range” describes the allocation rule’s outcomes; it does not describe a society with many repeated types. The theorem characterizes when that one-agent rule is implementable. It gives no computational problem with an input encoding, objective, algorithm, or complexity bound. The paper’s references to linear programming and network distances are proof machinery for a structural result; they do not supply a pricing algorithm or a complexity landscape.

A stronger proposed mirror might introduce a large population with type proportions \(\mu_t\), then ask for a truthful allocation rule or for the smallest share of people whose allocations must change. If the paper’s incentive constraints are retained, the proportions do no work: truthfulness must hold for every pair of reports, regardless of how common either type is. If the constraints are weighted by \(\mu\), rare types or deviations can disappear from consideration, changing the question to a Bayesian or almost-everywhere notion of incentive compatibility. And if an individual’s report affects allocations to other people, a distribution of types generally does not capture the unilateral deviations the incentive constraints compare. In an atomless limit, one person’s change has zero mass; preserving a non-negligible strategic effect would require a different model or a different strategic unit.

The same diagnosis applies to the other named results. Theorems 1–5 and the example characterize implementability, payments, or local-to-global structure. They do not assert computational complexity. A distribution over types could motivate a new mechanism-design problem, perhaps involving expected revenue, but the distribution would be a prior or an added objective, not the continuous population problem established by this paper.

So the negative case is strong for a *mirror of this paper*: its continuum concerns possible states of one agent, and its results are characterizations rather than computational results about a continuous society. A related multi-agent continuum mechanism-design project could be worthwhile, but it would need a new problem formulation and would not be a mirror of the paper’s results.
