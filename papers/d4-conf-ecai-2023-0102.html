The continuous mirror question

Given a fixed finite rooted directed network template, a finite menu of community profiles with rational valuations and rational population shares, a supply density \(q\), and a specified tie-breaking rule, form societies by replicating the communities and connecting their entry roles to seller \(s\). With \(m_k=\lfloor qhk\rfloor\) items for \(k\) copies of an \(h\)-buyer template, compute the limiting allocation and payment mass for each role when MUDAN runs as \(k\) grows through compatible denominators. Determine its worst-case limiting welfare factor over the allowed profiles against the top-\(q\)-mass benchmark, evaluating each tagged buyer's incentive comparison in the finite replicas before taking the limit.

The model it lives in

A continuum of copies of finite network communities, represented by rational masses over community profiles and buyer roles. Per-role allocation and payment masses are the outputs, inventory scales at density \(q\), and individual deviations remain tagged in finite replicas.

The objection that survived

A tagged deviation has zero population mass, so incentive compatibility must be carried by finite-replica constraints rather than the limiting type-mass mechanism alone; this confines the mirror to an explicitly tagged network-limit model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the single-demand MUDAN result in Theorem 8. It leaves aside Theorem 9's tightness claim, the multi-demand result in Theorem 12, the reward-based MUDAR results, and the experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a fixed template and tie-breaking rule, do MUDAN's per-role allocation and payment masses converge, and can their limits be computed from the profile masses?
  • What worst-case limiting welfare factor does MUDAN achieve against the top-\(q\)-mass benchmark?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is for a narrow, replicated-network mirror of MUDAN. This paper has no NP-hardness result to carry into a continuum. Its best computational anchor is a polynomial-time mechanism result; a second useful anchor is a tight welfare guarantee.

Take a large seller-run marketplace made up of many structurally identical local communities. Each community has a fixed, small social-network template: for example, the same connector and member roles, with the seller seeding the same entry roles in every community. Buyers in the same role have the same valuation, demand, and local neighbor-role pattern. Communities differ only by their copy labels, which the mechanism treats symmetrically. If there are \(k\) communities, each with \(h\) buyers, then \(n=kh\) grows while the number \(\tau\) of role types stays fixed. Let the seller’s inventory scale with the population, so that \(m_k=qkh\) for a fixed supply density \(q\in(0,1]\).

A type is a community class and a role within that community; it includes the buyer’s value and reportable local connections. A type’s mass is its fraction of all buyers. The continuous mechanism chooses an allocation mass \(a_t\) for each type, with \(0\leq a_t\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t a_t=q\), and a payment per allocated unit. Its welfare per buyer is \(\sum_t v_t a_t\). This preserves the paper’s central objects: private valuations, diffusion through reported neighbor sets, competition as the auction explores the network, and threshold payments. The co-modification is that supply scales with population.

The lead anchor is Theorem 8, proved in this paper. For single-demand multi-unit diffusion auctions, MUDAN terminates in \(O(n^2+|E|)\) time and satisfies incentive compatibility, individual rationality, non-deficit, non-wastefulness, and \(1/m\)-weak efficiency. Here, weak efficiency compares its welfare with the sum of the top \(m\) valuations among buyers for whom the final winner is not critical: that winner is not on every path from the seller to them.

The corresponding question is Replicated-Template MUDAN Limit. Its instance is a fixed finite rooted community template, a finite set of permitted community profiles, their rational population masses, and a rational supply density \(q\). Its solution is the exact limiting allocation and payment mass for each type when MUDAN is run on increasingly many copies, together with a proof that the limit exists and preserves IR, non-deficit, non-wastefulness, and tagged-buyer incentive compatibility. “Tagged-buyer” means that a buyer’s unilateral deviation is evaluated in the finite replicated auctions before taking the limit; it does not treat a deviation as a positive mass shift. The solver should also give MUDAN’s strongest limiting weak-efficiency factor. The theorem’s stated \(1/m_k\) bound itself tends to zero as supply grows, so the useful question is whether this fixed-type regime admits a nonvanishing bound. The expected outcome is Class A, at least for a fixed template: community exploration has finitely many local states, while masses and supply determine how much of each state is processed. The exact limit and its efficiency factor remain to be proved.

The second anchor is Theorem 9, also proved here. For every \(m\geq1\) and every constant \(\lambda>0\), there is a profile on which no \(m\)-unit, incentive-compatible, non-wasteful diffusion auction without rewards achieves \((1/m+\lambda)\)-weak efficiency. Together with Theorem 8, this makes the discrete \(1/m\) guarantee tight.

Its mirror is the Replicated-Template No-Reward Welfare Frontier. For the same finite template and a specified set of allowed rational type-mass profiles, consider mechanisms that allocate mass \(q\), charge nonnegative payments, and satisfy the tagged-buyer incentive constraints in their finite replicas. The question is to find the largest \(\alpha\) for which one such mechanism guarantees welfare at least \(\alpha\) times the welfare from allocating the top \(q\) mass. In this replicated setting, a single final winner lies in just one community, whose mass vanishes in the limit, so the continuous weak-efficiency benchmark becomes the top \(q\) mass of the population. A solution must give both a mechanism achieving \(\alpha\) and an upper-bound proof ruling out any larger factor. I expect the fixed-template version may admit a finite optimization formulation, but the paper does not establish that. The discrete \(1/m\) impossibility does not settle this question: with \(m_k\) growing with the population, its bound vanishes, and fractional allocation may remove the obstruction.

These are deliberately narrow mirrors of MUDAN’s single-demand, no-reward results. They do not cover the multi-demand extension, the reward-based MUDAR results, or the experiments. The regime should be recognizable to the authors because it retains their strategic reports, local diffusion, allocation, and payments while asking how those behave for a population with many repeated roles.

The soft spot is the network structure. MUDAN’s incentive problem depends on a named buyer’s neighbor report sometimes blocking paths to other buyers. In the replicated scenario, a unilateral report changes one community, whose population mass goes to zero. Tagged-buyer constraints preserve the individual’s utility comparison in finite approximations, but the aggregate limit may lose precisely the macroscopic pivotal effect that motivates diffusion auctions. A version where an individual broker controls access to a positive mass of buyers might better preserve that effect, but a finite-support type distribution alone does not describe such a broker cleanly. This case therefore supports a continuous mirror for repeated local networks, not for arbitrary large social graphs.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would put the objection on the network, not on the absence of a computational result: Theorem 8 is plainly one. MUDAN’s input is a named directed graph, and its exploration and incentive constraints depend on which particular paths a buyer can block by hiding particular neighbors. A histogram of buyer types does not determine those paths. The proposed repeated-template model supplies the missing graph structure, but its “types” identify buyers only up to copy labels; their actual reportable neighbor sets still differ.

That creates a mismatch for incentive compatibility. A deviation by one buyer in a replicated society changes zero mass in the limit. If the continuous mechanism instead lets a type’s report change all of its mass, it tests a coordinated deviation, not the individual deviation in Theorem 8. Tagged-buyer constraints restore the individual comparison, but then the proposed object is a limit of finite graph auctions with a separate tagged-agent game, not a mechanism defined by the population distribution alone.

The fixed-template restriction makes the proposed mirror most workable, and also narrows its computational claim. With finitely many roles and bounded local components, MUDAN’s exploration can be tracked by counts of copies in finitely many local states. Finding its limit may be a useful analysis, but it looks like compressing and taking the limit of this particular algorithm, rather than exposing a new continuous optimization problem. To retain richer graph structure and consequential neighbor reports, the input must retain more of the named graph, which undermines the type-mass compression.

Theorem 9 has a sharper version of the same problem. Its welfare benchmark is tied to criticality with respect to a final winner. In a replicated bounded template, that winner blocks only its own finite component, whose share tends to zero. The proposed top-\(q\)-mass benchmark is then a different welfare frontier; it does not preserve the theorem’s critical-buyer comparison. One can keep a macroscopic critical region by introducing a distinguished broker whose report controls access to a continuum of buyers. But that requires retaining a zero-mass, individually strategic node alongside the continuous population. The resulting frontier would depend on the broker’s position in the network, information flow, and allowed reports, none of which is encoded by the population masses.

So the negative case is strongest against Theorem 9: its particular tightness argument has no evident fixed-support population mirror, and the proposed replacement changes the benchmark. It is weaker against Theorem 8. A continuous audience behind a small set of strategically pivotal brokers is a plausible setting in which a tagged-buyer limit could be worth studying. I cannot honestly rule that out as a worthwhile mirror; I can argue that it requires a new network-limit model rather than just continuizing this paper’s population.
