The continuous mirror question

Fix a top-\(k\)-counting rule family satisfying Theorem 10’s assumptions, with \(g_k\) independent of \(m\), and take \(m\ge 2k\). Given \(m\) candidates, committee size \(k\), a finite support \(T\) of ranking types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether there is a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) such that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t g_k(|W\cap A_t|)\ge q\), where \(A_t\) is type \(t\)’s top-\(k\) set.

The model it lives in

A society is a finite support \(T\) of complete ranking types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\). The decision is a discrete size-\(k\) committee \(W\subseteq C\); its score is the expected type contribution \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t g_k(|W\cap A_t|)\).

What the mirror covers

Covers the winner-determination hardness of Theorem 10, the α_k-CC coverage hardness of Proposition 9, and the NearlyBloc approximation implication of Theorem 12. It leaves the fixed-majority characterization and the paper’s other approximation and parameterized results outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does winner determination admit efficient algorithms when the support size τ is bounded or parameterized?
  • Which restrictions on the incidence between ranking types and their top-\(k\) sets change the complexity?

The case FOR (proponent)

A continuous mirror is credible here because the paper’s winner determination objective already adds up each voter’s contribution. Replacing that sum with an expectation over voter types preserves the rule and makes the population the continuous object.

Let \(C\) be \(m\) candidates, let \(k\) be the committee size, and let \(T\) be a finite support of ranking types with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). Write \(A_t\) for type \(t\)’s top \(k\) candidates. For a committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\), define

\[ \Phi^g_\mu(W)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,g_{m,k}\bigl(|W\cap A_t|\bigr). \]

The continuous top-\(k\) winner problem takes \(C,k,\mu\), and a fixed rule family \(g\) as its instance. It asks for a size-\(k\) committee maximizing \(\Phi^g_\mu(W)\), with a maximizing committee and its score as the solution. Its threshold version also takes a rational \(q\) and asks whether some committee has score at least \(q\). The committee stays discrete; the mass of each voter type supplies its share of the score. Since these rules use only top-\(k\) membership, rankings with the same \(A_t\) can be grouped for this problem.

A plausible regime is a large federation or electorate choosing \(k\) representatives from \(m\) candidates, with many members using recurring ballot templates. A large customer base choosing \(k\) products from a catalog gives a parallel setting for the coverage rule below. There may be millions of agents and far fewer distinct relevant ballot types. The hardness constructions need not have constant support: their number of types can grow with the instance. Replicating every voter an arbitrary number of times keeps the masses and support unchanged while making the realized electorate arbitrarily larger than its number of types.

My lead is Continuous Convex Top-\(k\) Winner, anchored by Theorem 10, proved in this paper. In the paper’s standing regime \(m\ge 2k\), it considers a top-\(k\)-counting rule with counting functions \(g_k\) independent of \(m\). Each \(g_k(x)\) must be polynomial-time computable, with \(g_k(k)\) polynomially bounded in \(k\); and for all sufficiently large \(k\), \(g_k\) must be convex with \(k-\operatorname{sing}(g_k)\ge k/c\) for some constant \(c\), where \(\operatorname{sing}(g_k)\) is the first index at which its successive differences change. Theorem 10 states that deciding whether a committee has score at least a given threshold is NP-hard. Its proof reduces from CLIQUE on regular graphs. The continuous question is the threshold version of \(\Phi^g_\mu\), with the threshold scaled to population mass. Any discrete profile with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\) maps to \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and its committee score becomes \(n\Phi^g_\mu(W)\). Thus the decision threshold scales in the same way. I expect hardness to transfer, in Class B: the reduction’s combinatorics are in the committee and type structure, and the discrete profile embeds in the mass model. This is my strongest anchor because it gives a broad hardness result for convex top-\(k\) rules; NearlyBloc is a particularly concrete rule within its scope for \(k\ge2\). It also prompts a useful boundary question: does hardness persist when the number of relevant types is bounded, or does parameterizing by support size yield algorithms?

A second, more application-readable anchor is Proposition 9, proved in the paper with a short reduction building on Procaccia et al. It states that for \(\alpha_k\)-CC, deciding whether a committee has at least a given score is NP-hard, with \(k\) part of the input. Here \(g(x)=0\) for \(x=0\) and \(g(x)=1\) for \(x\ge1\), so

\[ \Phi^{\alpha_k\text{-CC}}_\mu(W)=\sum_{t:\,W\cap A_t\ne\varnothing}\mu_t. \]

The Continuous \(\alpha_k\)-CC Coverage problem asks whether a size-\(k\) committee can cover mass at least \(q\). The paper’s reduction from X3C uses a voter for each element and a candidate for each set; with committee size \(n\), score \(3n\) is attainable exactly when the universe of \(3n\) elements has an exact cover. In the mass version the threshold is full mass, \(q=1\), and the reduction’s element voters each receive mass \(1/(3n)\). This is a direct high-multiplicity coverage question: choose \(n\) products or representatives to reach the largest possible share of customer or voter types. I expect hardness to transfer. Follow-up questions include whether useful exact or parameterized algorithms emerge for bounded support, or for restricted incidences between types and top-\(k\) sets.

A third anchor sharpens the approximation picture. Theorem 12, proved in the paper, concerns NearlyBloc, with \(g(x)=\max\{x-1,0\}\). It states that no polynomial-time constant-factor approximation for computing the score of a winning committee exists unless a constant-factor approximation exists for Densest At Most \(K\) Subgraph (DAMKS). The proof shows that a \(\theta\)-approximation for NearlyBloc would give a \(\theta/2\)-approximation for DAMKS. The Continuous NearlyBloc Approximation problem asks for a size-\(k\) committee whose \(\Phi_\mu\) value is within a specified factor of the maximum. The score is normalized by total population mass, so the ratio is preserved by the discrete-to-mass map. I expect the same conditional approximation barrier to transfer. The natural next question is whether this implication can be paired with explicit hardness assumptions for the mass-input formulation, and where structural restrictions permit better approximation.

These mirrors cover the paper’s winner determination results for top-\(k\)-counting rules: Theorem 10’s convex-family hardness, Proposition 9’s \(\alpha_k\)-CC hardness, and Theorem 12’s NearlyBloc approximation implication. I would leave the fixed-majority characterization and the other approximation and parameterized results outside this case. The mirror keeps the paper’s rankings, scoring functions, committee size, and winning objective. An author should recognize the question as winner determination over a society described by type masses.

The weakest point is that every rational mass distribution can be represented as a finite election after multiplying through by a common denominator. These anchors therefore do not show that continuization makes winner determination easier; they show that these hardness results survive it. That is a modest positive case, but it is still a substantive Class B outcome: complexity remains even when a large electorate is summarized by its type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about what these mirrors add, not whether they are well posed. In all three, the committee is still a discrete \(k\)-subset of candidates, and the population enters only through a weighted sum. For a profile with \(N\) voters and \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) makes the continuous score exactly the discrete score divided by \(N\). So the proposed questions are clean high-multiplicity versions of the paper’s winner problems, but they introduce no new decision structure or continuous-optimization machinery.

That limits the novelty claim; it does not defeat the anchors. For Theorem 10, the threshold scales by \(N\), so the reduction carries over to rational masses. Its limitation is that the hard instances use growing type support. With fixed support \(\tau\), candidates can be grouped by which of the \(\tau\) top-\(k\) sets contain them. Enumerating how many committee members to select from each of the at most \(2^\tau\) groups gives a polynomial-time algorithm for every fixed \(\tau\). The theorem therefore does not show hardness in a small-support regime, though it remains a valid hardness result as support grows.

Proposition 9 transfers just as directly: give each element type mass \(1/(3n)\), and reaching coverage mass \(1\) is equivalent to the reduction’s score reaching \(3n\). Again, the support grows with the X3C instance, so the result does not establish hardness at fixed support. That is a boundary on the result’s reach, not a reason the continuous question is unworthy.

Theorem 12 also transfers its approximation implication: a mass-based approximation on the reduction’s rational distribution would give the corresponding approximation on the discrete instance. But this is conditional on the DAMKS approximation relationship, and the construction’s support grows with the graph. It supplies no barrier for small support. Still, the conditional reduction is a legitimate continuous mirror.

Thus I cannot honestly defeat all three anchors. Their weaknesses are shared: they show that hardness survives when profiles are represented by type masses, but they do not establish a new tractable continuous-optimization approach or resolve the low-support landscape. That is a case against treating these particular mirrors as evidence of a distinctive continuization payoff. It is not a strong case that no worthwhile mirror exists: the paper has named computational results, the mass model is natural, and the support dependence itself is a reasonable computational question.
