The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow algorithmic one: compute the paper’s rule from a high-multiplicity ballot profile. A plausible regime is a large electorate with many voters but relatively few distinct rankings. A type is a strict ranking; its mass is the fraction of the electorate reporting it. The decision is a lottery over candidates, judged by its worst-case expected metric distortion.

The closest anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: for every preference profile, the rule \(D^*=(D_V+D_{ML})/2\)—the equal mixture of the Veto Lottery and any Maximal Lottery—has distortion at most \(5/2\). This is a quantitative guarantee for a voting rule, not a running-time or complexity-class result. Theorem 6, also proved here, supplies a boundary: even allowing profile-dependent mixing weights and adaptive probability measures over veto times, no rule in that broader family has distortion below \(5/2\).

A continuous computational question suggested by Theorem 4 is High-Multiplicity Veto–Maximal-Lottery Construction. Given candidates \(C\) and a finite support \(\{(\succ_t,\mu_t)\}\) of strict rankings with binary-rational masses summing to \(1\), output an exact lottery \(D\) such that
\[ \sup_{d\ \mathrm{consistent\ with\ the\ ranking\ masses}} \frac{\sum_{i\in C}D(i)\,\mathrm{SC}_d(i)} {\min_{j\in C}\mathrm{SC}_d(j)} \le \frac52. \]
Here the voter space has total mass \(1\), partitioned into groups of masses \(\mu_t\); each member of group \(t\) reports \(\succ_t\). The paper’s \(D^*\) is a solution. I would expect this construction problem to be tractable: a maximal lottery is obtainable by linear programming, and the veto process has at most \(m\) candidate-elimination events, with rates computed from the \(\tau\) supported rankings. The resulting question is whether this can be made an exact algorithm with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), including its bit complexity. Follow-ups include finding the best member of the veto–maximal-lottery family for an individual profile and determining whether such profile-specific improvements can be computed efficiently. The scope is the construction and performance of this rule, not the rest of the paper’s analysis.

There is an important limitation to the case. Under the prompt’s strict anchor requirement—named results establishing complexity such as NP-hardness, polynomial time, or FPT—this paper has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor. Theorems 4 and 6 establish distortion bounds and a family-wide limitation, not complexity classifications. And the proposed population mirror is close to a restatement: the paper already normalizes the voter population to mass \(1\), reasons in voter shares, and defines a continuous-time veto process. The positive case is therefore for an algorithmic, high-multiplicity formulation of its rule, not a claim that the paper’s central model has yet to be continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Neither Theorem 4 nor Theorem 6 supplies the kind of computational result the programme is looking to mirror. Theorem 4 proves a distortion guarantee for a specified rule; Theorem 6 proves a lower bound for a family of rules. Neither classifies the complexity of a voting problem or gives an algorithmic result.

For Theorem 4, the proposed high-multiplicity input is already the paper’s profile semantics in compressed form. The paper normalizes the voter population to mass \(1\), defines its quantities using voter shares, and runs the veto process using those shares as rates. A rational mass vector over rankings can also be represented as an ordinary finite profile by clearing denominators. The theorem is not a discrete-to-continuous identity or limit result: it already applies to the mass-based profile. The proposed guarantee is therefore Theorem 4 restated on that input representation.

There is a plausible exact computation, but it reinforces the scope objection. Aggregate the ranking masses into the pairwise matrix and compute a maximal lottery by linear programming. For the veto lottery, compute each candidate’s veto rate from the supported rankings; the active set changes only when a candidate’s score reaches zero, so there are at most \(m\) elimination events. The proposed task is an implementation of the rule the paper has already defined, rather than a new computational question exposed by continuizing the population. It also lacks the sort of unresolved pricing, manipulation, control, or robustness problem that would make the continuous optimization toolbox central.

Theorem 6 offers no separate anchor. Its proof already uses voter groups with fractional masses, and it allows adaptive veto-time weights and profile-dependent mixing. A computational problem about choosing the best member of that family would be a new optimization question about the rule family; the theorem itself is a distortion lower bound, not a complexity result or a discrete population result awaiting a continuous counterpart.

I would not base the case on the absence of a high-multiplicity regime. Large electorates with relatively few rankings are entirely plausible. The negative case is narrower: this paper already describes its rule through aggregate voter mass, and its proposed continuous mirror mostly changes the input encoding while asking to compute that rule. That leaves a real weak point in the case against: an efficient exact implementation on support-size input could still be useful. It looks like a modest algorithmic follow-up, though, rather than a worthwhile continuization of these results.
