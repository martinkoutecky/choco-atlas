What the mirror covers

No continuous mirror is anchored. The paper presents three Monte Carlo algorithms, sample bounds, and empirical comparisons for approximating Myerson values on finite graphs.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of its own that can serve as an anchor. Algorithms 1–3 are numbered, but they are procedures, not Theorems, Lemmas, Corollaries, or Propositions. Section 4 derives unnumbered sample bounds for the estimators. Its “Theorem 2 [9]” is Hoeffding’s cited concentration theorem, not a result proved here about Myerson-value computation.

So there is no eligible anchor to mirror under the stated criteria. A possible continuous question—computing type-level Myerson values in a large network with many agents sharing a small number of communication roles—would require defining a continuum network game and establishing how its Myerson values relate to the paper’s finite-graph estimators. That may be worth studying, but it would be an unanchored extension, not a continuous mirror of a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s “no named result” point is too formal. The paper does have a named computational result: Algorithm 2, its hybrid estimator, supported by sample bounds and an empirical comparison. The arXiv abstract also describes those algorithmic contributions as the paper’s central work. A plausible anchor is therefore: how does this estimator, or its sample guarantee, change for a large population with repeated communication roles? ([paper record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00065))

The strongest objection is that a population distribution does not preserve the paper’s key input: an arbitrary graph whose individual vertices can have distinct network positions, paired with an arbitrary characteristic function on coalitions. In a mass-based model, adding one atomless player does not change a coalition’s type-mass vector, so the individual marginal value vanishes unless the output is rescaled into a value density. That density would require a new continuum allocation definition. Meanwhile, a graphon-style model can describe a continuum network, but its mass-based representation risks losing the influence of zero-mass connectors; preserving sparse local structure can instead leave each vertex’s neighborhood as part of its type, giving little or no multiplicity. Graphon games show that continuum network models are possible, but their established focus is equilibrium and intervention, not this paper’s Myerson-value approximation problem. ([graphon games paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.00080))

The best rescue is a block network: many people share a few communication roles, and coalition worth depends on role masses. That is sensible, but it restricts both the graph and the game; it replaces the paper’s arbitrary-graph, arbitrary-game setting with a new structured model. I would press that as the negative case. Still, it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists: the block model is plausible, and the authors could recognize it as their problem. The honest negative conclusion is therefore limited—the general result does not carry over cleanly—and the stronger claim that no scenario is worth pursuing remains weak.
