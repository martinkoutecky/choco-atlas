The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result to continuize. Theorems 7, 10, and 14 characterize classes of choice functions; Theorems 17 and 20–22 describe their order structure. None classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithm whose continuous counterpart could be studied. The paper’s central object is a choice function \(c:\mathcal P(X)\to\mathcal P(X)\), not a society of agents. A recognition problem for such functions could be invented, but it would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a result in this paper.

The strongest candidate is Theorem 17’s majority aggregation. One could treat revealed core-solutions as agent types, give each type mass \(\mu_c\), and aggregate each menu by whether more than half the mass chooses each outcome. That is a coherent continuous-population model, perhaps for many similar decision-making units. But the natural question—when this weighted aggregate is itself a revealed core—is a new structural characterization. The theorem’s result is about odd finite profiles, whose majority operation is the median; arbitrary mass weights do not inherit that guarantee. If instead the question is to compute the aggregate, it amounts to summing masses for each menu. Making it a harder optimization problem, such as finding the least mass transfer that changes a menu’s choice, adds an objective absent from the theorem.

So the negative case is strongest at the programme’s gate: even a plausible population interpretation of the paper’s aggregation result does not yield a computational result to mirror. I would not claim that no imaginable second-order population scenario could be useful; the paper’s explicit aggregation theme makes that too strong. But the best candidate shifts the work to a new, primarily axiomatic question, while computational variants require adding a problem the paper never studies.
