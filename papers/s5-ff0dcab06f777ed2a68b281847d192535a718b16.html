The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive clue is that the paper studies mechanisms for variable finite populations, and bidder values could naturally serve as types. A plausible high multiplicity setting would be a large ticket sale with many buyers but relatively few value classes, so \(n \gg \tau\). But that clue does not give me a qualifying anchor: the paper contains no named computational result asserting a complexity classification or algorithmic bound.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here: a direct mechanism is payment normalized, non-wasteful, symmetric, incentive compatible, and Sybil-proof if and only if it is a second-price auction with symmetric tie-breaking. Theorem 2 gives a Bayesian Sybil-proof lottery mechanism under stated conditions; Propositions 3 and 4 give a multi-unit counterexample and an extension of the characterization. These are mechanism-design characterization and existence results, not computational results of the kind the requested anchors require.

A continuous population of value types might motivate a related question, but I cannot honestly present it as a mirror of an eligible anchor. The central Sybil deviation is an individual creating an identity; in an atomless population, one individual has zero mass, so that constraint risks becoming vacuous. Treating a whole positive-mass type group as the attacker would change the strategic model. So I can make a modest case that the paper’s setting suggests a population-level research direction, but there is no named computational anchor here on which to build the requested continuous computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that none of the paper’s named results is computational. Theorem 1 characterizes mechanisms satisfying axioms; Theorem 2 proves a Bayesian mechanism exists; Propositions 3 and 4 give an example and an extension. None gives a complexity bound or algorithm for an input problem. A continuous model could inspire new mechanism-design questions, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The best candidate is Theorem 1, and the ticket-sale scenario makes repeated bidder types plausible. But its Sybil constraint concerns an individual changing the number of accounts. The proof exploits that structure: symmetry gives a newly created account a chance of roughly \(1/n\), and the argument accumulates those chances in a harmonic sum. In a society represented only by type masses, one account has zero mass. Giving a positive-mass group control of fake accounts would turn the individual deviation into a coalition problem. Keeping a distinguished attacker against a continuum of other bidders could preserve the deviation, but that is a hybrid model, and the resulting question would still be an axiomatic characterization rather than a computational one.

Theorem 2 does use a continuous value distribution \(F\), but the number of bidders remains finite. In its uniform example, the ticket price approaches the upper endpoint as \(n\) grows, leaving only \(O(1)\) qualifying bidders in the lottery; their mass vanishes in a normalized population. Fixing a threshold to retain a positive mass of qualifiers would give a different mechanism and a new Bayesian design question, not a continuous-population version of the theorem’s computational result.

Proposition 3 is the strongest counterpoint because supply could scale with population size, a natural co-modification for unit-demand buyers. Even then, the proposition establishes Sybil-proofness for a particular mechanism; its key constraint remains one bidder splitting bids across accounts. Treating a whole type group as that bidder changes the strategic model. Proposition 4 has the same individual-identity issue, while its divisible allocation concerns the good, not the population.

So the negative case is strongest as a boundary on this programme, not as proof that no continuous auction model could ever be worthwhile. Large ticket sales with repeated value types are a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But this paper offers no named computational problem to carry into that regime, and the atomless population representation erases the individual deviation at its core. A computational study of Sybil attacks against a continuum of buyer types could be interesting, but it would be a new problem rather than a mirror of these results.
