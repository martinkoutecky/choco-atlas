The case FOR (proponent)

This paper is a strong fit for the population model ChoCo wants to study, but it has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a mirror on. Its theorems establish existence and characterize equilibria; none says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or gives another algorithmic complexity classification. For example, Theorem 59 proves existence of a Walrasian equilibrium under its stated assumptions. Theorem 64 characterizes when the core and \(f\)-core coincide with Walrasian equilibrium. Neither is a computational result, so I cannot honestly present either as a required anchor.

The most promising *new computational question* suggested by the paper is Statistical Walrasian Equilibrium Search. An instance would give \(\tau\) economic types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to one. Each type completely specifies its feasible net trades \(T_\theta\) and preferences \(\succeq_\theta\), along with the endowment and production details those include. The question is to output prices \(p\ne0\) and, for each type, a finite distribution \(\pi_\theta\) over individually optimal net trades at those prices, such that
\[ t\in\arg\max\{\,\succeq_\theta:\ t\in T_\theta,\ p\cdot t\le0\,\} \quad\text{for every }t\in\operatorname{supp}(\pi_\theta), \qquad \sum_\theta\mu_\theta\int t\,d\pi_\theta(t)=0. \]
The distributions let a continuum of agents sharing a type receive different optimal bundles when needed; their aggregate trade must clear the market. In a plausible regime, many households or workers share a relatively small number of complete economic types, so the population is large while \(\tau\) is moderate. The paper itself develops this kind of continuum-economy model. Theorem 59 supplies an existence result for one nontrivial version, but not an algorithm for finding the equilibrium.

I would expect Class A to be worth investigating for explicitly encoded rational instances, such as polyhedral feasible sets and piecewise-linear concave utilities. That is a conjecture about a restricted computational problem, not a result of this paper. The relevant follow-up questions would include how complexity depends on the number of goods, support size, and bit length; whether exact and approximate equilibrium search differ; and which finite-economy computational problems embed in this model.

The strongest point for the positive case is that the proposed question asks for the paper’s own central object—prices and a market-clearing allocation—while making population mass the input. Its soft spot is decisive for the requested anchoring standard: the paper leaves preferences and type spaces highly abstract and supplies no finite input representation or computational bound. Also, continuum economies are already part of the paper, so the novel contribution would be a computational layer on that established model, not the act of continuizing the population itself.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in the paper to anchor a mirror. Theorem 59 proves existence of a Walrasian equilibrium; Theorems 64 and 73 give characterizations. None defines a finite input, an algorithmic task, or a complexity bound.

The proposed Statistical Walrasian Equilibrium Search is the best repair, but it is not a mirror of Theorem 59. That theorem already studies a continuum economy. Restricting it to rational masses on finitely many types and asking for equilibrium prices and allocations adds a computational question to an existing continuous model; it does not continuize a discrete problem or result from the paper.

A finite encoding—say, rational polyhedral feasible sets and piecewise-linear utilities—would make that search question well-posed. But then it is a new algorithmic market-equilibrium project, with its own input and output conventions. A more faithful version using the paper’s general preference domains lacks a finite representation for complexity analysis. Turning Theorem 64’s core characterization into a search or membership problem has the same gap: the computational task would be newly supplied, not mirrored from the theorem.

So the strongest negative case is that this paper offers no worthwhile paper-anchored continuous mirror: its population is already continuous, and its results are analytic or axiomatic. The case is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile computational project could arise from it. The equilibrium-search question may fit ChoCo’s general agenda; the material here just does not establish it as a mirror of a result in this paper.
