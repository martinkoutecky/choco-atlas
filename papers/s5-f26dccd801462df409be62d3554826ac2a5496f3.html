The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a valid positive case under the required anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational complexity result. Its results concern whether mechanisms exist, not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class.

The nearest candidate is Theorem 2, proved in the paper. It says that if the finite state distribution has a unique most likely state, then for every social choice function \(f\) from states to lotteries over outcomes, there is a finite mechanism that robustly implements \(f\). Here “robustly” means that, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there is an \(\eta>0\) such that every perturbation in which both agents are normal with probability at least \(1-\eta\) admits an equilibrium whose outcome is within \(\varepsilon\) of \(f\), conditional on every state. The theorem even gives an explicit mechanism. But it makes no complexity claim about an algorithmic problem, so it is not a qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 are similar constructive implementation results; Propositions 1 and 2 give implementation impossibilities, not hardness results for computational problems.

The most plausible population extension would be many repeated two-person investigative panels, with mass over complete panel types—including preferences, information costs, and beliefs—and a common mechanism seeking the target outcome in aggregate. That is a possible high-multiplicity *reformulation*, but it is weak as a mirror: each panel retains the paper’s two-agent problem, and the construction can be applied panel by panel. The distributions \(q\) and \(\Pi\) in the paper describe uncertainty about states and circumstances, not a continuous society.

So I would not hand a continuous problem to a prover as a mirror of a named computational result here. The proposed panel version is the strongest route to a population model, and its weakness is decisive: it adds replication without exposing a new computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is decisive: the paper gives no named computational result to mirror. The proponent’s nearest candidate, Theorem 2, is an existence theorem: under a generic state distribution, a finite mechanism robustly implements any social choice function. Propositions 1–3 likewise concern implementation possibilities, not the complexity of a computational problem. None specifies an algorithmic input problem or makes a complexity claim. That leaves no computational result for a continuous-population mirror to classify.

The strongest improved mirror would ask whether one can compute a robust mechanism for a population of repeated investigative panels, with panel types recording preferences, information costs, and beliefs. But if each panel runs the paper’s mechanism separately, the population extension adds replication; it does not create a new computational question. If instead panels’ reports jointly determine one aggregate outcome, the resulting strategic problem needs new coupling rules and a new objective. That could be worthwhile research, but it is no longer a mirror of Theorem 2.

One could also impose a new design objective, such as minimizing transfers or message-space size. The paper does not study those computational problems, and adding them would create a different anchor. Its stated construction already gives a finite mechanism for the implementation question it asks.

I cannot rule out an independently valuable model of large investigative populations. The negative case is strongest on the narrower point that this paper supplies no computational result for the continuization programme to mirror. The proposed panel scenario does not overcome that gap.
