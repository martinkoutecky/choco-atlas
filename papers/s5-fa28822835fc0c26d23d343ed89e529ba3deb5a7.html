The continuous mirror question

Given a binary rational \(v\ge 0\), let \(\Omega=[0,1]\) have Lebesgue mass and let allocations be measurable \(x\in L^1_+(\Omega)\) with \(\int_\Omega x\,d\mu=1\). For every measurable coalition \(A\subseteq\Omega\), define \(\Pi(A,x)=\int_A(x(a)+v)\,d\mu(a)\). For allocations \(x,y\), let \(W=\{a:y(a)>x(a)\}\) and \(L=\{a:y(a)<x(a)\}\), and say \(y\) dominates \(x\) when \(\Pi(W,x)>\Pi(L,x)\). Decide whether there exists a set of allocations satisfying internal and external stability under this dominance relation.

The model it lives in

A one-type nonatomic population with per-capita resource density \(x\), total per-capita supply \(1\), and measurable coalitions whose additive strength is \(\Pi(A,x)=\int_A(x+v)\,d\mu\). The finite input is \(v\); the decision is whether any internally and externally stable set exists under the paper’s strict dominance rule.

The objection that survived

The paper’s \(B_i\), \(M_i\), \(R_i\), and \(P\) decomposition has no demonstrated analogue for measurable coalitions, so the anchor offers no structural solution to the proposed limit problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror targets stable-set existence for the additive pillage model. Theorem 7’s uniqueness and construction motivate it, but the paper’s three-agent bound and other core and stable-set results are not shown to extend; the cited finite-game hardness is prior work, not a result of this paper.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which rational values of \(v\) does a stable set exist?
  • When stable sets exist, are they unique, and can they be described by finite symbolic formulas?
  • Do stable sets of finite homogeneous populations converge to stable sets in the nonatomic model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a population-limit version of the paper’s stable-set problem. The paper does not give a standard complexity result such as “in P” or “NP-hard.” Its closest named algorithmic anchor is Theorem 7, which gives a structural characterization that underlies Algorithm 2. It is proved here. The paper’s discussion of NP-completeness for finite abstract games cites Brandt et al.; that is prior work, not an anchor proved in this paper.

Theorem 7 says that for \(n=3\), if the power function satisfies anonymity (AN), continuity (CX), and responsiveness (RE), the core \(K\) is nonempty, and each \(R_i\) is nonempty, then there is a unique stable set:
\[ S=K\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}S_i\cup P. \]
Here \(S_i=R_i\cup(\{q_i\}\cap M_i)\), using the paper’s maximal allocations \(M_i\), their agent-favouring extremes \(R_i\), and midpoint \(q_i\) on the balance locus \(B_i\). \(P\) consists of the up to three midpoints \(p^{jk}\) defined in the paper. This is a constructive description of the stable set, though not a polynomial-time bound: the paper does not analyze the bit complexity of finding those loci and points.

A plausible mirror is the Nonatomic Pillage Stable-Set Problem. Consider a large population in which residents share the same coalition rights and power technology. Its type set has size \(\tau=1\), while the population itself is a continuum: let \(\Omega=[0,1]\) with Lebesgue mass \(\mu\). An allocation is a per-capita resource density
\[ x\in\mathcal X=\left\{x\in L^1_+(\mu):\int_\Omega x\,d\mu=1\right\}. \]
For a measurable coalition \(A\), use mass-normalized strength-in-numbers power
\[ \Pi(A,x)=\int_A (x(a)+v)\,d\mu(a), \]
where \(v\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\). This is the nonatomic limit of a homogeneous population with fixed per-capita resource supply; scaling the aggregate supply with population size is part of the mirror.

For allocations \(x,y\), let \(W=\{a:y(a)>x(a)\}\) and \(L=\{a:y(a)<x(a)\}\). Say \(y\) dominates \(x\) when \(\Pi(W,x)>\Pi(L,x)\), as in the paper. A solution is a set \(\mathcal S\subseteq\mathcal X\) satisfying internal and external stability under this dominance relation. Given \(v\), the problem is to decide whether such a set exists and, if so, give an exact description of one—proving uniqueness if there is one, or exhibiting distinct stable sets otherwise. A discretization or list of sample allocations would not count as a solution.

The regime is a mass society of many residents with the same legal standing and pillage technology—for example, residents in a polity whose bargaining power depends on their current resource holdings and population share. Mass means fraction of the population, and the action is a redistribution of per-capita resources. Because anonymity is central to the paper, a one-type population is a natural case; the current allocation is a state of the game, not a difference in agents’ fixed power attributes.

I expect this restricted, additive-power version to be Class A, tentatively: for fixed allocations, coalition power is an integral over gainers and losers, and the anonymity may let a measure-valued optimization describe the stable set. Theorem 7 gives some reason to seek such a structural reduction. It does not establish one.

The weakest point is the size of the leap. The paper’s result is for three strategic agents; its allocation simplex is already continuous, while its population is not. In the mirror, measurable coalitions and an infinite-dimensional allocation space replace the paper’s three-player geometry. In particular, responsiveness must be expressed for positive-mass coalitions, since a single agent has zero mass. Those changes may alter the stable-set structure substantially. The paper itself flags larger populations as open territory, so this is a plausible problem for its authors—but I would present it as a genuine limit question, not as a consequence of Theorem 7. Further questions include whether stable sets exist for each \(v\), whether they are unique, and whether discrete homogeneous populations’ stable sets converge to the nonatomic one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 7 is the only plausible anchor, but it characterizes a stable set; it does not give a complexity bound or an efficient algorithm. Algorithm 2 does decide and construct the stable set under its assumptions, but the paper explicitly leaves efficient computation of its interim sets unresolved. Its input is an arbitrary power function, with no finite encoding or running-time measure. So the result does not yet define the kind of finite computational problem whose continuous complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed population model is coherent, but it changes what the players are. The paper’s three agents are the units that form coalitions; anonymity means their labels do not affect power. If a polity has three large factions, giving each faction mass leaves three strategic players. If individual residents can form coalitions, then any measurable subset may act together. That adds coalition choices absent from the three-agent model. The first interpretation keeps the paper’s game but no longer continuizes its players; the second gives a new nonatomic coalition game, rather than a population relaxation of Theorem 7.

The strongest rescue is to study many interchangeable individuals under an additive power rule, with coalition power scaled by population mass. That could be a worthwhile model. But it connects to the paper’s general \(n\)-agent setup, not to the specific three-agent characterization: its loci \(B_i\), maximal points \(R_i\), and set \(P\) rely on three-agent geometry. To turn the new model into a computational problem, one would also need a finite encoding of the power technology and a finite representation of a stable set in the infinite-dimensional allocation space. Restricting to a parameter such as \(v\) may make an analytic classification possible, but that would not supply the missing complexity result in this paper.

That is the strongest negative case: the anchor does not provide a computational result with a defined input model, and the obvious population versions either retain three strategic blocs or introduce a different continuum game. It does not establish that no such continuum game is worth studying. The proponent’s model may be worthwhile on its own; the negative case is weak if the standard is to rule out every sensible population scenario.
