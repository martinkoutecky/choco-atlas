The case FOR (proponent)

The supplied paper contains no qualifying named computational result. Although it defines a theorem environment, it states no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. The simulation tables and conclusions report empirical comparisons, not such results. So there is no honest anchor from which to formulate the required per-anchor mirror questions.

The strongest plausible unanchored mirror would use mass over types \((\text{district},\text{community},\text{ranking})\). This fits the paper’s large-election, few-district, few-community scenarios: for a fixed number of parties, the possible rankings are finite, so many electors could share a relatively small number of types. Given those masses and a voting policy, one could compute district winners and the paper’s fairness scores. But that is a possible continuous-population version of the paper’s *evaluation procedure*, not a mirror of a named computational result. The paper’s stochastic valuation model and local-influence variant would also need more precise type-level specifications.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no named computational anchors, and I don’t see one to defeat: the paper’s claim that two-approval often improves some fairness measures is an empirical comparison over simulated elections, not a theorem about an algorithmic problem. There is no complexity result here for bribery, control, robustness, or policy design.

The proposed type space \((\text{district},\text{community},\text{ranking})\) is sensible for large elections; the paper’s scale makes a high-multiplicity regime credible. But for a fixed election policy, the reported outcomes are functions of finite aggregates. If \(x_{s,r}\) is the mass in district \(s\) with ranking \(r\), then a candidate’s tally is \(\sum_r x_{s,r}a_r(c)\), where \(a_r(c)\) is that ballot’s contribution under the policy. District winners follow from those tallies; the representation and Borda measures follow from the winner identities and the ranking masses. Community labels help generate ballots, but are not needed to evaluate the resulting election. The continuum gives a clean way to express the electorate and its fairness scores, but this paper supplies no computational question whose complexity that formulation would classify.

A stronger proposal would ask for the exact expected fairness under the stochastic model, or for the smallest mass of changed ballots that flips a party majority. Those are coherent questions, but the first is a probability and integration problem, while the second adds an intervention objective and costs absent from the paper. Optimizing policy under fairness constraints similarly adds a decision problem to a paper that compares a fixed menu of policies. Any of these might be worth studying; none is a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope argument, not as a claim that continuous electorates are unnatural. I cannot honestly rule out a useful ChoCo project inspired by this setting. I can argue that this manuscript offers no computational result to continuize, and that turning its simulations into a computational problem requires adding the central question rather than mirroring one.
