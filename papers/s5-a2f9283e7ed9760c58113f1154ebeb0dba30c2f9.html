The case FOR (proponent)

The best affirmative case is narrow: this paper has a natural high-multiplicity optimization object, and the author explicitly sketches a continuous-population version of it. But it has no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. So if that is a strict anchor requirement, there is no fully compliant positive case. The closest possible anchor is a quantitative approximation result.

That candidate is Corollary 3.10, proved in this paper. For a weighted game \([q;w]\) with \(\sum_i w_i=1\), \(0<q<1\), and \(\Delta=\max_i w_i\), every allocation \(x\) in the game’s nucleus—an allocation minimizing maximum coalition excess, a set containing the nucleolus—satisfies
\[ \lVert w-x\rVert_1 \le \frac{2\Delta}{\min\{q+\Delta,1-q\}} \le \frac{2\Delta}{\min\{q,1-q\}}. \]
This is a useful approximation guarantee: returning the weight vector gives a stated additive error for the nucleus. The paper does not give a running-time classification for that task, though, so this is an approximation anchor only under a permissive reading of your rule.

A plausible population regime is the one the paper itself suggests: an ocean of many small shareholders, grouped into a small number \(\tau\) of types with identical voting weight and behavior, plus a few large shareholders (“islands”). Here \(n\) can be very large while \(\tau\) and the number \(k\) of islands remain small. That makes the continuous population a sensible model, and the author’s conclusion explicitly proposes treating small voters as a continuum in a nucleolus approximation.

The concrete mirror would be Continuous Nucleus for Ocean-and-Island Voting Games. An instance has a finite island set \(H\), a finite type set \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational island weights \(b_h\), type weight densities \(a_t\), and a quota \(q\in(0,1)\), normalized so that \(\sum_{h\in H}b_h+\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t=1\). A coalition consists of a subset \(S\subseteq H\) and amounts \(z_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each ocean type; it wins when
\[ \sum_{h\in S}b_h+\sum_{t\in T}a_tz_t\ge q. \]
A solution is a rational allocation \((x_h,y_t)\), with \(x_h,y_t\ge0\) and \(\sum_hx_h+\sum_t\mu_ty_t=1\), minimizing the maximum excess over all such coalitions:
\[ \max_{S,z}\left(\mathbf{1}\!\left[\sum_{h\in S}b_h+\sum_ta_tz_t\ge q\right] -\sum_{h\in S}x_h-\sum_ty_tz_t\right). \]
Here \(x_h\) is an island’s allocation and \(y_t\) is the per-unit allocation density for type \(t\). This asks for the nucleus of the continuous weighted voting game itself, not for a continuous outcome space.

I would expect this problem to be tractable in the intended regime: polynomial time when there are no islands, and plausibly fixed-parameter tractable in \(k=|H|\). For a candidate allocation, separation can enumerate island subsets and solve a fractional knapsack problem over the type masses; that suggests a \(2^k\operatorname{poly}(k,\tau,L)\) route through LP optimization. Whether the dependence on \(k\) can be improved, or whether unrestricted \(k\) restores discrete hardness, are natural follow-up questions. The mirror covers the nucleus and its approximation by weights, not every power index in the paper.

The soft spot is substantial: Corollary 3.10 is a bound, not a named complexity result or an algorithm analyzed by the paper. Also, the conclusion already sketches a closely related continuous treatment of small voters. So this is a plausible, author-recognizable computational question generated by the paper’s approximation result, but it does not meet a strict requirement for an anchor that itself asserts a complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its theorems bound distances between a weight vector and a power allocation; they do not classify the complexity of computing either one. Corollary 3.10, the proponent’s anchor, says that every allocation in the nucleus is close to the weights under stated conditions. It gives no running time, algorithm, or computational task.

The proposed “Continuous Nucleus” is a sensible population model, but computing its nucleus is a new problem, not the continuous version of Corollary 3.10. In the atomless, no-island limit, the bound’s \(\Delta\) vanishes when \(q\) stays away from \(0\) and \(1\), so the weight allocation itself gives the approximation. With islands, exact nucleus computation could be interesting, but its complexity is independent of the corollary; the proposed separation approach would be the start of that separate analysis.

The paper’s conclusion already sketches a continuum approximation for small voters and a conjectural error bound. That is not an existing computational treatment, so it is not a novelty collision. It does, however, make the proposed model look like an extension of the paper’s own analytic direction rather than a computational mirror of a proved result.

That defeats the stated anchor under the programme’s strict computational standard. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: the continuous nucleus is a plausible object, and its computational complexity might merit study on its own. The negative case is strong on the mismatch between the paper’s results and the proposed anchor, but weak on ruling out that broader research direction.
