The continuous mirror question

Given a finite hospital set, a support of τ complete resident types with rational masses μ_r, rational per-capita capacities κ_h, and a current stable allocation state with hospital tie data, can a polynomial-time flow construction reroute mass f while preserving stability, increasing b = ∑_h min(a_h, κ_h) by f, and decreasing e = ∑_h max(a_h − κ_h, 0) by f, where a_h is the mass assigned to hospital h?

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution μ over complete resident types, each specifying strict resident preferences and hospital priority categories; hospitals have per-capita capacities κ_h. Assignment variables x_{rh} record the mass of type r assigned to hospital h. The continuous analogue replaces resident-node capacities of one with rational type-mass capacities and asks whether the flow-guided rerouting step preserves the paper's stability and progress invariants.

The objection that survived

The paper does not prove that its flow construction preserves the progress invariant when a type's mass is split across assignments; the network and tie handling need adaptation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the flow-based rerouting progress of Theorem 3.2. It leaves unaddressed the optimality of a maximum stable mass assignment, the cited NP-hardness results, and the empirical comparisons.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the flow network represent split type masses while preserving the tie and stability invariants?
  • What running time does the resulting algorithm achieve in the number of hospitals, support size, and input bit length?
  • Can repeated progress steps yield a maximum-cardinality stable mass assignment, or do they only certify heuristic progress?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest candidate is a continuous version of MAX-HROST, but there is an anchor problem: this paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition that says a maximum-stable-matching problem is hard or gives an algorithm for finding one. The paper’s numbered results concern correctness and progress of its heuristics. For example, Theorem 2.1, proved here, says Algorithm HROST-Heuristic-H returns a stable matching for the original instance; Theorem 3.2, also proved here, says that a maximum flow of value \(f\) in the constructed network lets the authors produce a stable allocation whose bound rises by \(f\) and whose excess falls by \(f\). Neither establishes a complexity classification or solves the maximum-cardinality problem.

The relevant hardness claims are in the introduction, not in a numbered result. There the authors report, citing [20], that MAX-HRT is NP-hard even when every hospital has capacity \(1\), every resident’s list is strict, and every hospital’s list is either strict or contains a tie of length \(2\). Citing [15], they also report NP-hardness when capacities are \(1\), all lists have length at most \(3\), and resident lists are strict. They say that these results imply NP-hardness for MAX-HROST and MAX-SMOSTI. These are the best substantive anchors for a mirror, but they do not satisfy the requested numbered-anchor rule; I would not invent a theorem number for them.

The mirror I would put to the authors is Continuous MAX-HROST. An instance has a finite set \(H\) of hospitals, a support \(\mathcal R\) of \(\tau\) complete resident types, rational masses \(\mu_r\) with \(\sum_{r\in\mathcal R}\mu_r=1\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_h\). A type specifies a resident’s strict ranking of acceptable hospitals and the priority category that each hospital assigns to that resident. Thus residents of one type are indistinguishable in every way relevant to the matching. Hospital rankings over types may contain ties, as in HROST. The capacity \(\kappa_h\) is the limit of the hospital’s discrete capacity divided by population size.

A solution is a mass assignment \(x_{rh}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_h x_{rh}\leq\mu_r\) and \(\sum_r x_{rh}\leq\kappa_h\). Unassigned mass represents unmatched residents. Each unit of mass gets one hospital; \(x_{rh}\) records how much of type \(r\) goes to \(h\), not a fractional job for an individual. The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{r,h}x_{rh}\), subject to weak stability.

Precisely, for an acceptable pair \((r,h)\), let \(D_{rh}\) be the mass of type \(r\) assigned to a hospital worse than \(h\), or left unmatched; let \(V_h=\kappa_h-\sum_s x_{sh}\) be \(h\)’s vacancy; and let \(W_{rh}\) be the mass assigned to \(h\) that \(h\) strictly ranks below type \(r\). The assignment is stable if, for every acceptable pair, either \(D_{rh}=0\), or both \(V_h=0\) and \(W_{rh}=0\). This is the paper’s blocking-pair condition expressed in masses. The requested output is an optimal stable assignment and its matched mass, for an instance encoded with binary rationals; the relevant complexity parameters are \(m=|H|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

A plausible regime is a large centralized placement of applicants into hospitals or training programs: many applicants, but a relatively small catalogue of complete preference templates and standardized priority bands. For example, \(N\) could be tens of thousands while \(\tau\) is in the hundreds and \(m\) is in the dozens. The paper’s NRMP and SFAS examples make large cohorts credible, but the paper does not establish that its actual instances have small support. The regime needs that extra concentration in preference and priority types.

This is recognizable as the authors’ problem: it preserves their two-sided matching, hospital capacities, weak stability, and maximum-cardinality objective. Scaling capacities with population is part of making a population limit meaningful. It does not continuize the outcome or stability concept. Nor does it collide with the paper’s model: the paper studies finite residents and integral assignments. A discrete instance with type masses and capacities on a \(1/N\) lattice is a special case of the mass description, while the continuous problem also admits finer divisions of a type’s mass.

My provisional expectation for the unrestricted mirror is Class C, continuum-specifically hard, rather than an automatic transfer of the discrete NP-hardness. Population mass removes indivisibility, but the exact blocking condition still has a disjunction: either no mass of a type wants a hospital, or that hospital has no vacancy and no lower-priority assignees. That interaction could retain hard combinatorics. Theorem 3.2’s max-flow step is a real reason to investigate a tractable flow or LP formulation, but it proves progress for the heuristic, not optimality. The key follow-up questions are whether the continuous optimum has a polynomial-time formulation; whether bounded or small \(\tau\) yields an efficient algorithm; and whether lattice instances admit rounding back to discrete maximum stable matchings.

The weakest point is the regime assumption. Applicants in real matching schemes may have many distinct preference lists or priorities, leaving \(\tau\) close to \(N\). The paper’s reported data do not demonstrate the compression the mirror needs, and the atomless blocking condition must be accepted as the intended stability notion. So this is a strong mirror of the paper’s central optimization problem in a credible but specific cohort regime—not a claim that the paper already supplies a compliant numbered hardness anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is about what this paper actually establishes. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary giving a complexity result for MAX-HROST. The NP-hardness statements are attributed to earlier papers [20] and [15]. The paper’s own Theorem 2.1 proves that its hospital-oriented heuristic returns a stable matching; Theorem 3.2 proves that a maximum-flow step improves the bound and excess of a stable allocation. Neither says how hard it is to find a maximum stable matching, or guarantees that either heuristic finds one. So the proposed continuous MAX-HROST is a plausible follow-up to the problem discussed in the introduction, but it does not mirror a computational result established by this paper.

Even taking that follow-up on its own terms, the proposed model needs a specific instance regime: many residents must share both a complete preference list and the priority categories that every relevant hospital uses to compare them. The paper gives large-cohort examples, but reports no support-size evidence for its SFAS data. Residents have strict lists, and hospital preferences vary; grouping residents by their full preference and priority profiles could leave \(\tau\) close to the number of residents. A regime with repeated preference templates and coarse priority bands is conceivable, but it is an added assumption whose plausibility this paper does not establish.

The named results do not supply a stronger mirror. A mass version of Theorem 2.1 would certify stability of a heuristic’s output, not maximum cardinality; its proof also relies on classifying individual residents as assigned or unassigned, which a split type mass cannot do without further bookkeeping. Theorem 3.2’s flow step is more promising, but it establishes progress for that heuristic, not optimality. Replacing resident capacities of one with type masses may yield a useful capacitated-flow subroutine, yet that would be a new algorithmic development rather than a continuous counterpart to a result about the optimum.

The proposed stability condition is coherent: for each type–hospital pair it requires \(D_{rh}=0\), or both \(V_h=0\) and \(W_{rh}=0\). It therefore survives the objection that the paper’s matching question cannot be expressed over masses. But it is a disjunction, so the mirror is not automatically a linear program or a direct application of the flow argument. That makes the computational work substantial; it does not show that the question is unworthy. And the population-size objection is limited: a cohort with shared templates and priority bands could make a sensible high-multiplicity regime.

Thus the negative case is strongest if “mirror” means a continuous counterpart to a computational result this article proves: on that reading, its proposed anchor is imported from prior work, while the article’s numbered results concern heuristic correctness and progress. The case is weak if the test is instead whether the article’s central optimization problem admits a worthwhile continuous research question. Continuous MAX-HROST is a credible question in the right regime, and I cannot honestly rule out that it merits the programme’s time.
