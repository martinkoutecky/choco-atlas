The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete agent types, per-capita resource capacities, integral outcomes \(\alpha\in\Gamma\), valuation vectors \(v_t\), and permutation-invariant polyhedral predictors \(T_t(\mu_{-t})\), compute an efficient aggregate allocation and each positive-mass type's normalized weakest-type price \(p_t=\min_{\widehat v\in T_t(\mu_{-t})}\left(\max_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\{\widehat v[\alpha]+B_{\mu,-t}(\alpha)\}-B_{\mu,-t}(\alpha_t^\star)\right)\), where \(B_{\mu,-t}(\alpha)\) is the normalized residual optimum when a tagged unit receives \(\alpha\), using time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input encoding relative to an aggregate welfare/separation oracle.

The model it lives in

An anonymous high-multiplicity allocation market in which \(\tau\) complete types carry masses \(\mu_t\), each unit receives an integral bundle or matching outcome, aggregate assignments \(x_{t,\alpha}\) satisfy per-capita capacities, predictors are represented by permutation-invariant polyhedra \(T_t(\mu_{-t})\), and \(p_t\) is the normalized weakest-type externality of a tagged unit.

The objection that survived

Theorems 3.8 and 3.9 are finite-instance statements and do not themselves establish polynomial complexity in \(\tau\) and the encoding length of \(\mu\), nor convergence of normalized tagged-unit prices.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers weakest-type computation and payment pricing from Theorems 3.8 and 3.9. It leaves the main welfare-revenue guarantees, uncertainty model, low-dimensional-subspace mechanism, Bayesian prior results, and affine-maximizer extensions unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the aggregate welfare and residual-welfare oracle be implemented in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the encoding length of \(\mu\), rather than merely supplied?
  • Under what tie-breaking and regularity conditions do finite-\(N\) weakest-type prices converge to \(p_t\) with an explicit additive error bound?
  • Can predictors depending on all reports be represented as permutation-invariant rules \(T_t(\mu_{-t})\) without destroying high multiplicity?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a Class A mirror for computing weakest-type payments in a high-multiplicity matching or combinatorial-auction market. This is an algorithmic mirror, not a hardness-collapse claim: the paper contains no named NP-hardness, W[1]-hardness, or FPT theorem. Its relevant results are Theorems 3.8 and 3.9, both proved in this paper.

Take a large market with \(N\) agents, but only \(\tau\) distinct complete types, where \(N\gg\tau\). A type \(t\) includes a valuation vector \(v_t\), eligibility and budget parameters, and the entire side-information rule \(T_t\). Thus two agents belong to the same type only when the paper’s mechanism treats them identically in every respect. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of agents of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Resource capacities scale with the population: if \(q_j\) is the per-capita supply of resource \(j\), the finite realization has \(Nq_j\) copies.

Each agent receives an integral bundle or matching outcome \(\alpha\in\Gamma\). The aggregate allocation is a mass table \(x_{t,\alpha}\): it records what fraction of type \(t\) receives \(\alpha\), subject to \(\sum_\alpha x_{t,\alpha}=\mu_t\) and the scaled capacity constraints. The mass table is fractional only as an aggregate description; each individual still receives an ordinary integral allocation. Efficient welfare is

\(\max_x\sum_{t,\alpha}x_{t,\alpha}v_t[\alpha]\).

For a tagged unit of type \(t\), let \(B_{\mu,-t}(\alpha)\) denote the maximum welfare obtainable by the rest of the population when that unit is assigned \(\alpha\), using the usual marginal, per-capita normalization of the \(N\)-agent limit. If \(\alpha_t^\star\) is the tagged unit’s allocation in a fixed tie-broken efficient allocation, define the weakest-type price by

\(p_t=\min_{\widehat v\in T_t(\mu_{-t})}\left(\max_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\{\widehat v[\alpha]+B_{\mu,-t}(\alpha)\}-V_{\mu,-t}^\star(\alpha_t^\star)\right).\)

This is exactly the paper’s weakest-type payment formula with the finite profile of other agents replaced by the aggregate high-multiplicity market. Total revenue is \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\). The question is not to encode the mechanism, but to compute the underlying efficient mass allocation, weakest predicted valuation, and type-level payment.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.9. The paper states:

Theorem 3.9. The linear program for weakest-type computation can be solved with polynomially many calls to \(w(\cdot)\) and additional \(poly(size(T_i))\) time.

Here \(w(\cdot)\) is the efficient-welfare routine, and the theorem is proved in the paper using the ellipsoid method. The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is:

Oracle Continuous Weakest-Type Pricing. Given rational masses \(\mu\), scaled resource capacities, valuation types \(v_t\), polyhedral predictors \(T_t\), and an exact oracle that returns an efficient aggregate allocation and welfare for any proposed valuation profile, compute every \(p_t\) exactly. The running time may use polynomially many calls to the aggregate welfare oracle, plus time polynomial in the number and encoding size of the predictor constraints and in \(\tau\).

I expect this to be tractable, Class A. The ellipsoid separation step has the same shape as in the paper: given a candidate weakest valuation and threshold \(z\), check the predictor constraints; then call the welfare oracle. If the oracle returns welfare greater than \(z\), the returned allocation supplies a violated constraint. Thus the exponential allocation space is accessed only through pricing/separation, precisely the computational architecture the continuization programme is designed to expose.

The second anchor is Theorem 3.8, also proved here. It states:

Theorem 3.8. If every \(T_i(\boldsymbol\theta_{-i})\) is a polytope, then the weakest type, its corresponding welfare \(\min_{\widetilde\theta_i\in T_i(\boldsymbol\theta_{-i})}w(\widetilde\theta_i,\boldsymbol\theta_{-i})\), and the payment \(p_i=\min_{\widetilde\theta_i\in T_i(\boldsymbol\theta_{-i})}w(\widetilde\theta_i,\boldsymbol\theta_{-i})-\sum_{j\ne i}\theta_j[\alpha^\star]\) can be computed in \(poly(|\Gamma|,size(T_i),n)\) time.

The corresponding continuous question is:

Explicit Continuous Weakest-Type Pricing. Given an explicitly listed finite \(\Gamma\), rational type masses and capacities, valuation vectors, and rational polytope predictors \(T_t\), compute an efficient mass allocation and, for every positive-mass type, a weakest valuation and its normalized weakest-type payment in time polynomial in \(|\Gamma|\), \(\tau\), the predictor descriptions, and the input bit length.

The expected answer is again Class A. For each type, the continuous pricing problem has the LP form

\(\min_{\widehat v,z} z\)

subject to \(\widehat v\in T_t\) and \(\widehat v[\alpha]+B_{\mu,-t}(\alpha)\le z\) for every \(\alpha\in\Gamma\). The aggregate allocation itself is a standard mass-assignment LP. This is a genuine high-multiplicity question: the complexity should depend on the number of distinct valuation-and-information types, not on the enormous number \(N\) of repeated agents.

The mirror is plausible because the paper itself names matching markets and combinatorial auctions as target applications, treats valuations as multidimensional vectors, and identifies the description complexity of predictors and the efficient-allocation oracle as the relevant computational bottlenecks. The continuous version preserves the paper’s actual object: weakest-type welfare and revenue under side information. It does not replace the agents by average valuations, and it does not make outcomes divisible for individuals. Mass is used only to describe repeated integral assignments.

It also generates useful follow-up questions: whether finite-\(N\) prices converge with an explicit additive error bound; whether the oracle theorem survives when \(\mu\) is part of the input rather than fixed; whether unions of predictor polytopes produce continuum-specific hardness; and whether the randomized mechanism \(\mathcal M_{L,\gamma}\) has analogous welfare and revenue guarantees at the type-mass level.

My weakest point is the atomless-agent issue. Literal VCG for a genuinely infinitesimal individual can yield zero pivotality. The mirror therefore needs the standard high-multiplicity marginal normalization: compute the price of a tagged unit in the \(N\)-copy economy and take its per-unit limit, or equivalently use the corresponding dual marginal price. That is a forced co-modification of the model, but it must be stated explicitly. If one refuses this normalization and insists on unscaled atomless VCG, the mirror degenerates. Subject to that qualification, the paper offers a credible positive case: its weakest-type computation is already an LP/separation problem, and the continuous population makes the high-multiplicity, type-level version mathematically natural rather than artificial.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror silently replaces the paper’s finite-agent mechanism by a different, anonymous marginal-pricing model, while treating the paper’s oracle theorem as if it already applied to that model.

Theorem 3.8 is a theorem about a fixed finite allocation space \(\Gamma\), a named agent \(i\), and a fixed profile \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-i}\). In a matching or combinatorial-auction market with \(N\) agents, the natural allocation space is instead \(\Gamma_N\), whose description and dimension generally grow with \(N\). The paper’s valuation vector \(\theta_i\in\mathbb{R}^{\Gamma_N}\) therefore also changes with \(N\). A fixed type \(v_t\) cannot simply be carried through the high-multiplicity limit unless one adds a new anonymous, separable valuation model.

The proposed mass table \(x_{t,\alpha}\) does exactly that. It may be a sensible model, but its aggregate residual value \(B_{\mu,-t}(\alpha)\) is not one of the coefficients in Theorem 3.8’s LP. Computing it requires solving a new population-level allocation problem, and the theorem gives no bound in terms of \(\tau\) and the encoding length of \(\mu\). If \(\Gamma_N\) is explicitly represented, the claimed efficiency still depends on \(N\). If \(\Gamma_N\) is compactly represented through an aggregate welfare oracle, then the proponent has introduced a new oracle assumption and has not shown that the paper’s theorem survives it.

Theorem 3.9 has the same problem in a sharper form. It says that weakest-type computation for a finite named-agent instance can be performed with polynomially many calls to the corresponding finite-instance welfare routine. In the proposed mirror, the welfare routine must instead solve the entire aggregate market, including residual capacities after a tagged unit is assigned. Supplying that oracle simply assumes the central combinatorial computation away; withholding it leaves the continuous pricing problem unanalysed. The theorem does not establish polynomial complexity in the number of types \(\tau\), nor does it establish exact bit-complexity or convergence of the tagged-agent prices as \(N\) grows.

There is also a structural problem with the side information. The paper allows \(T_i(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-i})\) to depend on every other named agent’s report. Replacing agents by masses is valid only if these predictors are permutation-invariant and can be represented as functions \(T_t(\mu_{-t})\). That is a substantive restriction, not a notational change. If one preserves the paper’s general predictor language, the predictor rule—including its dependence on identities and positions in the profile—must be part of the complete type. In generic instances that makes almost every agent a distinct type, so the supposed high-multiplicity compression disappears.

The atomless issue is therefore not merely a technicality. In the paper, \(p_i\) is a payment for a named agent whose report can change the finite efficient allocation. In a continuum, one agent has zero mass and cannot change the aggregate allocation. To obtain a nonzero price, the proponent must define a finite-\(N\) tagged-unit externality, normalize it, and take a directional derivative or select a dual marginal price. At aggregate ties, that derivative may be nonunique and can depend on the replication sequence or tie-breaking rule. Choosing one subgradient yields a natural market-pricing model, but it is no longer automatically the paper’s weakest-type VCG mechanism; incentive compatibility, individual rationality, and the revenue interpretation would need new proofs.

This attack is strongest for the paper’s common-outcome examples, such as choosing one floor for all occupants. With many repeated agents, one individual’s valuation has vanishing influence on the chosen floor, so the VCG and weakest-type externality typically collapse to zero. Per-capita normalization does not restore the individual payment; it scales the individual’s value down as well. A nondegenerate limit requires a different market structure in which capacities and assignments scale so that a tagged unit has a genuine marginal effect.

That said, this does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A repaired model with anonymous type-dependent valuations, capacities scaling with population, permutation-invariant polyhedral predictors \(T_t(\mu)\), and prices defined through aggregate dual marginals is entirely plausible. In such a model, the paper’s weakest-type LP and separation argument may well yield a genuine Class A result. Repeated bidders in auctions or repeated cohorts in matching markets are sensible high-multiplicity regimes.

Thus the defensible negative conclusion is narrower: the two anchors as stated do not establish the claimed continuous result, because they hide the population dependence in \(\Gamma_N\), the aggregate welfare oracle, and the marginal-price definition. But an honest “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” case is weak. The proponent’s repaired anonymous-market version remains a credible continuous mirror of Theorems 3.8 and 3.9.
