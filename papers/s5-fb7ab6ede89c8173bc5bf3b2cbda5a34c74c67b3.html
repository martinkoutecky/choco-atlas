The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) item categories, a finite set \(T\) of normalized monotone submodular valuation types with \(|T|=\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and type-level valuation-query access, determine the best worst-case welfare approximation achievable by a fixed valuation-independent continuous MIR range \(\mathcal R\) under a communication budget \(B=O(e^{m^{1/15}})\). The decision variables are masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\), with \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le q_j\), maximizing \(W_\mu(x)=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity combinatorial auction with valuation types \(T\), masses \(\mu\), per-capita supplies \(q\), and mass assignments \(x_{t,S}\) that give each unit of mass an integral bundle; a fixed MIR range is optimized under type-level communication constraints.

The objection that survived

The type-level communication oracle collapses \(N\) independently reported valuations into one report per type, while mass assignments convexify labelled partitions, so the original communication lower bound does not automatically transfer.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2.1's MIR welfare-approximation and communication lower bound for submodular bidders. It leaves Theorem 3.5's VCG-based truthfulness characterization, payment semantics, and the paper's broader mechanism-design claims unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the cover/intersection argument survive convexified mass allocations and yield an \(\Omega(\min\{\tau,m^{1/6}\})\) lower bound?
  • What is the correct communication parameter when \(N\gg\tau\), and can finite clone expansions characterize the continuous model?
  • Can a non-vacuous cohort-level payment and truthfulness theory be defined without changing the allocation-level anchor?

The case FOR (proponent)

I think the strongest positive case is a hardness-transferring mirror of the paper’s welfare-approximation result, rather than a claim that continuization makes the problem easy.

The lead anchor is Theorem 2.1, proved in this paper. It states that every maximal-in-range mechanism for welfare maximization with normalized, monotone, submodular bidders which uses \(O(e^{m^{1/15}})\) bits of communication has approximation factor \(\Omega(\min\{n,m^{1/6}\})\). The statement continues to hold for randomized and nondeterministic mechanisms. In other words, a MIR mechanism cannot obtain a substantially better welfare guarantee under that communication budget. The proof combines the paper’s cover-number argument, intersection-number communication lower bound, and the relation between those two measures.

The continuous problem I would put to a prover is:

Continuous MIR Welfare Approximation\(_\infty\). There are \(m\) heterogeneous item categories and a finite set \(T\) of bidder types, \(|T|=\tau\). A type \(t\) is a complete valuation \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\), including all information relevant to the mechanism, and satisfies normalization, monotonicity, and submodularity. The society is given by rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Type \(t\) therefore represents a mass \(\mu_t\) of otherwise indistinguishable bidders.

Because a fixed finite supply of indivisible items would make almost every bidder powerless in the limit, the natural large-market version gives item category \(j\) a rational per-capita supply \(q_j\). This is the standard replicated-market interpretation: for denominator \(N\), there are \(N\mu_t\) bidders of type \(t\) and \(Nq_j\) copies of item \(j\).

An allocation is a family of masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) bidders receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \]

for each \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le q_j \]

for each item category \(j\). Thus individuals still receive indivisible bundles; only the population of recipients is continuous. Welfare is

\[ W_\mu(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}v_t(S), \]

and the unrestricted optimum is

\[ \operatorname{OPT}(\mu)=\max_{x}W_\mu(x). \]

A continuous MIR rule has a fixed, valuation-independent range \(\mathcal R\) of such allocation patterns and must return

\[ x_{\mathcal R}\in\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal R\cap\mathcal F(\mu,q)}W_\mu(x). \]

The communication model gives each type the same value-, demand-, or other valuation-query access available to a bidder in the paper. Crucially, a type’s answer is not repeated once for every clone. The question is to determine the best worst-case approximation factor achievable by such a continuous MIR rule under a transcript budget \(B\), especially \(B=O(e^{m^{1/15}})\).

This is not merely replacing welfare by an average. The active decision variable is the mass assignment \(x\), while bundles remain integral. For rational \(\mu\), \(q\), and \(x\), clearing denominators produces an ordinary finite replicated auction; conversely, every such finite auction yields a mass allocation. That gives the required high-multiplicity bridge.

I would expect this problem to be Class B: hardness transfers, at least in its unrestricted worst-case form. The paper’s obstruction is fundamentally about the combinatorics of item partitions and the structure of the MIR range, not about having one uniquely named bidder for every unit of population. The intersection argument uses the item-side relation \(D_i\cap D'_j\neq\varnothing\), and that relation survives aggregation. A positive-mass cohort can carry the same valuation information as all of its clones, so cloning removes redundant population identity without removing the hard combinatorics over bundles.

The exact dependence on the paper’s \(n\) is itself an interesting part of the continuous question. In a high-multiplicity society with \(N\gg\tau\), one should not blindly replace \(n\) by \(N\): the meaningful parameter may be the number of distinguishable bidder roles or valuation types, \(\tau\), rather than the number of clones. A central conjecture would be that a suitable anonymous version still has a lower bound of order \(\Omega(\min\{\tau,m^{1/6}\})\), while the dependence on \(N\) disappears. Proving or refuting that would say exactly which part of the discrete lower bound was population multiplicity and which part was item combinatorics.

The paper’s Theorem 3.5, proved in Section 3 as a slight extension of the cited result [24], supports this choice of mirror: under its two valuation-domain conditions, every incentive-compatible VCG-based mechanism is welfare-equivalent to a maximal-in-range allocation rule. I would not use Theorem 3.5 as a second anchor, because its direct atomless strategic analogue is problematic. It explains, however, why the allocation-level CMIR problem is recognisably the computational core of the authors’ VCG-based question.

This mirror would be plausible to the authors in a concrete regime such as a large procurement or advertising market. There may be millions of clients, but only a relatively small number of stable valuation profiles—industry segments, firm sizes, campaign classes, or household categories. Clients of one type have the same submodular value for bundles of available services, and inventory is naturally replicated per capita across market cycles. Here \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\), and mass is genuinely the honest quantity.

The important scope limitation is that this mirrors Theorem 2.1’s MIR welfare-approximation content, not the full payment semantics of a continuum VCG mechanism. A literal atomless individual has zero influence on aggregate welfare, so individual dominant-strategy truthfulness becomes vacuous. The clean repair is to define the continuous object through coherent finite clone expansions: every rational society is realized by a finite replicated market on which the original VCG/MIR semantics apply, and the continuous problem studies the normalized aggregate allocation and communication complexity. A separate follow-up would ask whether a non-vacuous cohort-level payment theory exists.

The weakest point is therefore the necessary per-capita supply scaling and the finite-clone interpretation. If the referee insists on exactly one globally available copy of each item, the continuum population becomes degenerate. If the referee insists on pointwise individual truthfulness in an atomless market, the strategic part collapses. But those objections target the payment and supply conventions, not the paper’s central MIR welfare problem. With replicated inventory and clone-coherent allocation rules, the mirror remains a faithful, computationally meaningful high-multiplicity version of the paper’s main theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with a mismatch between Theorem 2.1 and the proposed continuum. The theorem is not primarily about a population distribution. It is a communication lower bound for \(n\) separately held valuation functions, followed by an MIR allocation of a labelled partition \(S=(S_1,\ldots,S_n)\). Its hard instances use independent bidder inputs and the geometry of labelled allocations.

A mass vector \(\mu\) does not contain that information. If \(N\) clones share a valuation type, \(\mu\) records their common true valuation, but not their reports. In the original mechanism, any one clone may misreport independently. There are then \(N\) reports, not one type-level report, and the aggregate society no longer describes the mechanism’s input. Per-capita supply fixes the fixed-inventory degeneracy, but not this informational one.

There are only three apparent repairs, and each changes the paper’s problem. If the valuation \(v_t\) is public, communication disappears and the problem becomes centralized welfare optimization. If a whole type cohort submits one report, the cohort has been replaced by a single coalition or principal; this is not individual incentive compatibility, and the communication parameter \(n\) has been replaced by \(\tau\). If individual reports are retained, the continuous society must again record an \(N\)-tuple of reports, so the population has not really been compressed into a distribution. In the atomless limit, one individual’s report has measure zero and the strategic constraint becomes vacuous.

The proposed allocation variable \(x_{t,S}\) introduces a second mismatch. It permits a type cohort to split across bundles. After clearing denominators this is implementable by clones, but it is an aggregate convexification of labelled allocations. The proof of Theorem 2.1 is built around subsets of discrete partitions and relations such as \(D_i\cap D'_j\neq\varnothing\). Those objects have no canonical meaning for a mass allocation. Conversely, forbidding type-splitting in order to preserve them makes the “continuum” artificially integral at the type level.

Theorem 3.5 does not repair this. Its proof relies on unilateral deviations by a named bidder, dense finite-dimensional valuation profiles, and the VCG payment identity. A measure-zero agent has no aggregate externality; a positive-mass cohort deviation is a different mechanism. Thus the paper’s VCG characterization has no non-vacuous atomless analogue without changing its strategic object.

This is a serious objection to a continuous mirror of the paper’s full mechanism-design result. But it does not defeat the proponent’s deliberately narrower anchor. Theorem 2.1 is itself stated as an MIR allocation and communication result, and the programme permits mirroring one computational result rather than every payment semantic. A replicated-market model with anonymous type cohorts, per-capita supply, and a type-level communication oracle is a coherent high-multiplicity configuration problem. The fact that it is a new model, and that the original lower-bound proof may or may not survive, is not an objection under the programme’s rules.

So the honest negative conclusion is limited: the proposed mirror does not faithfully preserve the paper’s private-information or VCG content. It cannot support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. If allocation-level high-multiplicity variants count, Theorem 2.1 remains a viable green anchor, and I would not recommend rejecting the paper on this basis.
