What the mirror covers

The proposed cohort feasibility formulation is not established by a named result in the paper; Theorems 1 and 2 address structural properties of mechanisms for a single finite-type bargaining game.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a cohort of standardized bilateral negotiations: a large mediation service handles \(N\) cases, with \(N\gg\tau\), where each case has two players, the same agreement set and utility functions, and private disagreement payoffs drawn from a small set of types. The type of a player is their role and disagreement payoff; the mass of a type pair \((t_1,t_2)\) is the fraction of cases with that pair. The mediator uses one reporting rule across cases. Each case still ends in a discrete agreement; mass records how many cases receive each outcome.

A precise candidate is Cohort Efficient Bargaining Feasibility. Given finite \(A\), disagreement outcome \(a_0\), utilities \(u_i\), type sets \(T_i\), and a full-support binary-rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T_1\times T_2\), find a table \(x^a(t_1,t_2)\). It must satisfy \(x^a(t)\geq0\), \(\sum_{a\in A}x^a(t)=1\), Bayesian incentive compatibility, interim individual rationality, and ex-post efficiency, \(x^{a_0}(t)=0\). It must also be type-dependent: for some player \(i\) and types \(t_i,t_i'\), their truthful interim utilities differ. A solution is the table \(x\), with the differing types identified. The entries can be read as lottery probabilities within a case, or as outcome shares across a continuum of identical cases.

The paper gives useful structural targets, though neither is a computational anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says that with a linear Pareto frontier and full-support prior, every efficient mechanism is type-independent. Theorem 2, proved here, says that with a non-linear frontier and full support, an efficient mechanism is type-independent if the smaller type set has size two; if both type sets have more than two types, an efficient, individually rational, type-dependent mechanism can exist. For the candidate feasibility problem, I would expect tractability: its incentive, rationality, and efficiency conditions are linear, and type-dependence can be checked by maximizing utility differences over pairs of types. That is an observation about this proposed problem, not a complexity result established by the paper. Natural follow-ups would test how the answer changes with the frontier, full support, and the number of types.

The obstacle is substantial: the paper’s \(f\) is a prior over the states of a two-player game, not a population distribution in a collective social choice problem. A cohort interpretation makes those probabilities frequencies across cases, but the cases remain separate; it does not create a shared societal decision. The paper contains no named result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified, so there is no qualifying anchor and no compliant per-anchor mirror to give. The paper’s footnote conjecturing an extension to interval type spaces is a closer mathematical continuation, but it continuizes the players’ type spaces, not the population, and remains an analytic question. My positive case is therefore a plausible application of the paper’s mechanism problem to a high-volume cohort, not a strong claim that this paper supplies a ChoCo computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 classify which incentive-compatible mechanisms can exist under different assumptions; they give no algorithm, hardness result, or computational problem with an input-size analysis. The proponent acknowledges this. “Cohort Efficient Bargaining Feasibility” may be a worthwhile problem someone could formulate, but it is a new mechanism-synthesis question, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The cohort of standardized negotiations is the strongest rescue, and it is a plausible high-volume setting. But its units are separate two-person games. The paper’s mechanism maps each pair’s reports to an outcome, and its incentive constraints use the conditional beliefs in \(f\). If a cohort has \(n_t\) cases of each type pair among \(N\) cases, then the relevant frequencies \(n_t/N\) are already expressible as the paper’s prior \(f\). Replicating the cohort leaves the mechanism and its constraints unchanged; \(N\) is not a parameter in the problem. The cohort makes the prior interpretable as frequencies, but adds no population-scale computational question. Pooling cases under a shared budget or other aggregate constraint could create one, but that would add a coupling the paper does not study and change the bargaining problem.

Theorem 1 therefore does not yield a population mirror: its claim of type-independent interim utilities is about the finite types of the two players in one game. Repeating that game across a continuum of cases leaves the claim exactly where it was. One could ask an algorithm to find an efficient, incentive-compatible mechanism or maximize its type-dependence, but the computation would concern the existing finite type space and prior, not the size of the cohort.

The same applies to Theorem 2. Its threshold—two types versus more than two—is a property of each player’s type set, not of how many negotiations are repeated. The paper already states the existence result for those finite type spaces. A continuum of cases with the same types leaves that threshold intact; a continuum of disagreement-payoff types would instead continuize the players’ type spaces, not the population. Even granting a corrected version of the proposed feasibility formulation, it would not supply the missing population axis.

So the negative case is strong against these anchors: neither theorem supports a worthwhile continuous-population mirror in the proposed cohort setting, and the paper has no named computational result to anchor another one. The limitation is that this does not prove no useful extension can ever be built. A bargaining platform with genuinely shared resources could motivate a computational population model, but its central constraints would come from the added institution, not from a continuization of this paper’s results.
