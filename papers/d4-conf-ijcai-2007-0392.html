The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper’s *model* has a natural population mirror, but the paper offers no eligible result to anchor that case.

In a large crowd, let a type be a pair \((p,s)\): social rank \(p\) and current orientation \(s\). Mass \(\mu_{p,s}\) is the fraction of the crowd with that type. Diffusion changes orientation mass according to the paper’s group influence and counteracting forces; one could ask whether the resulting process reaches consensus or a stable state meeting a specified diffusion-performance threshold. This is plausible as a high-multiplicity version of the authors’ problem: their model already makes strategy support and social rank jointly determine influence.

But there is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition in the paper asserting a computational complexity result. Algorithm 1 describes the simulation procedure, and the case studies report simulation outcomes; neither classifies an underlying problem as tractable or hard. So there is no qualifying anchor, and I can’t honestly present that candidate as a mirror of a named result or predict its place in the programme’s trichotomy.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper gives no eligible result to mirror. It has no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Algorithm 1 describes a simulation, and Section 4 reports simulation outcomes; neither establishes an algorithmic guarantee or a tractability or hardness result. The proponent’s \((p,s)\)-type population is plausible, but they correctly concede that it is not a mirror of a named result.

A stronger candidate would make types include rank, position, and orientation, then ask whether a society given by rational masses reaches consensus within \(K\) rounds, or what terminal performance it achieves. That is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario, and rank or position does not by itself make mass inappropriate. But the paper does not pin down the computational problem such questions would require: the influence functions are only constrained to be monotone, and the update amount, tie-breaking, and scheduling choices are not fully fixed. Even its stopping measure is not a clear substitute for state convergence: the reported Figures 5 and 6 have the same \(PS\) but different surviving strategies. Choosing a precise transition system and a decision problem could produce worthwhile new work, but those would be additions to the paper’s model, not computational results it already presents.

There is a further fit problem: the paper studies agent-based diffusion of social strategies, rather than a computational social choice rule or task. A continuum model of its dynamics may be a natural topic for social simulation or mean-field analysis; to make it a ChoCo contribution, one would still need to add a computational question and show why it belongs in that programme.

So the case against a paper-anchored mirror is strong: there is no anchor, and the best proposed replacement is newly formulated. I cannot honestly extend that into proof that no scenario could yield worthwhile computational work. The continuous-population model is plausible; what the paper fails to supply is a named computational result for that work to mirror.
