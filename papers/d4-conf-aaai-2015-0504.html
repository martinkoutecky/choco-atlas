The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is that the paper’s population structure is real: it studies many agents reporting on similar objects, and its results turn on whether those agents are homogeneous or heterogeneous. A plausible continuous-agent follow-up would take a finite set \(T\) of evaluator types, where each type specifies its signal-generating process and belief model, and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). As the number of evaluators \(N\) grows with \(\tau=|T|\) fixed, ask: for which \(\mu\) does truthful reporting form a weak Perfect Bayesian Nash equilibrium when agents use the paper’s Bayesian Truth Serum with Private Priors? Reports are the information and prediction reports; payoffs are the paper’s two scores. This is a recognizable population version of the paper’s question, and its conclusion explicitly points toward studying cases between homogeneous and fully heterogeneous populations.

That is only an adjacent mirror, though—not one supported by a qualifying anchor under the requested rules. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that Minimal Peer Prediction with Private Priors is strictly Bayes–Nash incentive compatible when the proxy sample \(\Sigma\) has at least twice as many elements as there are possible evaluation values, under the stated full-mixing and stochastic-relevance assumptions. Proposition 1 says that, in general, no mechanism can make honest reporting a strict Bayes–Nash equilibrium for heterogeneous agents. Theorem 2 establishes a best response involving truthful predictions and consistent information reports; Theorem 3 says truthful reporting is a weak Perfect Bayesian Nash equilibrium; and Proposition 2 extends that equilibrium claim when fewer than all agents report heuristically. These are incentive and equilibrium results, not results about computational complexity, so none qualifies as the required anchor. There is consequently no anchor-specific continuous problem to give.

The weakest point is decisive for ChoCo: the proposed follow-up asks for an equilibrium characterization, not for the complexity of an underlying computational problem. The population regime is plausible, but this paper supplies no named computational result whose discrete hardness might dissolve, or whose algorithmic tractability might transfer, in that regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against rests on the paper’s subject: it studies incentives and equilibrium, not computational problems. Theorem 1 proves incentive compatibility; Proposition 1 proves an impossibility; Theorems 2 and 3 and Proposition 2 concern best responses and equilibrium. None states a runtime, algorithm, or complexity bound for a computational problem. Under ChoCo’s scope, that leaves no computational result to mirror.

The proposed distribution \(\mu\) over evaluator types is a sensible population model. This paper is not a case where multiplicity is implausible: it explicitly studies groups of agents with shared characteristics, and an atomless population can still preserve individual deviations and peer-sampling payoffs. The objection is not that the continuous society is incoherent or that the limit destroys incentives.

The proposed question—when truthful reporting forms a weak equilibrium for a given \(\mu\)—is an equilibrium characterization. A richer version could specify finite-support types, their signal channels and beliefs, how they understand the population, and how object states are generated. Those details can make the game well-defined, but the question remains about equilibrium conditions. The paper’s results already ask whether particular mechanisms elicit truthful or consistent behavior; changing the population description does not supply the missing computational problem.

One could add a computational task, such as choosing payment rules or sample sizes to satisfy incentive constraints across supported types. That may be worth studying, but it requires choosing a mechanism class, an objective, and constraints that this paper does not formulate. It would be a new mechanism-synthesis project, rather than a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s results.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s remit and weak as a universal claim about every possible follow-up. The population setting is credible; what fails is the proposed bridge from this paper to a computational-complexity programme.
