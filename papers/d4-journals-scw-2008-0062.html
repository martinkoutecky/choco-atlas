What the mirror covers

No paper result is covered by a computational mirror; the proposed unanimous evaluation over ranking masses does not cover the paper’s axiomatic characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Corollaries 1–3, and Theorem 3 are axiomatic characterizations of aggregation rules and agendas. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or another complexity class. So I cannot honestly give a per-anchor mirror of the kind requested.

The closest positive foothold is Theorem 2(a), proved here: on agendas satisfying conditions (ii) and (iii), the only universal, deductively closed, unanimity-preserving, independent aggregation functions are oligarchies. Corollary 2, also established here, applies this to preference aggregation with at least three alternatives: the only such functions from profiles of strict rankings to collective strict partial orderings are oligarchies. With anonymity, Corollary 1(a) further says unanimity is the only one.

A plausible population regime is a large electorate judging a fixed finite agenda, with many voters sharing complete judgment sets; for the preference application, types are strict rankings, masses are population fractions, and the support size \(\tau\) can be much smaller than the electorate. The nearest continuous extension is: given a finite set \(K\) of alternatives and a binary-rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) strict rankings, compute the collective partial order
\[ xP_\mu y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{t:\,xP_ty}\mu_t=1. \]
This is unanimous aggregation over the support. It is computable in polynomial time, for example by checking each pair against each supported ranking. It covers the preference-aggregation application, not the paper’s results in general.

That is a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation of the preference problem, but it is not a computational result the paper proves: the paper classifies permissible rules rather than studying their computational complexity. The proposed computation is also an immediate, easy consequence of unanimity. Its weakest point is therefore decisive for this programme: it offers no discrete hardness whose fate in the continuum could be investigated.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The preference-aggregation result is the strongest candidate: a large electorate with many voters sharing a ranking is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. I would not object that population mass is unnatural here. The problem is that Theorem 2(a) and Corollary 2 characterize which aggregation functions satisfy certain axioms; they do not pose a computational problem. Corollary 1(a) narrows that characterization under anonymity, but remains axiomatic.

For a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, one can choose the anonymous case and compute the unanimous partial order
\[ xP_\mu y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{t:\,xP_ty}\mu_t=1. \]
That is a sensible continuous operation, but it is an added evaluation task, not a computational question raised by the results. Without anonymity, the theorems allow different oligarchies, and \(\mu\) alone does not specify which coalition’s judgments determine the output. Adding coalition masses would let us evaluate a chosen oligarchy, but the paper gives no computational objective for selecting one. Asking for an algorithm to select an oligarchy, or to characterize aggregation functions over distributions, would require a new problem; the latter would also be an axiomatic continuization, outside ChoCo’s scope.

The group-identification application is a weaker candidate because its output concerns the membership status of named individuals. But the preference application is already the more favorable case, and it still supplies no computational anchor. The proposed unanimous calculation does not rescue one: its being easy is not the objection; the objection is that it is not a computational result or problem from this paper.

So the negative case is strong against a *mirror of this paper’s results*: every named theorem and corollary here is axiomatic, and making it computational requires adding a task the authors did not study. The broader claim that no worthwhile computational question could ever be built around continuous judgment aggregation is weaker. The large-electorate regime is plausible, but any such question would be a new project rather than a continuous mirror of this paper.
