The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(\alpha,\beta,\rho,\varepsilon\) with \(0<\alpha<1\), \(\beta>0\), \(0<\rho<1\), and \(\varepsilon>0\), let a unit-mass society have valuations distributed as \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\), and let \(v_\rho\) satisfy \(F^+(v_\rho)=\rho\). Find \(\hat p\in[0,v_\rho]\) such that \(U(\hat p)\geq\max_{p\in[0,v_\rho]}U(p)-\varepsilon\), where \(U(p)=\frac{\rho}{F^+(p)}\int_p^1(v-p)f(v)\,dv\), and report the resulting lottery probability.

The model it lives in

A unit mass of agents has valuations \(v\in[0,1]\) distributed by the beta density \(f\). The agency has supply mass \(\rho\), posts a price \(p\), and allocates units uniformly by lottery among agents with \(v\geq p\), giving each eligible agent probability \(\rho/F^+(p)\). Choose \(p\in[0,v_\rho]\) to maximize aggregate expected consumer surplus \(U(p)=\rho\,\mathrm{MRL}(p)\).

The objection that survived

For finite rational valuation support, checking the value thresholds gives a direct solution; the proponent notes that smooth beta valuations need not have small support but does not establish a substantive computational obstacle for that family.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3’s market-versus-hybrid allocation choice for beta values with \(\alpha<1\), including its numerical beta example; it leaves Propositions 1 and 2, Corollary 1, the eta-function results, and the other distribution families aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What exact and additive-approximation algorithms solve the beta-family price problem, with running time stated in the input bit length and \(\varepsilon\)?
  • How does the mass allocation relate to finite populations of independent draws when supply scales as \(Q^*/N=\rho\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population mirror here, but the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Propositions 1–3 characterize optimal mechanisms from properties of the value distribution; none says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. I can’t honestly present one as a qualifying anchor.

The closest substantive anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It says: if the hazard rate is decreasing and then increasing, and \(\mu f(0)>1\), then (i) when \(HR'(v^*)<0\) and \(1/HR(v^*)\leq\mu\), market assignment at the clearing price maximizes expected consumer surplus; and (ii) when \(HR'(v^*)\geq0\), a hybrid lottery maximizes it. Here \(v^*\) is the clearing price and \(\mu\) is mean value. The proposition is proved here; the paper relies on cited results for the links between hazard-rate shape and mean-residual-value shape. This is an analytic optimality result, not a complexity classification.

If that is accepted as an algorithmic anchor, my lead mirror would be Beta-Continuum ECS Optimization. An instance gives rational \(\alpha,\beta,\rho,\varepsilon\), with \(0<\alpha<1\), \(\beta>0\), \(0<\rho<1\), and \(\varepsilon>0\). A unit-mass population has valuations \(v\in[0,1]\) distributed as \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\); each person wants at most one indivisible unit, and the agency has supply mass \(\rho\). Let \(v_\rho\) solve \(F^+(v_\rho)=\rho\). The agency chooses a price \(p\in[0,v_\rho]\); everyone with \(v\geq p\) is eligible, and each eligible person receives a unit with probability \(\rho/F^+(p)\). The objective is

\[ U(p)=\frac{\rho}{F^+(p)}\int_p^1 (v-p)f(v)\,dv =\rho\,\mathrm{MRL}(p). \]

The requested solution is a price \(\hat p\) with \(U(\hat p)\geq\max_{p\in[0,v_\rho]}U(p)-\varepsilon\), together with its implied lottery probability. At \(p=v_\rho\) this is market assignment; at an interior price it is hybrid assignment. This is the paper’s posted-price consumer-surplus problem with population mass and per-capita supply made explicit. I would expect the fixed-distribution approximation version to be tractable, but Proposition 3 does not establish a polynomial-time bound for it. Questions about exact computation, arbitrary encoded distributions, and convergence from finite populations remain open.

The regime would be a large public housing, school-seat, or medical-service allocation with a stable population distribution of willingness to pay and a supply rate that remains positive as the population grows. Mass is the fraction of applicants at each valuation; \(\rho\) is the fraction receiving units. The paper’s assumptions make this scenario recognizable to its authors.

My weakest point is that exact valuations under a smooth beta distribution need not form a small finite set of repeated types. That makes the high-multiplicity story less direct than one based on a few valuation categories. More fundamentally, the paper already uses \(F\) and mean residual value to characterize the same scalar objective. The mirror is mathematically natural, but it may add little computational content: the paper’s analysis nearly solves it already. I would not claim a new complexity result from this anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is the only named anchor, and it does not pass ChoCo’s computational gate. It characterizes which price maximizes expected consumer surplus given a value distribution. The paper defines that objective as \(Q^*\mathrm{MRL}(p)\) over a one-dimensional price interval; it gives no complexity result, encoded input class, or algorithmic bottleneck.

The Beta mirror is a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation. Set \(\rho=Q^*/N\), and the objective per person is exactly \(\rho\mathrm{MRL}(p)\). But for the proposed case \(\alpha<1\), the paper says \(\mathrm{MRL}\) increases to an interior maximum \(v_1\) and then decreases. The best price on \([0,v_\rho]\) is therefore \(v_1\) if it lies in that interval, and \(v_\rho\) otherwise. Finding \(v_1\) amounts to solving the scalar equation \(\mathrm{HR}(p)\mathrm{MRL}(p)=1\). An \(\varepsilon\)-approximation of this price is a numerical root-finding task, not a complexity landscape about population multiplicity.

A stronger discrete-support version leads to the same concern. Give a unit-mass society \(\tau\) valuation types with rational masses. Sort their values and compute tail masses and tail sums. Between valuation thresholds, the eligible set is fixed and the objective decreases linearly with price; an additive-\(\varepsilon\) solution can be found by checking prices at, or just above, those thresholds. This has no evident combinatorial obstacle. If hardness is introduced by allowing an arbitrarily encoded distribution, it comes from that new function representation, not from continuizing Proposition 3.

The housing or school-seat scenario and the per-capita supply adjustment are both natural; I would not reject the mirror on either ground. The case against is that the paper’s named result already reduces the agency’s choice to a scalar objective with explicit shape conditions. Adding enough constraints or decisions to create a substantial computational problem would produce a nearby new model, rather than a mirror of a result in this paper. That is a strong case against this anchor, though it cannot establish that every enriched allocation problem nearby would be uninteresting.
