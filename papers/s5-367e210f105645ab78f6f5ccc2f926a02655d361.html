The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give an anchor-compliant positive case: the paper has no numbered computational-complexity result. Its nearest candidate is Theorem 1, proved here: every SANN or SANP instance admits a connected equitable allocation. That is an existence theorem proved with Sperner’s lemma, not a result that the underlying computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. Corollary 1 and Proposition 2 are also existence results. They are not eligible anchors under the requested standard.

The closest computational result mentioned is prior work by Cechlárová and Pillárová [CP12]: the paper reports that no finite Robertson–Webb query algorithm finds a connected allocation that is both equitable and proportional, even for three agents, and gives an \(O(n\log(n/\varepsilon))\)-query algorithm for proportional \(\varepsilon\)-equitable division. Those results are cited in the related-work discussion; they are not numbered results of this paper.

There is a plausible *follow-up question*, though, and it shows where a positive case might begin. Consider \(N\) residents sharing one of \(\tau\) strictly positive, rational, piecewise-constant value densities over a divisible interval. Let type \(t\) have mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and fix an order of the types. When \(N\mu_t\) is integral, give each type a contiguous interval \(I_t\), then split that interval among its \(N\mu_t\) residents into connected pieces of equal value. The type-level question is to find an ordered partition \(I_1,\ldots,I_\tau\) and \(\lambda\) such that
\[ \int_{I_t} f_t(z)\,dz=\mu_t\lambda \quad\text{for every type }t. \]
Each resident then receives value \(\lambda/N\). The regime is \(N\) large and \(\tau\) small: for example, many residents seeking contiguous time slots whose preference curves fall into a few shared profiles.

This is a sensible high-multiplicity version of connected equitability: types specify all relevant preferences, mass records cohort size, and connectedness and equal individual utility survive the aggregation. I would expect the explicitly represented, piecewise-constant version to be tractable. But that is a proposed algorithmic question, not a result established or computationally studied by this paper. The main weakness is decisive for the requested case: the paper studies finite-agent existence over a continuous cake, not computational questions about a continuous population. I would not present the proposed mirror as anchored in its results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no anchor-compliant case: this paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1, Corollaries 1–4, and Proposition 2 are existence results. The Sperner proof gives no running-time or query bound for finding a division. The paper’s computational questions appear only as future work in the conclusion. The cited Robertson–Webb results are prior work on finite-agent query complexity, not computational results established here.

The proposed cohort model is the strongest possible mirror, and it is a coherent population continuization. With \(N\mu_t\) identical residents of each type, its equation
\[ \int_{I_t} f_t(z)\,dz=\mu_t\lambda \]
makes each resident’s value \(\lambda/N\), assuming the type block can be split into equal-value connected pieces. For the stated positive, piecewise-constant setting, this is an exact compression of a finite replication of the agents. The paper’s theorem supplies existence for those finite instances; specifying an input model and finding an efficient algorithm would be a new computational question. For this restricted case, the type intervals can be constructed successively for a fixed \(\lambda\) using cumulative-value inverses, so the proposed question may reduce to finding a scalar root. That makes it a less compelling complexity target, though it does not settle the algorithmic question.

I would reject this paper as an anchored computational-complexity mirror for ChoCo. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile follow-up exists: the cohort formulation survives as a sensible high-multiplicity problem. The negative case is therefore strong on the absence of an eligible result to mirror, and weak against the broader claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile research. Treating the cake’s continuity or the existing high-multiplicity literature as objections would not strengthen it.
