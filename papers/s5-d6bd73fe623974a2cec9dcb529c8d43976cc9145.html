The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational result: no numbered result classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results characterize equilibria and assignments. So there is no fully compliant, anchor-based case for a new ChoCo complexity mirror.

The strongest nearby anchor is Proposition 3, proved in the paper. It says that in the neighborhood-priority model under RevealMore, for every realized utility profile and lottery, there is a unique BNE characterized by school cutoffs. A student with lottery number at most \(x_1^\pi\), or who lives in \(s_1\)’s neighborhood, gets \(s_1\) by ranking it highest. For \(k\ge2\), a student whose number lies in \((x_{k-1}^\pi,x_k^\pi]\), or whose number exceeds \(x_k^\pi\) but who lives in \(s_k\)’s neighborhood, gets \(s_k\) by ranking it essentially highest. As printed, the proposition says \(x_1^\pi>x_2^\pi>\cdots>x_m^\pi\), but its intervals and the later statement \(x_m^\pi=n\) appear to require the reverse ordering.

If that equilibrium result is treated as a near-anchor, the clearest mirror is the RevealMore continuum cutoff-equilibrium problem. The instance has \(m\) ordered schools with rational capacity shares \(q_s\) summing to \(1\), disjoint neighborhood groups with rational masses \(n_s<q_s\), and a finite distribution \(\mu\) over complete student types, including utility vector and neighborhood. Total population mass is \(1\); each type’s mass is spread uniformly over lottery rank \(z\in[0,1]\). Students see their own rank and the neighborhood rank distributions, and choose a preference list of the allowed length. Each student maximizes their own utility. The task is to compute a BNE, its cutoff vector, and the resulting assignment masses; there is no planner objective in the paper.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for a large district admissions cohort with relatively few utility and neighborhood classes. Its likely answer is tractable: the paper’s unnumbered continuum discussion already gives
\[ x_k=\frac{\sum_{y=1}^k q_{s_y}-\sum_{y=1}^k n_{s_y}}{1-\sum_{y=1}^k n_{s_y}}, \]
which can be computed with \(O(m)\) rational arithmetic operations. Further questions could study finite-market approximation error, many priority classes, or equilibrium computation when lottery information is incomplete.

That is the strongest honest positive case, and also its weakness: the proposed mirror is an equilibrium-computation question rather than a complexity result of the paper, and the paper already presents the continuum population and its cutoff formula. It supports the claim that this is a sensible continuous object, but not a claim that ChoCo fills a missing computational gap for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative starts before modeling: this paper gives no numbered computational result to mirror. Its propositions characterize Bayesian Nash equilibria, assignments, and welfare comparisons; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic result. That leaves no direct ChoCo anchor.

Proposition 3 is the closest candidate, even after setting aside its apparent cutoff-order typo. A charitable mirror would ask for the RevealMore equilibrium and assignment masses in a continuum market. But the paper already formulates that population model: lottery ranks are uniform on \([0,1]\), so each neighborhood’s rank distribution is deterministic. It gives the cutoff formula
\[ x_k=\frac{\sum_{y=1}^k q_{s_y}-\sum_{y=1}^k n_{s_y}}{1-\sum_{y=1}^k n_{s_y}}. \]
For rational capacities and neighborhood masses, this directly computes the cutoffs and assignments in polynomial time. The closest mirror is therefore already solved in the paper itself. The existing high-multiplicity literature supports the regime’s plausibility; the objection here is narrower: the proposed RevealMore computation is not a missing question.

The most plausible rescue is to study the Cover policy: compute equilibria, or quantify how finite-market equilibria approach the continuum. That is a sensible direction, but it would be a new computational problem. The paper does not characterize Cover equilibria generally; it gives qualitative propositions and selected examples. A computational version would need to specify the equilibrium representation, input encoding, and output or approximation criterion. There is also a substantive limit issue. In finite markets, lottery permutations create random group counts and pivotal effects. In an atomless market, those group counts become deterministic, and an individual deviation cannot change aggregate congestion. Keeping the finite pivotal effects means studying a finite-\(n\) game; replacing them with deterministic masses gives a different strategic object. The paper’s own continuum discussion identifies the disappearance of lottery-composition uncertainty.

Adding many priority classes could make equilibrium computation harder, but that too would be an extension rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper. It would need its own case for why the resulting complexity problem is the authors’ question and why the continuum model adds something beyond a new market-design exercise.

I would not claim to have proved that no useful continuous-computational project could ever come from this paper. A continuum Cover-equilibrium problem with many student and priority types is the genuine weak point in the negative case: the authors might recognize it as a natural next question. The defensible verdict is narrower and strong: this paper supplies no computational-complexity anchor, its nearest continuous candidate is already explicitly solved, and the remaining candidate is a new project rather than a mirror of one of its results.
