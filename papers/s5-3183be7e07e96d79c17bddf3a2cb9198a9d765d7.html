The continuous mirror question

Given applicant types \(I\) with rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), their preferences and class memberships, and posts with rational per-capita quotas, compute a feasible allocation with lexicographically maximal aggregate rank vector \((s_1,\ldots,s_r)\), where \(s_k\) is the assigned mass receiving a rank-\(k\) post. For the normalized two-rank target in Theorem 5, ask whether a feasible allocation attains \((1/2,1/2)\).

The model it lives in

Use variables \(x_{ip}\ge 0\) for mass of type \(i\) assigned to eligible post \(p\). Require \(\sum_p x_{ip}\le\mu_i\), \(\sum_i x_{ip}\le q_p\), and \(\sum_{i\in C}x_{ip}\le\kappa_{p,C}\) for every post class \(C\). Maximize the aggregate rank vector lexicographically; each person still receives at most one whole post, while a type’s mass may be divided across posts.

What the mirror covers

Covers classified rank-maximal matching with overlapping post classes and mass-scaled quotas; it does not cover popular matching, the laminar flow algorithms, or maximum-cardinality matching. The SAT equivalence in Theorem 5 need not survive fractional allocation.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the fractional Mass-CRMM solution be rounded with additive rank-signature error bounded in terms of population size \(N\), support size \(\tau\), and the pattern of overlapping classes?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Mass-CRMM, the continuous form of classified rank-maximal matching. The anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper as a reduction from monotone 1-in-3 SAT; the underlying SAT hardness is cited to Schaefer [14]. For a formula with \(n\) variables and \(m\) clauses, the theorem’s constructed instance has a matching of signature \((3m+n,3m+n)\) exactly when the formula has a satisfying assignment. The construction uses \(2n+6m\) applicants and \(3n+m\) posts. Each applicant has a strict list of length two: \(a_i\) ranks \(p_i\) above \(p_i^t\), \(b_i\) ranks \(p_i\) above \(p_i^f\), and occurrence applicants \(a_{ij},b_{ij}\) rank their clause post \(p_j\) above \(p_i^t,p_i^f\), respectively. Applicants have quota one. Only posts have classes: each clause post has quota three, with quota-one classes \(\{a_{ij},b_{ij}\}\) for each occurrence, a quota-one class of its three \(a\)-applicants, and a quota-two class of its three \(b\)-applicants. Each \(p_i^t\) and \(p_i^f\) has quota \(k_i\), the number of occurrences of \(x_i\), and quota-one classes pairing its occurrence applicants with \(a_i\) or \(b_i\), respectively. Each \(p_i\) has quota one. Thus the hardness already holds with strict, length-two preferences and classifications on posts alone.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover. An instance of Mass-CRMM has a finite support \(I\) of applicant types, with rational masses \(\mu_i\geq 0\) summing to one. A type records everything relevant to matching: its strict preference list and eligibility, and its membership in every post class. Posts have rational per-capita quotas \(q_p\); each listed class \(C\subseteq I\) at post \(p\) has a rational quota \(\kappa_{p,C}\). Classes may overlap arbitrarily. Choose an allocation \(x_{ip}\geq 0\) for each eligible type-post pair, satisfying
\[ \sum_p x_{ip}\leq\mu_i,\qquad \sum_i x_{ip}\leq q_p,\qquad \sum_{i\in C}x_{ip}\leq\kappa_{p,C}. \]
For rank \(k\), let \(s_k(x)=\sum_{i,p:\,p\text{ is rank }k\text{ for }i}x_{ip}\). The task is to return a feasible \(x\) whose vector \((s_1(x),\ldots,s_r(x))\) is lexicographically maximal. Equivalently, the target-signature version asks whether a specified rank threshold can be achieved. If the Theorem 5 instance is normalized to total mass one, its signature target becomes \((1/2,1/2)\).

This is a population model, not fractional bundles for individual applicants. Each person still receives at most one whole post; \(x_{ip}\) records how a nonatomic cohort of type \(i\) is divided among posts. A natural regime is a large admissions or hiring clearinghouse: millions of applicants share a much smaller set of ranking, eligibility, and background profiles, while posts impose capacity and overlapping group quotas. There \(N\), the number of people, can be far larger than \(\tau=|I|\). Scaling seats and class quotas with the intake is a natural co-modification when assignments are expressed as population shares. The constructed hardness instances need not themselves have this repeated-cohort structure for the mirror to make sense.

I expect Mass-CRMM to be tractable. Its feasible region is a rational linear program with one variable per allowed type-post pair and linear constraints for type mass, post capacity, and every class. Non-laminarity adds overlapping rows but does not prevent solving the LP. Lexicographic optimization takes at most \(r\) sequential LP optimizations, fixing earlier rank totals each time, so the problem is polynomial in the explicit instance size and bit length. The paper’s integral hardness does not transfer automatically: the reduction relies on choices behaving as all-or-nothing variable assignments, while a nonatomic cohort can split across posts. This asks the authors’ underlying CRMM question with the population made continuous; it does not replace matching with an outcome lottery or change the objective.

The useful follow-up is what this tractable mirror buys for finite instances: characterize the integrality gap and find additive rounding guarantees in terms of \(N\), \(\tau\), and the pattern of overlapping classes. The paper’s laminar algorithm gives a contrasting boundary, since its flow structure can preserve integrality where the continuous LP alone would not.

My weakest point is that Theorem 5’s reduction itself has many distinct applicant profiles—its type support can grow with the formula—so the theorem does not demonstrate that its hard instances lie in a high-multiplicity regime. The positive case rests instead on the plausible large-cohort admissions or hiring regime and on preserving the paper’s exact rank-maximal objective and class-feasibility question there. That is a good case for studying this mirror, but it does not claim that the mirror explains away the reduction’s hardness on its own instances.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the gap between Theorem 5’s reduction and the proposed population regime. In that construction, an applicant’s type must encode its exact preference list and class memberships. The occurrence applicants are tied to particular clauses and posts, so the support grows with the formula and is essentially as large as the applicant set: \(\tau=2n+6m\). Normalizing the signature to \((1/2,1/2)\) does not make those instances high-multiplicity. The reduction therefore gives little evidence that the continuous question captures the regime responsible for the paper’s hardness. A large admissions or hiring cohort is plausible, but it changes the setting from the reduction’s individualized incidence pattern to a different application scenario.

There is also a case that the proposed mirror adds little computationally. With explicit types, posts, and class constraints, Mass-CRMM is a direct linear program with one variable per eligible type-post pair. Arbitrary overlap among classes just adds linear constraints; it creates no pricing problem. For the laminar case, the paper already gives a flow algorithm, and with integral quotas the corresponding flow formulation has the familiar integrality property. The continuous formulation may therefore look more like a routine fractional relaxation than a new complexity landscape. The LP for Theorem 5 can also meet its fractional signature target on instances where the reduction’s integral matching cannot, so it does not preserve the reduction’s SAT correspondence.

Those points make the payoff less compelling, but they do not defeat the mirror under the programme’s stated criteria. A cohort can be partitioned into people who each receive one whole post; \(x_{ip}\) records the mass assigned from that cohort. Rank-maximality depends on aggregate rank counts, not on tracking named applicants, and overlapping group quotas remain meaningful at the mass level. A course-allocation or hiring regime with recurring preference profiles and overlapping categories is a credible scenario. Theorem 4’s maximum-cardinality hardness also has a straightforward continuous counterpart.

So the honest negative case is weak. It can question whether the explicit LP is an interesting enough contribution and whether Theorem 5 supports the claimed high-multiplicity bridge. It cannot show that the mass matching question is ill-posed or rule out a worthwhile scenario; the proposed CRMM mirror survives those objections.
