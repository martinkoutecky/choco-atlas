The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-member endowments \(e_t>0\), per-member demands \(d_{t,j}\ge 0\) for rounds \(j\in[r]\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\ge 0\), choose a common per-member allocation \(x_{t,j}\) for each type, with \(0\le x_{t,j}\le d_{t,j}\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{t,j}\le q_j\). If \(D\) is a common denominator of the masses, maximize lexicographically the nondecreasing vector containing \(D\mu_t\) copies of \(\sum_j x_{t,j}/e_t\) for each type \(t\). Can an exact optimum be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau=|T|\), \(r\), and the binary input length \(L\), without expanding the \(D\)-member population?

The model it lives in

Types are complete workload profiles \((e_t,(d_{t,j})_{j\in[r]})\) with mass \(\mu_t\). Each type receives a common per-member allocation \(x_{t,j}\), and the objective is the lexicographically maximal mass-weighted multiset of utility per endowment. An aggregate flow can use source-to-type capacities \(\mu_t e_t\lambda\), type-to-round capacities \(\mu_t d_{t,j}\), and round-to-sink capacities \(q_j\); whether it preserves every repeated-entry tie-break is the central proof obligation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers computation of frugal LMMF allocations from Lemma 2. It makes no claim that the paper's envy-freeness, incentive, monotonicity, or sharing-incentive results transfer to the mass model.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the repeated-ratio lexicographic objective be optimized without expanding \(D\), in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(r\), and \(L\)?
  • Does the aggregate type-to-round flow preserve all tie-breaks of the \(D\)-agent LMMF objective?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make a narrow Class A case for computing the paper’s egalitarian allocation. I would not claim that its strategic or fairness guarantees have already been carried over.

The anchor is Lemma 2, proved in this paper. It says that, for every OAFD instance \(I\), the set of allocations output by algorithm \(\mathcal A\) is exactly \(\LMM(I)\), the frugal LMMF allocations. The paper builds a parametric flow network with one vertex per agent and object: source-to-agent capacity \(e_a\lambda\), agent-to-object capacity \(d(a,b)\), and object-to-sink capacity \(\min(s(b),\sum_a d(a,b))\). It uses the correspondence between lexicographic flows and frugal LMMF allocations. The paper gives the running time \(O((n+k)nk\log((n+k)^2/(nk)))\) by invoking the lexicographic-flow algorithm of Gallo et al.; the flow algorithm is prior work, while the allocation correspondence is proved here. This is an algorithmic anchor, though the paper does not state it as a formal “in P” theorem.

Lead question — Mass-LMMF Offline Sharing. Consider an offline resource pool serving \(r\) rounds. An instance consists of a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a positive per-member endowment \(e_t\) for each type, per-member demands \(d_{t,j}\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\) for each round \(j\). A type is the complete pair \((e_t,(d_{t,j})_{j=1}^r)\). Choose a common per-member allocation \(x_{t,j}\) for each type, subject to
\[ 0\le x_{t,j}\le d_{t,j}, \qquad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{t,j}\le q_j. \]
The utility per member of type \(t\) is \(u_t(x)=\sum_j x_{t,j}\). Let \(D\) be a common denominator of the masses. Form the length-\(D\) vector containing \(D\mu_t\) copies of \(u_t(x)/e_t\) for each type \(t\), sorted in nondecreasing order. The problem is to return an allocation \(x\) whose vector is lexicographically maximal.

This objective preserves the paper’s agent-by-agent LMMF criterion: it is the sorted utility-per-endowment vector of a finite population with those type proportions. Choosing a larger common denominator merely repeats every entry, so it does not change which allocation is optimal. The decision variable is a type’s per-member allocation; the objective is egalitarian utility per unit of endowment; and supply scales with the population through \(q_j\).

A plausible regime is a large organization sharing compute capacity over several known workload periods. Many accounts may have the same ownership tier and the same standardized demand forecast, while the number of such profiles \(\tau=|T|\) is far smaller than the number of accounts \(n\). That gives “mass” a direct interpretation as the fraction of accounts in each workload class. This is not a claim that every cloud workload is homogeneous: when \(\tau\) is close to \(n\), the compression has little value.

I expect this question to be Class A, with the important proviso that the compression argument must be proved. The natural network has one node per type, source capacity \(\mu_t e_t\lambda\), type-to-round capacity \(\mu_t d_{t,j}\), and round-to-sink capacity \(q_j\). If its lexicographic flow captures the objective above, the hoped-for running time is polynomial in \(\tau\), \(r\), and the binary input length \(L\), rather than in \(D\). The question for a prover is precisely whether this weighted type network preserves the lexicographic objective of the \(D\)-copy population and yields an exact algorithm with that bound.

That is also the weakest point in the case. Lemma 2’s stated running time is polynomial in the number of named agents; simply expanding rational masses into \(D\) virtual agents can take time exponential in their bit length. The paper does not prove that the quotient network preserves the weighted tie-breaking in the mass objective. Establishing that equivalence and the bit-complexity bound is the real continuization work, not a consequence of the original runtime alone.

This mirror covers the computation of frugal LMMF allocations in Lemma 2. It leaves the envy-freeness, incentive, monotonicity, and sharing-incentive results outside its claim. The paper’s allocation variables are already fractional, but that is continuity of outcomes; this mirror changes the society itself to a distribution over complete workload types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 2 is the strongest anchor, and it is a real one: it gives an exact algorithm for computing frugal LMMF allocations, with a polynomial-time implementation via lexicographic flow. The surrounding envy-freeness, incentive, and monotonicity results are not computational anchors in the same way, but that does not weaken this one.

The best objection is that the proposed scenario needs unusually stable types. A type must include the endowment and the full demand vector across all rounds; as the horizon or forecast detail grows, the support \(\tau\) may approach the number of accounts. The per-capita supply assumption also fits organizations whose capacity grows with membership better than organizations sharing a fixed pool. These points make the mirror’s useful regime narrower than “large cloud workloads” might suggest. They do not rule out a convincing regime: standardized workload cohorts and capacity budgets that scale with enrollment are plausible, and the programme expressly allows such co-modifications.

There is a technical gap in the proposed compression. The finite population’s LMMF objective sorts one utility ratio per agent, whereas a flow network with one node per type appears to sort one ratio per type. The mirror needs to show that the type-level flow preserves the finite population’s objective with each ratio repeated according to its mass. The paper does not prove that equivalence. But its allocation region is a convex flow polytope, and identical agents can be symmetrized, so this looks like a proof obligation—not a fundamental obstruction.

That leaves a modest case against the mirror’s *priority*: the paper already uses parametric flow, and merging repeated agent profiles may yield the continuous algorithm directly. If so, the result could be a short Class A extension rather than a new complexity landscape. But even that does not defeat the anchor: the exact running-time bound in \(\tau\) and the bit length \(L\) is the question the mirror poses, and an easy positive answer is still an answer. I cannot make a convincing case that no worthwhile mirror exists; the honest negative case is that its added contribution may be small. The Lemma 2 anchor survives.
