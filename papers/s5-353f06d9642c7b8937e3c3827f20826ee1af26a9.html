The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case has a hard limit: this paper contains no qualifying anchor. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Definition 1 is a preference definition; Algorithms 1 and 2 describe the framework; the tables report empirical performance. I can’t turn those experiments into a theorem about a problem being in P, NP-hard, or similar. So there are zero anchors, and no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover.

There is a plausible *unanchored* high-multiplicity extension. Let a type be \(t=(a,\gamma)\), where \(a\in[0,1]\) is an agent’s ideal location and \(\gamma\) is the planner’s social weight. A society is a rational-mass distribution \(\mu\) over these types. For \(K\) facility locations \(x\), its weighted cost is
\[ C_\mu(x)=\frac{\int \gamma\,\min_{k\le K}|a-x_k|\,d\mu(a,\gamma)} {\int \gamma\,d\mu(a,\gamma)}. \]
A deterministic mechanism maps a reported society to \(K\) locations. The regime could be a large city planning clinics or parks, with many residents but relatively few shared preference-and-weight types. One could ask for a mechanism minimizing expected \(C_\mu\) over sampled societies while limiting the gain from unilateral misreports. To preserve individual incentives, a report change must move one person’s mass \(1/n\); with truly infinitesimal agents, individual strategyproofness can become vacuous.

That is a credible extension of the paper’s objective and empirical incentive checks, but it is not a result the paper establishes. Its experiments use small populations and sampled misreports, so whether the authors would accept the high-multiplicity version as their problem remains the weak point. The honest positive verdict is therefore: plausible mirror to investigate, but no anchored computational case from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no anchored computational result to continuize. Its numbered items are a preference definition and algorithms; its evidence is empirical performance on sampled instances. It states an ideal optimization over strategyproof mechanisms, but proves no complexity, approximation, or algorithmic result about that problem. So there is no paper result whose continuous counterpart can be assessed under the programme’s anchor standard.

The proposed city-planning model is the best reply, and its high-multiplicity regime is plausible. But it exposes a modeling choice that the paper does not settle. If the society is nonatomic, one person’s report changes no mass, so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous. Keeping deviations of mass \(1/n\) preserves the paper’s individual incentive constraint, but makes population size and the finite mass grid part of the model. Allowing positive-mass deviations instead asks about group manipulation. Either could be a worthwhile new problem; neither is a continuous version of a computational result this paper establishes.

That leaves a narrow negative case: no qualifying anchor, and the strongest proposed mirror requires choosing a new incentive model. I would not claim this proves there is no worthwhile scenario. The city-planning extension is coherent, and the paper’s larger-instance experiments make it more plausible than the proponent suggests. The honest weakness is that I can reject the anchored case, but not rule out a valuable adjacent project.
