The continuous mirror question

Given three good categories, a finite support of complete types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), incomes \(b_t\), and rankings over bundles \(S\subseteq\{1,2,3\}\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\in[0,1]\), decide whether a CE exists and output one if it does. Find positive prices \(p_j\) and nonnegative assignment masses \(\lambda_{t,S}\) such that \(\sum_S\lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}\lambda_{t,S}=q_j\), and positive assignment to \(S\) is allowed only when \(S\) is a most-preferred bundle of type \(t\) among those affordable at prices \(p\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population represented by a finite distribution over complete types. Each person receives an integral subset of the three good categories; a type's mass may be split across such bundles. Prices are common across types, assignment masses clear the per-capita supplies, and each assigned bundle maximizes its type's preference among affordable bundles.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the three-good CE existence and computation result. It leaves aside the four-good and chores results, the fairness characterizations, and the paper's other analyses.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give an exact algorithm and running-time bound in the support size and input bit length for deciding existence and producing a CE, including price-boundary cases.
  • Determine whether an almost-everywhere income guarantee holds for fixed type masses and preference rankings.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I can defend rests on Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that with three goods, for almost every income vector and every monotonically increasing preference profile, a competitive equilibrium exists and can be found by Algorithm 1. The paper credits Babaioff et al. with the earlier existence result; its contribution here is a shorter constructive algorithm. This is an algorithmic anchor, though the paper does not state a complexity bound. With three goods, the algorithm sorts incomes and analyzes a fixed three-pick game, so under an explicit preference-table encoding its computation is polynomial in the input size. [Theorem 1 and Algorithm 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.04212) [The paper’s comparison with Babaioff et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.04212)

My lead mirror is Three-Good Type-Mass CE. Its instance is a finite set \(T\) of complete agent types, each specifying a monotone ranking over the eight bundles of three goods and an income \(b_t\). The population is given by rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and the market has rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\) of each good \(j\). The question is whether there are positive prices \(p_j\) and nonnegative masses \(\lambda_{t,S}\) assigned to bundles \(S\subseteq\{1,2,3\}\) such that
\[ \sum_S \lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:j\in S}\lambda_{t,S}=q_j, \]
and every bundle assigned positive mass to type \(t\) is a most-preferred bundle that type can afford at prices \(p\). A solution is such a price vector and assignment of mass. Each individual still receives an integral bundle; only the type-level totals can be fractional.

The regime is a large cohort of households or applicants competing for three categories of scarce in-kind goods. People with the same preferences and income share a type, so the population size \(N\) can be far larger than the support size \(\tau=|T|\). Supplies scale with the cohort: \(q_j\) is the amount of good \(j\) per unit of population. That scaling is a necessary co-modification to keep the market meaningful as the population grows.

I expect this fixed-three-good problem to be tractable in \(\tau\) and the binary input length \(L\). The relevant price thresholds are the hyperplanes \(p(S)=b_t\). With three goods, their arrangement has polynomially many cells and faces; within each, every type’s affordable and demanded bundles are fixed. A linear program can then test whether masses assigned to those bundles clear the market. This is a proposed algorithmic route for the mirror, not a result established by Theorem 1. Further questions are whether the paper’s almost-all-incomes guarantee has a type-mass analogue, and how complexity changes when the number of goods is part of the input.

The main weakness is the gap between the anchor and the mirror. The theorem varies individual incomes over a full-dimensional space; repeated complete types have equal incomes, placing those profiles on the very equality sets that its generic-income result excludes. And with only three fixed goods, the paper’s theorem does not itself give a high-multiplicity result about supplies scaled per capita. So I would claim this as a sensible new computational mirror of the paper’s equilibrium-finding problem, not as a transfer of its theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror changes the resource problem to rescue it from degenerating. The paper’s three-good theorem concerns three physical indivisible items and any number of agents. At most three agents can receive anything. If the population becomes nonatomic while those three items remain fixed, every positive-mass type receives the empty bundle; the items themselves cannot be represented as positive masses in the allocation. So there is no nontrivial type-mass version of the theorem with its original supply.

The proposed fix—giving each good category a positive per-capita supply \(q_j\)—is coherent, but it replaces the three-item partition problem with a replicated-stock market. The variables \(\lambda_{t,S}\) then split each type across integral bundles, and the question is whether those choices clear a vector of aggregate supplies. That may be a worthwhile problem, but it is not the problem Theorem 1 analyzes. The theorem’s “almost all incomes” varies the individual coordinates of a finite income vector; clones of a type have equal incomes, placing their finite approximations on equality sets the generic-income result excludes. This is a mismatch in the question being mirrored, not an objection based on what the new question’s answer might be.

There is also little complexity result in the paper to carry across. The theorem gives a constructive algorithm but no running-time bound or complexity classification; with three goods fixed, its finite-agent procedure can only allocate three items. And the proposed fixed-three-category mirror appears to have a direct route: partition three-dimensional price space by the thresholds \(p(S)=b_t\), including the arrangement’s boundary faces, then test aggregate assignment feasibility by linear programming in each region. That is a sensible computational problem, but the paper’s picking-sequence argument does not supply the hard part of it.

This defeats the claim that the type-mass model is a direct mirror of the theorem. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. Replicated supplies and mass assignments are a plausible, author-recognizable extension of indivisible fair allocation; the paper’s discussion of multiple units and apportionment gives that possibility some footing. I can make a strong case against this anchor as a mirror of the stated result, but I cannot honestly rule out that broader model as worthwhile.
