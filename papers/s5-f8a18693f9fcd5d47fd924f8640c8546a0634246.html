What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 and the compromise-rule characterization are axiomatic results; the paper contains no computational result for a continuous mirror to cover.

The case FOR (proponent)

The most credible population scenario is a tax system serving a very large population whose members fall into a small number of income types. A type could be a pre-tax income level; its mass is the fraction of the population at that income. A redistribution rule would assign post-tax income to each type, with total income conserved. That is a sensible high-multiplicity setting for the paper’s model.

But I cannot make this into the requested positive case. The paper contains no named computational result: its Theorem 1, proved here, says that a rule satisfies equal treatment of equals, additivity, and stability if and only if it is laissez-faire or full redistribution. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a result about complexity or algorithms. Turning it into a continuous characterization would continuize the axiomatic question while leaving the computational programme’s central question unanswered.

So I have no honest anchor from which to state a continuous computational problem. I could invent one—for example, optimizing a redistribution rule over a distribution of incomes—but the paper does not study that problem, and the authors might reasonably reject it as their problem. The strongest positive point is that a high-multiplicity tax population is plausible; the decisive weakness is that this paper offers no eligible computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The tax-system scenario is credible: a large population can be represented by masses of people at each income level. That establishes a sensible high-multiplicity regime, but it does not supply a computational problem to mirror.

The paper’s only named result, Theorem 1, classifies redistribution rules by axioms. Its strongest continuous analogue would classify operators on income distributions: for example, which mass-preserving, nonnegative, additive, stable operators treat equal-income mass equally. That remains an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. In the natural finite-support representation, applying either characterized rule just requires aggregating income across the \(\tau\) types and assigning the resulting amounts. The paper supplies no search, optimization, or complexity question for that calculation.

One could add a computational task by choosing a social-welfare objective, tax constraints, or a way to select among feasible redistributions. But the paper specifies none of these: it studies rules that map every income profile to an outcome, rather than choosing an optimal outcome for a given profile. Adding that choice would create a new tax-design problem, not a continuous mirror of this paper. So the negative case rests on the absence of a computational result, not on any objection to the population model.
