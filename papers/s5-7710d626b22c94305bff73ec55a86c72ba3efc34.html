The continuous mirror question

Given rational worker-type masses \(\mu_t\), job-type supplies \(\nu_s\), utilities \(u_{ts}\), and job priorities over worker types, can a polynomial-time oracle compute a finite lottery over internally stable feasible mass allocations such that every type with positive \(\mathrm{OSS}_t\) receives expected per-capita utility at least \(\mathrm{OSS}_t/(C\log(2+|T|))\), for a universal constant \(C\)? Here \(\mathrm{OSS}_t\) is the greatest utility attainable by positive mass of type \(t\) in any stable feasible allocation.

The model it lives in

An atomless market has worker types \(t\in T\) with masses \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and job types \(s\in S\) with supplies \(\nu_s\). Each type pair has utility \(u_{ts}\), and each job type ranks worker types, with members of the same type interchangeable. A support allocation is a measurable assignment of whole jobs to individuals, represented by flows \(x_{ts}\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_s x_{ts}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\leq\nu_s\); its type-\(t\) per-capita utility is \(\sum_s x_{ts}u_{ts}/\mu_t\). The output is a finite lottery over internally stable flows, with the expected utility guarantee in the mirror question.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the offline OSS-ratio objective and the guarantee in Theorem 3; it leaves incentive compatibility in Theorem 4, uncertainty results in Theorems 5–6, bandit results in Theorems 7–8, and the appendix’s job-side ratios aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a polynomial-time oracle attain an \(O(\log |T|)\) guarantee, or must the bound depend on type masses or job supplies?
  • What type-level blocking and internal-stability definitions preserve the paper’s allocation objective when cohort mass can be split across jobs?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s offline OSS-ratio problem: how fairly can a market distribute jobs when different workers can attain their best stable outcomes in different matchings? The paper contains no named NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity result. Its strongest computational anchor is Theorem 3, a polynomial-time approximation algorithm; Theorem 2 supplies the matching lower bound. Both are proved in the paper.

Take an atomless labor market with worker types \(t\in T\), of rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and job types \(s\in S\), with rational supplies \(\nu_s\). Each worker type has a utility \(u_{ts}\) for each job type; each job type has a strict priority order over worker types. The regime is a large labor market with many workers and job slots but relatively few distinct preference, skill, and priority profiles. For example, an employer might receive many applications from graduates with the same qualifications and job preferences, while many openings share the same requirements and priority rules.

An allocation is a measurable one-to-one assignment of people to job slots. Its aggregate flow \(x_{ts}\) records how much type-\(t\) worker mass receives type-\(s\) jobs. The entries of \(x\) may be fractional because a cohort can be split across jobs; each person still receives at most one whole job in any realized allocation. A lottery \(D\) over allocations gives type \(t\) expected per-capita utility \(u_D(t)=\mathbb{E}_{D}[\sum_s x_{ts}u_{ts}]/\mu_t\). Define \(\mathrm{OSS}_t\) as the best utility an individual of type \(t\) can attain in some stable allocation. The objective is to minimize \(\max_t \mathrm{OSS}_t/u_D(t)\), with infinite ratio if a type with positive OSS receives zero expected utility. For the internally stable version, every allocation in the lottery’s support must be internally stable under the paper’s blocking-pair rule. Cohort assignments can be symmetrized so workers with the same complete type get the same expected utility.

My lead question, Type-OSS Approximation Oracle, mirrors Theorem 3. Given the type masses, utilities, and job priorities, output a finite lottery over internally stable mass allocations and guarantee every type \(t\) expected utility at least \(\mathrm{OSS}_t/(C\log(2+|T|))\), for a universal constant \(C\). The requested output is the lottery itself, with each support allocation feasible and internally stable, plus the guarantee. Theorem 3 says: *for any instance, there is a distribution over internally stable matchings in which every worker receives at least a \(1/O(\log N)\) fraction of their OSS*, equivalently \(R_{\mathcal I}=O(\log N)\). The paper proves this here using Algorithm 1: it duplicates jobs \(m\) times, runs worker-proposing Gale–Shapley on the expanded market, then forms a uniform lottery over \(m\) internally stable matchings; \(m\) greater than \(1+\log_2 N\) suffices. This is the strongest anchor because it computes the object being optimized, rather than only bounding its possible value. I expect the mass version to be tractable in the number of types and input bit length, using a type-level matching procedure and continuous optimization. Whether it achieves \(O(\log |T|)\), or must also depend on small type masses, is an open part of the mirror.

A second question, Mass-OSS Value, mirrors Theorem 2. Given the same typed market, find a lottery over all feasible mass allocations minimizing the worst type’s OSS ratio; the question is whether there are instances for which every such lottery has ratio \(\Omega(\log |T|)\). Theorem 2 says: *there is an instance where every distribution over possibly non-stable matchings leaves some worker with at most a \(1/\Omega(\log N)\) fraction of their OSS*, so \(R_{\mathcal M}=\Omega(\log N)\). The paper proves this using a recursive family and a capacity argument: all workers have OSS \(1\), but any matching can deliver positive utility to at most as many workers as there are jobs. The lower bound should survive in the mass model as a structural approximation obstruction: scale each role and its associated capacity proportionally, and the same total-capacity constraint applies. This is not a computational-hardness transfer—the theorem proves a bound on achievable fairness, not NP-hardness. For arbitrary allocations, the outer lottery optimization also looks amenable to a fractional-matching LP once the OSS benchmarks are available. A prover should separately determine how the continuous model computes those benchmarks.

These questions preserve the paper’s central object: OSS relative to stable outcomes, followed by a lottery whose support may contain non-stable or internally stable allocations. The lottery is already part of the paper, but that makes this a population continuization only if the input market itself is represented by masses of worker and job types. The paper’s existing lotteries and its discussion of fractional matching are outcome-side continuity; they are not a collision with this mirror. I would not extend the mirror to Theorems 7–8: those results concern bandit observations over time, and continuing the horizon would change a different part of the model.

The regime matters. A platform or labor market with many applicants per profile and many openings per role is a plausible high-multiplicity setting; a small committee is not. A type must capture everything the problem uses, including worker utilities and job-side priorities. With that definition, the type count is not assumed to equal the headcount. The mirror covers the offline fairness results, not every contribution in the paper.

My weakest point is the paper’s strict ranking of workers by jobs. If a job’s ranking distinguishes two otherwise identical workers, that distinction belongs in their types and can make the support as large as the population. The proposed regime therefore needs priorities over worker classes, with arbitrary tie-breaking among indistinguishable people inside a class. That co-modification is plausible for markets that rank credentials or skill profiles, but it could make the authors reject the mirror if identity-level priorities materially affect stability. A related caution is that Theorem 3’s proof counts individual workers; its \(O(\log N)\) guarantee does not automatically become \(O(\log |T|)\). Establishing the right dependence on type support and type masses is exactly the substantive continuous question, not a detail to assume away.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the jobs’ strict priority over named workers. In the paper’s model, a worker’s complete type must include her position in every job’s priority order. Since each order is strict, no two workers have the same complete type: \(\tau=N\). Repeating a type at positive mass would require jobs to be indifferent among workers in that class. That changes the paper’s one-sided-ties model into one with ties on both sides, changing which pairs block and which matchings count as stable.

That change matters especially for the proposed mirror of Theorem 3. Its algorithm assigns each individual worker to one copy-indexed matching, and its proof counts distinct workers in a forest. In a priority-class model, a cohort can be split across job types within a mass allocation, while members of the same class no longer strictly outrank one another. The theorem’s proof does not carry over as stated. A new type-level stability and fairness problem may be worthwhile, but it would need its own justification; the theorem in this paper does not establish its guarantee or running time.

Theorem 2 is harder to dismiss. Its recursive construction depends on priority ranks, so retaining those ranks again gives \(\tau=N\). But with priority classes and scaled supplies, its capacity argument can plausibly survive as a bound on how much utility a lottery can deliver to all types. That would be a valid continuous result. The objection is narrower: Theorem 2 is a feasibility bound, not a complexity result by itself. It strengthens the oracle question, but does not independently supply the computational anchor the programme seeks.

So the negative case rests on a real mismatch: preserving the paper’s strict priorities leaves no repeated complete types, while making cohorts interchangeable changes the stability model that Theorems 2 and 3 analyze. A plausible labor-market story with credential classes and class-level priorities could overcome that mismatch, though. I cannot honestly rule out that the authors would recognize it as their problem, or that its type-level version would be valuable. The universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists is therefore weak; the evidence supports caution about calling this paper’s existing results continuized, not a decisive rejection of the best class-based mirror.
