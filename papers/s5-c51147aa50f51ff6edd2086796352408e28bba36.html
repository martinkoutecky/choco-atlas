The continuous mirror question

Given finite worker and employer type sets with rational masses, strict rankings, and schedule quotas, can one find a worker-optimal stable allocation? Represent each type’s population by a distribution over feasible integral schedules; require matching contract masses to balance and rule out any contract category that a positive mass of both endpoint types would accept through schedule changes under their choice maps.

The model it lives in

For worker types \(i\) and employer types \(j\), with masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\), use variables giving the mass assigned to each feasible integral schedule of each type. Require each type’s schedule masses to sum to its population mass, and require the worker and employer schedule distributions to induce equal mass on every type-pair-day contract. Stability means no positive mass can be reassigned at a contract category to schedules that the corresponding worker and employer types accept under their choice maps; select the worker-optimal stable allocation when one exists.

The objection that survived

Contract marginals alone do not determine which workers or employer units can accept a blocking contract under multi-day quotas; the mirror needs distributions over complete schedules, which the proponent gestures toward but does not fully specify.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers stable schedule existence under revealing choice maps and the worker-optimal refinement. It leaves the paper’s broader comparisons among choice-map properties and stable-set definitions, and its illustrative finite examples, outside the proposed computational question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a worker-optimal stable allocation be computed in time polynomial in the number of types, feasible schedules, and input bit length?
  • Under what conditions does a stable schedule distribution exist when each type’s per-person quotas are scaled by its mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a narrow one: the paper’s problem of finding a stable schedule, with the population represented by masses of worker and employer types. The schedule already involves days and quotas, but that outcome structure is not what makes this a continuous-population mirror. The relevant change is that workers and employer units are represented by their type masses.

The honest limitation is that the paper contains no named result classifying computational complexity: no theorem says a problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to a parameterized class. The closest anchor is Theorem 3.7, proved in this paper using a fixed-point argument: *if the worker and firm choice maps are both revealing, there exists at least one stable set of contracts.* The authors note that the theorem is mathematically equivalent to a result of Fleiner. Remark 3.8 adds that the stable sets form a complete lattice, with worker-optimal and firm-optimal stable sets, and gives an iterative algorithm for finite contract sets. That is an existence result with a constructive finite-market companion, not a runtime classification. It is the paper’s strongest anchor for a continuous mirror, but it should not be presented as a result the paper proves about polynomial time.

My lead question is Reveal-Stable Mass Schedule. Consider a finite set of worker types and employer-unit types, with rational masses over the combined, side-labelled type set. A worker type specifies a common ranking of employer-type/day contracts, acceptable contracts, and schedule quotas; an employer type specifies the corresponding information about worker-type/day contracts. A type is complete: its members share every preference and constraint used by the model. For example, many workers in a regional staffing market might share a qualification and schedule pattern, while repeated employer sites or standardized hiring units share hiring rules. The regime is plausible if there are hundreds of thousands of workers and many employer units, but only tens or hundreds of distinct types on each side. Unique personal rankings or constraints would simply create additional types.

For every contract category \(e=(i,j,d)\), let \(x_e\geq 0\) be the mass of type-\(i\) workers matched to type-\(j\) employer units on day \(d\). Each type has a choice map on vectors of offered contract masses: it accepts available mass in its preference order, subject to its overall and disjoint subgroup quotas, with each per-person quota scaled by that type’s mass. Thus, for instance, a worker type with a one-employer-per-day limit and a four-day weekly limit has aggregate caps equal to its mass and four times its mass, respectively. A type’s aggregate schedule may be fractional, while a decomposition of its mass into individual schedules gives each worker and employer unit an integral bundle of contracts.

A solution is a feasible vector \(x\) whose contract masses agree on both sides, are individually acceptable under the type-level choice maps, and admit no positive-mass blocking contract. Concretely, a category \(e\) blocks if, for some feasible \(\epsilon>0\), both endpoint types’ choice maps accept more of \(e\) when offered \(\epsilon\) additional mass alongside \(x\). This is the mass version of the paper’s revealing-choice stability condition, which for revealing maps is equivalent to individual rationality plus no blocking contract. There is no scalar objective in the underlying problem: the task is to output a stable schedule. A worker-optimal stable schedule, in the paper’s lattice sense, is a natural refinement.

I expect this constructive problem to be Class A, with an important qualification: that is a conjecture for the mirror, not a conclusion of the paper. With finite supports and explicitly ranked categories, the lifted choices are greedy, piecewise-linear operations on a finite-dimensional mass vector. That gives a plausible route to a compressed algorithm whose running time depends on the numbers of types, days, contract categories, and input bits, rather than on the number of people. Theorem 3.7 supplies the existence template; the continuous algorithm and its bit complexity would be new results.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem. Their contracts already combine worker, firm, and day; their choice maps encode preferences and schedule quotas; and their stability notion is built to accommodate these choices. The quotas scaling with type mass and the fractional aggregate schedule are necessary co-modifications of the model. They preserve integral schedules for individuals while allowing fractional type-level averages. The mirror covers the revealing-choice schedule-existence result and its worker-optimal refinement for this explicit quota family, not every possible choice map or every example in the paper.

The weakest point is precisely the algorithmic lift. The finite iteration in Remark 3.8 works over subsets of a finite contract set; Tarski’s theorem on a mass lattice gives existence but does not by itself give a terminating, polynomial-time procedure. The mirror therefore still needs to prove that the aggregate operators preserve the relevant revealing property and that a worker-optimal fixed point can be found with a finite, bit-bounded computation. Those are substantial open questions: does the construction run in \(\mathrm{poly}(\tau,L)\); how does its output relate to discrete markets with many repeated types; and can rounding a stable mass schedule preserve stability, or does that introduce a separate source of difficulty?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror is not anchored in a computational result of this paper. Theorem 3.7 proves existence by a fixed point argument; it does not classify the complexity of finding a stable schedule. Remark 3.8 supplies an iteration for finite contract sets, and Remark 7.2 explains why its set-valued sequence stabilizes. Neither gives a complexity analysis in an encoded input size, or an approximation or hardness result. So the proponent’s continuous algorithm is a new computational question inspired by an existence theorem, rather than a continuization of a computational result the paper establishes.

That objection does not make the proposed population regime implausible. Large labor markets with repeated worker and employer types are a sensible setting, and scaling per-person quotas with type mass is a reasonable co-modification. Nor does the proponent’s contract-mass vector fully settle the model: with multi-day quotas, identical contract marginals can come from different distributions of individual schedules, with different masses willing to accept a blocking contract. A more careful version could track masses of complete schedules. That raises the modeling and algorithmic cost, but it is a credible research problem.

The better version therefore survives as a possible project: encode rational type masses and schedule types, represent allocations by distributions over feasible individual schedules, and ask for a stable allocation or a worker-optimal one. The paper’s fixed point proof does not supply an algorithm for this mass-valued problem or a bit-complexity bound. But those gaps could be worthwhile to investigate; they do not show that no useful mirror exists.

So the negative case is narrow and weak against the universal claim. It is strongest if ChoCo requires a paper to contribute a named computational result to continuize: this paper’s main contribution is existence, choice-map conditions, and finite-set construction. If the programme also welcomes new computational problems built from such results, the labor-market mirror remains plausible, and I cannot honestly rule it out.
