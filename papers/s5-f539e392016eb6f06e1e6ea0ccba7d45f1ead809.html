The continuous mirror question

Given finite patient-type and doctor-type sets \(P,D\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) each summing to \(1\), and strict rankings of opposite-side types, compute an exact rational flow \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) with row and column sums \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) such that every pair \((p_i,d_j)\) satisfies \(A_{ij}=0\) or \(B_{ij}=0\), where \(A_{ij}=\sum_{j':\,d_j\succ_{p_i}d_{j'}}x_{ij'}\) and \(B_{ij}=\sum_{i':\,p_i\succ_{d_j}p_{i'}}x_{i'j}\).

The model it lives in

A society consists of patient and doctor mass distributed over finitely many complete types, with each type sharing a strict ranking of opposite-side types. The decision variables are matching flows \(x_{ij}\); each feasible flow matches all mass on both sides, and stability means no type pair has positive mass on both sides that would strictly prefer to match together. There is no optimization objective: the task is to compute a stable flow.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered result in this paper asserting a complexity classification such as membership in P or NP-hardness. The paper reports an \(O(kn^2)\) running-time bound for TOMHECs in its prose, but it is not a numbered result. The closest named computational anchor is Lemma 1, which I use in the algorithmic-output sense: it is proved here and says that, for any category \(c_i\), TOMHECs produces a stable allocation for the requesting party, whether patients or doctors. Stability means there is no unmatched patient–doctor pair who would both prefer each other to their assigned partners. The lemma establishes that outcome property; it does not establish a complexity class.

A plausible mirror is Stable Mass Matching for E-Consultation. Consider a large exchange that matches patient episodes to one-consultation doctor slots in a specialty and time period. The patients and slots fall into a modest number of complete types: all members of a patient type have the same ranking over doctor types, and all members of a doctor type have the same ranking over patient types. Those rankings, along with the relevant eligibility and service characteristics, are part of the type. Think of standardized referral cohorts and service profiles, not preferences that depend on named individuals. There may be many episodes and slots but relatively few distinct types. Normalize each side’s total mass to \(1\), as in the paper’s equal-population setting.

An instance consists of finite type sets \(P=\{p_1,\ldots,p_r\}\) and \(D=\{d_1,\ldots,d_s\}\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and strict rankings of the opposite-side types. The decision variable is a matching flow \(x_{ij}\geq 0\): the mass of patient type \(p_i\) matched to doctor type \(d_j\). It must satisfy \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}=\nu_j\). This flow represents a population of integral one-to-one matches; it does not give any patient a fractional doctor.

For each type pair \((p_i,d_j)\), let
\[ a_{ij}=\sum_{d_{j'}:\,d_j\succ_{p_i}d_{j'}}x_{ij'} \quad\text{and}\quad b_{ij}=\sum_{p_{i'}:\,p_i\succ_{d_j}p_{i'}}x_{i'j}. \]
The flow is stable if, for every pair, \(a_{ij}=0\) or \(b_{ij}=0\). Otherwise positive mass on both sides could be rematched in a way that both strictly prefer. The problem is to compute an exact rational stable flow. This is the continuous analogue of Lemma 1’s stable-allocation question.

I expect Class A: a mass version of deferred acceptance should let patient types propose down their finite rankings, with doctor types retaining their most preferred offered mass up to capacity. The algorithmic question is whether this can be implemented and proved polynomial in \(r,s\), and the mass bit length \(L\), without expanding the masses into individually named agents. The authors should recognize the mirror: it keeps their central question—whether the allocation has a mutually improving pair—and changes the representation to interchangeable cohorts. The mass flow is also a natural co-modification of one-to-one matching, not a fractional service bundle.

The mirror covers only TOMHECs’ stable-allocation guarantee, not the paper’s claims about requester optimality or strategy-proofness, and not RAMHECs. Its weakest point is that the paper’s strict rankings are over individual opponents. In this regime, members instead rank counterpart types and are indifferent among members of each type. That is sensible for standardized cohorts, but if named doctors’ reputations or patients’ individual circumstances drive rankings, the number of types could grow with the population and erase the compression. A further question is whether the continuous stable flow can also be made patient-optimal under a precise type-level preference comparison; unequal total masses and partial rankings would be natural extensions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers a thin complexity target: its running-time bound is stated in prose, while the named results establish stable, requester-optimal, and truthful outcomes. But Lemma 1 is still a computational result: TOMHECs produces a stable allocation. That gives the proponent a legitimate anchor.

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror changes the preference model. The paper ranks named patients and doctors; the mirror ranks types and treats members of each type as interchangeable. Refining types to include location, clinical profile, and other relevant characteristics could make the number of types grow with the population, removing the compression. Yet standardized referral cohorts and consultation slots with shared rankings remain plausible. That is enough to keep the mass-matching question meaningful.

There is also prior continuous work on stable matching that finds stable outcomes using an analogue of deferred acceptance.[Arnosti’s continuum model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881) makes the mirror less novel. Under ChoCo’s stated rule, though, an outside answer does not defeat a well-posed mirror, and the paper itself is still a finite-agent model.

I cannot honestly make the universal negative case. The cohort model survives the identity and multiplicity objections, and Lemma 1 supplies a computational anchor. My case against is weak; this anchor stands.
