The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive case that meets your anchor rule: the paper has no numbered result asserting a computational complexity classification.

Lemma 1, proved here, says that Bayesian–Nash gradient dynamics for games with two players and two types are never structurally stable. That is a result about the dynamics, not the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 2, attributed to Blumberg and shelat (2004), gives a sufficient Lyapunov-function condition for asymptotic stability; it is also an analytic result, not a complexity classification.

The strongest foothold for a future mirror is the paper’s automated mechanism-design search: it formulates mechanism choice as an optimization problem and demonstrates the method on a divorce-settlement example. A plausible high-multiplicity regime might have many agents sharing a small set of complete types, with type mass recording population shares. But the paper’s type distributions describe each finite player’s private uncertainty; they do not make the population continuous. Turning the work into a continuum-population mechanism-design problem would therefore be a new model, not a mirror anchored by one of the paper’s named computational results.

So I have no continuous problem to state *per anchor*. Treating the LP formulation, the worked example, or either stability result as a qualifying anchor would overstate what the paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no qualifying anchor, and that is the strongest objection to a continuous mirror here. Lemma 1 is a statement about structural stability in a finite game; Theorem 2 is a sufficient condition for asymptotic stability. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The automated mechanism design LP is the closest candidate, but presenting a formulation and solving examples is not a complexity result to continuize.

There is a deeper modeling problem with making that LP into a population mirror. In this paper, the mechanism acts on a joint profile of reports, and its incentive constraints ask what happens when a particular player deviates. That dependence on a player’s action is central to the question. If the mechanism responds only to aggregate report masses, an infinitesimal agent cannot change those masses, so the finite-player deviation that defines the Bayesian incentive constraints disappears. If instead the mechanism retains an individual’s effect on outcomes, it must keep individual reports or positive-mass agents in its input; the society’s type distribution alone no longer captures the original problem.

One can imagine useful models of anonymous mechanisms for large markets, or of stability in mean-field learning. But those would require new assumptions about how outcomes depend on reports and how an individual’s incentives work. They would pose new computational questions, rather than provide continuous mirrors of the paper’s named results. The paper’s type distributions encode private uncertainty for a fixed set of players; they are not population shares. Replicating players with identical roles might create a plausible large-market setting, but it would not preserve the two-player structural-stability result or the paper’s mechanism-design problem in any direct way.

So the negative case is strongest at the programme’s anchor test: there is no numbered computational result here to mirror, and the tempting replacements lose the individual strategic deviations on which the paper’s questions depend. I cannot rule out a worthwhile new project on continuum mechanism design. I can say that this paper gives little reason to prioritize one as its continuous mirror.
