The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror: it proves no problem is in P, NP-hard, W[1]-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest named result is Lemma 1, proved in the paper. It says that in any symmetric equilibrium of the appendix’s two-student, two-school game, there is a threshold \(v\) such that student \(a\) searches when \(\theta_{a1}\le v\) and student \(b\) searches when \(\theta_{b2}\le v\). That is an equilibrium-structure result, not a complexity result.

One could imagine a population-continuous model of school search—for example, types defined by students’ local and nonlocal match values, travel costs, and priorities, with mass representing the share of incoming students of each type. But the paper’s named result does not establish a computational question about that model, and turning it into one would go beyond the required anchors. So I can’t honestly give a per-anchor continuous problem here. The appendix’s continuously distributed match values are uncertainty over the values of two students, not a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s case concedes the key point: it names no continuous computational question. Its only anchor is Lemma 1, which characterizes search thresholds in a symmetric equilibrium of a game with two students and two schools. The paper states no complexity result, algorithmic problem, or computational objective to mirror. Its other contributions are discussion, examples, and a research agenda—not formal computational results.

A large-cohort version is easy to imagine: define student types by location, match values, and search costs, scale school capacities with the cohort, then study equilibrium or welfare under type-targeted search subsidies. But that is a new model. It must specify how information choices affect mass assignments, how priorities and ties work, and what policy decisions are feasible. In the paper’s finite game, each student’s incentive depends on the other named student’s search and on who fills a particular seat. A continuum model would recast those interactions in terms of aggregate behavior. That could be a sensible analytic or computational project, but Lemma 1 does not supply its computational question or make it a mirror of the paper.

The appendix’s continuous match values do not change this conclusion: they describe uncertainty for two students, not a continuous population. Nor does the paper’s planner calculation anchor a complexity question; it maximizes a one-dimensional welfare expression in a small example.

So the negative case is strong for this paper: the proposed anchor does not survive as a computational mirror, and the better cohort model would need a separate formulation before there is a computational problem to classify. The broader claim that *no* scenario could produce worthwhile ChoCo work is weaker. A well-specified large-district model might be worth studying; the paper simply does not provide the anchor for it.
