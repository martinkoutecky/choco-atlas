What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns the price-of-anarchy bound in §4 and its tightness result in §6.1; it leaves the paper’s consistency characterizations, incentive-compatibility result, and bounds under inconsistent advice unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a sensible continuous-population mirror here, but it is a weak fit for ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme.

The natural regime is a large hiring consortium or platform with many comparable hiring units drawing from a shared candidate pool. A unit’s type records everything the paper’s model uses about it: which advice technologies it can access, which signals are common or private, and the distributions those technologies produce. If many units share those features, the population is large while the number \(\tau\) of types is small. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: a platform can meaningfully have, say, a fraction of its hiring units using a particular mix of vendors and internal advisers.

Here is a concrete mirror. Take finitely many candidate classes \(J\), with per-capita supplies \(\nu_j\), and a finite rational distribution over shared candidate-value vectors \(x\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}^{J}\). Normalize the hiring population to mass \(1\). It is divided among \(\tau\) employer types with rational masses \(\mu_t\). Each type has a finite menu of ranking technologies, including any shared technologies and its private technologies; each technology specifies a ranking distribution conditional on \(x\). A strategy \(\sigma_t\) gives the fraction of type \(t\) using each available technology.

Give infinitesimal hiring units uniform priorities in \([0,1]\), and process them in priority order. Each takes the highest-ranked candidate class in its sampled ranking that still has supply. Shared technologies generate one common ranking sample for their users; private technologies generate independent samples. Utility is the expected value of the assigned class, and welfare is the total expected value assigned. Define equilibrium by the nonatomic condition that no type can improve its utility by moving an infinitesimal amount of mass to another available technology.

Call the resulting problem Continuum Advice-RSD Price of Anarchy: given this finite rational data, determine the ratio of maximum welfare over strategy profiles to minimum welfare over equilibria. A solution is the ratio, with profiles witnessing or approaching the two extrema. The theorem-level question closest to the paper is whether every instance with stochastically consistent ranking technologies has this ratio at most \(2\).

The supplied text omits theorem counters, so I cannot quote their numbers reliably. The lead anchor is the first theorem in §4: for Obedience-Constrained Random Serial Dictatorship with a stochastically consistent advice space, the price of anarchy is at most \(2\). It is proved in this paper. A second useful anchor is the theorem in §6.1: the bound is asymptotically tight, with a family of instances whose price of anarchy approaches \(2\). That result is also proved here. Its continuum counterpart asks whether there is a sequence of continuum instances satisfying the same consistency condition whose ratios approach \(2\).

These are strong anchors for a *welfare-bound* mirror: the first suggests the upper bound should survive, and the second gives a concrete reason to expect sharpness. The paper’s result holds for every finite population size, and its tightness construction grows with the number of firms. But neither anchor classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. The paper has no named computational-complexity result; its theorems concern equilibrium welfare, consistency conditions, and incentives. Computing exact price of anarchy from the rational continuum input would be a natural ChoCo follow-up, but it would be a new question, not a result this paper anchors. The A/B/C complexity trichotomy therefore does not apply to these two theorem mirrors.

The weakest point is the equilibrium change. A unilateral deviation by one firm can affect who gets scarce candidates in the finite game; an infinitesimal deviation cannot change aggregate allocation in the continuum game. The mirror therefore uses a Wardrop-style equilibrium, and the paper does not prove that its finite Nash equilibria converge to that equilibrium concept. Scaling candidate supply with the population is also a substantive co-modification, though a natural one for a mass-allocation model. So the positive case is that the authors could plausibly recognize the continuum welfare question as their problem in a high-multiplicity hiring regime. It is not a positive computational-complexity case under ChoCo’s stated scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that neither anchor is a computational result. The §4 theorem bounds the price of anarchy for every finite instance; it does not give an algorithm, a complexity classification, or even an encoded computational task. Its continuous counterpart could ask whether a nonatomic hiring game has the same welfare guarantee. That is a coherent question, but it remains an equilibrium-welfare theorem. To make it a ChoCo complexity problem, one must add a task such as computing the worst-equilibrium price of anarchy, then specify finite encodings for the value distribution, ranking technologies, common signals, and equilibrium. That task may be interesting, but it is newly supplied by the analyst rather than mirrored from the paper.

The proponent’s hiring-platform scenario is plausible. Repeated firms with the same technology menus form a sensible high-multiplicity population, and scaling candidate supplies is a reasonable co-modification. The difficulty is that the proposed continuum game changes the strategic object: a firm’s unilateral deviation becomes an infinitesimal deviation with no effect on aggregate allocation. The paper’s result concerns finite Nash equilibria, and it establishes no connection between those equilibria and the proposed Wardrop equilibria. That gap does not make the new question ill-posed; it makes the claimed mirror less direct.

The §6.1 tightness theorem is a second welfare result, not an algorithmic one. Its construction grows the candidate pool to \(2^n\) and relies on ranking particular candidates among a large set. A continuum version can be designed, perhaps with growing candidate classes or rescaled supplies and values, but that requires a separate construction. If it succeeds, it would support sharpness of a continuum welfare bound. It still would not supply the missing computational task.

I cannot make a strong universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The population regime is credible, and the proponent’s finite-data model is a coherent adjacent problem. My negative case supports treating this as a weak fit for ChoCo’s complexity programme: both named anchors are price-of-anarchy theorems, and turning them into computational questions requires specifying a new problem and changing the equilibrium concept. If “worthwhile” includes new computational work in nonatomic matching games, the universal negative does not hold.
