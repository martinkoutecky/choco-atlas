The continuous mirror question

Given finite worker-type and firm-type sets \(I\) and \(J\), rational masses \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_j\) of equal total, and each type's strict rankings over opposite-side types in states \(A\) and \(B\), does there exist a mass assignment \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}=\beta_j\) such that, for every state \(r\in\{A,B\}\) and type pair \((i,j)\), either \(U^r_{ij}(x)=0\) or \(V^r_{ij}(x)=0\)?

The model it lives in

A two-sided labor market with finitely many preference types and rational mass per type. The decision variable \(x_{ij}\) assigns mass of worker type \(i\) to firm type \(j\), with exact mass balance on both sides; robustness requires no type pair to have positive mass on both sides that prefers to deviate in either state.

The objection that survived

With a fixed number of changing individuals, their population share tends to zero as the market grows, so Corollary 2's polynomial-time guarantee gives no complexity prediction for positive-mass changing cohorts.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the robust-existence decision and construction question. It leaves aside the paper's lattice structure, optimal robust matchings, Birkhoff representation and enumeration, and polytope integrality.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of robust mass matching as the number of types grows?
  • Can feasibility be decided in time polynomial in the type support, improving on enumeration of the stability disjunctions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Robust Stable Mass Matching. Consider a labor market with worker types \(I\) and firm types \(J\). Each type has a positive rational mass, \(\alpha_i\) or \(\beta_j\), with \(\sum_i\alpha_i=\sum_j\beta_j=1\). A type is the pair of complete preference rankings its members use in market states \(A\) and \(B\). Each ranking is over the opposite-side *types*: members of a type are interchangeable, while preferences between types are strict. A type can therefore describe a large cohort whose preferences change together between the two states.

A solution is a mass assignment \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of type \(i\) matched with type \(j\), satisfying
\[ \sum_j x_{ij}=\alpha_i,\qquad \sum_i x_{ij}=\beta_j. \]
It must be robustly stable in both states. Precisely, for each state \(r\in\{A,B\}\) and pair \((i,j)\), define
\[ U^r_{ij}(x)=\sum_{j':\,j\succ_i^r j'}x_{ij'},\qquad V^r_{ij}(x)=\sum_{i':\,i\succ_j^r i'}x_{i'j}. \]
These are the masses on each side that would strictly prefer to match with the other type. Stability requires \(U^r_{ij}(x)=0\) or \(V^r_{ij}(x)=0\): if both are positive, some positive mass on each side can be paired to improve. Every unit of mass still receives one partner; \(x\) records how cohorts are split among partner types.

My named anchor is Corollary 2, proved in this paper from its algorithm. It says that when the number \(p+q=k\) of individual agents whose preferences change is constant, deciding whether a robust stable matching exists and finding one are polynomial-time tasks, and all such matchings can be enumerated with polynomial delay. The continuous question keeps the same underlying decision—whether one matching is stable in both states—but moves from a bounded number of exceptional people to large cohorts of interchangeable people. The paper’s full-change NP-completeness entry is credited to Miyazaki and Okamoto [MO19], rather than stated as a numbered theorem proved here, so I treat it as context rather than as a named anchor.

A natural regime is a large, repeated labor market: many applicants share a credential or experience profile, and many positions share a role profile. The number of people can be far larger than the number of types. In the two market states, all members of a type have the same pair of rankings. This is a substantive co-modification of the paper’s model: agents rank categories rather than named counterparts, with indifference among members of a category. It is a plausible high-multiplicity market, and the paper itself studies stable matching under weak or partial preferences, so this should be recognizable as a stable-matching question rather than a change to the outcome space.

I expect Robust Stable Mass Matching to be fixed-parameter tractable in the number of types, though not necessarily polynomial when that number grows. For each state and each type pair, choose which side has zero mass wanting to deviate, then impose that choice as a linear equality alongside the mass-balance constraints. There are \(2^{2|I||J|}\) such choices, and each gives a rational linear feasibility problem. Enumerating them yields a running time \(2^{O(|I||J|)}\operatorname{poly}(N)\), where \(N\) includes the input bit length. This is an inferred algorithm for the stated mass problem, not a result claimed by the paper. It gives a concrete question for whether large populations with moderate type support are tractable, while leaving open whether the dependence on support can be improved or whether hardness survives as support grows.

The mirror covers the paper’s robust-existence question. It does not claim to continuize the lattice, its enumeration order, or the paper’s polytope-integrality results. The main weakness is that the paper’s agents have strict rankings over named individuals, while this mirror has category rankings and treats any positive mutually willing mass as a blocking opportunity. Corollary 2 does not establish a limit from its finite-instance regime to this cohort model. The case rests instead on the plausibility of this high-multiplicity market and on retaining the paper’s central question: does a matching stable across preference changes exist?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The Corollary 2 anchor has a scaling problem. Its tractable regime fixes the number \(k\) of changed individuals while the market grows. Their share is \(k/n\), which tends to zero. In an atomless mass model, those exceptional individuals have no mass, so their preference changes disappear from both the allocation and any blocking test that requires positive mass. The proposed model avoids that collapse by making entire cohorts change preferences. But then the number of changed people grows with the market: it is no longer the regime Corollary 2 explains. It is a different robustness question, not a continuous version of that corollary’s parameterized result.

That mismatch does not dispose of the stronger cohort model. A market matching graduate cohorts to role types, with preferences over categories and mass split among partners, is coherent. It does change strict rankings over named individuals into indifference among members of a type. The paper itself engages with weak and partial preferences, so an author could reasonably recognize this as a stable-matching variant. Continuum matching work already models allocations as distributions over matched types, and gives existence results; that establishes the model’s legitimacy, though it does not answer the robust computational question. [Greinecker and Kah](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/ECTA16228), [Arnosti](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881).

The proposed feasibility algorithm also looks immediate: for each state and type pair, choose which side has zero mass wishing to deviate, then solve the resulting linear system. Enumerating the choices gives \(2^{O(|I||J|)}\operatorname{poly}(N)\) time. That supplies a fixed-parameter upper bound in the number of types, though it does not settle the growing-support complexity question. Under ChoCo’s criteria, an easy first algorithm is not itself a reason to reject a well-posed mirror.

So the strongest negative case is narrow: the analyst has not shown that Corollary 2’s constant-number-of-changes result has a nondegenerate continuous analogue. I cannot honestly extend that objection to every positive-mass cohort model. Those models preserve a recognizable robust-stability question, and the cited continuum literature does not already settle its computational complexity. My case against a worthwhile mirror is therefore weak beyond the specific anchor mismatch.
