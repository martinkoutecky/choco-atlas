What the mirror covers

The proposed model restates the \(p\)-ordering rule on distributional margins and covers its \(p=1\) identity with Kemeny–Young; it does not mirror a computational result or provide complexity results for the large-\(p\) correspondence or the \(p\)-spectrum.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(p>0\), what is the complexity of computing a \(p\)-ordering from a finite support of ballot types with rational masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

A natural continuous mirror is the \(p\)-ordering problem over a distribution of ballots. Given candidates \(C\) and a finite support of complete rankings \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), define the normalized pairwise margin
\[ M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl(\mathbf 1[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf 1[b\succ_t a]\bigr). \]
For a fixed \(p>0\), the task is to return a complete candidate ordering minimizing the \(p\)-norm of its violated margins:
\[ \left(\sum_{\substack{i<j\\ M_\mu(c_i,c_j)<0}} |M_\mu(c_i,c_j)|^p\right)^{1/p}. \]
The type is a ballot ranking; mass is the fraction of society with that ranking; the decision is the social ranking. A plausible regime is a large electorate with many repeated ballots and relatively few distinct ranking types. This is a faithful population model for the paper’s rule, which depends only on pairwise margins.

The nearest named result is Proposition 2, proved in the paper: at \(p=1\), minimizing the \(p\)-norm gives exactly the Kemeny–Young ordering. That makes the proposed \(p=1\) continuous problem especially direct: it asks for a Kemeny ranking of the distribution’s normalized margins. Its expected classification is hardness transfers: an explicit finite election maps to its rational ballot proportions, and multiplying all margins by the same positive factor leaves the minimizing order unchanged.

But Proposition 2 is not a computational-complexity result. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar, and it gives no computational formulation that it solves. Theorem 2 relates large-\(p\) orderings to Ranked Pairs; Proposition 9 establishes a finite \(p\)-spectrum. Neither supplies a qualifying complexity anchor. So this is a credible mirror of the paper’s *rule*, but I cannot honestly present it as an anchor-based case under your stated requirement.

The weak point is precisely that gap: the paper develops properties of \(p\)-orderings, not their computational complexity. The proposed mirror would be sensible to the authors as their rule applied to a continuous population, but its complexity question would be new work rather than a continuization of a computational result they proved.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 2 is the proponent’s best anchor, but it proves an identity: at \(p=1\), minimizing the defined score gives the Kemeny–Young ordering. For a society of ballot types with masses \(\mu_t\), the same ordering can be computed from the weighted margins \(M_\mu(a,b)\). That is a coherent model, and a large electorate with repeated ballot types is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But Proposition 2 says nothing about the complexity of finding that ordering, or about an algorithm whose performance could be carried over.

The best rescue would be to ask for the complexity of computing the \(p\)-ordering from a succinct distribution, perhaps across the whole \(p\)-spectrum. That could be worthwhile research. It would, however, add a computational question the paper does not answer. Theorem 2 and Proposition 9 do not supply better anchors: they establish a large-\(p\) correspondence and finiteness of the spectrum, respectively, not computational bounds or algorithms. Asking how fast to compute the threshold or spectrum would likewise be a new problem.

So the negative case is strong on fit with this programme’s stated target, but weak as a universal claim. The proposed population model is sensible; what fails is the claim that it mirrors a computational result of this paper. A standalone complexity study of p-orderings may still be worth pursuing.
