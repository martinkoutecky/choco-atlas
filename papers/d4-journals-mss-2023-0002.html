What the mirror covers

The proposed extension adds computation of priced assignments and effort levels; it does not computationally cover Propositions 2–4, which establish sorting equilibria and welfare comparisons.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no eligible computational anchor. Proposition 2, proved in the paper, says a top-down-sorting assignment equilibrium exists under decentralized effort choice; Proposition 3 says the same under centralized effort choice. Proposition 4 compares the welfare of top-down sorting and integration for the paper’s CRRA specification. These are equilibrium-existence and welfare results. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise classifies its complexity. I would not present any of them as the required kind of anchor.

There is still a plausible high-multiplicity setting behind the model: a large school cohort or membership organization divided among a fixed, small number of groups, with many people sharing each of a modest number \(\tau\) of complete types. A type could be a member’s public-good valuation \(\xi\), since the paper holds the other relevant primitives fixed. Mass would be the fraction of the population of each type assigned to each group; group averages determine contributions, and posted fees govern membership choices. That is a natural population mirror, not continuity of the outcome space. The paper’s assumption that one member has negligible effect on a group’s average type makes the limit especially plausible.

An unanchored computational question suggested by that model would be: given rational type masses, group capacities, and the paper’s payoff primitives, find prices, a mass assignment, and group effort levels forming a priced-group equilibrium. The assignment must respect capacities; each type may receive mass only in groups giving it maximal utility net of the posted price; each group’s effort must solve its prescribed contribution problem; and a group that is not full must have zero price. The paper’s sorting results suggest this may be tractable in the single-crossing regime, by ordering types and computing mass cutoffs and fees. That is a conjecture about a new problem, not a complexity result established by this paper.

So the positive case is that the paper’s economic model admits a credible continuous-population formulation. Its weak point is decisive for the requested verdict: the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor the mirror, and it does not analyze the complexity of computing the equilibria it proves exist.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper gives ChoCo no computational result to mirror. Propositions 2 and 3 establish sorting equilibria; Proposition 4 compares welfare under sorting and integration. None defines an algorithmic problem or studies its complexity. Treating those propositions themselves as anchors would turn analytical equilibrium and welfare theorems into computational results they are not.

That is not because a continuous population is implausible. A large school cohort or membership organization with many people of each valuation type is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Nor does mass lose information the paper needs: its results depend on types and group averages, not named individuals. In fact, the paper already does much of the analytical aggregation: effort and utility are expressed through group composition, and the sorting arguments use how utility changes with average type. Those features make a continuum natural, but they do not supply the missing computational question.

The strongest repair is the proponent’s proposal: given rational type masses, capacities, and explicit payoff functions, find masses assigned to groups, prices, and effort levels that form an equilibrium. That can be made into a well-posed computational problem. But it adds an algorithmic task to the model rather than mirroring a problem the paper formulates. Entrepreneurs have no specified objective or strategic equilibrium condition for their prices; the paper defines assignment equilibrium given posted prices. One could study feasibility of assignments and prices under a chosen encoding, but that is a new equilibrium-computation project, not a complexity question raised by Propositions 2 or 3.

The same distinction applies to Proposition 4. A planner’s search over all mass partitions could be an interesting extension, but the paper compares top-down sorting with integration under its CRRA assumptions; it does not formulate an algorithmic optimization problem over arbitrary partitions. Calling that optimization a mirror would make the anchor a newly added problem rather than the paper’s welfare result.

So the negative case is mainly about fit and provenance, not a fundamental obstacle to continuization. I would not claim that no sensible continuous population model exists, or that high multiplicity is inappropriate here. A computational equilibrium project may be worthwhile under a broader algorithmic-game-theory remit. The case against is that this paper itself offers no computational result to anchor it, and the best proposed repair is a new project built on its economic model rather than a continuous mirror of a computational social-choice result.
