What the mirror covers

The proposed extension covers Proposition 4's optimal contract for each type; it leaves Proposition 1's Pareto test and Propositions 5–8's comparative statics outside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result about computational complexity: none says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its propositions are analytic results about bargaining, optimization, and comparative statics. The closest to a computational anchor is Proposition 4, proved in the paper: under Assumption 1, the optimal proportional insurance contract is unique. It satisfies the first-order conditions (3.6) when \(P_+(1)\le P_-(1)\) or condition (3.7) holds; otherwise it is full coverage, \((1,\hat P)\), with \(\hat P\) solving (3.8). This characterizes the optimizer, but gives no running-time bound or computational input model. I would not present it as a qualifying anchor.

The strongest positive mirror I can make is a population of many bilateral insurance negotiations. Call it Continuum Nash Insurance Bargaining. An instance has a finite support \(T\) of complete bargaining-pair types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one. A type records both parties’ wealth and utility functions, bargaining power \(\delta_t\), deadweight cost \(\tau_t\), and the joint law of their loss \(X_t\) and the insurer’s background wealth \(W_t\). Each unit of mass is a matched insured–insurer relationship with that same type. For type \(t\), choose a proportional contract \((\theta_t,P_t)\in[0,1]\times[0,M_t]\), with indemnity \(\theta_tX_t\), subject to both parties’ participation constraints:
\[ \Delta U_t = \mathbb{E}\!\left[u_t(w_t-X_t+\theta_tX_t-P_t)\right] -\mathbb{E}\!\left[u_t(w_t-X_t)\right]\ge 0, \]
\[ \Delta V_t = \mathbb{E}\!\left[v_t(W_t+P_t-(1+\tau_t)\theta_tX_t)\right] -\mathbb{E}\!\left[v_t(W_t)\right]\ge 0. \]
The question is to find feasible contracts maximizing the population-weighted Nash objective
\[ \Phi=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\bigl((1-\delta_t)\log\Delta U_t+\delta_t\log\Delta V_t\bigr), \]
with strictly positive gains, as ensured typewise by Assumption 1. The solution is a contract for each supported type. For a precise approximation problem, one could encode each type’s loss/background-risk law by finitely many rational atoms, use rational quadratic utilities valid on the relevant wealth ranges, and ask for a feasible rational contract vector with objective at least \(\mathrm{OPT}-\varepsilon\).

A plausible regime is a large insurance pool or mutual market with many repeated bargaining relationships but relatively few standardized combinations of risk class, wealth, insurer background risk, and bargaining power: \(n\) relationships with \(n\gg\tau\). Mass then means the fraction of relationships of each complete type. The paper’s random loss variable alone is not the continuization; the population of bargaining relationships is.

This is a credible extension because each type’s local decision is exactly the paper’s proportional-insurance Nash bargaining problem. Under an explicit, efficiently evaluable representation of the inputs, the log objective and participation constraints give separable concave optimization, so an approximation algorithm looks like a Class A possibility. That expectation depends on the encoding and does not follow as a complexity theorem from Proposition 4. The mirror covers the paper’s central optimal-contract result, but supplies no hardness result whose disappearance could be studied.

The soft spot is substantial: without shared capital, capacity, or portfolio constraints, the type subproblems separate, and changing the masses does not change any type’s contract. This is a faithful high-multiplicity replication, but it may offer little beyond compressing repeated instances. Adding a common insurer portfolio constraint could make population mass computationally consequential, but would require a further model change that Proposition 4 does not analyze.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is decisive at the paper level: there is no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–8 characterize Pareto efficiency, the Nash contract, and comparative statics. Proposition 4 gives a unique optimizer and conditions for full coverage, but it states no complexity result or algorithmic bound. The paper leaves no discrete computational problem whose high-multiplicity version could reveal a complexity change.

The proponent’s replicated bargaining pairs are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, so type compression itself is no objection. But under their formulation, each type chooses its own contract subject to its own participation constraints. Writing its log Nash objective as \(f_t(\theta_t,P_t)\), the population objective is
\[ \Phi=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t f_t(\theta_t,P_t). \]
The feasible set is a product across types, so maximizing \(\Phi\) simply means solving each type’s original two-variable bargaining problem separately. Every positive \(\mu_t\) is only a scale factor: the masses do not affect the contracts or create trade-offs between types. It is a batch of Proposition 4 instances, not a population relaxation that changes the computational problem.

The same issue defeats a stronger mirror of Proposition 1. Pareto efficiency for the population requires checking each supported type’s own marginal condition: a profitable Pareto improvement for any positive-mass type defeats efficiency. Averaging the conditions across types would instead permit gains for some participants to mask losses for others. The paper’s comparative statics likewise apply type by type. In the faithful pairwise model, the population adds bookkeeping, not a new algorithmic object.

There is a plausible way to make masses matter: give insurers shared capital or portfolio constraints, or let one insurer negotiate a menu of contracts with a continuum of insureds. But that changes the bargaining problem. One must specify how the insurer’s single gain is compared with many insureds’ gains, how portfolio risk and dependence enter, and how the joint contract choice is negotiated. The paper supplies no population-level bargaining rule for those choices. Such a model could be worthwhile insurance research, but it would be a new constrained portfolio or collective bargaining problem, rather than a continuous mirror of the results this paper proves. Choosing a finite input encoding would make its computational questions studyable; it would not provide the missing connection to a discrete complexity result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed mirror as a ChoCo complexity contribution: the faithful version separates into independent instances, while a coupled version changes the bargaining object. I cannot make the universal claim airtight, though. A researcher could develop a convincing shared-insurer model that the authors recognize as a natural extension; the paper alone does not rule that out.
