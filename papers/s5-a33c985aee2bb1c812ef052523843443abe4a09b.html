The continuous mirror question

Given buyer types \(T\) with masses \(\mu_t>0\), valuation vectors \(a_t\), per-capita budgets \(B_t\), and per-capita bids \(\beta_t\), choose a common aggregate bid \(z\) for a designated cohort \(t_0\), with \(z_j\ge 0\) and \(\sum_j z_j=\mu_{t_0}B_{t_0}\), while holding other cohorts fixed. Let \(\theta_j=\sum_{t\ne t_0}\mu_t\beta_{tj}\). Compute the \(z\) maximizing \(\sum_j z_j\ln(a_{t_0j})+\sum_j(\theta_j+z_j)(1-\ln(\theta_j+z_j))\), using the convention \(0\ln 0=0\).

The model it lives in

A finite distribution over buyer types, where a designated type cohort of mass \(\mu_{t_0}\) acts as one coordinated bloc with aggregate budget \(\mu_{t_0}B_{t_0}\). Its decision variable is the aggregate bid vector \(z\); opposing types contribute aggregate bids \(\theta\). The bloc maximizes its associated potential against those fixed bids.

The objection that survived

The cohort's action is not the independent best response of each member, and maximizing the associated potential is not the same as maximizing the sum of members' associated utilities; the proposed mirror therefore depends on treating the cohort as one coordinated strategic bloc.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow: Proposition 1, proved in this paper, gives a computational anchor for a continuous type-cohort version of the associated-game best-response problem. It says that for every buyer \(i\) and every fixed bid profile \(b_{-i}\) of the other buyers, the best response maximizing that buyer’s associated utility \(\tilde u_i\) is unique and can be computed in polynomial time. The appendix gives a sorting algorithm taking \(O(m\log m)\) operations. This is the paper’s clearest named complexity result; its main convergence theorems are important, but they are not complexity classifications.

A plausible regime is a large market in which many buyers fall into a small number of standardized purchasing classes. For example, many local offices might share the same values for goods, per-capita budget, and deployed bidding policy. Let class \(t\) have population mass \(\mu_t\), valuation vector \(a_t\), per-capita budget \(B_t\), and current per-capita bids \(\beta_t\). The number of buyers may be very large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct classes is modest. The type includes the current bid state, since that state matters to the response calculation. Suppose a platform can update a whole class under one common policy.

Call the resulting problem Continuous Type-Cohort Associated Best Response. Given the class masses and states, choose the new common bid for a target class \(t_0\), leaving the other classes fixed. Write its aggregate bid as \(z_j=\mu_{t_0}\beta_j\), and the other classes’ aggregate bids on good \(j\) as
\[ \theta_j=\sum_{t\ne t_0}\mu_t\beta_{tj}. \]
The target must satisfy \(z_j\ge 0\) and \(\sum_j z_j=\mu_{t_0}B_{t_0}\). Its prices are \(p_j(z)=\theta_j+z_j\). The question is to compute the unique \(z\) maximizing
\[ \Psi_{t_0}(z) = \sum_j z_j\ln(a_{t_0j}) + \sum_j p_j(z)\bigl(1-\ln p_j(z)\bigr), \]
with the usual \(0\ln 0=0\) convention. The solution is the maximizing aggregate bid vector; dividing by \(\mu_{t_0}\) gives the per-capita bid.

This is a natural type-level response in the paper’s associated potential game. The mass-scaled action \(z\) has total budget \(\mu_{t_0}B_{t_0}\), and the objective has exactly the form of the paper’s associated utility for one buyer facing fixed aggregate opposing bids. The cohort’s joint action internalizes its effect on prices. That is the co-modification that makes this a meaningful population mirror: class mass changes both the budget and the price contribution.

The mirror should be tractable (Class A). Aggregate the opponents’ bids to form \(\theta\), sort goods by \(a_{t_0j}/\theta_j\) (handling \(\theta_j=0\) separately), and examine prefixes \(S\). For each prefix compute
\[ c_S=\frac{\sum_{j\in S}a_{t_0j}} {\mu_{t_0}B_{t_0}+\sum_{j\in S}\theta_j}. \]
Choose a maximizing prefix and set \(z_j=(a_{t_0j}/c_S-\theta_j)_+\). With binary-rational masses, bids, and valuations, the aggregation and sorting are polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). The paper’s Proposition 1 supplies the structural basis for this algorithm; the mirror asks whether the same response computation remains efficient when the opposing market is supplied as a type distribution.

This case covers Proposition 1’s best-response computation only. It does not claim a continuous version of Theorem 1’s adversarial-schedule convergence result, nor does the paper offer a hardness result to anchor a discrete-to-continuous hardness question. Useful follow-ups are whether the same type compression works when members of a class update independently, and whether adversarially scheduled updates of whole cohorts converge.

The weak point is that a cohort-level action is coordinated: Proposition 1 is stated for one buyer’s unilateral best response, while the mirror lets a positive mass move together. The shared potential and the matching optimization form make that extension credible, but it is still a co-modification of the strategic model. If the authors require every buyer to act independently, this anchor does not establish the corresponding continuum result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the only computational anchor: it computes one identified buyer’s best response to fixed bids from everyone else. The strongest objection is that this computation depends on buyers being atomic. A buyer’s bid enters the price \(p_j=\theta_{ij}+b_{ij}\), so changing it changes the price the buyer faces. In an atomless population, an individual has zero mass and cannot change the aggregate price. The associated-game best response then loses the price-impact term that gives Proposition 1 its water-filling structure. It becomes a price-taking choice, rather than the same computation over a continuous society.

The proposed cohort model restores that price impact by giving a whole mass \(\mu\) one common action. But that changes who the strategic actor is. With per-capita bids \(\beta\) and aggregate bids \(z=\mu\beta\), the proposed objective \(\Psi\) is the market potential as the cohort moves. It is not the sum of its members’ associated utilities: multiplying a member’s utility by \(\mu\) gives a price term weighted by \(\mu\), whereas \(\Psi\) weights that term by \(1\). Alternatively, treating the cohort as a single representative buyer with budget \(\mu B\) makes \(\Psi\) that buyer’s associated utility—but concentrates the cohort’s budget and price impact in one strategic player. Neither interpretation is the independent-buyer high-multiplicity version of the paper’s best response.

A platform that deliberately coordinates a standardized class might make the cohort action sensible. That is the best version of the mirror, and the paper’s discussion of simultaneous subset updates makes it conceivable. But it is a class-level control problem introduced to preserve atomic price impact; the paper’s Proposition 1 does not establish that this is the problem its authors would recognize. And if the members instead act independently, a type defined by current bids can split as members update at different times. Keeping the type intact then requires a coordinated update rule, while allowing it to split changes the type distribution and removes the one-buyer response being mirrored.

So the negative case is strongest against the claimed direct mirror: its continuum either removes the strategic effect Proposition 1 computes, or restores it by changing the unit of agency. It is weaker against every possible scenario. A market platform coordinating a large standardized cohort is plausible, and its potential-improving action may be worth studying. I cannot honestly rule out that mirror universally; the case against it depends on whether ChoCo regards that coordinated control problem as a worthwhile population-computation question.
