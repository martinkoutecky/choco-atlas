The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchor-based case under your rules: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. The closest named results are Theorem 2, which says that a monotonic bipolar set relation is complete, transitive, satisfies preferential independence, and is an unbiased refinement of \(\succeq_{\mathrm{BiPoss}}\) exactly when it is \(\succeq_{\mathrm{Lexi}}\); and Proposition 9, which represents \(\succeq_{\mathrm{Lexi}}\) by comparing \(\sigma^+(A^+)-\sigma^-(A^-)\), for two capacities, and represents \(\succeq_{\mathrm{BiLexi}}\) by the corresponding two-sided comparisons. Those are representation results, not results about the complexity of a problem.

The paper’s conclusion does give two computational claims, but neither is a numbered result: comparing two alternatives takes \(O(|X|)\), while optimization over combinatorial alternatives has an NP-complete decision problem, attributed to Fargier and Wilson (2007). That second claim is the strongest possible hook for a continuous mirror, but it is not an eligible anchor from this paper.

The plausible mirror would be Lexi Mass Optimization. Let feasible alternatives be \(\mathcal F=\{a\in\{0,1\}^d:\Phi(a)=1\}\), with \(\Phi\) given succinctly, and let \(\lambda_1>\cdots>\lambda_q\) be the finite salience levels. A type \(t\) describes one argument contribution: its feature \(j_t\), salience level \(\ell_t\), and polarity \(\epsilon_t\in\{+,-\}\). The input gives a binary-rational distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) supported types, summing to one. Interpret \(\mu_t\) as the fraction of a large pool of contributors who share that argument type. For each alternative \(a\), define \(P_i(a)\) as the mass of positive arguments at level \(\lambda_i\) whose feature is present in \(a\), and \(N_i(a)\) as the corresponding negative mass. The question is to return a feasible \(a\) maximizing \((P_1(a)-N_1(a),\ldots,P_q(a)-N_q(a))\) lexicographically; a decision version asks whether some feasible alternative’s vector is at least a given rational threshold vector. A solution is an optimal feasible alternative, or a yes/no answer to the threshold question.

A credible regime is a very large feedback population contributing coded reasons about products or plans drawn from a finite feature catalog, with common salience levels and recurring feature-and-polarity types: the contributor count is large while \(\tau\) stays moderate. This preserves the paper’s levelwise counting in \(\succeq_{\mathrm{Lexi}}\), replacing integer argument counts with rational masses. If the paper’s cited hardness claim is accepted as the anchor, I would expect hardness to transfer: discrete multiplicities can be represented by normalized masses \(n_t/n\). This also raises questions about which feasible-set encodings admit efficient lexicographic optimization, and how the optimum changes under perturbations to \(\mu\).

The weak point is the interpretation. The paper studies a decision-maker comparing alternatives from their arguments; the mirror treats arguments as contributions from a population. The authors might accept this as the same Lexi comparison over replicated argument types, but the population enters through the provenance of the arguments rather than through a problem about collective choice. So this is a plausible continuous problem, not a positive case resting on a qualifying named result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the proponent already identifies: the paper has no numbered computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 characterizes when a preference relation is \(\succeq_{\mathrm{Lexi}}\); Proposition 9 represents that relation using two capacities. Both are results about preference representation, not algorithmic complexity. The conclusion’s \(O(|X|)\) comparison claim is unnumbered, and its NP-completeness claim concerns optimization over combinatorial alternatives, is attributed to Fargier and Wilson (2007), and has no population parameter. Under the stated anchor rule, none qualifies.

Theorem 2 is the stronger representational anchor, but its object is one decision-maker’s relation over sets of arguments. Its \(X\) contains criteria, not people; its levels rank arguments, not voter types. Treating criteria as a continuous measure would continuize the argument space. To make a population the continuous object, one must add people and specify how their views produce a collective choice.

Proposition 9 gives a natural route for trying that: replace counts of equal-strength arguments with rational masses. The proponent’s Lexi Mass Optimization does this, but its type is one argument contribution—feature, level, and polarity—not a complete description of an agent. A more faithful population model would give each type a full bipolar profile over alternatives, including its salience information, and put a distribution \(\mu\) over those profiles. One could then aggregate argument masses and select an alternative. That is a plausible new model, but the collective aggregation and optimization problem are additions: Theorem 2 and Proposition 9 only say how an individual relation is represented. They supply no computational claim about the resulting electorate.

A continuous population of evaluators using Lexi could still motivate a worthwhile follow-on project. That is the negative case’s weak point: I cannot rule out the authors recognizing such an application. But it does not rescue the paper as a mirror of a named computational result. It requires a new population model and a new collective-choice question, while the paper’s eligible anchors remain axiomatic representation results.
