The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is to treat the basin’s producers as a high-multiplicity population behind the two regional bargaining parties. A type could specify a producer’s region, crop or livestock, water source, and production technology; mass would be that type’s share of the relevant production population. Given rational type masses, water constraints, disagreement payoffs, and the paper’s production functions, ask for a feasible water allocation and side-payment that yields the regions’ Nash bargaining solution—or, separately, their Kalai–Smorodinsky solution. A solution would report the allocation, transfer, and regional payoffs. This keeps the paper’s central question—how joint investments and transfers change the gains from cooperation—while making producer populations the continuous object.

That is a plausible extension: the basin has many producers, and aggregating like producers into types could be sensible. Its weak point is that the paper models two regions as the bargaining agents; it does not model producers as strategic agents or specify their type-level population. The proposed version would need to show that producer-level masses are a faithful way to derive the regional payoff functions.

There is no eligible anchor in the paper as supplied. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result such as tractability or hardness. Table 2 reports calibrated NBS and KS payoffs, including results with side-payments, but those numerical findings are not named computational-complexity results. So the producer-population question is a possible research extension, not a mirror anchored to a named result of this paper; assigning it a class in the programme’s trichotomy would overstate what the paper establishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. It gives a calibrated two-region bargaining model and numerical comparisons of Nash and Kalai–Smorodinsky solutions, but no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its central claims concern how investments and side-payments change welfare. Those are meaningful results, but they are not a complexity landscape with an algorithmic question for ChoCo to mirror.

The proposed producer-population version does not supply that missing anchor. In the paper, the bargaining agents are the two regions; producers do not bargain, receive allocations, or have separately modeled utilities. The model already maps regional water use to regional payoffs. If producer types merely aggregate into those same payoff functions, their masses add no new computational object. If producers instead become agents whose water allocations or transfers matter individually, the proposal needs new preferences, constraints, and bargaining rules. That could be worthwhile research, but it would be a new model rather than a continuous mirror of a result here.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a ChoCo mirror: none of its reported results is an eligible computational anchor, and the proposed alternative either reproduces the existing regional model or changes the problem. The broader claim that no scenario could ever make a continuous-population study worthwhile is harder to defend; a new producer-level model might be convincing. The paper itself gives little basis for saying that such a study would be a mirror of its computational results.
