The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered computational result that can anchor a continuous mirror under your criteria. Its named results are economic existence and comparative statics, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest results are Proposition 1, which proves that under linear inverse demand the core is nonempty for all sufficiently large \(n\), and Proposition 2, which proves that—under weakly concave inverse demand—a first-order dominant distribution over the number of outsider coalitions yields a core containing the core under the dominated distribution. Corollary 1 extends the large-\(n\) nonemptiness result to distributions that dominate the paper’s benchmark. These are meaningful mathematical results, but they do not analyze the complexity of deciding core nonemptiness, finding a core allocation, or computing coalition values. The introduction’s reference to NP-hard coalition-structure generation is attributed to Sandholm et al.; it is neither a numbered result proved in this paper nor the paper’s own problem.

There is a plausible setting for a related continuous model: a large market of firms sharing a small number of cost and technology types. Type mass could represent each type’s share of productive firms; a deviating coalition could choose its mass and aggregate output, with value based on a distribution over partitions of outsider mass. The paper’s identical-firm model and large-\(n\) analysis make that scenario credible. But defining the continuum of outsider partitions and coalition worths would require substantial new modeling, and there is no eligible named computational result to mirror. So I cannot give a lead anchor or a per-anchor continuous problem without inventing one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that none of the proposed anchors is a computational result. Proposition 1 proves eventual core non-emptiness; Proposition 2 compares cores under stochastic dominance; Corollary 1 extends the first existence claim. They establish economic properties, not the complexity of deciding or computing anything. Recasting one as “decide whether the core is nonempty” would attach a new algorithmic question to the paper, rather than mirror a computational result it contains.

The suggested large market of firms with a few types is plausible as a setting, but it does not fix that mismatch. In the paper, coalition worth depends on the *number* \(j\) of outsider coalitions, and the benchmark probability of that count includes the number \(K_{n-s,j}\) of partitions of individually identified firms. In a continuous population, type masses do not determine either quantity. Preserving the count-based model requires adding a law for how outsider mass forms coalitions; replacing it with a law over mass partitions requires changing the model’s uncertainty and worth construction. Either could motivate a new paper, but neither supplies a continuous version of these propositions’ claims.

The better computational reframing—asking for core non-emptiness or a core allocation in a continuum market—also has a weak connection to this paper’s results. Its firms are symmetric, and its core test already reduces to comparing per-firm worth across coalition sizes. Its main work is proving inequalities about equilibrium profits, not overcoming a computational bottleneck. Heterogeneous firm types might create a more substantial optimization problem, but that adds the structure that would generate the computational question; it does not reveal a hidden computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong for the stated task: the proponent identifies no eligible anchor, and the plausible continuous setting they offer leads either to another analytic core-existence question or to a newly specified continuum coalition model. That does not show no interesting continuous oligopoly model could be built; it shows why this paper supplies little basis for a worthwhile *computational mirror*.
