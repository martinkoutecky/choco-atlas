The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying named computational result: it states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about complexity or algorithms. Its integer program in §3.1 is unnumbered, and the table’s NP-completeness statement is attributed to earlier work. So I cannot honestly give an anchor-linked prover question under your rules.

The strongest positive case I can make is for a fluid version of the paper’s per-round deceased-donor-initiated chain allocation model. I would call it Mass-DDIC Clearing. This is a candidate mirror of the unnumbered model, not a qualifying anchor.

A type would be a complete graph-node profile: deceased donor, pair registered only for KEP, pair registered for both KEP and the wait-list, or wait-list recipient, with all compatibility and scoring attributes used by the model. In the paper’s simulation regime, the profiles can be specialized to blood-group categories and unit edge weights. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the registry population of type \(t\). The plausible regime is a large regional or national registry with many people but relatively few repeated profiles—most convincingly, the blood-group-only regime the paper simulates. Each chain still gives every participating patient a whole transplant; only the aggregate number of chains of each type is fractional.

Let \(q\) range over feasible, simultaneous, deceased-donor-initiated chain patterns of at most \(k\) transplant edges, beginning at a DD and ending at a wait-list recipient. Let \(z_q\ge 0\) be the mass assigned to pattern \(q\), \(a_{tq}\) the number of type-\(t\) units it uses, and \(w_e\) the paper’s weight for edge \(e\). One precise problem is:

\[ \max_{z\ge 0}\ \sum_q z_q\sum_{e\in q}w_e \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad \sum_q a_{tq}z_q\le \mu_t\quad\text{for every type }t. \]

A pair registered in both systems is one resource in these capacity constraints, so the same recipient cannot be counted twice. The DD’s other kidney can remain allocated directly to the wait-list, as in the paper’s fairness proposal. The question is to compute the optimum and an optimal chain-mass allocation.

This seems recognizable to the authors: it keeps their simultaneous chains, compatibility edges, edge weights, chain-length bound, and treatment of registry overlap. It continuizes the population, not the kidney or the transplant outcome. For fixed \(k\), including the paper’s \(k=2\) simulation, the typed chain patterns can be enumerated in polynomially many cases in the support size \(\tau\), and the resulting problem is a rational LP. I would expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). It covers the per-round allocation core, not the paper’s long-term arrival, dropout, and waiting-time simulation.

Further questions follow naturally: how closely can an optimal mass allocation be rounded to individual chains, with loss bounded in terms of \(\tau\) and \(k\)? What happens when \(k\) is variable? Can the simulated multi-round system be represented by a useful fluid model of arrivals, waiting lists, and dropouts?

The weakest point is that the paper’s richer edge scores use attributes such as HLA mismatch, age, waiting time, and rank. If those attributes distinguish nearly every patient, then complete types may be nearly as numerous as patients, and the hoped-for population compression largely disappears. The positive case is strongest for the paper’s blood-group and unit-weight simulation regime; extending it to the richer model would need a convincing repeated-type regime and a rounding analysis.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is decisive under the required anchor standard: the paper states no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about an algorithm or complexity. Its integer program is unnumbered, its long-run findings come from simulation, and the NP-completeness claim is attributed to earlier work. The proponent acknowledges this. Mass-DDIC Clearing is therefore a proposed new problem, not a mirror of a qualifying result in this paper.

The best version of that proposal is still coherent. In the paper’s blood-group-only, unit-weight regime, with fixed chain length \(k=2\), there are few types; one can enumerate chain patterns and solve a rational LP. But this clean tractability depends on a very compressed model. To preserve the actual compatibility and scoring information, a type must capture attributes such as immunological compatibility, age, waiting time, rank, and registry overlap. Those profiles can make the support size \(\tau\) approach the number of patients, eliminating the hoped-for compression. Coarsening to blood groups preserves the simulation’s simplifying assumptions while discarding much of the patient-specific structure of the allocation problem. The resulting LP may be a useful baseline, but it says little about the paper’s long-run waiting-time and dropout results.

Rounding fractional chain allocations, allowing variable \(k\), or building a multi-round fluid model could each lead to worthwhile work. The multi-round version would need to model arrival and dropout dynamics and track waiting cohorts. That is a substantial new population model; it does not supply the missing computational result in this paper.
