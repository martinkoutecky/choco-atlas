The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible mirror is narrow: computing the outbreak threshold from a population’s degree and exposure distributions. The chapter has no numbered theorem that explicitly classifies a problem as being in P or NP-hard. Theorem 6 is the best anchor because its exact threshold characterization gives a direct algorithm for a well-defined decision problem.

Theorem 6 considers the SIS mean-field model with effective spreading rate \(\lambda=\nu/\delta\), where \(D\) is the maximum degree, \(\nu\) the infection parameter and \(\delta\) the recovery probability. Under non-monophilic contagion, the update is \(x_{n+1}(k)=x_n(k)+\frac{1}{M}\big((1-x_n(k))\nu k\theta_n^X/D-x_n(k)\delta\big)\), with \(\theta_n^X=\sum_k P(k)x_n(k)\); a positive stationary solution exists if and only if \(\lambda>D/\mathbb{E}[d(X)]=\lambda_X^*\). Under monophilic contagion, the same update uses \(\theta_n^Z=\sum_k q_Z(k)x_n(k)\), where \(q_Z\) is the degree distribution seen by a random friend of a random node; a positive stationary solution exists if and only if \(\lambda>D/\mathbb{E}[d(Z)]=\lambda_Z^*\). The chapter says the proof is in reference [69], so this result is cited from elsewhere, not proved here.

A continuous mirror of that result is Continuous SIS Threshold Decision. An instance gives a finite set \(\mathcal K\) of positive-mass degree types, rational masses \(p_k\) summing to one, \(D\), rational \(\nu,\delta\) with \(\delta>0\), and a contagion mode. For the monophilic mode, it also gives the induced exposure distribution \(q_Z\), or the degree-mixing data needed to compute it. A type is \((k,s)\): degree \(k\) and current infection state \(s\in\{0,1\}\). Agents of a type share the same SIS parameters; their mass is \(p_kx(k)\) when infected and \(p_k(1-x(k))\) when susceptible. The problem asks for the exact critical rate and whether a positive stationary infection fraction exists at the supplied rate. The answer is \(\lambda_X^*=D/\sum_k kp_k\) or \(\lambda_Z^*=D/\sum_k kq_Z(k)\), and “yes” exactly when \(\nu/\delta\) exceeds that threshold. There is no optimization objective here: the solution is the exact threshold and decision.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for a large online community or other network with many members sharing a small number of degree and response types: millions of members, perhaps dozens of occupied degree classes, and shared infection and recovery parameters. The mean-field state \(x(k)\) is the infected fraction within each class. For fixed listed degree data, computing the threshold is Class A: a scan over the \(\tau=|\mathcal K|\) types suffices, with polynomial bit complexity in the rational input length. The mirror covers only the threshold result in Theorem 6, not the chapter’s polling estimators, Bayesian filter, or reactive-network dynamics.

The authors would likely recognize this as their SIS threshold question: the degree-class population state and the two contagion rules are central to their analysis. Further questions include how the threshold changes when the exposure distribution is uncertain, and how to allocate a limited intervention budget across degree types to prevent a positive stationary infection fraction.

The weak point is substantial. Theorem 6 is an analytical mean-field result, not a complexity theorem; the polynomial-time conclusion is a straightforward consequence of its formula. The chapter already works with degree-class fractions and mean-field dynamics, so the continuous model is close to its existing one. This is a credible, tightly scoped computational reframing, but it is thin evidence for a broader continuization programme and does not reveal a rich complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6 is the only anchor here, and it is an analytic characterization, not a complexity result. Its continuous model already uses degree-class fractions \(x(k)\) and a degree distribution \(P(k)\). For the proposed input, the theorem gives the answer directly: compute \(D/\sum_k k p_k\), or \(D/\sum_k k q_Z(k)\), and compare it with \(\nu/\delta\). That takes a scan over the listed types. More importantly, the chapter has already supplied the exact input-output rule the proposed “Continuous SIS Threshold Decision” asks for. Calling its evaluation a computational problem adds a decision-problem wrapper; it leaves no threshold question for a continuous mirror to resolve.

The proposed high-multiplicity setting is plausible: a large network can have many members in each degree class, and the positive-fraction threshold does not vanish in the limit. I would not reject the mirror on those grounds. The difficulty is that, under Theorem 6’s assumptions, the population description and the threshold calculation are already present in the paper. If the mirror adds degree correlations, the needed exposure distribution or mixing data can be supplied and summed over. If it asks for intervention allocation, it must add actions, costs, and their effects on transmission—ingredients the chapter does not model. That may be worthwhile epidemic-control research, but it is a new problem rather than a mirror of this result.

The other substantial results do not supply a stronger computational anchor: the reactive-network theorem gives a limiting ODE with a stationary-distribution constraint, while the polling results establish estimator and error properties. Turning either into optimization over types would again require a new objective or decision problem. An election-campaign version would need preferences, candidates, and a winner condition absent from the paper’s SIS model.

So the negative case is narrow. The paper describes a sensible continuous population regime, and it may motivate new computational work in epidemic control. But for the anchor raised, the continuous threshold is already explicit, and more ambitious computational variants require adding the problem the chapter never posed. That makes this a poor fit for a programme seeking new computational complexity questions in social choice.
