The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) goods and a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) buyer types, each with rational mass \(\rho_t>0\), budget \(B_t>0\), and valuation vector \(v_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\rho_t=1\), compute prices \(p\) for a Fisher market with unit per-capita supply and type-level average bundles clearing supply. For linear or quasi-linear utilities under the paper's nondegeneracy assumptions, require the log-price vector \(z_j=\log p_j\) to satisfy \(F_\mu(z)-\min_z F_\mu(z)\le\varepsilon\), where \(F_\mu(z)=\sum_{j=1}^m e^{z_j}-\sum_{t\in T}\rho_tB_t\max_{j\in[m]}(\log v_{tj}-z_j)\) for linear utilities, and the maximum includes an outside option of value zero for quasi-linear utilities. Determine the iteration and bit complexity in \(m,\tau\), the input bit length, and \(\varepsilon\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population with distribution \(\mu\) over complete buyer types \(t=(v_t,B_t)\), finite support size \(\tau\), and unit per-capita supply of each divisible good. Each type has an average bundle \(x_t\), with market clearing \(\sum_t\rho_t x_{tj}=1\); the price decision is \(z_j=\log p_j\), minimizing the mass-weighted Fisher objective \(F_\mu(z)\). The goods remain divisible, while the population is the object represented continuously.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the APM price guarantee in Theorem 4.1 and the adaptive exact-price guarantee in Theorem 5.2; the type-weighted objective also supports the approximate-allocation result and optimality test. It does not establish a polynomial bit-complexity bound for exact recovery, and the paper gives no hardness result to mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can \(\Delta^*\) be bounded from the rational type-distribution input to obtain a bit-complexity bound for exact CE recovery?
  • How do equilibrium prices and allocations change when a finite market's type proportions approximate \(\mu\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is a type-compressed Fisher market. Take a large population of buyers and describe each buyer by their complete market type \(t=(v_t,B_t)\): their valuation vector over the \(m\) goods and their budget. Let \(\rho_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\), with support size \(\tau\). Normalize the market so that mass is one and each good has unit supply. This is the proportional-growth version of the paper’s market: if the finite market grows, supplies grow with it, and the normalized type masses remain fixed.

The scenario I find credible is a large resource-allocation market represented by many copies of a relatively small set of buyer profiles—for example, repeated household or campaign templates with the same valuations and budget. The regime is \(N\) buyers but only \(\tau\ll N\) distinct types. Exact sameness is a modeling assumption, so this is strongest for markets built from repeated profiles, not arbitrary buyers with individualized valuations.

For prices \(p\), a type’s agents choose affordable utility-maximizing bundles, and the mass-weighted bundles must clear each good’s supply. With type-level average bundles \(x_{tj}\), the market-clearing condition is \(\sum_t\rho_t x_{tj}=1\). Averages work even when identical agents split across different optimal bundles: their average remains affordable and optimal. The optimization variable in the paper’s price method is \(z_j=\log p_j\). Its objective compresses exactly by replacing each buyer’s budget weight with \(\rho_t B_t\). For linear utilities, for example, it is
\[ F(z)=\sum_{j=1}^m e^{z_j} -\sum_t\rho_t B_t\log\!\left(\max_j\frac{v_{tj}}{e^{z_j}}\right). \]
The quasi-linear version uses the paper’s outside option as well. Thus the mirror keeps the price-adjustment problem intact while replacing a long list of buyers with a distribution over complete buyer types.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that APM finds an \(\varepsilon\)-CE price vector in at most \(\widetilde O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})\) iterations. Here “\(\varepsilon\)-CE” means the paper’s objective-gap criterion: for \(z_j=\log p_j\), \(F(z)-\inf F\le\varepsilon\). The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is:

Type-Distribution Approximate Fisher Price Computation. The input is \(m\), a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types, rational masses \(\rho_t>0\) summing to one, rational budgets \(B_t>0\), rational valuation vectors \(v_t\), a choice of linear or quasi-linear utility, and \(\varepsilon>0\). Assume the paper’s nondegeneracy conditions. Output prices \(p\) whose log-price vector has objective gap at most \(\varepsilon\). Count arithmetic iterations in \(m,\tau\), and state the precision and input-size dependence.

The expected outcome is tractable in the paper’s iteration model. Substituting \(\rho_tB_t\) for \(B_i\) in the objective and gradient makes each APM iteration depend on the support types, so its work is \(O(m\tau)\), rather than \(O(mN)\). This is the clearest continuous mirror because the objective and the quantity being computed are unchanged in substance; only the representation of repeated agents changes. The authors should recognize it as their price-adjustment problem with identical buyers aggregated. I would not call it a bit-polynomial-time result: the theorem is an iteration bound, and its arithmetic and precision costs need separate accounting.

A second worthwhile anchor is Theorem 5.2, also proved here. It says adaptive APM finds exact CE prices in finitely many iterations, with a bound expressed using the smallest bang-per-buck gap \(\Delta^*\) at equilibrium. It motivates a separate problem:

Type-Distribution Exact Fisher Price Computation. Use the same input as above. Output an exact CE price vector for the type-distribution market, with a type-level allocation certifying affordability, optimality, and market clearing. The algorithm may use adaptive APM and the paper’s optimality test.

I expect this to be finite-step solvable, by applying the paper’s adaptive method to the mass-weighted type objective. But the theorem does not establish a polynomial bound in the input length: the step bound depends on \(\Delta^*\), and the paper leaves a conditional bound on that gap as future work. Natural follow-up questions are whether \(\Delta^*\) can be bounded from the rational input data, what bit complexity exact recovery requires, and how well a finite market approximates the type-distribution prices and allocations.

The paper gives no named hardness result to anchor a hardness-transfer question. These two anchors therefore make a positive case for its algorithmic contributions, not for a broader claim that continuous Fisher markets eliminate hardness. The weakest point is the instance regime: the result is compelling when the market really has many repeated full types. If buyers’ valuation vectors and budgets are mostly unique, then \(\tau\) approaches \(N\), and the continuous representation offers little computational compression.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the population axis barely enters this paper’s mathematics. Buyers contribute additively to an objective over the \(m\) prices; their individual identities and allocation histories are irrelevant. The algorithms’ real structure is the geometry of price space and the demand ties at a price vector. For generic markets, complete types \((v,B)\) are nearly all distinct, so replacing buyers by a distribution gives no compression. Approximate grouping would require robustness bounds the paper does not provide.

That makes the ChoCo contribution look narrow: where the support is small, the continuous formulation is exact aggregation of repeated buyers. The paper’s experiments, including the movie-rating market, do not establish that such low-support markets are typical. This is a fair reason to expect limited reach. It does not, however, defeat the proposed mirror under the programme’s stated standard: instance regimes matter, and a market built from repeated buyer profiles is plausible.

Theorem 4.1 survives in that regime. For type masses \(\rho_t\), its objective becomes
\[ F(z)=\sum_j e^{z_j}-\sum_t \rho_t B_t\log\!\left(\max_j\frac{v_{tj}}{e^{z_j}}\right), \]
with the paper’s outside option in the quasi-linear case. The same iteration then costs \(O(m\tau)\). Calling this a direct aggregation rather than new continuous theory may weaken its novelty, but it does not make the computational question ill-posed or unworthwhile: the promised reduction from population size \(N\) to support size \(\tau\) is precisely the high-multiplicity gain.

Theorem 5.2 survives by the same aggregation. Each type has one demand set, weighted by its aggregate budget \(\rho_tB_t\); the recovery oracle and optimality test can work over those types. Its finite-step guarantee depends on \(\Delta^*\), and it does not establish a polynomial bound in input bit length. Those are limits on the strength of the result, not reasons to reject the exact-price mirror.

I cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case. The strongest objection is that both mirrors may be easy extensions whose payoff is confined to markets with few repeated complete buyer profiles. But the paper has named computational results, and the repeated-profile regime is a sensible population-continuous model. The proponent’s Theorem 4.1 anchor alone survives that objection; Theorem 5.2 does too.
