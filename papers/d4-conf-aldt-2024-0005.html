What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a case with a qualifying anchor: the paper has no numbered result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest results are all proved in this paper, and they concern strategic guarantees for tournament rules:

  • Theorem 1 says the \(d\)-ary randomized single-elimination rule is Condorcet-consistent, monotone, and \(k\)-strongly non-manipulable with \(\alpha_{d,k}\le 1-\frac{2(d)_k}{d^{k+1}}\), for \(2\le k\le d\).
  • Theorem 2 says SIGNIFICANTONLY is Condorcet-consistent, monotone, \(2\)-SNM-\(1/3\), and \(3\)-SNM-\(1/2\), with tightness claimed.
  • Theorem 3 says that a top-cycle-consistent, \(k\)-SNM-\(\alpha\) rule for \(n\) teams can be extended to one for \(n'>n\) teams with \(\alpha'\le\alpha(1-\frac{(k-1)^2}{n})+\frac{(k-1)^2}{n}\).

These are results about rule design and manipulability. The paper’s discussion of an LP for optimal rules cites prior work; it gives no numbered complexity result that could serve as an anchor here.

A continuous team-population model is conceivable, but it would be a weak fit for these theorems. A type would need to capture a team’s match behavior across the other teams. In arbitrary tournaments, that behavior can require nearly as many distinct types as teams. Also, a coalition of at most \(k\) teams has vanishing mass as \(n\) grows. Giving coalitions positive mass would change the strategic guarantee the theorems establish. Theorem 3 scales the number of discrete teams, but does not give a high-multiplicity model.

So there is no per-anchor prover question to state under the requested standard. A mass-based tournament-rule design problem could be proposed as new research, but the paper’s results do not anchor it as a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 bounds how much a coalition can improve its chance of winning under RdSEB; Theorem 2 gives strategic guarantees for SIGNIFICANTONLY; Theorem 3 extends a rule from \(n\) teams to more teams. These are results about rule properties and design, not complexity or algorithms for a computational problem. The LP for optimal rule design is discussed as prior work, and the tournament-fixing problems appear only in related work. Neither supplies a result of this paper to mirror.

The best population-based reinterpretation still has a basic mismatch. In these theorems, teams are the vertices of an arbitrary tournament: their identities and pairwise match outcomes define the input. A team type that preserves all information relevant to manipulation would have to preserve its relationships to the other teams. In general those relationships distinguish teams, so grouping many teams into a small set of types loses the tournament the rule acts on. Giving type pairs match probabilities or outcomes introduces a different, stochastic or graphon-style input model.

That mismatch is especially clear in each candidate theorem. For Theorem 1, a fixed coalition of at most \(k\) teams has zero mass in an atomless limit, so its manipulation guarantee disappears. Scaling the coalition with the population would pose a different question. For Theorem 2, the rule’s “significant” objects are particular MW pairs and triples; they have zero mass in such a limit. Giving positive mass to coalitions requires replacing the theorem’s finite structural classification with a new one. Theorem 3 scales the number of teams—the alternatives in the tournament—rather than a high-multiplicity society. Its most direct limit therefore continuizes the wrong axis; a fixed coalition again vanishes if teams are treated as a population.

One could formulate a new model of continuum teams, positive-mass coalitions, and winner distributions over team identities. That may be mathematically interesting, but it would need its own input representation, strategic notion, and computational questions. It would not be a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. The negative case is strongest on that narrower point; it cannot rule out the value of inventing such a new tournament model.
