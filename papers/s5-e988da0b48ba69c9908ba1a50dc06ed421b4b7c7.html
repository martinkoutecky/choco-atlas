What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers complete-ranking aggregation over a distribution of ranking types. It does not mirror the paper’s heuristic design, unnumbered runtime analysis, partial-ranking experiments, or label-ranking application as computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying named anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Its closest claim is the unnumbered statement in the Introduction that rank aggregation is NP-hard with four rankings, cited to Bartholdi et al. (1989). The paper reports experiments and algorithmic improvements, but those do not meet the requested anchor rule.

That unnumbered claim points to a plausible mirror, though it cannot serve as a qualifying anchor here. Let each type \(t\) be a complete ranking of the \(m\) labels, and let \(\mu_t\) be its rational share of a large electorate. Given the supported types and their masses, find a complete ranking \(\pi\) minimizing
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\, d(\pi,t), \]
where \(d\) is Kendall distance. This is the high-multiplicity form of the paper’s complete-ranking aggregation problem: repeated voters with the same ranking are represented by their mass. A plausible regime is a large electorate with many repeated ballots and relatively few distinct rankings. The four-ranking hardness claim suggests hardness should transfer, since equal masses reproduce the four-ranking instance; the obstacle lies in the number of labels, not the number of voters.

That is the strongest positive case I can make from the text, but it rests on an unnumbered result cited from elsewhere. Under your stated anchor requirement, the paper has no anchor on which to base the requested continuous-problem deliverable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is an anchoring objection. The paper proves no numbered result about the complexity of rank aggregation. Its NP-hardness sentence is an introductory claim attributed to Bartholdi et al.; the paper’s own contribution is a heuristic and empirical comparisons. Section 3.7 gives an unnumbered runtime analysis of HER, but no complexity classification or guarantee for the aggregation problem. So the proposed continuous Kemeny question is not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

That objection does not defeat the stronger mirror itself. Given ranking types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), minimizing \(\sum_t \mu_t d'(\pi,t)\) is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of the paper’s aggregation objective. Large recommendation or survey populations with repeated ranking patterns are plausible settings, and the ranking is a complete type for this objective. The objective can be compressed to pairwise weights, yielding weighted Kemeny / linear ordering. That makes the mirror close to established weighted formulations, but closeness is not a valid objection here: a Class B result or an exact identity can still be worthwhile, and high-multiplicity evidence supports the regime.

So I can reject the proponent’s *paper-result anchor* under the stated rule, but I cannot honestly defeat their continuous Kemeny problem as an unanchored extension.
