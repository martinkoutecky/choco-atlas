The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(O\) of benefit types and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over patient types, where each type \(t\) specifies a finite π-net \(\mathcal N_t\), an explicitly listed finite context \(M_t\), and benefit multisets \(A_t,B_t\subseteq M_t\) induced by the same two treatment options, determine whether \(\sum_{t:\,A_t>_{\mathcal N_t}^{M_t}B_t}\mu_t\ge q\), where \(A_t>_{\mathcal N_t}^{M_t}B_t\) means a worsening-flip sequence confined to \(M_t\).

The model it lives in

A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete patient-preference types. Each type carries its preference net, its finite benefit context, and the benefits induced by the common treatment options; bundles remain integral, while the population mass is continuous.

The objection that survived

The paper studies personalized preferences for an individual decision, while majority aggregation across patients adds a collective decision rule it neither studies nor endorses.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 4’s confined multiset-dominance reasoning as used for treatment-evidence comparisons; it leaves unrestricted unbounded π-net reasoning and the paper’s other preference-encoding results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the complexity bounds in the support size \(\tau\), net size, context size, and binary input length?
  • How do restrictions on the preference nets change the complexity of computing the mass that favors a treatment?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I can defend is a population version of the paper’s confined multiset-dominance question, using its treatment-evidence example. It does add a social aggregation step, so it is a weaker mirror than a direct continuization of the paper’s single decision-maker problem.

The lead problem is Continuous Majority Confined Dominance. An instance has a finite set \(O\) of benefit types and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) supported patient types. A type \(t\) completely specifies a finite \(\pci\)-net \(\mathcal N_t\), two treatment-benefit multisets \(A_t,B_t\), and an explicitly listed finite evaluation context \(M_t\) containing both. Each patient in that type has the same preferences and treatment comparison. Define \(A_t >_{\mathcal N_t}^{M_t} B_t\) when there is a worsening-flip sequence from \(A_t\) to \(B_t\) whose multisets all lie within \(M_t\). Given a rational threshold \(q\), ask whether

\[ \sum_{t:\,A_t >_{\mathcal N_t}^{M_t} B_t}\mu_t \ge q. \]

A solution gives the answer and the exact mass on the left. Bundles and individual patients remain integral; only the population is continuous.

A plausible regime is a large health system comparing treatments across many patients, with a relatively small catalogue of complete preference-and-evidence profiles. The type must include every feature relevant to the comparison. Millions of patients but dozens of exact profiles is a meaningful high-multiplicity regime; if profiles are mostly unique, this model offers little compression.

The closest named computational anchor is Corollary 4, proved in this paper. It states that for a satisfiable \(\mci\)-net \(\mathcal N_M\) on a finite multiset \(M\), dominance between any \(M_a,M_b\subseteq M\) holds exactly when dominance holds between their canonical encodings \(\overline{M_a},\overline{M_b}\) in the corresponding CI-net on indexed copies of the objects. Proposition 4 proves the flip-sequence correspondence underlying this equivalence, and the paper computes with the translation in its examples. This gives an exact finite reasoning route for each supported patient type. It is not a complexity-class result, though. The paper has no numbered theorem or proposition classifying the central problem as polynomial-time, hard, or parameterized; its PSPACE-completeness statements about CI-net dominance appear as background prose citing earlier work.

For the proposed population problem, I expect hardness to transfer. The paper reports that dominance in satisfiable CI-nets is PSPACE-complete, and CI-nets are a special case of the paper’s multiset framework. With one supported type of mass \(1\) and \(q=1\), the proposed question is precisely an individual dominance query. With an explicitly listed context, the paper’s bounded-context translations give PSPACE membership by reducing each supported query to finite CI-net reasoning and summing the rational masses; the one-type case supplies PSPACE-hardness. Thus the aggregate problem is expected to be PSPACE-complete, with the difficulty coming from preference reasoning rather than population size.

This covers the paper’s computational treatment of confined preference dominance, especially its use to compare treatment-benefit multisets. It does not continuize the entire \(\pci\)-net framework or the paper’s unbounded reasoning claims. Further questions include whether the indexed-copy expansion can be avoided when multiplicities are large, and how the complexity changes with restrictions on preference nets or on the number of supported types.

The weakest point is that the paper studies an individual’s preference relation, while this mirror asks for the share of patients whose relation favors one treatment. The paper’s personalized treatment-evidence setting makes a patient population a credible use case, but majority aggregation is an added social-choice layer. Also, under a strict reading that requires a numbered complexity-class result as the anchor, this paper supplies no qualifying anchor; Corollary 4 supports the exact computational bridge, not a complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that none of the paper’s numbered results classifies the complexity of one of its own computational problems. The PSPACE-completeness statements concern CI-nets in the background and are attributed to prior work; the conclusion says complexity questions for \(\pci\)-nets remain to be explored. Corollary 4 is a formal result, but it proves an encoding equivalence for confined dominance, not a complexity bound or algorithmic classification. So it is a poor anchor for a programme whose central question is how continuization changes computational complexity.

Corollary 4’s multiset \(M\) is a bound on the *goods being compared*. Its construction replaces each copy of a good with an indexed object, then preserves dominance between the resulting finite sets. It contains no population of decision makers. The proposed Continuous Majority Confined Dominance problem adds patients around that single-agent reasoning task: it answers each type’s query, then sums the masses of types for which the answer is yes. As stated, types can even have different queries and contexts, so this is not a collective choice between common treatments. It is a population statistic built on top of the paper’s relation.

A stronger version could give every patient the same treatment options and aggregate their preferences over those options by majority. A finite catalogue of complete preference profiles could make that a sensible high-multiplicity regime; I would not reject it on the ground that patients are individualized. But the common agenda and aggregation rule would supply a new social-choice problem that the paper neither studies nor proves a result about. Conversely, making the paper’s multiset of benefits itself continuous would continuize the outcome space, not the population.

So I do not think the proponent has established a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. The honest limit of the negative case is that a population-level clinical decision problem could be worthwhile on its own. The case against is strongest as a rejection of this paper as its anchor, not as proof that no valuable population model could be built around the clinical application.
