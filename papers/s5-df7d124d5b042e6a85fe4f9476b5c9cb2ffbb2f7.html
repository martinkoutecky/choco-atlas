The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for one narrow question: evaluating the large-population limit of Freixas’s \(F\)-rule for a candidate. The paper has no numbered result that classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, or another complexity class. Its best computationally relevant named anchor is Proposition 3, proved in the paper: a sequence of OWAs satisfies invariant average reduction—replacing an ordered vector \(x\) by \(y_i=(x_i+x_{i+1})/2\) leaves the aggregate unchanged, so \(G_n(x)=G_{n-1}(y)\)—if and only if it is the sequence of \(F_n\)-rules, with weights \(q_i^n=2^{-(n-1)}\binom{n-1}{i-1}\). The paper uses this result to give a recursive calculation using \(n(n-1)/2\) pairwise averages. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a formal complexity theorem, and I would not present it as one.

My lead mirror is the Continuum \(F\)-Limit Evaluation problem. A type is a grade assigned to one candidate from a fixed finite grade set \(\Gamma\); \(\mu_g\) is the fraction of evaluators assigning grade \(g\). The instance is the positive-mass grades and their binary-rational masses, with total mass \(1\). Write \(F_\mu(g)=\sum_{h\le g}\mu_h\), and define
\[ \ell=\min\{g:F_\mu(g)\ge \tfrac12\},\qquad u=\min\{g:F_\mu(g)>\tfrac12\}. \]
The task is to output the candidate’s limiting \(F\)-score, \((\ell+u)/2\). Thus, if there is a unique median grade, the answer is that grade; if exactly half the mass lies below a gap in the grades, the answer is the midpoint of the two grades bordering the gap.

This is the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s actual aggregation problem. For rational \(\mu\), replicate the grade profile to sizes \(n\) that realize its proportions exactly. Since \(q_i^n\) is the probability that \(i=1+B\) for \(B\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n-1,\tfrac12)\), \(F_n\) averages the grade at a rank concentrated around the middle of the ordered profile. As \(n\) grows, the aggregate converges to the value above; when the median falls in a gap, the symmetric weights split evenly across its two sides. The mirror’s solution is therefore an exact rational score, computable by sorting the support and summing masses in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length.

There is a plausible regime in the paper itself: online ratings of a movie or restaurant, where many users assign scores from a small fixed scale. A type is the score, mass is the share of users giving it, and the number of users can be enormous compared with the number of score types. That is a setting the paper explicitly discusses. This mirror covers evaluation of one candidate under the \(F\)-rule; applying it candidate by candidate also gives a limiting ranking. I expect this problem to be Class A, since its output reduces to finding the central grade or grades. It raises further questions about finite-population convergence rates, especially when mass is close to half at a grade boundary, and about conditions under which the limiting score accurately predicts the finite-\(n\) \(F\)-score.

The weakest point is substantial: the limit is median-like, while the paper presents \(F\) partly as an alternative to the median, citing the median’s ties and weaker discrimination. The authors might accept this as the large-population limit of their aggregation problem, but might resist calling it a faithful continuous form of the rule they propose. Also, Proposition 3 gives a recursive characterization rather than a named complexity classification. So this is a credible, deliberately limited algorithmic mirror—not evidence that the paper’s main contribution is a complexity result, or that the continuum preserves the finite-panel advantages of \(F\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper does not establish a computational result for Proposition 3 to mirror. Proposition 3 characterizes the \(F\)-rule axiomatically: it is the unique sequence of OWAs satisfying invariant average reduction. The pairwise-averaging calculation in Section 5.4 is a way to compute the rule, but the paper does not study a search, decision, or optimization problem or analyze its complexity. Calling the proposition an “algorithmic anchor” does not change what it proves.

The proposed ratings example is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, and the limit question is well formed. But it asks for the limiting value of the rule from a distribution of grades; it does not continuize Proposition 3’s characterization. That result concerns a relationship between finite panels of successive sizes. The proposed task instead studies what the already-defined aggregator returns on a population distribution. Its answer may be useful, but it does not extend the paper’s named theorem or expose a computational problem the paper leaves open.

The strongest broader version would represent each judge by their vector of grades across candidates and ask for the resulting ranking. That still asks only for evaluation of the specified rule. To get a problem involving intervention—such as finding the least mass of altered grades needed to change the winner—we would have to add a cost model and permitted changes. Those could make a worthwhile ChoCo project, but they are new choices of problem, not continuizations of a computational result in this paper. The paper’s brief discussion of manipulation does not formulate or analyze such a task.

I would not argue that the population regime is implausible, or that a simple answer makes a question unworthy. The negative case is instead about fit: the paper offers an aggregation rule and its properties, not the kind of computational problem the continuization programme is designed to classify. That case is strong for Proposition 3 as the claimed anchor, but not airtight against someone who counts continuous evaluation of the rule itself as a worthwhile mirror.
