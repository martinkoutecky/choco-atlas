The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. It proves no named result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity.

The closest named results are Proposition 4, which characterizes a subgame-perfect equilibrium of the five-stage minority-voting process, and Proposition 7, which gives a condition for simple majority voting to be strictly preferable to minority voting from an ex ante social-welfare perspective. Both are economic equilibrium and welfare results, not computational-complexity results, so neither can honestly serve as an anchor under the requested criteria.

A plausible population mirror could use a distribution \(\mu\) over project-valuation types, with mass representing population shares, and ask for the equilibrium project decision and financing scheme. But that would recast the paper’s analytic mechanism question; it would not mirror a computational result the paper establishes. The paper’s ex ante uncertainty over individual valuations is also not itself a continuous-population model. Thus there is a conceivable continuous analogue, but no anchored positive case of the kind requested.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a worthwhile ChoCo mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Proposition 4 characterizes equilibrium in a finite game; Proposition 7 compares welfare under two voting rules. Neither classifies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. That matters here because the paper’s contribution is an economic mechanism and its equilibrium properties, not a computational task whose complexity changes when the population becomes a distribution.

The best mirror of Proposition 4 would give project valuations as a finite-support distribution and compute the equilibrium outcome and financing. In this one-project model, that means sorting the \(\tau\) valuation types, finding the relevant majority threshold, and checking the paper’s financing inequalities. It is a direct calculation in \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) time, followed by arithmetic on the input rationals. Proposition 7 similarly becomes an aggregate welfare comparison: replace sums over citizens by weighted sums over types, or by integrals for a specified density. The paper’s suggested optimization of the tax cap would add a one-dimensional problem. These are legitimate continuous economic questions, but they do not expose the optimization or pricing bottleneck that makes a computational mirror valuable. With a density supplied in some exotic encoding, integration might be hard; the difficulty would come from that encoding, which the paper does not motivate.

There is also a real limit to preserving the paper’s mechanism in an atomless society. Its first-stage logic depends on individual pivotality: supporting citizens must be pivotal, and the finite voter who becomes agenda setter can gain a private benefit. In a continuum, one person has zero voting mass, so their vote cannot change whether the project wins. A random agenda setter can be drawn from the population measure, but no particular person has positive probability of selection, so the paper’s application and voting incentives no longer follow from its finite-population game. Replacing individuals with voting blocs of positive mass could restore pivotality, but it changes the strategic players and the mechanism. Even if the authors accepted a mass-based version, its equilibrium and welfare questions would still reduce to threshold and integral calculations.

The agenda setter’s redistribution makes that limit especially awkward. For example, the pure-redistribution equilibrium gives one person a subsidy proportional to \(N\), while that person is selected with probability \(1/N\). The finite model’s expected payoff can retain a nonzero contribution from this rare, large transfer. An ordinary continuum welfare integral over the population loses the transfer at a measure-zero recipient; preserving it requires separately modeling the officeholder’s lottery and payoff. That is possible, but it is extra structure without a corresponding computational challenge in this paper.

The honest limitation of this negative case is that richer extensions—multiple projects, additional constraints, or more complex types—could produce worthwhile computational problems. They would need to introduce the hard structure themselves. As presented, this paper offers a natural high-multiplicity *economic* model, but no anchored complexity question; its best continuous versions remain analytic and straightforward to compute.
