The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying anchored case for a continuous computational mirror. The paper’s numbered results are existence and stability claims, not computational results: none classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or otherwise, and none gives a computational formulation that the paper uses to compute a solution.

Propositions 6–8 come closest: they give constructive procedures for selecting stable coalitional networks under component additivity—respectively with component-wise egalitarian allocation and simple majority, majoritarian allocation and simple majority, or dictatorial allocation and unanimity. But each procedure selects maxima over connected subnetworks; the paper gives no complexity bound. Calling one a tractability result would overstate what is proved.

A plausible setting for a future model might be an industry with many firms sharing a small number of payoff and link-cost types. But a continuous version would need to represent links, connectivity, and coalition consent across a continuum of agents. The paper provides no computational problem or named result for that version to mirror. So there is no honest lead anchor, no per-anchor problem to hand a prover, and no expected complexity class to assign.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case names no continuous mirror to defeat: it expressly says the paper has no computational anchor. The strongest candidates are Propositions 6–8, whose procedures select contractually stable networks under particular allocation and consent rules. But they select maxima over connected subcoalitional networks without specifying an input representation or giving a running-time bound. With an arbitrary partition value function \(v\), “compute a maximizer” has no useful complexity classification until we know how \(v\) is represented. The other propositions establish existence or discuss stability and efficiency; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Turning one of these into a question about finding a stable network would be interesting in its own right, but it would not mirror a computational result this paper proves.

The best proposed setting is a large industry with many firms sharing a small number of payoff and link-cost types. That makes repeated firm types plausible, but a distribution over those types does not specify the object the paper studies. The same masses of firm types can be arranged into different networks and coalitions, with different paths, components, payoffs, and consent constraints. To preserve those features, a continuum model would need to represent relationships as well as types—for example, through a graph limit and a measurable coalition structure. Sparse links may disappear in a dense graph limit; finite coalitions may have zero mass; and scaling payoffs or consent rules to avoid those problems would require substantive modeling choices. Those choices might yield a worthwhile new network-game problem, but the paper supplies no computational result to anchor it, and a type distribution alone cannot recover its stability question.

That is the strongest negative case, not a proof that no useful continuum model could ever be built. A structured payoff family and a carefully chosen relational limit might support computational questions the authors would recognize. The weakness is that this would start from a new representation of network structure, rather than continuizing a computational problem or result actually present in the paper.
