The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest case with a qualifying anchor: the paper contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a parameterized complexity classification.

The closest results are Theorems 3.1–3.3, proved in the paper as topological existence theorems. For example, Theorem 3.1 says that when \(r=p^\nu\), closed, covering, equivariant preferences on the cake-cut-and-allocation configuration space admit a cooperative envy-free division. That is not a result about computing such a division or the complexity of doing so.

There is also a scope mismatch: the paper’s \(r\) players are finite, while the cake and the space of possible cuts are continuous. That is continuity in the commodity and outcome space, not in the population. One could invent a computational search problem for finding a division, but the paper gives neither a computational result to anchor it nor an input representation for its arbitrary closed preference sets. Presenting that as a mirror of the paper’s results would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case raises no mirror anchor: it names Theorem 3.1 only to explain why it does not qualify. The strongest case against a mirror is the paper’s lack of a computational result. Theorems 3.1–3.3 are existence theorems for arbitrary closed, covering, equivariant preference sets. They give no input encoding, algorithm, or complexity claim. In particular, “find a division” has no defined running time when the preferences themselves may be arbitrary closed sets. Choosing a finite representation and studying search complexity could be worthwhile, but that would add the computational problem the paper never formulates.

The best rescue is to repeat a finite set of cooperative preference types across a growing population. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but it does not carry over the theorem’s central object unchanged: a bijection between \(r\) distinct players and \(r\) distinct plates. With a fixed number of types, aggregation naturally replaces that bijection with a mass-transport assignment; with one identity per player, the number of types can grow with \(r\). Giving plates positive capacity in the limit requires another model change. Those changes may define good research questions, but the resulting existence or complexity claim is not a continuization of a computational result in this paper.

The same issue affects the dragon theorems: their conclusions are finite tree and incidence statements over named players and plates. A mass version would need a new notion of those structures and a new theorem. The chocolate example suggests plausible applications, but a computational model for its preferences and a continuous-population matching problem would have to be supplied from outside the paper.

So the negative case is strongest as a judgment about this paper’s fit for the programme: its results are topological existence claims, and the computational, continuous-population problem would have to be built largely from scratch. That does not prove no useful extension exists; a finite-type mass version might be worth studying. It does make the universal claim—that no such version could ever be worthwhile—weak.
