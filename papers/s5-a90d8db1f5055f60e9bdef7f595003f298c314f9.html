The continuous mirror question

Given \(d\), \(b\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) over a finite support of survey records \(t=(S_t,\mathcal G_t)\), with separator positions \(p_{t,a}\) and rank-breaking edges \(E_{t,a}\), compute an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal \(\widehat\theta\in\Omega_b\) maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{a=1}^{\ell_t}\frac{1}{\kappa_t-p_{t,a}}\sum_{(i,i')\in E_{t,a}}\left[\theta_{i'}-\log\left(e^{\theta_i}+e^{\theta_{i'}}\right)\right]\).

The model it lives in

Types are query designs together with reported partial rankings; each type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t\). The decision is the shared utility vector \(\theta\in\Omega_b\), and the objective is the mass-weighted rank-breaking log-likelihood. The PL model describes how reports arise around the shared utility vector.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the rank-breaking estimator and its general separator and weight guarantee. It leaves the exact MLE for arbitrary posets, the Cramér–Rao lower bounds, and the special top- and bottom-separator results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a population law generated by the PL model, when does the mass-weighted rank-breaking objective uniquely recover \(\theta^*\), and how does its stability depend on the comparison graph’s spectral gap?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I believe is a narrow one: a continuous population version of the paper’s PL rank-breaking estimator. It is a plausible Class A problem, though it does not show discrete hardness dissolving.

The natural regime is a very large survey or choice panel with a small set of repeated offer sets and query formats. A type is a query design together with the partial ranking reported under it; its mass is the fraction of respondents producing that record. For example, millions of users might choose a favourite from one of a few bounded menus. The PL model supplies the paper’s account of variation in their reports around a shared utility vector \(\theta^*\). This keeps the population itself continuous; the PL probabilities are the response model, not what is being continuized. The regime is strongest when the number \(N\) of respondents is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct records.

There is no numbered result in the paper asserting NP-hardness or a conventional complexity classification such as membership in P. The best named algorithmic anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It is a finite-sample estimation guarantee rather than a complexity theorem, so a strict complexity-class-only reading of the anchor requirement would leave this paper without a qualifying anchor.

Theorem 2 says the following. There are \(n\) users and \(d\) alternatives; user \(j\) receives an offered set \(S_j\) of size \(\kappa_j\) and reports a partial ordering drawn under the PL model with true utility \(\theta^*\in\Omega_b\), where \(\Omega_b=\{\theta:\sum_i\theta_i=0,\ |\theta_i|\le b\}\). Write \(\ell_j\) for the number of separators and \(p_{j,a}\) for their positions, and let \(\ell_{\max}=\max_j\ell_j\). Build the comparison graph with edge weights \(A_{ii'}=\sum_{j:i,i'\in S_j}\ell_j/(\kappa_j(\kappa_j-1))\), Laplacian \(L\), and \(D_{\max}=\max_i\sum_{j:i\in S_j}\ell_j/\kappa_j\). Define \(\alpha=(d-1)\lambda_2(L)/\sum_j\ell_j\), \(\beta=(\sum_j\ell_j)/(dD_{\max})\), \(\gamma=\min_j(1-p_{j,\ell_j}/\kappa_j)^{\lceil 2e^{2b}\rceil-2}\), and \(\eta=\max_j\kappa_j/\max\{\ell_j,\kappa_j-p_{j,\ell_j}\}\). If
\[ \sum_j\ell_j\ \ge\ \frac{2^{11}e^{18b}\eta\log(\ell_{\max}+2)^2} {\alpha^2\gamma^2\beta}\,d\log d, \]
then the rank-breaking estimator using weights \(\lambda_{j,a}=1/(\kappa_j-p_{j,a})\) satisfies
\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt d}\|\widehat\theta-\theta^*\|_2 \le \frac{4\sqrt{2}e^{4b}(1+e^{2b})^2}{\alpha\gamma} \sqrt{\frac{d\log d}{\sum_j\ell_j}} \]
with probability at least \(1-3e^3d^{-3}\).

The corresponding continuous problem is Mass-Profile PL Rank-Breaking Estimation. An instance gives \(d\), a bound \(b\), an exposure \(N\), and a finite support of records \(t=(S_t,\mathcal G_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Each record specifies its separator positions \(p_{t,a}\), its number of separators \(\ell_t\), and its rank-breaking edges \(E_{t,a}\). Ask for a vector \(\widehat\theta\in\Omega_b\) that is an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal solution to
\[ \max_{\theta\in\Omega_b}\ \sum_t\mu_t\sum_{a=1}^{\ell_t} \frac{1}{\kappa_t-p_{t,a}} \sum_{(i,i')\in E_{t,a}} \left[\theta_{i'}-\log\!\left(e^{\theta_i}+e^{\theta_{i'}}\right)\right]. \]
Here \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with record \(t\); \(N\) is the scale needed to express how much sampled evidence those masses represent. The solution is the utility vector, or its induced alternative ranking. The objective is concave, and it has at most a polynomial number of pairwise terms in the support description, so the expected classification is Class A: approximate convex optimization in \(d\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. Theorem 2’s statistical guarantee carries over when these masses are empirical frequencies of the stipulated PL samples, with \(\sum_j\ell_j=N\sum_t\mu_t\ell_t\). Arbitrary masses still define the optimization problem, but the theorem does not promise its error bound for arbitrary profiles.

That is a recognisable mirror of the paper’s rank-breaking utility-estimation problem: it preserves the alternatives, PL model, partial-ranking records, weighted comparisons, and objective. The mirror covers the data-driven rank-breaking results, not exact MLE for arbitrary posets, mixed MNL, or rank aggregation objectives outside this estimator. The paper’s cited discussion of users grouped by shared preferences is supporting evidence that repeated user types are a sensible regime, not a novelty collision.

The further questions are whether the mass profile identifies \(\theta^*\) exactly when the comparison graph is connected; how optimization and estimation error depend on its spectral gap; and how to allocate mass across query designs to improve \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) under a survey budget.

The weakest point is that Theorem 2 is about random finite samples, while a normalized mass profile alone does not encode sample size or sampling uncertainty. Including \(N\) addresses the latter for empirical profiles, but the theorem still does not establish guarantees for every deterministic continuous profile. Also, the paper already formulates rank-breaking as a convex optimization, so this mirror mostly compresses repeated records and gives the same estimator a population description; it does not uncover a new complexity separation. That limits the payoff, but the underlying continuous estimation question remains one the authors could reasonably recognize as their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 2, with Corollary 3 and Theorem 5 as special cases. Its main claim is a finite-sample error guarantee for a particular estimator under independent Plackett–Luce reports. It does not give a running-time bound or classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper does formulate rank-breaking as a convex optimization, but the theorem’s contribution is the statistical guarantee.

The best continuous version groups repeated records \(t=(S_t,\mathcal G_t)\), including their separators and rank-breaking edges, and assigns each record mass \(\mu_t\). Let \(f_t(\theta)\) be its weighted rank-breaking log-likelihood contribution. For a finite dataset with \(n_t\) copies of each record, the paper’s objective is
\[ \mathcal L_n(\theta)=\sum_t n_t f_t(\theta), \]
while the proposed population objective is
\[ \Phi_\mu(\theta)=\sum_t \mu_t f_t(\theta). \]
When \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), \(\Phi_\mu=\mathcal L_n/n\), so both objectives have exactly the same optimizer. This is a sensible compressed representation of a large panel with repeated menus and reports. It gives the mirror a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but leaves the computational target identical to the paper’s estimator.

That equivalence also marks the limit of the Theorem 2 mirror. The theorem’s error bound relies on \(N\) random reports and concentration; a mass profile alone carries no sampling uncertainty. Include \(N\) and empirical frequencies, and the instance is precisely the paper’s sample expressed as a histogram. Use arbitrary masses, and the theorem’s guarantee no longer applies. One can study the deterministic population objective or its identifiability, but that is a different result from Theorem 2’s finite-sample claim.

The strongest redesign is a very large survey with a small set of repeated query designs and a population distribution over responses. That scenario is credible, and the paper’s cited work on grouped users supports the general regime. Still, with the paper’s single shared utility vector \(\theta^*\), response variation is modeled as sampling noise around that parameter. A type that includes the realized report is useful for compressing the data, while the estimated object remains the common parameter. Giving respondents persistent, heterogeneous preference parameters would make a richer population model, but would change the PL estimation problem and would not mirror Theorem 2.

So the negative case is that the anchor yields no distinct continuous computational problem: its faithful mass formulation is the same weighted convex program, and its statistical guarantee either loses its sampling premise or reduces to the original finite-sample setting. This case is weak on the universal claim. The population M-estimation question is well-posed, and the repeated-survey regime is one the authors might recognize; the objection is that it offers little new computational work for ChoCo, rather than that no worthwhile mirror could exist.
