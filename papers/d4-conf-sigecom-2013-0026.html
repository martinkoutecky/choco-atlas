The continuous mirror question

Given a rational finite-support value distribution \(\mu\) on \(K\) values, a rational nonincreasing step supply profile \(w:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) with \(S\) steps, and a common per-advertiser budget \(B\), compute in time polynomial in \(K\), \(S\), and the input bit length a finite description of a direct rule \(a_\mu(z),p_\mu(z)\). Require \(a_\mu\) to be nondecreasing and \(p_\mu(z)=z a_\mu(z)-\int_0^z a_\mu(s)\,ds\), with \(0\le p_\mu(z)\le B\). For truthful value quantile \(v(q)\), set \(x(q)=a_\mu(v(q))\) and require \(\int_0^r x(q)\,dq\le\int_0^r w(q)\,dq\) for every \(r\in[0,1]\), with the truthful outcome individually rational and envy-free. The rule's welfare \(\int_0^1 v(q)x(q)\,dq\) must be at least \(\tfrac12\operatorname{EFO}_W(\mu,w,B)\), where the benchmark maximizes welfare over feasible, envy-free, individually rational outcomes with per-advertiser payments at most \(B\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of advertisers grouped by value, with each type carrying mass \(\mu_t\), identical per-advertiser budget \(B\), and service represented by a probability. Position supply is a nonincreasing rank profile \(w\) scaled with the population; the rule assigns service and payment by reported value, and aggregate welfare is measured over the mass distribution.

The objection that survived

The proposal does not establish that finite clinching auctions converge to the stated schedule at positive-mass ties, including how tie handling and off-support reports preserve feasibility and payments.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 4.3's factor-\(2\) welfare guarantee for symmetric position environments with a common budget. It leaves the paper's revenue approximation, cardinal environments, and benchmark characterizations outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the continuous clinching rule and its payments be represented and computed in time polynomial in the support size, supply-profile size, and input bit length?
  • How should positive-mass ties be handled so that the finite clinching rules converge to a feasible, incentive-compatible schedule?
  • Does the factor-\(2\) guarantee against the continuous envy-free benchmark follow, including at value atoms?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror I see is narrow: the paper’s welfare result for budgeted position auctions.

The anchor is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper’s Section 4.2. It says that in every position environment with a common budget, the clinching auction’s welfare is at least half the envy-free optimal welfare; the factor \(2\) is tight even in a single-item environment. The paper gives no named hardness result, so this is an algorithmic approximation anchor. Its setting is a good fit for a population mirror because feasibility already describes how service is distributed across ranked agents.

Consider a large, standardized auction market with many small advertisers. Each advertiser has a value per service, such as value per click, and the same per-advertiser budget cap \(B\). Advertisers with the same value are interchangeable in this model. There may be \(n\) advertisers but only \(K\) distinct value types, with \(K\ll n\). To keep the position market nontrivial as the population grows, let its supply grow with the advertiser population too. That co-modification is part of the mirror: the normalized quality of position at rank \(q\) is a nonincreasing profile \(w(q)\).

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover, Continuous Budgeted Position Clinching. Its input is a finite-support value distribution \(\mu\), a rational nonincreasing step function \(w:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), and a common budget \(B\). Write \(v(q)\) for the nonincreasing value quantile, where \(q=0\) is the highest-value end of the population. Mass on an interval of \(q\) represents that fraction of advertisers; an advertiser’s service remains a probability, as in the paper.

The output is a direct allocation and payment rule for this market. For a tagged advertiser reporting \(z\), the rule gives service probability \(a_\mu(z)\) and payment \(p_\mu(z)\). Require \(a_\mu\) to be nondecreasing and, with \(p_\mu(0)=0\), require

\[ p_\mu(z)=z\,a_\mu(z)-\int_0^z a_\mu(s)\,ds,\qquad 0\le p_\mu(z)\le B. \]

At the truthful population \(\mu\), let \(x(q)=a_\mu(v(q))\) and \(p(q)=p_\mu(v(q))\). Require position feasibility,

\[ \int_0^r x(q)\,dq\le \int_0^r w(q)\,dq \quad\text{for every }r\in[0,1], \]

and require the truthful outcome to be envy-free and individually rational. The objective is normalized welfare \(\int_0^1 v(q)x(q)\,dq\). The benchmark \(\operatorname{EFO}_W(\mu,w,B)\) is the maximum of that objective over all feasible, envy-free, individually rational outcomes whose per-advertiser payments are at most \(B\). A solution to the continuous problem is a finite description of a truthful rule whose welfare at \(\mu\) is at least \(\tfrac12\operatorname{EFO}_W(\mu,w,B)\).

I expect this problem to be Class A. The likely continuous analogue of clinching has a simple shape: it irons the allocation over a leading quantile interval, then follows the position supply below that interval. The budget determines the cutoff. With \(K\) value levels and \(S\) steps in \(w\), the natural target is an algorithm polynomial in \(K\), \(S\), and the input bit length. Establishing the cutoff rule, its payment schedule, and the factor-\(2\) guarantee is the concrete work for a prover. The paper’s Theorem 4.3 supplies the discrete anchor; it does not itself prove this continuous characterization or running-time bound.

The high-multiplicity bridge is direct. A finite auction with \(n_t\) advertisers of value \(v_t\) has mass \(\mu_{v_t}=n_t/n\). The normalized position supply is the rank profile \(w\), and dividing the finite welfare by \(n\) does not change an approximation ratio. Individual budgets remain \(B\); they are not averaged into a population budget. The rule above also keeps the strategic question visible through the tagged advertiser’s report schedule, rather than assuming that a zero-mass individual has no incentive constraints.

This case covers Theorem 4.3’s welfare guarantee in symmetric position environments with a common budget. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s revenue result for cardinal environments. That theorem’s biased sampling construction is a separate problem and would need its own mass-level sampling model.

The soft spot is the regime: a credible instance needs many interchangeable advertisers, a common cap, and position supply that scales with the market. Those assumptions are restrictive, and the paper’s tight example uses a single extreme bidder whose vanishing population share may not yield the same tightness statement in the continuum. That limits how broadly I would sell the mirror; it does not erase the specific high-multiplicity auction problem above.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only strong anchor is Theorem 4.3: the clinching auction gives a tight \(2\)-approximation to envy-free optimal welfare in symmetric position environments with a common budget. It is a named algorithmic result, and welfare does not track individual identities. So the paper does have a result to mirror, and aggregation does not obviously erase its objective.

The best objection is strategic. In the finite auction, a bidder’s report can change their rank and the empirical profile by \(1/n\). In the proposed model, a tagged bidder has zero mass, so a deviation leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. The proposed schedule \(a_\mu(z)\), with payments defined by the envelope formula, describes an atomless, price-taking incentive problem. That is a meaningful model, but its strategic semantics are not an automatic limit of the finite mechanism. To establish that bridge, one would need to show how the schedule handles ties in the value distribution, rank changes at atoms, and budget feasibility for every report.

That concern does not defeat the better mirror. The authors could define the continuous problem directly as an atomless population of advertisers, with a report menu for each tagged agent, per-agent budget \(B\), mass-based position feasibility, and aggregate welfare. This preserves the pointwise incentive question while making the population continuous. With finite-support values and a stepwise supply profile, it is a finite-input computational problem; scaling supply with the market gives a plausible nondegenerate regime. The paper’s position auction setting makes that co-modification recognizable, even if fixed-slot auctions would not have the same limit.

So I cannot make the requested universal case honestly. The atomless incentive layer is a real modeling and bridge obligation, and it may narrow the mirror’s relevance. But the proposed model addresses that obligation, and I see no fundamental loss of identity, multiplicity, or meaningful welfare that rules it out. My negative case is weak; Theorem 4.3’s continuous mirror survives.
