The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) items, \(\tau\) complete buyer types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), prices \(p\), per-capita supplies \(\beta\), bounded integral bundle domains, and strong-gross-substitutes valuations, let \(B_t(p)\) be the minimal preferred bundles of type \(t\). Find \(y_t\in\mu_t\operatorname{conv}(B_t(p))\) and \(s\) maximizing \(\sum_{e\in E}s_e\), subject to \(0\le s_e\le\beta_e\) and \(s_e\le\sum_t y_t(e)\); also return the inclusion-wise minimal maximizer of \(\Delta_p(S)=\sum_t\mu_t\min_{z\in B_t(p)}z(S)-\beta(S)\) over \(S\subseteq E\).

The model it lives in

A society consists of \(\tau\) complete valuation types with masses \(\mu_t\), each type choosing integral preferred bundles whose aggregate type-level average lies in \(\mu_t\operatorname{conv}(B_t(p))\). With per-capita supply \(\beta\), the auction step maximizes matched aggregate supply and identifies the minimal maximal overdemanded set; complexity should be stated in \(m,\tau\), input bit length, and the demand or exchange oracle costs.

The objection that survived

Common-denominator cloning may take time exponential in the mass bit length, and efficient optimization over the scaled type polytopes needs its own oracle analysis.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the polymatroid-sum optimization and minimal maximal overdemanded-set computation for one auction price update; it leaves the full dynamic auction, its iteration bounds, equilibrium-price results, and price-monotonicity theorems unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the mass-weighted polymatroid-sum problem and its minimal maximal overdemanded set be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length under suitable demand or exchange oracles?
  • How do continuous optima relate to finite markets with rational type proportions and supplies, including exact realization or rounding guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is the price-update computation at the heart of the paper’s dynamic auctions. Imagine a large wholesale market serving many small, standardized outlets. Each outlet buys an integral bundle of differentiated inputs; its format determines its full valuation and bundle constraints, and the valuations satisfy strong gross substitutes. There might be thousands of outlets but only a few dozen formats. The auctioneer posts one price per input category, and total supply scales with the number of outlets.

A type is a complete outlet format: outlets of that type have the same valuation, feasible bundles, and demand behavior. Normalize the population to mass \(1\), with rational type masses \(\mu_t\), and let \(\beta_e\) be supply of item \(e\) per outlet. A bundle received by any one outlet remains integral. What becomes fractional is the aggregate: a mass of \(0.3\) outlets of one type can receive one bundle and the rest another. This is population continuization, not divisible goods or a lottery over outcomes for an individual.

Here is the precise mirror I would put to a prover: Continuous Polymatroid-Sum Auction Step. Given \(m\) item types, a rational price vector \(p\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-outlet supply \(\beta\), and strong-gross-substitutes valuations \(v_t\) with fixed bounded integral bundle sets, define \(D_t(p)\) as type \(t\)’s utility-maximizing bundles and let \(B_t(p)\) be its *minimal* preferred bundles—the same demand objects used in the paper’s overdemand definition. Choose a mass \(x_{t,z}\ge 0\) for each \(z\in B_t(p)\), with \(\sum_{z\in B_t(p)}x_{t,z}=\mu_t\). Let \(a_e=\sum_t\sum_{z\in B_t(p)}x_{t,z}z(e)\). The task is to find an optimal mass assignment for

\[ \max \sum_{e\in E}s_e \quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\le s_e\le \beta_e,\qquad s_e\le a_e. \]

Thus the objective is \(\sum_e\min(a_e,\beta_e)\): the total supply that can be matched to minimal preferred bundles. The auction-step output should also include the inclusion-wise minimal maximizer \(S\) of

\[ \Delta_p(S)=\sum_t\mu_t\min_{z\in B_t(p)}z(S)-\beta(S), \qquad S\subseteq E. \]

That is the continuous analogue of the minimal maximal overdemanded set the auction needs in order to update prices. The instance has support size \(\tau\), not one entry per outlet; a prover should state complexity in \(m,\tau,L\), plus the costs of the valuation oracles.

The anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper in Section 4.3. In full, it says that for \(n\) M-convex sets \(B_i\subseteq[0,b]_{\mathbb Z}\), one can find \(z_i\in B_i\) maximizing

\[ \sum_{e\in E}\min\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_i(e),b(e)\right); \]

the paper’s push-relabel algorithm returns an optimum in time \(O(n\cdot DO+nm^3\cdot ExO)\), where \(DO\) and \(ExO\) are demand- and exchange-oracle query costs. The result is established here, not merely cited. In the mirror, each type’s minimal-preferred-bundle set has a polymatroid base polytope \(P_t=\operatorname{conv}(B_t(p))\), and its mass assignment lies in \(\mu_tP_t\). The displayed optimization is an LP over these scaled polytopes. Its bundle-indexed formulation may have many variables, so the algorithmic question is whether linear optimization or separation over each type’s polytope can be carried out efficiently with the paper’s kind of demand and exchange access. That gives a credible Class A expectation: continuous optimization plus the M-convex structure, with the oracle-to-pricing step made explicit rather than assumed away.

I think the authors would recognize this as their auction-step problem. For a finite market with \(N\mu_t\) outlets of each type and \(N\beta_e\) units of supply, assigning mass in increments of \(1/N\) is exactly an assignment of outlets to integral preferred bundles. The continuum allows arbitrary masses and fractional type-level averages, but it does not give any individual outlet a fractional bundle. Whether every continuous optimum has an equally good finite realization at matching denominators is a natural part of the high-multiplicity dictionary to prove.

The mirror covers the per-price computation of overdemand, not the paper’s entire dynamic auction, its equilibrium-price monotonicity theorems, or its convergence and iteration bounds. This paper’s central named computational anchor is algorithmic; it offers no comparable hardness result to prefer. So this is a case for a tractable continuous counterpart, not a claim that discrete hardness dissolves.

My weakest point is also the main technical obligation: Theorem 5 is an oracle algorithm on individual integer bundles, while this mirror optimizes over type-level convex hulls. A polynomial LP formulation does not by itself prove polynomial runtime if pricing over a type’s preferred-bundle set is expensive. The other open question is whether the normalized finite and continuous optima agree, or whether only an approximation or rounding guarantee holds. Those are substantial questions, but they do not make the mirror artificial: the repeated-type regime is plausible, and the mass assignment is exactly the aggregate of choices that the paper’s auction already handles one buyer at a time.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the proposed mirror is not covered by Theorem 5 as stated. That theorem takes one integral M-convex set per buyer. The mirror replaces repeated buyers with rational type masses and optimizes over scaled convex hulls. Cloning each type to a common denominator \(D\) recovers a finite instance, but \(D\) can be exponential in the bit length \(L\). So the theorem’s running time does not establish an efficient continuous algorithm. A prover would need a rational-weight polymatroid-sum algorithm with suitable pricing or separation access; the paper’s exchange-oracle bounds do not supply it automatically.

That is a real gap in the proponent’s tractability claim, but it does not defeat the mirror. It identifies the computational question the mirror would ask. Nor does the paper lack a named computational anchor: Theorem 5 gives an algorithm and running time for a concrete optimization problem, and the continuous mass-assignment problem remains well-posed even if its answer turns out to be easy or difficult.

The proposed setting also survives the stronger scenario check. A large procurement platform with many buyers sharing a small number of complete valuation and bundle-constraint types is plausible; each buyer can still receive an integral bundle while type mass is split across bundles. The fact that the paper’s full auction and equilibrium-price results are not mirrored is no objection under the stated standard: this one price-update computation is enough.

So the negative case is weak. I can challenge the claimed algorithmic transfer, but I cannot honestly argue that the mirror is artificial or not worth asking. Theorem 5’s anchor survives.
