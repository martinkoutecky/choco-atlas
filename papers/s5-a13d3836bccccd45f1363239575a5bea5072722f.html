The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(T\), where each type \(t\) has a shared nonincreasing marginal-value schedule \(v_{t,1}\ge\cdots\ge v_{t,K}>0\), masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and supply \(\kappa\in(0,K)\) units per population member, compute an optimal mass allocation \(y_{tq}\). Each member receives an integer bundle \(q\in\{0,\ldots,K\}\), while \(y_{tq}\) may be fractional. Maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{q=0}^{K}y_{tq}V_t(q)\), subject to \(\sum_q y_{tq}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,q}q\,y_{tq}=\kappa\), and \(y_{tq}\ge0\), where \(V_t(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{q}v_{t,i}\). For each assigned bundle \(q\), compute the limiting Vickrey charge \(P_t(q)=V_t(q)-D_t\), where \(D_t=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}[W(\mu,\kappa)-W(\mu-\epsilon e_t,\kappa)]/\epsilon\) and \(W\) is optimal welfare.

The model it lives in

A finite type set \(T\) carries masses \(\mu_t\), each type shares a complete nonincreasing marginal-value schedule, and supply is \(\kappa\) units per population member. The variables \(y_{tq}\) are masses assigned indivisible bundles \(q\); maximizing welfare gives an LP, and the one-sided welfare loss \(D_t\) under removal of type mass yields limiting charges \(P_t(q)=V_t(q)-D_t\).

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 13 through the homogeneous-goods, uniform-matroid specialization in §4.3: efficient allocation and limiting Vickrey charges. It leaves arbitrary matroids, private-value elicitation, and the truthful ascending implementation unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give an exact algorithm and bit-complexity bound for computing the one-sided welfare losses \(D_t\) and the resulting charges when the LP has multiple optima.
  • Establish finite-population convergence bounds for allocations and Vickrey charges under rational type proportions and per-capita supply.
  • Determine whether the limiting allocation and charges admit a truthful ascending implementation, or whether that requires a different strategic solution concept.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s homogeneous-goods specialization, not its full arbitrary-matroid auction. That is a particularly credible setting because §4.3 explicitly derives the homogeneous-goods auction from the matroid model.

My anchor is Theorem 13, proved in this paper: for every finite matroid with positive integer valuations and no bidder holding a monopoly, Auction 5 finds an efficient allocation and charges Vickrey prices. In the paper’s model, bidders own disjoint sets of matroid elements, value them additively, and the allocation is a maximum-weight basis. The theorem establishes the auction’s outcome and prices; it does not claim a complexity class or a running-time bound. The paper has no comparable named hardness result, so this algorithmic correctness result is the best available anchor.

Lead mirror — Homogeneous-Goods Continuum VCG. Consider many bidders drawn from a finite set \(T\) of valuation types. A type \(t\) is a complete, shared marginal-value schedule \(v_{t,1}\ge\cdots\ge v_{t,K}>0\). Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the reference population with that schedule, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). There are \(\kappa\) units of supply per reference-population member, where \(\kappa\) is rational and \(0<\kappa<K\). Thus a finite approximation has \(n\mu_t\) bidders of type \(t\) and \(n\kappa\) units, for suitable \(n\). The regime is \(n\) large and \(|T|=\tau\) modest: many bidders, relatively few shared schedules.

The decision variable \(y_{tq}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) bidders who receive exactly \(q\) whole units. So the units remain indivisible for each bidder; only the population allocation is continuous. Let \(V_t(q)=\sum_{i=1}^q v_{t,i}\), with \(V_t(0)=0\). The welfare problem is

\[ \begin{aligned} W(\mu,\kappa)=\max\quad &\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{q=0}^{K}y_{tq}V_t(q)\\ \text{subject to}\quad &\sum_{q=0}^{K}y_{tq}=\mu_t &&(t\in T),\\ &\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{q=0}^{K}q\,y_{tq}=\kappa,\\ &y_{tq}\ge 0. \end{aligned} \]

The continuous problem is to return an optimal \(y\) and the limiting VCG charge for each type and awarded quantity. For type \(t\), define its marginal product by the one-sided loss in optimal welfare when a small mass of that type is removed, holding supply fixed:

\[ D_t=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0} \frac{W(\mu,\kappa)-W(\mu-\epsilon e_t,\kappa)}{\epsilon}. \]

The charge to a type-\(t\) bidder receiving \(q\) units is \(P_t(q)=V_t(q)-D_t\); bidders receiving no units pay zero. This is the limit of the finite VCG formula: with \(n\) agents, removing one type-\(t\) bidder changes optimal welfare by \(n[W(\mu,\kappa)-W(\mu-\frac1n e_t,\kappa)]\). The no-monopoly condition is respected in the limit when \(\kappa<K\), so a single removed bidder does not make supply infeasible. A complete solution gives the exact optimal masses, the one-sided slopes, and hence the charges. The LP has \(O(\tau K)\) variables, so I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A), with exact rational computation.

The authors should recognize this as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime: §4.3 already models homogeneous goods with decreasing marginal values as a uniform-matroid basis problem. Scaling supply with population is part of making that same allocation problem meaningful at population scale. Further questions include whether the limiting allocation and charges have finite-\(n\) error bounds, whether the LP prices yield a continuous ascending implementation, and how far the construction extends to other matroid families.

The weak point is that Theorem 13 does not establish polynomial time, and the mirror covers only its uniform-matroid specialization. Also, the payment here is a limit of finite VCG charges: in a literal atomless population, a single agent has zero mass. That makes this an honest high-multiplicity mirror of the outcome computation, but not by itself a theorem about strategic behavior in an atomless game or a continuous implementation of the whole arbitrary-matroid auction.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 13 is not a complexity result. It proves that Auction 5 returns an efficient allocation and Vickrey prices, but gives no running-time bound or hardness claim. The paper’s contribution is an incentive-compatible auction implementation for private values. The proposed mirror instead takes the valuation distribution as known input and asks a planner to solve an aggregate welfare LP. That is a sensible limit question, but it is not a continuous version of a complexity claim made by the paper; it adds a new computational question.

For the general matroid result, there is also a serious multiplicity problem. A type would have to capture an element’s role in the matroid’s dependence structure as well as its owner and value. In a graphic matroid, for example, an edge’s role depends on the graph’s cuts and cycles. Repeating its value does not make another edge the same type, so the support size may remain close to \(|E|\), with no clear population limit.

That objection does not defeat the proponent’s homogeneous-goods mirror. Section 4.3 supplies exactly the symmetric special case needed: bidders can share a marginal-value schedule, and supply can scale with population. The LP is a coherent high-multiplicity model. Its VCG charge is also a plausible per-bidder limit when derived from the finite markets. Calling it merely easy would not refute it: a tractable continuous mirror can still be worthwhile.

The more fundamental reservation is narrower. In the atomless model, one bidder has zero mass, so the derivative-based charge is not by itself a VCG mechanism for strategic agents in that model. It is an externality recovered by taking a limit of finite markets with a specified population scale. The proponent acknowledges this, and that limit may still be worth studying, but it does not mirror the paper’s distinctive auction implementation or establish truthful behavior in a continuum.

So the case against is that the paper offers no named complexity result to mirror, and its general matroid theorem lacks a natural high-multiplicity regime. But the homogeneous-goods specialization remains a credible exception. Under the programme’s stated standards, I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
