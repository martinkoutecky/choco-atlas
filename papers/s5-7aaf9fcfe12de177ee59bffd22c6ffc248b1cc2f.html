The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchor-compliant positive case: the paper contains no named computational result. Its numbered results concern equilibrium existence, uniqueness, and characterization—not whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

The closest footholds are Proposition 1 and Proposition 2, both proved in the paper. Proposition 1 characterizes when a lower no-delay SPE expected outcome exists. Proposition 2 gives parameter conditions for multiple such outcomes and describes their range; otherwise, the no-delay SPE expected outcome is unique. These are substantive results, but they are analytic equilibrium results, not complexity results. Treating either as an algorithmic anchor would overstate what the authors proved.

A plausible *follow-on* continuous problem would be: let the types be \(A\)-preferring agents with discount factor \(\delta\), \(A\)-preferring agents with discount factor \(\mu\), and \(B\)-preferring agents with discount factor \(\delta\), with rational masses \(p_1,p_2,p_3\) summing to \(1\). Each round, draw the proposer’s type with probability equal to its mass; that type proposes a policy in \([0,1]\), and the types respond under unanimity and the paper’s orderly-voting convention. Given \((p_1,p_2,p_3,\delta,\mu)\), ask for the minimum and maximum expected policy among no-delay SPE, where the expected policy is the mass-weighted average of type proposals. A natural regime is a large deliberative body whose many members fall into these three preference-and-patience types.

That preserves the paper’s bargaining question and makes the population continuous, but it is not a result the paper establishes or computes. Its weakest point is central: the paper’s distinctive patient type is one individual, whose mass vanishes as the population grows. Giving that type positive mass changes the model; retaining a zero-mass individual while preserving their veto power needs a separate formal treatment. So this is a plausible research question, but it cannot support the requested anchored case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 characterize the set of no-delay SPE outcomes and give conditions for multiplicity; Proposition 2 even supplies an explicit threshold in \((\delta,n,n_B)\). Those are equilibrium-characterization results, not complexity claims about an algorithmic problem. Asking how hard it is to compute such equilibria would create a new computational question. It would not turn either proposition into a computational result of the paper.

The proposed three-type model does not fix that gap. If each type is treated as a strategic player, it is a three-player bargaining game with a mass-weighted proposer lottery. If the types stand for actual populations, unanimity raises a substantive modeling issue: a single dissenter can block agreement regardless of their mass. Replacing unanimity with approval by almost everyone removes that individual veto; retaining it means the game’s equilibrium logic cannot be read off from type masses alone. The paper’s distinctive mechanism depends on one unusually patient agent exercising precisely this kind of veto.

A closer extension could keep an exceptional patient agent while making the other groups large, or give the patient type positive mass. The first is a hybrid atom-and-continuum model; in a population limit the agent’s proposer probability vanishes while their veto power remains. The second changes the paper’s single exceptional agent into a positive-mass group. Either could be a sensible bargaining-theory project, but neither supplies a computational result that this paper established. And the proposed fixed three-type version has no evident growing combinatorial structure: it asks for an equilibrium characterization in a small weighted game, while the paper already gives explicit characterizations for its own parameter regime.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s stated computational remit: Propositions 1 and 2 are not anchors for a complexity mirror. I would not claim the universal version literally—that no continuous-population bargaining model could be worthwhile. An atom-and-continuum extension might be worth studying in equilibrium theory. Its value, however, would be as a new model and question, not as a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.
