Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1.4, proved in this paper in Section 5.7. It says that on the complete graph \(K_n\), the asynchronous plurality protocol of Section 5 converges to the initially most frequent opinion \(A\) within \(\Theta(\log n)\) time with high probability, provided the number of opinions is \(k=O(\exp(\log n/\log\log n))\) and the initial support of \(A\) is at least \(1+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{bias}}\) times the support of every other opinion, for a constant \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{bias}}>0\). The paper has no named NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity result to anchor on; this proved runtime guarantee is its strongest computational result for the mirror.

A concrete scenario is a large, globally mixed online consultation in which participants repeatedly sample other participants and update one of a fixed set of stances. Participants use the same protocol, have the same communication role and clock rate, and are indistinguishable to the protocol apart from their current state. The continuum society has mass \(\mu_c\) on each initial stance \(c\), with rational masses summing to one. The support size \(\tau\) is the number of stances actually present. For the regime in Theorem 1.4, the population is much larger than the number of stances: with \(N\) as a population-resolution scale, \(k\) is subpolynomial in \(N\).

Here is the mirror I would ask someone to analyze: Mean-Field Asynchronous Plurality Stabilization at Resolution \(1/N\). The input is \(N\), a finite stance set \(C\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over its \(\tau\) supported stances, with unique plurality \(A\) satisfying \(\mu_A\ge(1+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{bias}})\mu_c\) for every \(c\ne A\), and \(k\le K\exp(\log N/\log\log N)\) for a fixed constant \(K\). Each infinitesimal participant follows exactly the protocol in Section 5, with its local state including its opinion, bit, intermediate opinion, clock counters, and stored samples; replace the protocol’s \(n\) parameter by \(N\). Each participant has a rate-one Poisson clock, and every neighbor sample is an independent draw from the current population distribution over full protocol states, as appropriate for a complete graph with sampling with replacement.

Let \(\nu_t\) be the resulting measure over full states at time \(t\). The question is to determine the first time
\[ t_N(\mu)=\inf\{t:\nu_t(\text{opinion }A)\ge 1-1/N\}, \]
taking \(t_N(\mu)=\infty\) if the threshold is never reached. A solution is a bound on this hitting time, supported by the measure-valued trajectory induced by the stated update rule. The expected result is \(t_N(\mu)=O(\log N)\), with constants depending on the fixed bias and protocol constants. This keeps the problem about the population itself: mass flows between stance and protocol-state types, and the success criterion is that nearly all population mass holds the original plurality opinion.

I expect this mirror to be tractable in the sense of a Class A analysis: derive the nonlinear law evolution and bound how the protocol’s two-choice, propagation, synchronization, and endgame stages move mass. There is also a natural \(\Omega(\log N)\) scale: a positive fraction of initially non-\(A\) mass has not had a clock tick by time \(t\) proportional to \(e^{-t}\), so reducing that mass below \(1/N\) takes logarithmic time. Further questions are whether the finite-\(n\) high-probability guarantee converges uniformly to this law over \(O(\log N)\) time, whether the synchronization gadget is still needed in the mean-field model, and what sharper dependence on \(\mu\) and \(k\) is possible.

The authors would have a reasonable basis to recognize this as their problem’s continuous analogue: their model is already expressed through opinion counts, ratios, and rate-one Poisson clocks, and the mirror preserves the local rules and plurality objective. It covers only Theorem 1.4’s asynchronous convergence guarantee, not the paper’s synchronous results or lower bounds.

The weak point is real: a continuum process cannot reach exact consensus in finite time when every stance begins with positive mass. The \(1/N\) threshold is therefore a co-modification of the target, representing the finite population’s one-agent resolution. Also, the paper’s result depends on finite-population random fluctuations and clock synchronization; a law-level analysis must establish the runtime bound anew. This is a credible, but less direct, mirror than one where a named complexity theorem survives unchanged.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1.4 is the proponent’s only anchor. I would not claim the paper lacks a named computational result: it proves a runtime bound for a distributed algorithm. The narrower objection is that the proposed mirror turns that result into a question about a nonlinear population process. In the finite algorithm, \(n\) sets the synchronization schedule and the target is agreement among finitely many nodes. In a continuum, there is no intrinsic \(N\). Keeping the \(1/N\) threshold means studying a family of accuracy targets; fixing a threshold \(1-\delta\) gives a well-posed mean-field question, but now the task is to analyze a measure-valued trajectory. That is close to the analytic mean-field work the programme distinguishes from its computational-complexity gap.

Giving each infinitesimal participant the protocol’s full local state makes the model more faithful, but also makes the state space grow with the schedule, counters, and stored samples. One could ask for an efficient algorithm to approximate the resulting hitting time or classify the initial distributions that reach \(A\). That is a coherent new computational problem. It is not, however, supplied by Theorem 1.4: it requires choosing an output problem and a complexity measure on top of the continuum dynamics. So the proposed mirror is less direct than it first appears, though the issue is scope, not that its answer would be uninteresting.

The more serious obstacle to a universal negative is the paper’s stability result, Theorem 1.2. Its adversary may change \(F=c_1(c_1-c_2)/(8n)\) opinions per round. In mass units, that budget is \(\mu_A(\mu_A-\mu_B)/8\), so a natural mirror asks how much mass an adversary must reassign each round to prevent \(A\) from prevailing. This has a clear high-multiplicity interpretation, uses a finite set of opinion types, and connects directly to winner robustness. It cannot be dismissed on grounds of individual identity or because high-multiplicity work already exists. One might argue that optimizing an adversary’s schedule is a new control problem rather than the computational question studied by the paper; but the paper explicitly studies dynamic adversaries, and the programme includes robustness and campaigning. That objection is not decisive.

My strongest case against is therefore limited: Theorem 1.4’s \(N\)-resolution mirror looks more like a mean-field analysis of a randomized protocol than a continuization of a computational social-choice problem. I cannot honestly defend the broader claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The mass-adversary robustness question survives as a plausible one, and the paper’s opinion dynamics setting makes it difficult to rule out on scope alone.
