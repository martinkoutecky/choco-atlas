The continuous mirror question

Given a task set \(J\), a precedence DAG \(G\), a rational threshold \(q\), and a distribution \(\{(\pi_r,\mu_r)\}_{r=1}^{\tau}\) over preferred task orders, does there exist a permutation \(S\) satisfying \(G\) such that \(\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r\sum_{j\in J}\max\{0,C_j(S)-C_j(\pi_r)\}\le q\)?

The model it lives in

Model employees’ preferred orders of common unit-length training modules as types \(\pi_r\) with rational population masses \(\mu_r\); retain an integral schedule \(S\) satisfying the imposed precedence DAG \(G\), and minimize the mass-weighted average total tardiness \(\sum_r\mu_r\sum_j\max\{0,C_j(S)-C_j(\pi_r)\}\).

The objection that survived

Each additive objective depends only on the marginal position distribution for each task, so correlations among voters’ complete rankings do not affect cost; this narrows the mirror to precedence scheduling with mass-derived cost curves, but leaves a society-derived computational problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 14’s total-tardiness problem and Proposition 15’s late-task problem with imposed chain precedence constraints. It leaves the EMD approximation results, inferred precedence results, time-constraint algorithms, and axiomatic results outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the problem admit useful parameterized or approximation algorithms in the number of task types, tasks, or precedence chains?
  • Does the complexity change when the number of supported preferred orders is fixed?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s imposed precedence graph setting with order preferences. A type is a complete preferred schedule \(\pi_r\), a permutation of the tasks; its mass \(\mu_r\) is the fraction of the population sharing that schedule. An instance consists of the task set \(J\), a precedence DAG \(G\), and a support list \(\{(\pi_r,\mu_r)\}_{r=1}^{\tau}\), where the rational masses sum to \(1\). The voters’ preferred schedules need not obey \(G\); the returned schedule must.

The decision variable remains an integral schedule \(S\), a permutation satisfying \(G\). Write \(C_j(S)\) for task \(j\)’s completion slot. For the tardiness mirror, ask for a feasible schedule minimizing
\(\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r\sum_{j\in J}\max\{0,C_j(S)-C_j(\pi_r)\}\).
For the late-task mirror, minimize
\(\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\mu_r\sum_{j\in J}\mathbf{1}[C_j(S)>C_j(\pi_r)]\).
Equivalently, a decision instance asks whether a feasible schedule has objective at most a rational threshold. These are the paper’s underlying scheduling problems with average dissatisfaction in place of total dissatisfaction; the schedule and its constraints are unchanged.

A plausible regime is a large organization arranging a common sequence of unit-length training modules. Many employees share one of a small set of role-based preferred orders, while external accreditation requirements impose precedence chains. The population might number in the tens of thousands while only dozens of complete order types occur. That scenario is a property of the instance regime, not a claim about every scheduling application. It also fits the paper’s own utilitarian framing: it explicitly identifies the sum divided by \(v\) as average voter dissatisfaction. Grouping identical schedules and assigning them their population shares is therefore a natural high-multiplicity version of its problem.

My lead anchor is Proposition 14. It states that \(\sigma T_{\mathrm{graph}}\), minimizing the sum of voters’ total tardiness subject to imposed precedence constraints, is strongly NP-hard even when the precedence graph consists of chains. The paper proves this proposition by reduction from a known NP-hard single-machine scheduling problem with unit tasks, due dates, and chain precedences; that source hardness is cited, while the reduction and proposition are proved here. In the mirror, the question is exactly the weighted-average tardiness optimization above, even for a precedence graph that is a disjoint union of chains. I expect hardness to transfer: for a discrete profile with \(v\) voters and \(a_r\) voters of type \(\pi_r\), set \(\mu_r=a_r/v\). The mirror’s cost is the discrete total tardiness divided by \(v\), so it has exactly the same optimal schedules; a decision threshold is divided by \(v\) as well. The reduction uses polynomially bounded profile sizes, so this normalization preserves the hardness result.

A second, distinct anchor is Proposition 15. It states that \(\sigma U_{\mathrm{graph}}\), minimizing the total number of late tasks subject to imposed precedence constraints, is strongly NP-hard even when the precedence graph consists only of chains. The paper proves this by reduction from a strongly NP-complete scheduling problem, cited to Lenstra (1980). The continuous question is the weighted-average late-task optimization above, with the same chain restriction. Again I expect hardness to transfer: aggregating identical voter schedules into rational masses divides the discrete objective by \(v\) without changing which feasible schedules minimize it. This anchor covers the binary lateness criterion separately from total tardiness.

These questions stay within scope because only the population is continuized. Each person has the same complete preference, costs and parameters as everyone else of their type; the common precedence graph remains an imposed constraint, and the outcome remains a schedule. No claim is made about fractional schedules or interval preferences. Replicating every voter in a discrete profile any number of times leaves its masses and optimum unchanged, so arbitrarily many people can share each type. The reductions do require type support to grow with the task set, however; they do not establish hardness for a fixed number of types. Natural follow-ups are whether either problem has useful parameterized or approximation algorithms in \(m\), \(\tau\), or the number of chains, and whether the fixed-\(\tau\) restriction changes the picture.

The main weakness is also the reason to expect Class B: these hardness results derive their combinatorics from the task agenda and precedence constraints, not from having many individually distinct voters. Continuization compresses repeated schedules into masses, but it does not dissolve this hardness. I think the authors would recognize the mirror because it preserves their schedule, imposed constraints, preferences, and per-capita dissatisfaction objective. Its contribution is a continuous-population version of their computational question, not a promise that the answer becomes tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the population’s full distribution over rankings is more detail than either objective uses. If type \(r\) has mass \(\mu_r\) and places task \(j\) at slot \(p_{rj}\), then the tardiness cost of putting \(j\) at slot \(s\) is
\[ F_j(s)=\sum_r \mu_r\max\{0,s-p_{rj}\}, \]
and its late-task cost is
\[ H_j(s)=\sum_r \mu_r\mathbf{1}[s>p_{rj}]. \]
The objectives are just \(\sum_j F_j(C_j(S))\) and \(\sum_j H_j(C_j(S))\). Thus correlations between tasks within voters’ rankings disappear: the society can be compressed to each task’s distribution over positions. The resulting problem is precedence-constrained scheduling with population-derived cost curves, rather than a problem that needs the full voter-type distribution. That limits what these results might teach ChoCo about continuous optimization.

But it does not defeat Proposition 14. Its continuous mirror is well-defined, and any rational masses can be represented by a finite electorate after clearing denominators. The weighted average is the discrete total tardiness divided by electorate size, so feasible schedules and optima correspond exactly. Many employees sharing a small set of preferred training-module orders is a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

Proposition 15 survives the same check: replacing the electorate’s counts by rational masses scales the late-task objective without changing the scheduling question. Its imposed chain constraints and task-based preferences also admit the same kind of shared-training scenario. The need for the reduction’s support to grow with the task set does not rule out a mirror; support size is an input parameter, and the proposed problem can still ask what happens as it varies.

So the marginal-cost compression is a reason to question the distinctiveness of these mirrors, not their validity. I cannot make the universal negative case honestly: neither anchor depends on voter identity, a vanishing continuum, or an implausible population regime, and neither is already answered by a continuous computational treatment. Both anchors survive.
