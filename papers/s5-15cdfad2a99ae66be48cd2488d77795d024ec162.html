The continuous mirror question

Given \(C=\{1,2,3\}\) and rational \(\mu\in\Delta(S_3)\), compute \(p\in\Delta(C)\) minimizing the worst-case distortion over all measurable hidden metric realizations consistent with an atomless population whose ranking map has distribution \(\mu\), and determine whether \(\sup_{\mu}D^*(\mu)=2.02613\), using the generalized \((0,1,2,3)\)-metric LP as a certificate.

The model it lives in

Use ranking types \(T=S_3\) with mass \(\mu\); candidate probabilities \(p\) are the decision variables, hidden metrics are adversarial environments, and the objective is the ratio of expected social cost to minimum social cost. For \(m=3\), generalized \((0,1,2,3)\)-metric certificates give ranking-determined distance signatures and reduce the mechanism computation to a finite LP.

The objection that survived

The paper already expresses the relevant instance through real proportions, comparison matrices, and plurality vectors, while voters with the same ranking may have different latent metric distances; a convincing mirror therefore needs an explicit atomless model and a complete-type or common-environment interpretation.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified but real. The paper has no named NP-hardness, \(P\), FPT, or parameterized-complexity theorem. Its eligible anchors are approximation and LP-based mechanism results. I would lead with Theorem 1, supported by Theorems 3 and 6.

The mirror is a continuous metric-distortion problem. Let \(T=S_m\) be the set of complete rankings of the \(m\) candidates, and let \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\) give the mass of each ranking type. Represent the society by an atomless population \(u\in[0,1]\), with ranking map \(\sigma(u)\) inducing \(\mu\). A hidden metric realization is a measurable metric \(d\) on the candidates and population points such that

\[ c\succ_{\sigma(u)}c' \Longrightarrow d(c,u)\le d(c',u). \]

Define

\[ \mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(c;d)=\int_0^1 d(c,u)\,du. \]

A randomized mechanism observes \(\mu\) and returns \(p\in\Delta(C)\). Its distortion in realization \(d\) is

\[ \frac{\sum_{c\in C}p_c\,\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(c;d)} {\min_{c'\in C}\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(c';d)}. \]

The continuous problem is to compute

\[ D^*(\mu)= \min_{p\in\Delta(C)} \sup_{d\in\mathcal D(\mu)} \frac{\sum_c p_c\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(c;d)} {\min_{c'}\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(c';d)}. \]

Only the population is continuized. Candidates remain discrete, the chosen outcome remains one candidate, and randomization remains the mechanism’s action.

This is plausible in a large facility-location market: millions of customers choose among a small number of possible sites, with many customers sharing the same ordinal ordering of the sites. The same story works for a large electorate with a small number of recurring ideological rankings. The relevant regime is \(n\gg |T|\), not “all voting is continuous.” Rational \(\mu\) is exactly a finite electorate after clearing denominators, while the atomless model removes the irrelevant denominator and permits exact population masses.

The main anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(m\ge3\), there is a positive constant \(\gamma_m\) such that no randomized election mechanism on \(m\) candidates can guarantee distortion below \(2+\gamma_m\). In particular, \(\gamma_3\ge0.02613\), \(\gamma_4\ge0.04957\), and the limiting lower bound is at least \(2.11264\).

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous Worst-Case Metric Distortion: given \(m\), determine

\[ \rho_m^\infty=\sup_{\mu\in\Delta(S_m)}D^*(\mu), \]

and produce a mechanism attaining the best possible worst-case guarantee. A solution consists of a rule \(\mu\mapsto p(\mu)\), together with a proof of its guarantee; a lower-bound certificate consists of a mass distribution \(\mu\) and compatible hidden metrics showing that every \(p\) has distortion at least the claimed threshold.

Theorem 1 transfers directly. Its \(m=3\) witness uses only three positive ranking masses,

\[ \mu_{(1,3,2)}=a,\qquad \mu_{(2,3,1)}=b,\qquad \mu_{(3,2,1)}=1-a-b, \]

with approximately \(a=0.473356\) and \(b=0.423961\). The adversarial metrics are the paper’s \((0,1,2,3)\)-metrics. Thus the obstruction does not depend on individually named voters or population granularity: it already exists with a constant number of types and exact masses. Under ChoCo’s trichotomy, I would call this a transfer-of-obstruction result rather than continuum-specific hardness. The difficulty lives in candidate comparisons and hidden metric geometry, not in the number of voters.

The second anchor is Theorem 3, also proved here. It states: “Any election with 3 candidates has a randomized election mechanism that guarantees distortion at most \(2.02613\).” Its continuous counterpart is Three-Candidate Continuous Metric-Distortion Optimization: given rational masses \(\mu\) over the six rankings of three candidates, compute \(p\in\Delta_3\) minimizing the worst-case distortion over all compatible hidden metrics.

This is the clearest Class A mirror. The proof reduces the infinitely many metric constraints to finitely many generalized \((0,1,2,3)\)-metric constraints, so the mechanism can be obtained from a finite LP whose coefficients are functions of \(\mu\). The resulting algorithm should be polynomial in the encoding length of \(\mu\). Together with Theorem 1, the paper gives the exact worst-case continuous value for three candidates, up to the displayed numerical precision:

\[ \rho_3^\infty=2.02613. \]

The third, weaker anchor is Theorem 6, proved here. It states that the LP optimizing over \((0,1,2,3)\)-metrics always has optimal objective at least \(1.75487\); equivalently, when the hidden metric is restricted to that class, there is a mechanism guaranteeing distortion at most \(2.13968\).

The matching continuous problem is Restricted Continuous Metric-Distortion Optimization. Given \(\mu\), construct its comparison matrix

\[ M^\mu_{ij}=\Pr_{\sigma\sim\mu}[i\succ_\sigma j] \]

and plurality vector \(q^\mu\). Compute nonnegative weights \(x\) solving

\[ \max \sum_i x_i \quad\text{subject to}\quad M^\mu x\le \mathbf 1-q^\mu,\qquad x\ge0, \]

then normalize \(p_i=x_i/\sum_jx_j\). This is an ordinary finite LP, hence a strong Class A candidate, polynomial in \(m\), the number of listed ranking types, and the bit length of \(\mu\). The further question is whether this restricted LP remains sufficient for the unrestricted metric problem when \(m\ge4\), which is close to the paper’s Conjecture 3.

The paper’s Proposition 1 supplies the bridge: if LP (A) has optimum \(\beta\), then the optimal randomized mechanism has distortion \(1+1/\beta\). In the continuous model, the sums defining social costs simply become integrals, while the LP remains over mechanism weights and has one constraint for each compatible hidden metric.

The authors should recognise these mirrors: the observable input is still an ordinal ranking distribution, the hidden object is still a metric, social cost is still average distance, and the decision is still a randomized candidate choice. The continuous version does not fractionalize candidates or outcomes.

The weakest point is that this paper is already very close to a continuous-population paper. Section 2 explicitly permits real-valued voter proportions and says that \(n\) should be thought of as very large. Its matrices and proofs already depend on \(\mu\)-like quantities rather than voter identities. Therefore, merely replacing \(n^{-1}\sum_v\) by \(\int\) would be a collision or near-collision. The positive case survives only if the mirror is taken to include the exact atomless model, the high-multiplicity complexity parameters \((m,|T|,L)\), the separation problem over hidden metrics, and the finite-to-continuum approximation theorem.

A second vulnerability is that latent metric locations may vary among voters with the same ranking. The clean defence is to regard the metric as an unobserved common environment rather than part of the observable type. For the restricted \((0,1,2,3)\)-metric anchor, one can make the model fully finite-type by fixing a hidden metric world and giving every ranking type its corresponding fixed distance signature. If a referee rejects that information-type interpretation, the full-metric mirror weakens, but the restricted LP mirror remains a sensible positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this is not really a computational-complexity paper. None of Theorems 1, 3, or 6 states an \( \mathrm{NP} \)-hardness result, a polynomial-time algorithm as a function of an encoded instance, or a parameterized-complexity classification. They are distortion bounds and structural LP arguments. A continuous mirror would therefore have to manufacture a new computational problem around the paper, rather than continuize one of its computational results.

Theorem 1 is the most difficult anchor to defeat, but it is also the clearest collision. The paper already formulates elections through real-valued proportions, explicitly treats \(n\) as arbitrarily large, and conducts its lower-bound analysis entirely through the ranking masses, comparison matrix, and plurality vector. The proposed quantity

\[ \rho_m^\infty=\sup_{\mu}D^*(\mu) \]

is consequently just the paper’s worst-case distortion problem with the empirical distribution written as a measure. Every rational \(\mu\) is exactly a finite election after clearing denominators, and the paper’s witness already has only three positive ranking masses. Passing from a finite electorate to an atomless copy neither introduces a new type-space issue nor exposes a new computational regime. The claimed “continuous” lower bound is already present in the paper’s high-multiplicity algebra.

Theorem 3 does not rescue the proposal. For \(m=3\), the ranking type space has only six elements and the generalized-metric reduction produces only finitely many constraints. The proposed continuous optimization is therefore a constant-dimensional LP whose coefficients are exactly the proportions already used in the proof. It is not a new scalable continuous-population algorithm. Making \(m\) variable would be a worthwhile new question, but it would no longer be a mirror of Theorem 3: the paper explicitly says that its reduction and case analysis do not scale, and the resulting problem is essentially the paper’s open general-\(m\) problem.

Theorem 6 is even closer to a literal restatement. Its LP

\[ \max \mathbf 1^\top p \quad\text{subject to}\quad M^\mu p\le \mathbf 1-q^\mu,\qquad p\ge0 \]

already takes the population only through aggregate weighted frequencies. Computing \(M^\mu\) and \(q^\mu\) from a distribution over rankings and solving this LP is precisely the paper’s displayed construction, not a newly exposed continuous optimization problem. Restricting the hidden metrics to the \((0,1,2,3)\)-class makes the population continuum disappear from the model altogether except as a way of supplying the coefficients.

There is also a deeper problem with the proposed finite type space \(T=S_m\). In the paper, voters sharing a ranking may have different latent distances to candidates. Those distances affect social cost and therefore belong to the complete agent description required by the programme’s high-multiplicity notion. If they are omitted, ranking is not a complete type. If they are included, the type space is generally continuous and agents need not repeat. Fixing one distance signature per ranking restores finite multiplicity only by imposing a promise that is not the paper’s full metric-distortion model. The proponent’s fallback restricted mirror is therefore a different, already finite LP problem.

This negative case is not airtight: Theorem 1 certainly admits a sensible mass formulation, and the three-type witness makes high multiplicity entirely plausible. If the standard is merely “can one ask a coherent continuous version?”, the positive case wins. The stronger conclusion is narrower: this paper already works at the level of population proportions, and its named results supply no unmirrored computational-complexity problem. A continuous mirror would mostly rename the paper’s existing aggregate formulation rather than launch the computational continuization programme.
