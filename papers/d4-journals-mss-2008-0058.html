The case FOR (proponent)

There is a strong continuous-population connection here, but no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, establishes existence of an equilibrium in the finite voting game, characterizes the threshold behavior of voters, and proves that the equilibrium outcome is unique. Theorem 2, also proved here, shows that when discrete ideal-outcome distributions converge to a continuous distribution \(F\), their equilibrium outcomes converge to the unique \(e^\ast\) satisfying \(e^\ast=1-F(e^\ast)\). Theorem 3 extends the finite-game result to abstention. These are equilibrium and convergence results; none asserts a computational complexity classification or studies an algorithmic problem. Under your anchor rule, I cannot honestly present them as computational results.

The closest candidate, if an equilibrium characterization is accepted as a nonqualifying near-anchor, is a high-multiplicity version of Theorem 1. Let a society have finitely many types \(t\), each with rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Type \(t\) consists of voters with single-peaked preferences over the fraction of votes for \(A\), with ideal fraction \(q_t\). Let \(a_t\) be the mass of type \(t\) voting for \(A\), and \(e\) the resulting fraction of votes for \(A\). The problem is to find the outcome and type-level voting masses satisfying \(e=\sum_t a_t\), with \(a_t=0\) when \(q_t<e\), \(a_t=\mu_t\) when \(q_t>e\), and \(0\le a_t\le\mu_t\) when \(q_t=e\). Equivalently, for \(F(x)=\sum_{q_t\le x}\mu_t\) and \(f(x)=\sum_{q_t=x}\mu_t\), find \(e\) such that \(1-F(e)\le e\le1-F(e)+f(e)\), splitting the boundary type’s mass as needed.

This has a plausible regime: a very large electorate whose members fall into a small number \(\tau\) of preference cohorts with shared ideal vote shares. The action is which alternative each mass votes for; mass is the fraction of the electorate; the outcome is the aggregate share for \(A\). It mirrors the paper’s mandatory-voting equilibrium, and Theorem 1’s threshold characterization supplies the mathematical basis. Because an individual has no effect in a literal nonatomic game, the continuous solution should be defined as a limit of finite replications of these types. With rational masses and feasible ideal shares, those replications recover the paper’s finite-game setting. The authors would likely recognize that as their problem.

For this candidate, I would expect a polynomial-time answer: sort the \(q_t\)’s and scan the cumulative masses for the unique \(e\). That is a computational extension of the theorem, not a complexity result established by it. Theorem 2 is even more explicitly about a continuous population, but its atomless distribution over ideal shares need not have small finite support.

The weakness is substantial: the paper already studies the continuum limit and gives its fixed-point characterization, while the finite-type version appears straightforward to compute from that characterization. So the honest positive case is that the paper offers a natural, high-multiplicity equilibrium-computation question—not that it contains a named computational result for that question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the proponent concedes: there is no named computational result here to mirror. Theorem 1 proves an equilibrium characterization and uniqueness; Theorem 2 proves convergence to the continuous fixed point; Theorem 3 extends the equilibrium result to abstention. None studies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic classification. That leaves no computational anchor, even though the paper has important results.

The proposed finite-type society is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Voters sharing an ideal vote share can form cohorts, and aggregate mass is natural. So I would not reject this mirror on population grounds. But its proposed task—find the equilibrium outcome from rational masses and ideal shares—is already specified by the paper’s characterization: find \(e\) with \(1-F(e)\le e\le 1-F(e)+f(e)\). With finite support, sorting the ideal shares and scanning the cumulative masses computes it. That may be a well-posed computational corollary, but it is not a mirror of a computational result in the paper; it extracts a routine calculation from an equilibrium theorem.

The alternative, using the paper’s atomless distribution \(F\), does not supply a better complexity question. Theorem 2 already identifies the limiting outcome as the unique solution of \(e=1-F(e)\). To ask about running time, one would first have to choose how \(F\) is represented or accessed; that choice is absent from the paper and would determine the computational problem. And in a literal nonatomic game, each voter has no effect on the outcome, so every strategy profile is an equilibrium. The finite-replication limit is the meaningful selection, and the paper already characterizes it.

Abstention adds no new computational structure: Theorem 3 preserves the same outcome characterization. A computational question about uncertainty, robustness, or multiple alternatives might be interesting, but it would introduce a new problem rather than mirror one of this paper’s results.

The negative case is not airtight in the broadest sense: the cohort setting is plausible, and someone could study exact computation of its equilibrium. Its weakness is that this would be a small algorithmic follow-on to a paper that already studies the continuous population and gives the outcome characterization—not a worthwhile continuous mirror of a computational result.
