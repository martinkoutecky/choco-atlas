The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible anchor in this paper for a continuous computational mirror. Theorem 1, proved here, says that every reliable deterministic collaboration strategy—one whose accuracy is always at least that of the least accurate agent—must essentially defer to one fixed agent on interior prediction tuples, with a fixed choice when that agent predicts \(1/2\). The paper allows overrides when an agent is certain. This is a structural impossibility theorem, not a result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another computational class. Propositions 6, 7, and 9 and Lemma 8 support its proof; they do not supply a complexity result either.

The closest mass-based formulation would take a type to be a prediction vector \(p=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\), with mass \(\mu\) over prediction profiles and outcomes calibrated so that \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid P_i]=P_i\) for each agent \(i\). The action is a deterministic rule \(C(p)\in\{0,1\}\), and the objective is expected 0–1 accuracy. One could ask whether a single rule is always at least as accurate as the least accurate agent. But that is Theorem 1’s own question written in mass notation: the paper already quantifies over distributions of cases. Its agents remain a fixed small set, and the paper studies no computational problem over a high-multiplicity population. Treating this as the requested mirror would restate the paper’s theorem while missing the programme’s computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 1. It characterizes which deterministic rules guarantee accuracy at least as high as the least accurate agent: essentially, rules that defer to one fixed agent. That is a structural impossibility result. The theorem takes a collaboration rule and quantifies over settings; it gives no algorithmic problem with an encoded society as input, and no complexity or algorithmic result to mirror. Propositions 6, 7, 9, and Lemma 8 support its proof rather than adding computational anchors.

The proposed type space of prediction vectors \(p\in[0,1]^n\) makes the mismatch clear. A mass distribution over those vectors is the distribution of prediction cases already implicit in the paper’s \(D\). Requiring a rule to be reliable across all compatible distributions restates Theorem 1 in mass notation. If the joint outcome distribution is given, the best classification at each prediction vector is determined by its conditional positive-label probability; with finite support, this is a direct per-type calculation. If only the agents’ marginal calibration is known, the robust guarantee is the theorem’s existing fixed-agent result. Neither version produces a population-complexity question.

A more ambitious proposal would make the collaborators themselves a high-multiplicity population. That requires a type to capture an agent’s forecasting behavior across cases, calibration, and information—potentially a whole prediction function. Exact copies are redundant; genuinely different predictors retain the heterogeneity that makes the paper’s agent identities and joint behavior matter. A finite collection of such predictor types could be a sensible new model, but optimizing a policy over it would need additional ingredients such as review capacity, costs, or learned joint information. Those may motivate useful work on learning to defer, yet they are not problems this paper formulates or analyzes.

One could also encode a collaboration rule as a circuit and ask whether it is reliable. That would turn the theorem into a function-verification problem whose complexity depends on the circuit representation. It would not make the society continuous or expose a high-multiplicity algorithmic problem.

I cannot rule out a worthwhile future model involving many interchangeable reviewers and a constrained allocation of review effort. That is the honest weak point in a universal negative: new assumptions can create new problems. But the strongest candidate mirror here either repeats the paper’s distributional guarantee or becomes a different policy-optimization problem. The paper itself offers no computational result for a continuous society to anchor that work.
