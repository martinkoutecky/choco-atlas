The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying named computational anchors: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Its numbered items are equations. Section 5 reports empirical findings—for example, Real-Time Borda is faster between rings, while Real-Time Majority has lower positional error—but those are not named complexity results under your criterion. So there is no anchor to restate, and no honest way to present a continuous problem as the mirror of a numbered hardness or algorithmic theorem.

The strongest positive case is an unanchored mirror of the method in Section 4.1.4: *Mass-Weighted Real-Time Borda Aggregation*. Consider a large remote-operations crowd watching the same UAV feed and using the paper’s four-key interface. At a decision time, a worker’s type records how many votes they submitted for each of the four directions in the rolling window of length \(d\). A type is therefore \(t=(q_\uparrow,q_\downarrow,q_\leftarrow,q_\rightarrow)\). If the interface caps each worker at \(K\) votes per window, the possible type set is finite; a worker’s type contains everything this Borda calculation uses about them.

The instance consists of that type set, the rational mass \(\mu_t\) of workers of each type, and the paper’s mapping from each directional vote to Borda points for the nine combined linear-and-angular output actions. For an output action \(a\), let \(b_v(a)\) be the points contributed by a vote for direction \(v\). The score is
\[ S_\mu(a)=\sum_t \mu_t\sum_{v\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow,\leftarrow,\rightarrow\}}q_v b_v(a). \]
The question is to return an action maximizing \(S_\mu(a)\), with any maximizer accepted as a solution. The expected answer is tractability by direct summation over the \(\tau\) supported types and nine actions. This preserves the paper’s input votes, Borda rankings, expanded output set, and score-maximizing decision. The cap \(K\) is an operational assumption for a fixed finite type domain; the paper itself does not impose it.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime would be a large online workforce assigned to repeated UAV surveys: thousands of operators, one standardized interface, and a bounded number of votes per operator per short window. If many workers submit the same vote histogram, then \(N\) can be far larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct types. Authors could plausibly recognize this as scaling their aggregation rule to a population distribution. It covers the Borda aggregation computation, not the paper’s claims about flight speed, path accuracy, or reaction time.

This does not establish that any discrete hardness dissolves: the paper gives no such hardness result, and the continuous computation is already a small weighted-sum problem. The further questions are whether a mass-based implementation preserves finite-crowd behavior under a precise tie rule, how its action changes under adversarial mass, and how to update type masses as votes enter and leave the rolling window.

The weakest point is the gap between this regime and the evidence in the paper: its experiments had at most 13 simultaneous users, and the reported contribution is empirical performance, not a complexity landscape. The mass formulation is a credible population-scale version of the Borda method, but it cannot supply the requested theorem-anchored continuization case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no theorem-anchored result to mirror here. The paper’s numbered items are equations; its findings are experimental comparisons of aggregators in simulated UAV scenarios. It does not state a complexity result for a computational problem. That is the strongest negative point.

The proposed mass-weighted Borda calculation is a valid continuous snapshot of one method. A stronger version could let types record each worker’s current rolling-window input, avoiding the artificial vote cap. But the action still depends only on a fixed set of nine scores. This recasts the aggregation formula in terms of masses; it does not mirror a complexity claim the paper makes. And it does not capture the paper’s main empirical claims about flight time, path error, and reactions to obstacles. Those depend on the ordered stream of votes, the changing camera view, and the drone’s motion. Modeling those would require a distribution over worker behavior through time and its interaction with the environment. That may be useful research, but it is a substantial new model, not the proposed snapshot mirror.

The negative case is weaker against a broader follow-on. The paper explicitly calls for robustness guarantees under malicious crowd members. One could study how a mass of unreliable or adversarial worker types affects performance in repeated UAV tasks. That is a plausible continuous-population question the authors might recognize. But it needs a formal definition of beneficial input and a dynamic model; the paper provides neither as a computational problem.

So the proposed Borda anchor does not establish a worthwhile complexity mirror, and there are no named computational results to anchor one to. Still, I cannot honestly rule out every useful continuous follow-on: the paper’s own robustness agenda leaves that door open.
