Why no mirror

Theorems 3.3 and 3.4 show that ordinal feedback can leave every post-training algorithm with high distortion; they do not show that computing a solution is hard or provide an algorithmic guarantee. The proposed equal-mass society simply rewrites the paper’s expected utility over queries as a mass-weighted sum, while arbitrary rational masses would be an extension not established by these results.

What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation matches Theorems 3.3 and 3.4 in their uniform-query cases. The anchors establish no result for arbitrary rational masses; Theorem 3.5 is attributed to prior work, and Section 4 reports experiments and interpretation.

Open questions for a prover

  • For nonuniform rational masses \(\mu_t\), what distortion bounds hold for preference-only routing over a specified feasible policy class?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s query-routing problem. The paper itself treats queries as voters and pretrained circuits as alternatives. A continuous version lets query *occurrences* carry mass, while preserving the model’s central decision: how to route queries through its limited representations to circuits.

A plausible regime is a large tutoring service handling repeated standardized questions. An agent is an incoming request; a type is the complete prompt, context, and evaluation setting that determine the available circuit responses, their utilities, and how the model can route the request. Exact recurring requests share a type. If millions of requests arrive over a period but there are far fewer recurring types, then \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of request traffic of type \(t\). Open-ended traffic with mostly unique prompts is a weaker regime; the case here is repeated, standardized workloads. This scenario fits the authors’ own analogy particularly well because their “voters” are queries.

Here is a continuous problem that preserves the model’s capacity constraints. An instance has a finite support \(T\) of query types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a pretrained circuit set \(\mathcal S_0\), representations \(Z\), and an allowed family \(\Phi\) of query-to-representation maps. A feasible post-training policy is \(\pi(t)=g(\phi(t))\), with \(\phi\in\Phi\) and \(g\) routing representations to circuits, as in the paper’s post-training model. Each type has cardinal utilities \(u_t(s)\) for circuits \(s\), but the algorithm receives only preference information. Its goal is to return a feasible policy maximizing

\[ U_\mu(\pi;u)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{s\in\mathcal S_0}\pi(t)(s)u_t(s). \]

The solution is the routing policy; its quality is measured by distortion, the ratio of the best feasible policy’s utility to the returned policy’s utility. The policy class retains the paper’s joint routing and circuit-selection decision, rather than reducing the task to selecting one circuit for the whole population.

Lead anchor — Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper. Its continuous question is Continuous Linear-BT Routing: given the workload, pretrained circuits, and routing class, what worst-case distortion can a preference-only algorithm guarantee when it has access to pairwise comparisons generated by the linear Bradley–Terry model,

\[ \Pr[s_i\succ_t s_j]=\frac{u_t(s_i)}{u_t(s_i)+u_t(s_j)}? \]

The algorithm returns a feasible routing policy, and an evaluator scores it against the best feasible policy under the hidden cardinal utilities. Theorem 3.4 says that for *any* pretrained model and post-training algorithm, there is an objective for which the resulting model’s distortion is at least \(\Omega(|\mathcal S_0|)\), even when utilities are bounded in \([0,1]\), in the regime \(|Q|\gg |S|,|Z|\). Thus even access to the comparison probabilities, representing unlimited noisy preference data, cannot guarantee low distortion.

Supporting anchor — Theorem 3.3, also proved in this paper. Its question is Continuous Ordinal Routing, with noiseless ordinal rankings for each query type and circuit pair. Theorem 3.3 says that for any pretrained model and any post-training algorithm, there is a utility objective under which the output has distortion at least \(\Omega(\sqrt{|\mathcal S_0|})\) in the same large-query regime. This lower bound still holds with utilities in \([0,1]\), and even when utilities sum to one across circuits for each query. This is a distinct feedback model: it establishes a lower bound under exact rankings and the stronger per-query normalization condition. Theorem 3.4 is my lead because its bound grows faster and survives the specified noisy comparison model.

For both questions, the theorem directly applies to the equal-mass continuous instance \(\mu_t=1/|T|\), using the paper’s finite query set as the support. Repeating each type many times leaves the objective and distortion unchanged while making the number of request occurrences much larger than the number of types. The paper proves its bounds for this uniform subfamily; extending them to arbitrary rational masses is a natural further question, not a claim I would attribute to these theorems.

I expect these mirrors to land closest to Class B in the programme’s trichotomy: the obstruction already appears in the paper’s finite-query setting and survives when those queries represent mass. The result is an approximation or information lower bound, however, rather than NP-hardness or a runtime lower bound. It says that no amount of computation repairs the loss of cardinal information under the stated assumptions. Further questions include how distortion depends on \(|\Phi|\), \(|Z|\), support size, and nonuniform masses; how many cardinal probes suffice for a routing model with districts; and when supplied cardinal utilities make the best feasible routing policy efficiently computable.

The mirror covers Theorems 3.3 and 3.4 and the underlying routing decision they analyze. It does not rely on the Section 4 experiments about reasoning behavior, or on Theorem 3.5, which the paper attributes to earlier work.

My weakest point is that continuization gives a natural workload model, but it does not create the paper’s lower bounds: those are already proved for a finite, uniform query electorate. Nor are they computational-complexity results in the usual running-time sense. The regime also depends on genuinely repeated, fully specified requests; grouping merely similar free-form prompts into a type could change the routing behavior and utilities that define the problem. The positive case rests on the narrower claim that, in repeated-query deployments, the authors’ own routing problem has a direct and recognizable continuous-population form.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed continuous routing problem is already built into the paper’s formal model. The paper treats queries as voters and evaluates a routing policy by \(\mathbb E_{q\sim D}[u(q,\pi(q))]\), with \(D\) uniform over \(Q\). For finite \(Q\), set \(T=Q\) and \(\mu_q=1/|Q|\). Then the continuous objective is exactly the paper’s objective. If each query is repeated \(r\) times, so the request population has size \(r|Q|\), its type masses are still \(\mu_q=1/|Q|\); the policy class, comparison data, and objective are unchanged. This makes the proposed equal-mass mirror a restatement of the distributional model, not a new high-multiplicity problem.

That applies to both anchors. Theorem 3.3 asks what distortion is unavoidable with noiseless rankings over circuits for each query; Theorem 3.4 asks the corresponding question with linear Bradley–Terry comparisons. Replacing the query index \(q\) with a type \(t\) and writing an expectation as a weighted sum changes neither question. Theorem 3.4’s comparison probabilities and Theorem 3.3’s rankings are already indexed by the units whose mass the mirror would aggregate. The two theorems are distinct feedback assumptions, but they do not provide two distinct population continuizations.

There is a real constraint on finding a more compressed type space. A type must preserve everything the routing problem uses: the query’s circuit responses, its preferences, and how the permitted maps in \(\Phi\) route it. Grouping merely similar prompts could merge queries that differ on one of those features, so it would change the problem. Keeping types fully faithful can leave \(\tau=|Q|\). Repeating those types raises the number of requests without changing the number of routing decisions or introducing population-level constraints: there is no mass transfer, capacity constraint, or winner condition coupling individual requests. Mass only weights the average utility.

The best alternative is to make traffic frequencies explicit and ask the distortion question for arbitrary rational masses \(\mu_t\), rather than the paper’s uniform \(D\). That is a coherent extension, but it is a new weighted preference-learning question, not a result established by either theorem. And it still would not, as stated, chart the algorithmic complexity that motivates ChoCo: the algorithm receives preference information while cardinal utility remains hidden, and the theorems allow unlimited computation. The paper also gives no finite input representation for \(\Phi\) from which running time could be analyzed. Supplying known utilities and an explicit encoding of \(\Phi\) could produce a conventional optimization problem, but that would no longer mirror the information-theoretic question in Theorems 3.3 and 3.4.

Section 4 does not offer another computational anchor: its reasoning results are experiments and interpretation. Theorem 3.5 is attributed to prior work, and the proponent correctly does not rely on it.

This case against has a clear limit. Repeated standardized tutoring queries are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and exact repeated prompts can be full types; lack of a sensible population interpretation is not a sound objection. The case is that the equal-mass question is already expressed by the paper’s expected-utility model, while the more interesting weighted or runtime-bounded question requires a new formal problem. If ChoCo counts distributional information bounds for model routing as computational social choice, the proponent’s mirror may survive. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile related project; the negative case is strongest for the narrower claim that this paper supplies a distinct continuous mirror.
