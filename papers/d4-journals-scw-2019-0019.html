What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns the existence and impossibility conditions in Theorems 1–3; it leaves Proposition 1’s rule characterization, Proposition 2’s symmetry characterization, and Corollary 1’s quota-rule impossibility outside its scope.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a very large expert body whose members draw from the same four evidence types, with the same signal accuracies and common preferences. A society could be represented by masses \(\mu_t\) of voters of each type, with at most four types even as the population grows. That is a natural population-level description.

The closest continuous question would ask whether a rule \(F\) from aggregate ballot masses to judgments can make informative voting yield the full-information-efficient judgment for every type-mass distribution. But in an atomless population, one voter cannot affect the outcome, so truthful voting is an equilibrium without meaningful incentive constraints. The question would retain the information-aggregation aim while changing the paper’s strategic-voting problem.

The named results do not provide eligible computational anchors. Theorem 1, proved here, says that for any common utility function, a rule making informative voting efficient exists exactly when any two type profiles with the same set of \(pq\)-types have some common efficient decision. Theorem 2, proved here, rules out such a rule under simple preferences. Theorem 3, proved here, says that under consequentialist preferences, such a rule exists exactly when \(pq\) is efficient only for the unanimous all-\(pq\) type profile. These are existence and impossibility characterizations; none states a complexity classification such as membership in P or hardness. Theorem 3’s parameter condition could motivate a separate algorithmic problem, but the paper does not analyze its running time or complexity.

So the positive case is that the paper’s finite type structure fits a high-multiplicity population naturally. Its weakest point is decisive for this task: a continuous version would lose individual pivotality, and there is no named computational result to anchor the requested continuous complexity question. I cannot honestly supply a per-anchor problem or assign it a place in the programme’s complexity trichotomy.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The expert-population story is plausible: the paper has four evidence types and common signal accuracies, so many voters can share a type. I would not reject a mirror on multiplicity grounds. The stronger objection is that the proposed mirror drops the paper’s central strategic problem, and none of its named results supplies a computational problem for ChoCo to study.

In the paper’s Bayesian game, an individual’s vote can affect the collective decision when that voter is pivotal. In an atomless society, changing one voter’s ballot changes no aggregate mass, so it changes no outcome. Truthful voting is then an equilibrium for that reason alone. One can still ask whether an aggregate rule maps informative votes to full-information-efficient judgments, but that is a question about existence of an aggregation rule, not the paper’s question about incentives in equilibrium. Preserving strategic force by letting an entire type group deviate would make groups the players and replace individual Nash deviations with coordinated coalition deviations. Keeping voters pivotal instead requires retaining finite agents, or adding a sampling or noise mechanism. Those may motivate other models, but they do not preserve this paper’s game while continuizing its population.

That mismatch applies to each theorem the proponent cites. Theorem 1 characterizes when a rule exists, using a condition on which type profiles share an efficient decision. A continuous version could ask for the analogous existence condition over mass profiles; it would still be a structural characterization, not a complexity result. Theorem 2’s impossibility under simple preferences could likewise be restated for mass profiles, but it remains an impossibility theorem about efficient aggregation. Theorem 3 gives an existence condition under consequentialist preferences, and Proposition 1 describes the resulting rules. A mass-based version would still ask which rules exist. None asks for an exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithm, or classifies the complexity of a computational task. The paper’s explicit \(A,B\) condition does not turn that characterization into a complexity anchor.

There is a further warning about carrying over the signal model literally. Signals are independent across voters conditional on the state, and each signal is more likely to support the truth than its negation. In the continuum limit, the realized proportions of signal types settle at their state-conditional proportions. Those proportions identify which propositions are true. Thus the limit changes both pivotality and the uncertainty that motivates the paper’s strategic analysis. One could seek a continuous model with persistent aggregate uncertainty, but that would require additional structure not supplied by this paper.

So the strongest case against is specific: this paper does not furnish a computational result to mirror, and its natural continuous-population translation turns its incentive question into a different kind of aggregation question. I cannot rule out a useful continuation in continuum voting theory; the weakness of the negative case is that the large expert-body regime is credible. But making it computationally rich would require adding a new problem, rather than extracting a ChoCo complexity mirror from Theorems 1–3.
