The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of agent types \(T\), a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), binary eligibility profiles over item types, and an adversarially ordered sequence of \(H\) arrival batches with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), what is the minimum worst-case number \(B\) of advice bits needed by a possibly randomized online mechanism to attain the offline maximum matched mass on every sequence? For the egalitarian variant, what is the minimum \(B\) needed to maximize the minimum expected per-capita utility among supported types? At batch \(j\), the mechanism irrevocably assigns eligible unmatched mass \(x_{tj}\), subject to \(\sum_t x_{tj}\le q_j\) and \(\sum_j x_{tj}\le\mu_t\); the oracle knows the full sequence.

The model it lives in

A finite-support population with type masses \(\mu_t\), item types with binary eligibility profiles, and ordered batches of per-capita item supply. Online decisions are irrevocable type-to-batch mass flows \(x_{tj}\); an oracle that knows the future may send at most \(B\) bits. Measure performance by total matched mass or minimum expected per-capita utility across supported types, and compare with the corresponding offline optimum.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s advice lower bounds for expected matching size and binary-utility egalitarian welfare. It leaves the competitive-ratio results for the named mechanisms and the other advice bounds unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the minimum worst-case advice \(B\) for exact online optimality in matched mass and in minimum supported-type utility, as a function of \(\tau\), \(H\), and the input bit length?
  • Does randomization reduce the minimum advice requirement when advice describes mass flows rather than named recipients?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3, which states that no randomized online algorithm using fewer than \(\lceil\log_2 n!\rceil\) advice bits can maximize expected matching size (ES); even with \(0/1\) utilities, no such algorithm can maximize egalitarian welfare (EW). The theorem is proved in this paper. Its ES argument uses the deterministic advice lower bound from Theorem 2 of Miyazaki et al. (2014); the paper gives the randomized reduction and the EW argument using Birkhoff’s theorem.

A natural high-multiplicity setting is a large allocation system—say, assigning scarce service slots to a large recurring population. The agents may be many, while their complete eligibility profiles are relatively few: two agents have the same type precisely when they have the same utility for every item type. Items arrive in ordered waves, and an oracle that knows the future can send advice to an online mechanism. Batch sizes scale with the population, so the mass model still represents each person receiving at most one whole item.

Here is the continuous problem I would ask the authors to study: Type-Mass Advice Complexity for Online Matching. An instance has a finite type set \(T\), with \(|T|=\tau\), and a society distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of agents of type \(t\). Each type has a binary eligibility profile over a finite set of item types. The input also has an ordered sequence of \(H\) arrival batches. Batch \(j\) contains \(q_j\) units of per-capita supply of item type \(g_j\), with rational \(q_j\); take total supply to be \(1\) for the perfect-matching case. Masses and supplies are encoded as binary rationals.

At batch \(j\), an online mechanism chooses mass \(x_{tj}\) of currently unmatched type-\(t\) agents to receive the arriving item type. Choices must respect eligibility, batch supply, and remaining type mass:

\[ x_{tj}=0 \text{ if type }t\text{ is ineligible for }g_j,\qquad \sum_t x_{tj}\le q_j,\qquad \sum_{i\le j}x_{ti}\le \mu_t. \]

The mechanism sees batches in order and may use at most \(B\) bits of advice from an oracle that knows the full sequence. Its offline comparator sees the whole sequence and maximizes total matched mass \(\sum_{t,j}x_{tj}\). The problem is to determine the minimum worst-case advice \(B\) needed for an online mechanism to attain that offline optimum on every adversarial sequence. The same definition can use EW instead: maximize the minimum, over supported types \(t\), of the type’s expected assigned share \(\sum_j x_{tj}/\mu_t\). A solution should give a mechanism and an advice upper bound, together with a matching lower bound.

I expect the sharp advice-complexity question to be continuum-specifically hard (Class C), though the underlying offline allocation is a rational flow or LP problem. The discrete \(\log_2 n!\) lower bound does not automatically survive when agents with the same complete profile are grouped: advice can specify mass by type instead of naming recipients. With rational inputs, an offline flow plan has a polynomial-size encoding, giving a polynomial advice upper bound; the open computational question is how small the worst-case advice can be, and whether randomization helps. Any remaining lower bound would need to arise from future-dependent choices among types and batches, rather than permutations of named agents.

This directly mirrors the paper’s advice question and ES/EW objectives while changing only what high multiplicity requires: type-level mass replaces individual recipient identities, and item supply scales with population. It does not cover the paper’s competitive-ratio results for its four named mechanisms, its axiomatic discussion, or its other advice bounds.

The weak point is that batching changes the operational model. A mass allocation can split a wave across types, whereas the paper’s mechanism makes one recipient decision per arriving item. Batches are a plausible per-capita supply adjustment for a large market, and the online order and oracle remain central, but the authors or a referee could reasonably ask whether the resulting advice problem is still their problem. More importantly, Theorem 3’s factorial lower bound depends on recipient identities—the feature the continuous mirror deliberately removes. That is why the mirror is strongest as a new advice-complexity question, not as a claim that the paper’s lower bound transfers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 3’s lower bound is tied to advice about *which named agent* receives each item. In a high-multiplicity model, agents with the same complete type are interchangeable, and advice can describe flows between types and arrival batches. With \(\tau\) types and \(H\) batches, that plan has at most \(\tau H\) flow values. So the theorem’s factorial advice bound is a poor guide to the continuous question: the proposed mirror is really advice complexity for online type-capacity matching.

That weakens the connection to this paper’s particular result, but it does not defeat the mirror. A recurring allocation system with finitely many eligibility profiles, per-capita item supply, and an adversarial sequence of arrival waves is plausible. Mass choices can preserve irrevocable online decisions, and the advice question remains well-defined. The paper’s expected matching size becomes matched mass; its egalitarian welfare can be the minimum expected per-capita utility across supported types. Those are recognizable continuizations of its objectives.

The batch objection is also not decisive. A batch can represent many indivisible items arriving over a short interval, with different agents receiving whole items even though the type-level allocation is fractional. The model can retain the online order and finite-support population without treating outcomes as lotteries over a fixed finite society. Theorem 1’s competitive-ratio questions and Theorem 4’s partial-advice questions could be posed in the same setting.

So I cannot honestly make the universal negative case the prompt asks for. The identity dependence of Theorem 3 explains why its exact lower bound need not transfer, but the programme explicitly treats that kind of change as a potentially valuable result. The mass-based advice problem survives as a worthwhile mirror; my negative case is weak at that point.
