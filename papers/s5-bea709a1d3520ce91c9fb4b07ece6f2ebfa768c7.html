The continuous mirror question

Given rational total masses \(A,Q\) of identical collectors and tasks, with \(Q>A\), a finite initial partition into coalition mass pairs \((a_j,q_j)\), and rational \(\mu,\lambda,\delta\), does every maximal sequence of legal switches terminate after finitely many moves? Fix \(\beta=\tfrac12\) and reliable direct transmission. For a coalition with \(a,q>0\), let \(\rho=q\lambda/(a\mu)\); when \(\rho<1\), let \(D(a,q)=\rho^2/[2a\mu(1-\rho)]\), \(L(a,q)=q\lambda\), \(V(a,q)=\delta L(a,q)^\beta/D(a,q)^{1-\beta}\), and the per-member payoff be \(u(a,q)=V(a,q)/(a+q)\). Give empty or unstable coalitions value zero. A cohort may move any positive rational mass to another coalition or a singleton if its destination payoff is strictly higher; it records the coalition it left, while any remaining mass retains its prior history. Collector mass cannot leave if that would strand positive task mass without collectors. A negative answer means an infinite legal switch sequence exists.

The model it lives in

A distribution of rational mass over two complete types, collectors and tasks, partitioned into coalitions; each coalition's value and per-member payoff are determined by its collector and task masses through the paper's throughput-delay utility. Decision variables are the mass amounts transferred between coalitions, with histories tracked for each resulting cohort. The question is whether every sequence of strictly improving cohort switches must be finite.

The objection that survived

The deviator becomes a positive-mass cohort rather than a named individual, and repeated splits can create cohorts with distinct histories; this materially extends the dynamics even though the proponent specifies cohort moves and histories.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's termination guarantee and the related no-profitable-switch endpoint of Proposition 2. It leaves aside route selection, relaying, wireless performance, and the paper's simulation results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an instance admit an infinite legal sequence of rational-mass switches?
  • Is there a potential function or a restriction on moves that guarantees termination?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest named computational anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that from *any* initial network partition, the proposed hedonic coalition formation phase always converges to a final partition of disjoint coalitions. The proof uses the history sets to rule out revisiting partitions, then uses the finiteness of the set of partitions. This is an algorithmic guarantee, but not a polynomial-time result: the paper gives no complexity class or polynomial bound for convergence.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large sensor deployment in one compact area, with many identical source queues and a smaller population of operator-owned, identical collector nodes. A task type completely specifies its arrival rate, location, and transmission conditions; an agent type specifies its capacity and other relevant properties. For example, there may be millions of queues and collectors but only two types, so the support size stays fixed as the population grows. Co-location makes this a clean restriction of the paper’s task-allocation model: collectors still pool their capacity across tasks, while relays and travel between tasks are absent.

My lead mirror is Mass-Switch Universal Termination. Its instance consists of rational agent and task masses \(A,Q\), with \(Q>A\), an initial finite partition into coalition mass pairs \((a_j,q_j)\), and rational \(\mu,\lambda,\delta\). All tasks are identical queues with arrival rate \(\lambda\); all agents are identical collectors with capacity \(\mu\). Fix \(\beta=\tfrac12\) and assume reliable direct transmission. For a coalition with agent mass \(a\) and task mass \(q\), define \(\rho=q\lambda/(a\mu)\). When \(a,q>0\) and \(\rho<1\), the mass extension of the paper’s zero-switchover delay term is \(D(a,q)=\rho^2/[2a\mu(1-\rho)]\), and its throughput is \(L(a,q)=q\lambda\). Set \(V(a,q)=\delta L(a,q)^\beta/D(a,q)^{1-\beta}\), and each member’s payoff to \(u(a,q)=V(a,q)/(a+q)\), the mass version of the paper’s equal-fair allocation. Give \(V\) value zero for empty or unstable coalitions.

A move transfers a positive rational mass \(\varepsilon\) of one type from its current coalition to another coalition or to a singleton, when the moved mass strictly prefers its destination payoff to its current payoff. The moved mass inherits a record of coalitions it has left, as in the paper’s history rule; collector mass cannot leave if that would strand positive task mass without collectors. The question is: does every maximal sequence of legal switches from the given initial partition terminate after finitely many moves at a partition with no profitable legal switch? A solution is a yes/no answer, with “no” meaning there is an infinite legal sequence.

This is a faithful question about the paper’s coalition-formation phase: the decision variables are task and collector masses assigned to coalitions, and the move criterion is the paper’s individual payoff preference extended to a mass transfer. The authors could plausibly recognize it as their problem in a high-multiplicity batch-allocation regime. The paper’s companion stability result makes “no profitable switch” the relevant endpoint.

I expect this mirror to be a Class C candidate: the finite Bell-number argument no longer works, because coalition mass profiles range over infinitely many states and histories rule out exact revisits without giving a finite bound. A potential function might still guarantee termination, so this is not a hardness claim. The paper itself does not establish the answer for the continuous version. The next questions are whether some instances admit infinite improving runs, and whether a Lyapunov-style quantity or an approximate stopping rule can guarantee convergence.

The weak point is that the paper moves individual players, while this mirror moves positive mass cohorts. A truly atomless individual has no effect on coalition totals, so simply replacing people with infinitesimal players would erase the strategic externality that drives the paper’s switches. Cohort moves preserve that interaction, and batch assignment is plausible for operator-managed sensor deployments, but authors might regard it as a co-modification of the decision process. This mirror also omits route selection and relaying; it covers the coalition-formation convergence result, not the paper’s full wireless system.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest on the proposed anchor’s *kind* of result. Theorem 1 proves that a particular finite-player switching process terminates; Proposition 2 identifies the resulting partition as Nash-stable. Neither gives a complexity bound or an algorithm for a standard input/output problem of the kind ChoCo is charting. Section IV-C’s operation counts do not change that: the paper offers no named complexity result for task allocation. So Mass-Switch Universal Termination stretches the paper’s result into a new continuous-dynamics question.

The proposed mass version also lacks a canonical analogue of the paper’s move. The original switch is made by one named player, whose history affects whether they may return to a coalition. In an atomless population, one player has zero mass and cannot change coalition payoffs. A positive-mass move restores that effect, but it makes a cohort the deviating actor; repeated splits can give different parts of a type different histories. Tracking those histories can generate unboundedly many cohorts, while allowing any positive rational \(\varepsilon\) permits arbitrarily small moves and potentially infinite sequences. These are not cosmetic changes: they determine what counts as a legal switch and what termination means.

The best repairs still shift the question. A minimum move size or mass grid restores a finite-state process, but the answer then depends on the chosen resolution. Dropping history gives a memoryless dynamics whose stability properties need not mirror this algorithm. An approximate stopping rule or a continuum equilibrium could be worth studying, but neither is the result the paper proves. In the proposed co-located, zero-switchover setting, much of the wireless structure disappears into a ratio of load to capacity; keeping locations, routes, and relays makes coalition value depend on which tasks are present, information a simple mass-pair description does not retain.

I would not use “there are only a few agents” as the objection: the paper’s sensor-cohort setting could plausibly have high multiplicity. And I cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A richer, carefully specified cohort model might be useful. The case against is that the sole anchor does not yet yield a well-defined continuous computational problem without changing the move semantics that make the original theorem work.
