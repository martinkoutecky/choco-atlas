The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is the paper’s actual contribution: its named results are about implementability, balanced transfers, equilibrium multiplicity, and revelation. They are not complexity results. The closest candidate is Proposition 4, which gives two systems of \(2n\) equations for bounding equilibria. But it specifies neither an encoded computational problem nor an algorithm with a running-time guarantee. One could ask how hard it is to solve a suitably encoded version of those systems; that would be a new computational question built around the proposition, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The best proposed mirror is a large public-goods population: represent agents by a distribution of valuation types, then ask how to design transfers that preserve truthful reporting and supermodularity, or how to compute equilibrium bounds. That population regime is plausible. The difficulty is that the paper’s model has finitely many strategic players, each with a private type, an individual report, and an individual allocation and transfer. A population model would need new mechanism and incentive definitions. In a standard anonymous model, one agent’s report has zero effect on aggregate reports; preserving the paper’s individual incentive interactions may require different primitives or scaling. That is substantial economic modeling before any computational question is even specified.

So I would reject the proposed extension as a mirror of this paper’s results: it is a potentially interesting continuum-agent mechanism-design project, but the paper supplies no computational result for it to mirror. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous-agent version could ever be developed; the large-population public-goods setting is the honest weak point in that broader claim.
