The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest computational-mirror case from this chapter under your anchor rule: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result.

The closest results are Facts 1–3. Fact 1 gives the exact threshold for supermajorities to support a minimally inconsistent set of \(k\) propositions: this is possible at or below \((k-1)/k\). Fact 2 says a threshold above \((K-1)/K\) guarantees consistency, where \(K\) is the largest minimally inconsistent set available in the agenda. Fact 3 says a threshold above \(1-1/k\) preserves \(k\)-consistency. Fact 2 draws on Dietrich and List (2007); Fact 3 is presented as a corollary of the chapter’s overlap argument. These are substantive mathematical guarantees, but they do not classify the computational complexity of a problem.

There is a plausible population model nearby: in a large expert community considering a fixed agenda, each type is a consistent (or \(k\)-consistent) belief set, and \(\mu_J\) is the fraction of the community with belief set \(J\). A proposition’s support is the total mass of types that believe it. One could ask which support threshold guarantees that the accepted propositions are \(k\)-consistent. The chapter’s threshold arguments appear to extend naturally to such masses. But that would mirror its consistency analysis, not a named computational result. There is therefore no eligible anchor here, and no per-anchor computational question or tractability class to report.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

No eligible computational anchor appears in this chapter. Facts 1–3 are mathematical guarantees about consistency under a supermajority rule. They do not state the complexity of a computational problem or give an algorithmic result.

The best candidate is the proponent’s society of belief types \(J\), with mass \(\mu_J\), and the question of which threshold guarantees \(k\)-consistency. But Fact 3 already answers that question for arbitrary masses: if \(r\le k\) propositions each have support greater than \(1-1/k\), the intersection of their supporter sets has positive mass, since the union of their complements has mass less than \(r/k\le1\). An individually \(k\)-consistent agent cannot support a minimally inconsistent set of at most \(k\) propositions. Facts 1 and 2 have the same direct extension. This is a continuous-population interpretation of the argument, but it leaves no computational question to solve.

A stronger proposal might ask for the least threshold that guarantees consistency for a given agenda. In the chapter’s worst-case result, that threshold depends on \(K\), the size of the largest minimally inconsistent set. If the agenda is fixed, \(K\) is fixed too; if the agenda becomes an input, the proposed task is to compute an agenda property. A realized society might motivate a different task, such as finding the lowest threshold that keeps its accepted propositions consistent. That could be a research question, but it adds a computational objective absent from the chapter. The mass calculations themselves are just sums over the supported types; any harder work concerns the agenda’s logic, and does not arise from making the population continuous.

Large communities with repeated belief types are plausible high-multiplicity settings, so I would not reject the model on population grounds. My negative case rests on the missing computational result to mirror, and the fact that the natural continuous reading of the chapter’s facts is already answered by their proofs. I cannot establish the broader claim that no worthwhile computational study could ever be built around epistemic populations. Such a study would be a new research direction, though, rather than a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
