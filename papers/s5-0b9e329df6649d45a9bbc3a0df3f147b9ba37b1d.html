The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) of complete pair types, where each type specifies its center and compatibility and value relations, and allowed cycle patterns \(e\) of length at most three, does there exist a feasible mass plan \(x_e\ge 0\) with \(\sum_e \ell_e x_e\ge q\) that is rejection-proof? Feasibility requires \(\sum_e a_{te}x_e\le \mu_t\) for every type \(t\), where \(a_{te}\) counts its participants in pattern \(e\) and \(\ell_e\) is the pattern's transplant count. For each center \(i\), define its best rejection response \(R_i(x)\) by maximizing \(\sum_e w_{i,e}y_e\) subject to the same type-mass capacities, allowing only internal patterns and proposed shared patterns, with \(y_e\le x_e\) for shared patterns. Require \(R_i(x)\le \sum_e w_{i,e}x_e\) for every center.

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over complete pair types, each labeled by center and with type-level compatibility and values; nonnegative mass \(x_e\) assigned to allowed cycle patterns under type-capacity constraints; and, for each center, a best-response mass plan \(y\) that can rematch internally and use shared patterns only up to their proposed mass. The decision asks whether some plan reaches transplant threshold \(q\) while its proposed value for every center is at least that center's best rejection-response value.

The objection that survived

The paper and the proposed scenario do not establish that real pools have few complete compatibility types relative to their size; with exact compatibility, the number of supported types may approach the participant count and leave little compression.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4's maximum-value rejection-proof decision problem in the pairs-only, unit-value, cycle-length-at-most-three case. It leaves the MaxInt mechanism, withholding strategies, and the experimental comparisons outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether such a rejection-proof mass plan exists, and can the response constraints be represented by a tractable formulation?
  • Under what conditions can finite high-multiplicity rejection-proof solutions be approximated by mass plans, or mass plans be rounded while controlling lost rejection incentives?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Rejection-Proof Kidney Exchange (CRP-KEP), a continuous version of the paper’s maximum-value rejection-proof problem.

The anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper (with the reduction in Appendix C). It says that, for a set of agents and a directed compatibility graph partitioned among them, with fixed maximum cycle length \(K\ge 3\), any maximum chain length \(L\), and an integer threshold \(t\), it is \(\Sigma_2^p\)-complete to decide whether there is a rejection-proof exchange solution of value at least \(t\). The paper proves hardness even for unit transplant values. This is the strongest anchor because it asks whether optimizing social value subject to the paper’s central strategic guarantee is computationally hard.

Here is a mass version of that same question. Consider a large regional or national exchange pool in which each center has many patients, but compatibility and value are determined by a much smaller catalog of complete participant types. A type records the center, whether the participant is a donor–recipient pair or a non-directed donor, and all compatibility and value information the model uses. Compatibility must be determined by types: if two pairs have different compatibility relations, they are different types. In the intended regime, the pool might have hundreds or thousands of participants but only tens or hundreds of supported types. This is a scenario-specific claim, not a claim that all kidney exchange pools have low type diversity.

For the direct counterpart of the hardness subcase, take pairs only, unit transplant values, and cycles of length at most three. Let \(\mu_t\) be the rational mass of type \(t\), and let \(\mathcal E\) be the allowed type-level cycle patterns. A pattern can use a type more than once; write \(a_{te}\) for the number of its participants of type \(t\). A plan assigns nonnegative mass \(x_e\) to each pattern and obeys
\[ \sum_{e\in\mathcal E} a_{te}x_e\le \mu_t \qquad \text{for every type }t. \]
Each unit of \(x_e\) represents a whole ordinary exchange cycle for that mass of participants. Fractional values mean that a fraction of a type takes part in a pattern; no individual receives a fractional transplant.

The strategic condition should follow the paper’s rejection game closely. Let \(E_i\) be the cycles internal to center \(i\), and let \(E_{\mathrm{sh}}^i\) be the shared cycles involving \(i\). If \(w_{i,e}\) counts center \(i\)’s patients transplanted in pattern \(e\), accepting the plan gives that center \(u_i(x)=\sum_e w_{i,e}x_e\). Its best rejection response has value
\[ R_i(x)=\max_y\left\{\sum_e w_{i,e}y_e: \sum_e a_{te}y_e\le\mu_t\ \forall t,\quad y_e\ge0,\quad y_e=0\ \text{outside }E_i\cup E_{\mathrm{sh}}^i,\quad y_e\le x_e\ \text{for }e\in E_{\mathrm{sh}}^i \right\}. \]
The bound \(y_e\le x_e\) preserves the paper’s rule that a center can use a shared cycle only if it was proposed. The bound does not restrict its internal rematching. A plan is rejection-proof when \(R_i(x)\le u_i(x)\) for every center.

The question: given the type masses, compatibility relation, cycle patterns, and rational threshold \(q\), does there exist a feasible plan \(x\) with total transplant mass at least \(q\) that is rejection-proof? A solution is the mass plan \(x\) itself, with rejection-proofness checked against each center’s best response. This mirrors only Theorem 4’s maximum-value rejection-proof problem; it does not try to continuize the paper’s withholding-strategy experiments or its whole mechanism comparison.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it keeps their centers, proposed exchanges, rejection actions, and “no center can gain by rejecting” predicate. It is an explicit continuous extension, though, not an established equivalence to Theorem 4. Clearing rational masses to integer multiplicities gives a high-multiplicity discrete instance, but the mass model permits a center to split a type across patterns and to use fractional response plans. A discrete rejection-proof solution therefore need not remain rejection-proof against the stronger mass-valued response. Proving a useful two-way correspondence or a rounding theorem is an open obligation.

I would tentatively expect continuum-specific hardness (Class C), but Theorem 4 does not establish that. Its reduction uses indivisible graph gadgets, so hardness does not automatically transfer once participants can be split by type. On the other hand, for a fixed proposed plan, \(R_i(x)\) is an LP; the hard part is choosing \(x\) while ensuring its value dominates every response. In the LP dual, the bounds \(y_e\le x_e\) contribute terms that multiply plan variables by dual prices, so the outer rejection-proof condition does not immediately become a plain LP. That makes a new source of difficulty plausible, while leaving tractability open.

The weakest point is the type regime. Real compatibility can depend on detailed immunological information, and if nearly every pair has a distinct compatibility profile then \(\tau\) approaches the participant count and the population compression buys little. The other concern is fractionalization: the continuous response may change the strategic condition as well as the representation. Those are substantial caveats, but they do not erase the paper-grounded mirror; they specify the regime and the mathematical questions that would make it a successful one. Further work should test whether the rejection-proof mass problem admits a compact formulation, whether the proposed nonconvexity can support a hardness proof, and when rational mass plans can be rounded while preserving rejection-proofness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor that survives is Theorem 4: this paper does have a named computational result, and Continuous Rejection-Proof KEP is a coherent question. I would not argue that the result is already continuous, or that its hardness makes a mirror pointless. The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirror has not established a credible high-multiplicity regime for kidney exchange, and its mass version weakens the guarantee that makes the paper’s problem matter.

The natural mirror is: given rational masses of complete pair types and type-level cycle patterns, is there a feasible mass plan with at least \(q\) transplants such that no center can improve its transplant mass by rejecting and internally rematching? That keeps the centers and their rejection choices. But a complete type must preserve the compatibility and value information used by the mechanism. If compatibility is not uniform within a type, the pattern model can offer cycles that do not exist for the actual participants. If types instead include enough information to make compatibility exact, the number of types may grow with the pool, leaving little population compression. Coarsening participants into broad clinical categories would address that, but then compatibility would need to be modeled as uncertain or approximate, changing the computational problem.

There is also a strategic gap even in the strongest, exact type model. The paper calls a solution rejection-proof when no center can gain by even one transplant. In a pool of \(N\) participants, a deviation that changes one cycle can improve a center’s normalized transplant mass by only \(O(1/N)\). An atomless model discards that gain in the limit; it checks for profitable deviations involving positive mass. A fixed tolerance would ignore some finite-pool incentives, while a tolerance small enough to preserve a one-transplant gain must depend on \(N\). So the mass condition can express “no profitable macroscopic rejection,” but that is weaker than the guarantee in Theorem 4.

One could restrict attention to very large synchronized pools with many repeated, exactly compatible types. That is the best version of the mirror, and it might be useful as a study of macroscopic incentives. Yet the paper’s examples are finite exchange graphs—up to 180 pairs in its experiments—and combining successive runs to create a larger population would change the static cycle-packing model, since participants and compatibility are tied to particular exchange rounds. The paper does not supply evidence for a large contemporaneous pool with enough repeated complete types for compression to be compelling.

So this is not a decisive argument that continuous rejection-proof exchange is mathematically uninteresting. It is a narrower case against prioritizing it for ChoCo: the plausible continuous model either preserves detailed compatibility at the cost of type compression, or aggregates participants and studies a different, macroscopic rejection guarantee. The weakness in my case is that a sufficiently large pool with repeated exact types could make that second question valuable—and the paper does not rule such a regime out.
