What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the single-facility path selector and its \(5/3\) performance threshold; it leaves the multi-facility and egalitarian results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is the paper’s single-facility, utilitarian, SGSP problem on a path. Consider a large municipality with residents at a finite set \(X\subseteq[0,1]\) of known sites. Each resident either dislikes the proposed facility or is indifferent to it. A type is \(t=(x,a)\), with \(x\in X\) and \(a\in\{\text{dislikes},\text{indifferent}\}\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these types. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime when many residents share a site and aversion type, so the population size \(N\) is much larger than the support size \(\tau\). Residents can misreport their aversion, while their locations are public.

For a facility at \(y\), a disliking resident at \(x\) has welfare \(|x-y|\); an indifferent resident has welfare \(\max(x,1-x)\), as in the paper. Define society welfare by \(W_\mu(y)=\sum_t\mu_t w(t,y)\). A mechanism maps reported type masses to a facility location. Its continuous SGSP condition says that whenever a coalition of positive mass misreports and some positive mass in that coalition gains, some positive mass in it loses. This treats each type as a divisible population; the facility itself remains a single point.

My lead computational question is: given the location masses, compute the paper’s robust facility choice. Write \(D_x=\sum_a\mu_{(x,a)}\). For each site, let \(q_x\in[0,D_x]\) be the indifferent mass; the rest dislike the facility. Let \(W_{D,q}(y)\) be the resulting welfare. For each \(y\in\{0,\tfrac12,1\}\), define
\[ \beta(D,y)=\sup_{q:\,0\le q_x\le D_x}\ \sup_{z\in[0,1]} \frac{W_{D,q}(z)}{W_{D,q}(y)}. \]
The question is to return a \(y\) minimizing \(\beta(D,y)\), with a fixed tie-breaking rule. The supremum considers every possible preference split consistent with the known location masses and compares the chosen location with the best facility location for that split.

This is anchored by Theorem 8, proved in the paper: Mechanism 4, which chooses the lexicographically least minimizer of \(\beta(D,y)\) over the endpoints and midpoint, has an \(O(|D|\log |D|)\)-time implementation. In the rational-input version, the sites and masses have binary encodings, and the sorting and ratio comparisons can be done with polynomial bit complexity. This is a genuine computational mirror: the input is a population mass profile, and the algorithm returns a facility location.

Two further named results give the performance meaning of that selector. Theorem 9, proved here, says Mechanism 4 achieves approximation ratio \(5/3\). In the continuous formulation above, it supplies an SGSP mechanism: it depends only on locations, so changing reported aversions does not change the outcome. The theorem’s distribution \(D\) already allows fractional numbers of agents, so this upper-bound analysis directly covers mass inputs.

Theorem 5, also proved here, says that any single-facility SGSP \(\alpha\)-efficient DOFLG mechanism, for positive constant \(\alpha\), must have \(\alpha\ge 5/3\). Its construction uses \(3n\) agents: one disliker at each endpoint, \(n\) dislikers at the midpoint, and \(n-1\) indifferent agents at each endpoint. It compares the truthful profile with two profiles in which one endpoint’s indifferent agents report that they dislike the facility. SGSP forces the facility location to be the same across those instances; in either case the ratio approaches \(5/3\) as \(n\) grows. Each such finite profile embeds as rational type masses, assigning mass \(1/(3n)\) to each agent. Thus a continuous mechanism with a uniform guarantee below \(5/3\) would induce a finite SGSP mechanism contradicting Theorem 5.

Together, these results make the continuous mechanism-design question precise: over all direct SGSP mechanisms on these mass societies, determine the smallest uniform utilitarian approximation factor \(\alpha^\star\). A solution consists of a mechanism and a matching guarantee and impossibility proof. The paper’s results imply \(\alpha^\star=5/3\): Theorem 9 gives the mechanism, and Theorem 5 gives the lower bound. The selector is tractable, so this is a Class A mirror. The lower bound is an approximation impossibility that transfers from finite instances; it is not a claim of NP-hardness or running-time hardness.

The scope is deliberately narrow: one facility, a path, social welfare, and strong group strategyproofness. It does not mirror the paper’s egalitarian results or its arbitrary-\(k\) setting. Natural follow-ups are the optimal SGSP ratio for multiple facilities, and whether the WGSP egalitarian lower bound has a useful mass-based analogue.

The soft spot is novelty. Theorem 8 and Theorem 9 already analyze fractional agent masses, so this is an unusually author-recognizable mirror and a strong demonstration that the population-continuous question is sensible. It is also largely answered in the paper itself. I would present it as a successful continuous formulation whose exact \(5/3\) threshold is a result, not as an unfilled gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that it is already in the paper. Section 4.1.1 explicitly permits fractional numbers of agents at each location and represents them by a weighted distribution \(D\). Under the change of coordinates from \([0,1]\) to \([-1,1]\), the proposed \(\beta(D,y)\) is the paper’s \(\beta\): it ranges over all preference masses consistent with the location masses and compares the welfare at \(y\) with the best achievable welfare. Theorem 8 gives the \(O(|D|\log |D|)\) algorithm for choosing among the endpoints and midpoint. With rational locations and masses, this is precisely a finite-support mass-input computation. It is not merely a discrete theorem whose proof happens to tolerate fractional counts.

Theorem 9 covers the proposed continuous performance question too. Mechanism 4 depends only on locations, so reports cannot change its outcome; it is therefore SGSP under the natural positive-mass coalition definition. The theorem proves its \(5/3\) guarantee over the weighted distributions used in that section.

Theorem 5 supplies the matching lower bound on the same rational mass domain. Normalize its \(3n\)-agent construction to total mass one: the location masses are \(1/3\) at each of \(0,\tfrac12,1\), and the dislike and indifference masses at those locations are rational. The construction’s report changes are shifts of mass between aversion types. Normalization leaves welfare ratios unchanged, and the bound approaches \(5/3\) as \(n\) grows. Thus the proposed exact-threshold question is already answered by the paper’s fractional-input upper bound and its finite-support lower-bound construction.

The best version of the proponent’s type model—types \((x,a)\), with site and aversion both part of the type—does not escape this overlap. It is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and it is exactly the regime represented by \(D\) and its preference splits. Treating the locations as diffuse instead would leave the finite-support model the programme is asking about; it would be a different input model, not a stronger version of this mirror.

So I would reject these anchors as an open continuous-computation project: they restate an already continuous algorithm and its matching bound. My negative case is weaker on the universal claim. The paper may have other promising mirrors—for example, its WGSP egalitarian lower bound or its open multi-facility questions—and the supplied case did not anchor them. I cannot honestly infer from defeating Theorems 8, 9, and 5 that every possible mirror of the paper is already done.
