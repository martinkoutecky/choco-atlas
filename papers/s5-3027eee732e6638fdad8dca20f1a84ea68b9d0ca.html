The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest positive computational case from this paper: none of its numbered results asserts a complexity or algorithmic result for a computational problem.

The closest candidate is Theorem 1, proved here. It characterizes the voting rules \(f:X^N\to X\) on a bounded distributive lattice: \(B_{\mathcal X}\)-monotonicity, strategy-proofness on the full unimodal domain, strategy-proofness on the full locally strictly unimodal domain, median tree-automaton representability, and generalized weak committee representation are all equivalent. That is a structural characterization of rules, not a result about the complexity of finding or computing one. Theorems 2 and 3 give further strategy-proofness and impossibility results, but likewise make no computational claim. Calling any of these a hardness anchor would misstate the paper.

Theorem 3 might inspire a new continuous existence question, with preference preorders as types and their population shares as masses. But the theorem concerns a finite electorate. For an atomless population, changing one agent’s report leaves the distribution unchanged, so individual strategy-proofness of a distribution-based rule is vacuous. Replacing it with deviations by positive-mass coalitions would define a new property that Theorem 3 does not establish. Moreover, the paper’s lattice is an outcome space; continuizing it alone would not meet the programme’s population-continuization scope.

So I have no anchor-backed continuous problem to hand a prover. The most promising seed is Theorem 3, but turning it into a valid mirror would require a new model and new results, rather than continuizing a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I agree with the proponent’s main diagnosis: the paper has no numbered computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes which rules are strategy-proof; Theorems 2 and 3 establish incentive and impossibility properties. None poses an algorithmic problem or makes a complexity claim. Theorem 1’s median-tree-automaton representation does not supply one: it gives a representation involving up to \(2^n\) phantom constants indexed by coalitions, with no input encoding or running-time analysis.

A large population with repeated preference types is a plausible regime here, so lack of multiplicity is not my objection. The best attempt to build on Theorem 1 would extend its lattice-polynomial rules to distributions over preference types and ask how to compute their outcomes. But the theorem supplies no canonical family of rules across population sizes: its committees and phantom constants can depend on named voters and on \(n\). Choosing anonymous rules, a mass-based extension, and an input encoding would define a new computational problem. The paper gives no reason to prefer one such construction.

Theorem 2’s comparison of individual and coalitional strategy-proofness likewise invites a continuous axiomatic question. Theorem 3 is the strongest seed: one could define deviations by positive-mass coalitions, avoiding the vacuity of unilateral deviations in an atomless population, and ask whether anonymous, locally sovereign, locally neutral rules can be coalition-proof. That is a coherent possible research question. It remains an existence or characterization question, though. Turning it into a complexity problem would require additional choices about the rule representation, the preference-type space, and which mass deviations are allowed; Theorem 3 provides none of them.

So the negative case is strong as a judgment about ChoCo’s remit: no result in this paper anchors a population-continuous computational question. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile adjacent project could be built from the paper. In particular, the mass-coalition version of Theorem 3 could be worth studying on its own. I would classify that as a new axiomatic project, rather than a continuous computational mirror of this paper.
