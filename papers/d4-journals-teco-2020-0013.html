The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), target \(c^\star\), a rule \(\mathcal R\) from Corollary 2, finitely many complete voter types \(\Theta\), type data \((\rho_\theta,\sigma_\theta,a_\theta)\) where applicable, and a rational distribution \(\mu\) with \(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta=1\), choose \(x_{\theta,\rho}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{\rho}x_{\theta,\rho}=\mu_\theta\) to minimize \(\sum_{\theta,\rho}\kappa_\theta(\rho)x_{\theta,\rho}\), where \(\kappa_\theta(\rho)\) is the admissible-swap cost from \(\rho_\theta\) to \(\rho\), subject to \(c^\star\) winning under \(\mathcal R\) in the resulting weighted profile, with approval states retained for SP-AV and fallback.

The model it lives in

High-multiplicity swap bribery: each type carries a ranking \(\rho_\theta\), swap-cost table \(\sigma_\theta\), and any approval data; mass \(\mu_\theta\) is transported through \(x_{\theta,\rho}\) to final rankings or states, with linear swap cost and aggregate weighted winner constraints.

The strongest residual concern is that an explicit finite type set yields a generic LP over \(\tau\) types and rankings, so the paper's \(n\)-fold machinery does not itself establish a nontrivial continuous complexity theorem.

It leaves the internal \(n\)-fold-IP results, Lemmas 14 and 15, and results outside these anchors alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the implicit transfer LP be solved with a pricing or separation oracle over permutations in time polynomial in \(m\) and the type encoding?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the high-multiplicity version of \(\mathcal R\)-Swap Bribery, with Corollary 2 as the lead anchor.

Corollary 2, proved in this paper, states that \(\mathcal R\)-Swap Bribery is fixed-parameter tractable when parameterized by the number \(m=|C|\) of candidates, for every natural scoring protocol, every C1 rule, and the maximin, Bucklin, SP-AV, fallback, and Kemeny rules. This is a particularly good anchor because the result allows arbitrary voter-dependent swap costs; it is not merely the uniform-cost case.

The continuous problem I would put before a prover is \(\mathcal R\)-Swap Bribery\(_\infty\). An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a target \(c^\star\), a rule \(\mathcal R\) from Corollary 2, and a finite set \(\Theta\) of complete voter types. A type \(\theta\) contains:

  • a complete ranking \(\rho_\theta\);
  • its swap-cost table \(\sigma_\theta\);
  • and, for SP-AV or fallback, its approval count \(a_\theta\).

The society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu=(\mu_\theta)_{\theta\in\Theta}\), with \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\). Thus \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of the population having exactly that ranking, cost table, and approval count.

For each source type \(\theta\) and possible final ranking \(\rho\), let \(\kappa_\theta(\rho)\) be the minimum cost of the admissible swaps that transform \(\rho_\theta\) into \(\rho\). For complete orders, the set of reversed pairs uniquely determines the admissible swaps, so this is exactly the paper’s cost model. A feasible solution is a mass-transfer matrix \(x_{\theta,\rho}\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_\rho x_{\theta,\rho}=\mu_\theta\). The final mass of ranking \(\rho\) is \(\nu_\rho=\sum_\theta x_{\theta,\rho}\). The objective is to minimize \(\sum_{\theta,\rho}\kappa_\theta(\rho)x_{\theta,\rho}\), subject to \(c^\star\) winning under \(\mathcal R\) in the weighted final election.

For SP-AV and fallback, the final state is \((\rho,a_\theta)\), rather than just \(\rho\), since swaps do not change the approval count. For example, under a scoring rule the winning constraints are \(\sum_\rho\nu_\rho s(\rho,c^\star)\geq\sum_\rho\nu_\rho s(\rho,c)\) for every \(c\). Pairwise rules replace voter counts by pairwise masses, and Kemeny uses the weighted agreement score of a ranking against the final mass distribution. No candidate, ranking, or approval decision is fractional: every unit of mass still has an integral ranking and receives an integral swap transformation. Only the population and the number of voters transformed are continuous.

This is a genuine high-multiplicity regime, not merely a fractionalized election. Imagine a national election with \(n\) in the millions, a small number \(m\) of candidates, and voters falling into a few hundred standardized segments: the same ballot, the same campaign-contact tariff, and the same approval cutoff. Then \(n\gg\tau\), where \(\tau=|\Theta|\). The cost table belongs to the type because two voters with different prices are different types; this is precisely the programme’s definition of high multiplicity, not a loss of the paper’s arbitrary-cost feature.

A discrete election with \(n_\theta\) voters of type \(\theta\) is recovered by setting \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/n\). A discrete bribery plan is a transfer matrix whose entries are multiples of \(1/n\). The continuous problem therefore removes only the one-voter granularity. It does not replace bribery by divisible outcomes, lotteries, noise, or an axiomatic profile simplex.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A. If the possible final rankings are listed explicitly, it is a finite linear program. Even when all \(m!\) rankings are implicit, the problem is naturally fixed-parameter tractable in \(m\): scoring and C1 rules can be handled by linear constraints after guessing the relevant pairwise pattern; maximin and Bucklin require the same bounded guesses used in the paper; and for Kemeny one can enumerate a ranking \(R^\star\) with \(c^\star\) first and impose that its weighted agreement is at least that of every other ranking. The resulting running time should have the form \(f(m)\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\), with the real open issue being whether the permutation pricing problem can be made polynomial in \(m\) as well.

The broader Theorem 1, also proved here, supports the same interpretation. It establishes fixed-parameter algorithms for \(\mathcal R\)-Multi-Bribery parameterized by \(m\), in time \(2^{O(m^6\log m)}n^3\) for natural scoring protocols, C1 rules, and SP-AV; \(2^{O(m^6\log m)}n^4\) for maximin, Bucklin, and fallback; and \(2^{O(m!^6\log m)}n^3\) for Kemeny. Its continuous counterpart would simply add push actions and active/latent status to the type description and let \(x_{\theta,q}\) transfer mass from an initial type to a final state \(q\). The swap-only problem is the cleaner lead, but the same mirror extends to the paper’s main model.

The main questions generated by the mirror are substantive:

  • Can the implicit LP be solved by a separation or pricing oracle over permutations?
  • For which scoring rules and swap-cost representations is that pricing problem polynomial?
  • How large is the additive gap between the continuous optimum and the nearest realizable \(n\)-voter solution?
  • Does Kemeny remain merely fixed-parameter tractable, or does its continuous version expose a sharper complexity boundary?

My weakest point is that Corollary 2 itself does not prove the continuous result. The paper’s \(n\)-fold IP algorithm relies on integer bricks, whereas the mirror replaces them by mass flows and may make the problem substantially easier. Also, if almost every voter has an idiosyncratic cost table, then \(\tau\) approaches \(n\) and the high-multiplicity regime disappears. But that weakness concerns the regime or the eventual complexity classification, not whether the question is a faithful mirror. In the standardized-segment regime, the population is genuinely continuous, the actions are exactly the paper’s swaps, and the objective and winner condition are unchanged after replacing counts by masses.

The paper’s named lower bounds, Lemmas 14 and 15, concern the internal \(n\)-fold-IP machinery rather than a voting or bribery problem, so I would not present them dishonestly as hardness anchors. Corollary 2 is therefore the strongest native anchor available for the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is that Corollary 2’s distinctive content does not survive high multiplicity intact. Its selling point is arbitrary voter-dependent swap costs: every voter may have a different table \(\sigma^v\), and the \(n\)-fold formulation gives one integer brick to each such voter. In a genuine high-multiplicity model, however, voters with different cost tables are different types. If the costs are genuinely arbitrary, the number of types \(\tau\) can be \(n\), and the “continuous” instance is merely the original discrete election with masses \(1/n\). If \(\tau\ll n\), then one has imposed repeated cost tables and discarded precisely the individual heterogeneity that made Corollary 2 technically significant.

The proposed national-election story does not remove this tension. It is a plausible new instance regime, but it is a restricted model of repeated voter segments, not a continuization of the paper’s full arbitrary-cost setting. A more ambitious repair—allowing each individual to retain a distinct price while describing prices by a distribution or a compact generator—would no longer make cost a property of the voter type, contrary to the programme’s high-multiplicity definition. Thus the mirror either loses the theorem’s central generality or loses the population compression.

The same objection applies to Theorem 1. Its \(n\)-fold algorithm succeeds because it preserves a separate integer block for every voter while exploiting the common structure of those blocks. Replacing the blocks by divisible mass flows changes the computational object fundamentally: the integer choice of which voters are altered disappears, and the individual influence, activation, and deactivation costs become ordinary per-unit linear costs. The resulting model is a transportation LP, not a continuous form of the paper’s \(n\)-fold integer programme. It may be useful, but its usefulness comes from replacing the paper’s central difficulty rather than from exposing it in a continuous setting.

There is also a more general concern about worthwhileness. With a finite explicit type set, the proposed problem can always enumerate the \(m!\) final rankings and formulate an LP with roughly \(\tau m!\) transfer variables. For fixed \(m\), this already gives an \(f(m)\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) algorithm for scoring rules, pairwise rules, and even Kemeny by writing the relevant aggregate inequalities. Thus the continuous mirror does not initially reveal a sophisticated analogue of the paper’s single-exponential \(n\)-fold machinery; it produces a fairly generic finite-state LP. The implicit pricing problem may still be interesting, but that is a new optimization question about the chosen representation, not something established by this paper.

These objections do not defeat the mirror itself. The swap-bribery objective depends only on final rankings, costs, and aggregate scores or pairwise counts; it does not track voter identity. A mass-transfer formulation therefore retains all information used by the voting problem, and standardized voter segments are a legitimate high-multiplicity scenario. Approval counts and control status can likewise be included in the type, while one-time costs have a natural per-unit interpretation. No collapse or vacuity occurs.

Accordingly, I cannot honestly sustain the requested universal negative. Corollary 2 and Theorem 1 are imperfect anchors because their important integer and individual-cost structure is partly removed by continuization, but that is not a fundamental obstruction. The proponent’s mirror is a well-posed, faithful high-multiplicity relaxation; at most, the paper supplies motivation for it rather than solving its continuous complexity questions.
