The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(J\) of item types, a monotone submodular coverage valuation \(f:2^J\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), and rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\in(0,1]\), let \(\lambda_C\) be the mass of a unit-mass cohort receiving integral bundle \(C\subseteq J\), with \(\sum_C\lambda_C=1\) and \(\sum_{C\ni j}\lambda_C\le b_j\). Is it NP-hard, unless \(P=NP\), to approximate \(Z_{\mathrm{mass}}=\max_{\lambda}\min_{C:\lambda_C>0}f(C)\) within \(1-1/e+\varepsilon\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\)?

The model it lives in

One complete agent type with valuation \(f\), a unit-mass population, rational per-capita capacities \(b_j\) for finitely many item types, and a distribution \(\lambda_C\) over integral bundles; optimize the minimum \(f(C)\) among bundles receiving positive mass.

The objection that survived

On the proposed \(b_j=1/m\) grid, the mass model is exactly the configuration LP, and with fixed \(J\) positive-value allocations disappear as \(m\) grows; the proponent mentions scalable stocks but anchors the question on this grid.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror asks whether Corollary 1’s \(1-1/e+\varepsilon\) approximation hardness persists for mass allocations with fixed per-capita supplies; it does not cover the greedy \(2/5\) guarantee, the cardinality-constrained case, or the matroid-constrained result.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(1-1/e+\varepsilon\) hardness survive when assignments are distributions over bundles and supplies are fixed per capita?
  • What approximation guarantees can configuration-LP separation provide for this mass-allocation objective?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a mass version of Submodular Max-Min Allocation. It keeps agents’ bundles integral and makes the population continuous.

Take a finite catalogue \(J\), a monotone submodular value oracle \(f:2^J\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), and a population of mass \(1\) whose members all have the same complete type: the same valuation \(f\). Let \(b_j\in[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\) be the per-capita supply of item type \(j\). An allocation is a distribution \(\lambda_C\ge0\) over integral bundles \(C\subseteq J\), with
\[ \sum_{C\subseteq J}\lambda_C=1, \qquad \sum_{C\ni j}\lambda_C\le b_j\quad\text{for each }j\in J. \]
Here \(\lambda_C\) is the fraction of the population receiving bundle \(C\); the supply constraint accounts for the item copies used by that mass. The question is to maximize \(z\) subject to \(\lambda_C=0\) whenever \(f(C)<z\). A solution is a feasible mass allocation and its achieved minimum value.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large cohort of otherwise identical residents receives bundles assembled from a scalable catalogue of services or resources, with stocks growing in proportion to cohort size. There may be millions of agents and just one agent type, so \(N\gg\tau=1\). Each person still gets an integral bundle; it is the fraction of the cohort assigned to each bundle that can be fractional. When each item has stock one in a finite cohort of \(m\) agents, its normalized supply is \(b_j=1/m\). That grid case gives a direct bridge to the paper’s configuration LP.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: *the configuration LP for max-min allocation with identical submodular valuations has integrality gap at most \(3\).* On the \(b_j=1/m\) grid, the mass allocation is the configuration LP after aggregating the player-indexed variables. The theorem therefore says the finite integral allocation loses at most a factor of \(3\) relative to this continuous population value. It is a concrete discrete-to-continuous result, not a claim that the finite model was already continuous. The authors should recognize the mirror: their configuration variables already describe how much player mass receives each configuration, and identical valuations let those masses be pooled.

The corresponding question is: for a finite instance \(J,f,m\), with continuous population mass \(1\) and supplies \(b_j=1/m\), what is the maximum minimum value \(Z_{\mathrm{mass}}\), and what is the ratio between it and the integral optimum \(Z_{\mathrm{int}}\)? Theorem 3 establishes \(Z_{\mathrm{mass}}/Z_{\mathrm{int}}\le3\). I expect constant-factor treatment of the continuous value to be Class A: the paper describes approximate separation through submodular knapsack, with a \(1-1/e\) loss. That does not give exact polynomial-time optimization, and the theorem’s proof itself is nonconstructive. Natural follow-ups are whether the factor \(3\) is tight, whether the bound has a constructive rounding algorithm, and how it behaves beyond the unit-supply grid.

A second worthwhile anchor is the approximation-hardness Corollary 1, cited from Khot et al. (2008), rather than proved in this paper: *unless \(P=NP\), Submodular Max-Min Allocation with identical valuations cannot be approximated within a factor \(1-1/e+\varepsilon\), for any \(\varepsilon>0\).* Its continuous question is whether one can compute a feasible mass allocation with value greater than \((1-1/e+\varepsilon)Z_{\mathrm{mass}}\), or whether such an approximation is impossible. I would expect the hardness to transfer if the coverage structure in the item choices survives scaling: its combinatorics lie in the catalogue and valuation, not in the number of named agents. The task for a prover is to give a gap-preserving construction for positive-mass cohorts. The paper’s corollary alone does not establish that construction.

The mirror covers the identical-valuation max-min problem and its configuration-LP bridge. It does not claim to continuize the cardinality-constrained or matroid-constrained results. Nor does it treat fractional bundles: individuals receive integral bundles, while only the assignment of mass across bundles is fractional.

My weakest point is the hardness transfer. The cited corollary is a discrete result, and fractional mass assignments could erase a gap that depends on a small exceptional set of players. That is why Theorem 3 is the firmer anchor: its configuration LP provides an explicit, paper-proved bridge to the continuous population model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The lead anchor is exactly the configuration LP already studied in the paper. At a fixed threshold, aggregate its player-indexed variables as
\[ \lambda_C=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{p\in P}x_{p,C}. \]
Then \(\sum_C\lambda_C\ge 1\), and each item has total use at most \(1/m\). The mass can be trimmed to total \(1\) without changing its supported configurations. Conversely, any such mass allocation gives a feasible configuration-LP solution by assigning the same distribution to each player. So the proposed \(b_j=1/m\) model asks for the value of that LP, and Theorem 3 already bounds its ratio to the integral optimum by \(3\); the paper also gives a \(4/3\) lower bound. That is a real discrete-to-LP bridge, but asking for this ratio as a new continuous question repeats the paper’s result.

It is also not a stable high-multiplicity regime with a fixed catalogue of unique items. The total available mass of nonempty bundles is at most \(\sum_j b_j=|J|/m\). With fixed \(J\), as \(m\) grows, almost all mass must receive the empty bundle, so the max-min value becomes \(0\). Keeping positive per-capita supply requires changing the model to replicated item types or a growing catalogue. That may be a sensible co-modification, but it is not the proposed unit-supply-grid mirror.

The hardness anchor fares worse as stated. Corollary 1 concerns the integral optimum; it does not establish hardness relative to the mass-LP optimum, which can be larger. A transfer would need a reduction preserving the approximation gap after fractional mass assignments are allowed. The paper itself points to the relevant continuous-LP obstacle: Section 4.2 discusses submodular-knapsack separation, its \(1-1/e\) approximation, and matching hardness. That discussion does not settle every possible hardness question for the mass objective, but the cited corollary supplies no new continuous-population hardness result.

A stronger model with fixed positive per-capita stocks is coherent. For rational supplies \(b_j\), one can scale to a finite cohort and make \(M b_j\) copies of each item type, with each person’s valuation depending on which types they receive. That creates a genuine high-multiplicity extension. But it remains a configuration-allocation problem with identical submodular valuations, close to the paper’s existing LP and matroid framework. Any new contribution would need to be a compressed algorithm or a direct hardness result in the binary-encoded supply sizes; neither follows from the anchors.

So I would reject these two anchors as evidence of an *unanswered* continuous mirror: one is already the paper’s LP theorem, and the other’s discrete hardness does not transfer. I cannot honestly defend the stronger claim that no scenario could make this worthwhile. In particular, a complexity result for fixed per-capita stocks that runs in the support size and supply bit length could be valuable ChoCo work. The negative case is strongest against the mirrors offered here, and weak against the universal claim.
