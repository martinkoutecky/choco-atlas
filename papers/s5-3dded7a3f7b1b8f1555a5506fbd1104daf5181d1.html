The continuous mirror question

Given artists \(C\), a finite support of complete engagement types \(w^t \in \mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{|C|}\setminus\{0\}\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and \(k\) and \(\gamma>0\), decide whether there are \(U\subseteq C\), \(|U|\le k\), and masses \(0\le\nu_t\le\mu_t\) such that \(P_\mu(U,\nu)\ge\gamma\). Let \(q=\sum_t\nu_t\), \(\lambda=\mu-\nu\), \(a_t(U)=\sum_{j\in U}w^t_j\), and \(b_t=\sum_{j\in C}w^t_j\); then \(P_\mu(U,\nu)=\alpha\frac{\sum_t\mu_ta_t(U)}{\sum_t\mu_tb_t}-\alpha(1-q)\frac{\sum_t\lambda_ta_t(U)}{\sum_t\lambda_tb_t}-q\), with the second term set to zero when no mass remains.

The model it lives in

A population is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete user engagement vectors; the suspected cohort is a submeasure \(\nu\le\mu\). Choose an artist set \(U\) of size at most \(k\) and suspected mass \(\nu\) to maximize per-capita suspicious profit \(P_\mu(U,\nu)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.5’s suspicious-artist detection problem. It leaves the axiomatic results, mechanism properties, fairness proposition, and experiments outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does NP-hardness persist when suspected users can be removed fractionally?
  • For fixed \(U\), can the optimal suspected mass be computed efficiently?
  • What approximation or parameterized algorithms are possible in \(k\), \(m\), and support size \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s suspicious-artist detection problem, anchored on Theorem 3.5. It says: given an instance \(\mathcal I=(N,C,\mathbf w)\), a bound \(k\le |C|\), and \(\gamma>0\), it is NP-hard to decide whether there is a set \(U\subseteq C\), \(|U|\le k\), with \(PSP(U)\ge\gamma\). The authors prove this theorem here by reducing from Small Set Bipartite Vertex Expansion; the paper cites prior work for that starting problem’s NP-completeness. This is the paper’s named computational hardness result. Its other numbered results establish axiomatic properties, rather than computational complexity.

Here is a mass version of that same detection question. Let the \(m\) artists be \(C\), and let a user type \(t\) be the user’s full engagement vector \(w^t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^m\setminus\{0\}\). A society is a finite distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) such types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The full vector matters: global-pro-rata payments depend on total engagement as well as on each user’s relative listening shares. Write \(b_t=\sum_{j\in C}w^t_j\), \(a_t(U)=\sum_{j\in U}w^t_j\), \(A_\rho(U)=\sum_t\rho_ta_t(U)\), and \(B_\rho=\sum_t\rho_tb_t\). A candidate fraudulent cohort is a submeasure \(\nu\le\mu\), of mass \(q=\sum_t\nu_t\); the remaining society is \(\lambda=\mu-\nu\). Define its per-capita suspicious profit by
\[ P_\mu(U,\nu)=\alpha\frac{A_\mu(U)}{B_\mu} -\alpha(1-q)\frac{A_\lambda(U)}{B_\lambda}-q, \]
taking the second term to be zero when \(\lambda\) is empty. The continuous problem asks whether \(\max_{|U|\le k,\,0\le\nu\le\mu}P_\mu(U,\nu)\ge\gamma\). A solution is the artist set \(U\) and the mass vector \(\nu\) witnessing that threshold.

This retains the paper’s question: which small set of artists could be vehicles for fraud, given the largest net gain from a suspicious user population? The terms still represent the original calculation: current global-pro-rata revenue to \(U\), minus revenue after removing the suspected cohort, minus that cohort’s subscription cost. In an empirical society of \(n\) users, if \(v_t\) users of each type are removed, taking \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and \(\nu_t=v_t/n\) gives \(P_\mu(U,\nu)=PSP(U)/n\). Thus the continuous objective is exactly the per-capita version on the discrete grid, extended to fractional mass.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a subscription service with a fixed, moderate creator catalog and a fixed billing period, where per-user interaction counts are discrete and bounded by the period. Full engagement profiles then come from a finite type space, and a large listener population can have \(n\gg\tau\) because many listeners share exact profiles. That is a narrower scenario than the paper’s broad streaming-platform story, but it is coherent: the type includes everything this objective uses, and each unit of mass bears the same subscription cost. The paper’s reported dataset of hundreds of thousands of users does not itself establish this regime; its high user count is not evidence that it has few distinct full engagement types.

My expectation is that the artist-selection combinatorics will keep the mass problem hard, so I lean toward Class B. The reduction in Theorem 3.5 has a graph-selection structure involving a small artist set, which should remain a source of difficulty. But the theorem does not prove hardness of this mirror: its suspected-user set \(V\) is discrete, whereas \(\nu\) can select fractional mass. Whether that relaxation preserves the reduction is a central question, not something to assume. Useful follow-ups are whether the inner mass optimization is tractable for fixed \(U\), whether hardness survives fractional removal, and what approximation or parameterized algorithms are possible in \(k\), \(m\), and \(\tau\).

The scope is just Theorem 3.5’s detection problem. This does not continuize the artists, the payout outcomes, or the paper’s axioms, and its fractional payments and real-valued engagement weights do not already make its population continuous. The portioning connection is also about outcome-space continuity, not this population model.

My weakest point is the exact-profile assumption. On a large platform with hundreds of thousands of artists, full engagement vectors may be nearly unique, making \(\tau\) close to \(n\); grouping merely similar profiles would change the problem. There is a second real gap: fractional cohort removal may make the continuous problem easier than the paper’s discrete one. Those limits narrow the scenario and leave the complexity result to be established, but they do not erase the mirror: it asks the same suspicious-profit question over a population whose units are genuinely interchangeable and whose suspected cohort is measured in mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about the regime, not the formulation. Theorem 3.5 is a genuine computational result, and its suspicious-artist problem has a coherent population mirror. With \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and \(\nu_t=v_t/n\), the proposed objective is exactly \(PSP(U)/n\) on the discrete grid. It preserves the question of which small set of artists can generate suspicious profit; it does not rely on continuizing artists, outcomes, or axioms.

The difficulty is that the type must be the user’s complete engagement vector. Global-pro-rata payments depend on both which artists a user streams and how much they stream, so grouping users by listening shares, genre, or a coarse activity band can change the objective. In the paper’s open-ended streaming setting, these vectors are high-dimensional and sparse, and exact profiles may be almost unique. The reported user count alone does not establish high multiplicity. The hardness reduction reinforces that concern: it uses graph-specific user profiles and many dummy users with distinct profiles, so its NP-hardness gives no evidence that the hard instances have \(\tau\ll n\).

That is a real weakness in the proposed *scenario*, but it does not defeat the mirror. A fixed catalog and billing period with bounded interaction counts could produce repeated exact profiles, making fractional cohort removal a sensible high-multiplicity question. Even if the discrete reduction’s hardness disappears there, discovering that would be a worthwhile result under the programme’s stated aims. The possible narrowness of the regime also does not establish that no such scenario is useful.

So the honest negative case is weak. I can argue that the paper does not establish a high-multiplicity regime for its motivating platform, and that the reduction does not transfer that evidence. I cannot honestly argue that every plausible mirror fails. Under the stated grading rule, Theorem 3.5’s mirror survives.
