The continuous mirror question

Given a project set \(P\), costs \(c\), budget \(b\), and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval ballots with \(q_p=\sum_{t:p\in t}\mu_t>0\) for every project \(p\), decide whether there is a bundle \(\pi\subseteq P\) with \(c(\pi)\le b\) such that every type \(t\) with \(\mu_t>0\) has \(\sum_{p\in\pi\cap t}c(p)/q_p\ge\min\{b,\sum_{p\in t}c(p)/q_p\}\).

The model it lives in

A society is a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete approval-ballot types. Project costs and the budget remain as in the paper, and the outcome remains an indivisible project bundle; each type’s normalized share is \(\sum_{p\in\pi\cap t}c(p)/q_p\), where \(q_p\) is the mass approving of \(p\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s fair-share feasibility question and also admits the proposed EJS search question from Theorem 6; it leaves the other fairness criteria, MESshare guarantees, and experimental results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does fair-share feasibility admit useful fixed-parameter algorithms in the number of supported ballot types \(\tau\), or under restrictions on ballot supports?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is that the paper’s fair share question has a direct continuous-population form. A plausible regime is a citywide participatory-budgeting process with tens of thousands of residents, a few dozen proposed projects, and a much smaller set of recurring approval ballots. A type is a complete approval ballot \(t\subseteq P\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents with that ballot. Residents share the same project costs and voting weight, so \(n\) can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of supported ballot types.

For project \(p\), let \(q_p=\sum_{t:p\in t}\mu_t\) be its share of the population’s support. Define the normalized share of type \(t\) from a bundle \(\pi\) by
\[ s_\mu(\pi,t)=\sum_{p\in\pi\cap t}\frac{c(p)}{q_p}. \]
This is the paper’s per-voter share multiplied by \(n\) when \(\mu_t\) is the ballot’s frequency in an \(n\)-person profile. The corresponding fair-share target is \(f_\mu(t)=\min\{b,s_\mu(t,t)\}\). The population is continuous; the allocation remains a feasible subset of the named projects.

My lead is Continuous Fair-Share Feasibility. Given \(P\), costs \(c\), budget \(b\), and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval ballots, decide whether there is a bundle \(\pi\subseteq P\), with \(c(\pi)\le b\), such that \(s_\mu(\pi,t)\ge f_\mu(t)\) for every positive-mass type \(t\). A solution is such a bundle when one exists.

The anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper: given a PB instance and profile, deciding whether there is a feasible budget allocation satisfying FS is NP-complete, even when all projects have unit cost. In the continuous mirror, every finite profile becomes the rational distribution \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). The share and fair-share inequalities are exactly the original inequalities multiplied by \(n\), so the existence question is preserved. The proposition’s reduction from 3-SET-COVER therefore gives NP-hardness for the continuous problem as well; a bundle is a polynomial-size certificate whose type shares can be checked using rational arithmetic. This is a direct mirror of the paper’s own fairness question, and its hardness is driven by choosing projects, not by a need to distinguish individual voters. I expect Class B: hardness transfers.

A second, useful anchor is Theorem 6, also proved here: unless \(P=NP\), no polynomial-time algorithm always computes a feasible budget allocation satisfying EJS. The proof reduces from SUBSET-SUM. This gives a distinct continuous question, Continuous EJS Search: on the same kind of input, output a feasible bundle satisfying EJS. For a project set \(X\), represent a cohesive subpopulation by masses \(\nu_t\) with \(0\le\nu_t\le\mu_t\), supported only on types approving every project in \(X\), and with \(\sum_t\nu_t\ge c(X)/b\). Require that each such cohesive subpopulation contain positive mass of some type \(t\) whose share from the output bundle is at least \(s_\mu(X,t)\). A solution is a feasible bundle satisfying this condition for every \(X\) and every cohesive subpopulation.

The single-type case anchors this mirror particularly firmly. If \(\mu_t=1\) for a ballot approving all projects, then every feasible project set \(X\) is cohesive, and its reference share is \(c(X)\). EJS requires the output bundle’s share, also its cost, to be at least \(c(X)\) for every feasible \(X\). Thus the problem contains choosing a maximum-cost feasible bundle; the paper’s subset-sum reduction applies directly. Replicating that one ballot across a large population changes nothing. I also expect Class B here. This anchor covers the paper’s result about computing a fair allocation, while the FS anchor covers whether a particular fairness guarantee can be met at all.

These questions should be recognizable to the authors: approval ballots, equal division of project cost among supporters, cohesive groups, and the criteria being tested all come from their model. Existing high-multiplicity election work would support treating repeated ballots as a sensible regime. Further questions include how the FS problem behaves when the project set is restricted, and whether EJS search has useful cost or approximation restrictions: the one-type case already shows that bounding the number of types alone cannot remove the project-selection hardness.

My weakest point is the continuous interpretation of EJS’s existential witness. In a population described only by masses, “there exists an agent” needs a convention; I require a positive-mass witness within each cohesive subpopulation. That is a meaningful continuous analogue of finding at least one represented type in a finite group, but it is not the only possible limit convention. The Theorem 6 hardness instance avoids this ambiguity because all agents have the same type and share, so every member of a cohesive subpopulation either meets the condition or none does. The FS mirror is less exposed: its type-by-type requirement translates directly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that both proposed mirrors add very little mathematical structure. For fair share, if \(q_p=\sum_{t:p\in t}\mu_t\), the continuous share is exactly \(n\) times each voter’s share in any finite profile with those type frequencies. Feasibility is therefore preserved exactly. The EJS mirror is similarly exposed by the one-type case: its condition reduces to requiring the output bundle to have at least the cost of every feasible project set, so the population adds no computational variable to that instance. Both anchors in the paper are genuine computational results—FS feasibility is NP-complete, and computing an EJS allocation is NP-hard—but their continuous versions look like compressed encodings of the same project-selection questions. ([Maly et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.07517))

That is a real novelty concern, but it does not defeat either anchor under the programme’s rules. A discrete-to-continuous identity can itself be a result, and transferred hardness is an accepted outcome. Large PB processes with many residents sharing approval ballots provide a credible high-multiplicity regime; projects can remain indivisible. Neither the discreteness of the outcome nor the fact that the one-type EJS case is simple at the population level rules out a mirror.

The positive-mass witness in the proposed EJS definition is a genuine modelling choice. But the one-type reduction removes the ambiguity: every member has the same relevant share, so a cohesive subpopulation either meets the condition or none of it does. Quantifying over all rational submass vectors gives a natural broader version too. I cannot make that choice invalidate the underlying question.

So the negative case is weak. The mirrors may yield limited new structure beyond a high-multiplicity formulation, but that is not enough to argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. I cannot honestly defeat either anchor.
