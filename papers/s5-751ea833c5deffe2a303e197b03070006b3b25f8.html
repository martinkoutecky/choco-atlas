The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), and a society \(\mu\) supported on \(\tau\) top-truncated ballot types, does there exist a committee \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|=k\) such that, for every \(\ell\in[k]\) and \(X\subseteq C\), \(M_\mu(X)\ge \alpha\ell/k\) implies \(|W\cap X|\ge\min(|X|,\ell)\)? Here \(M_\mu(X)\) is the total mass of types ranking every candidate in \(X\) above every candidate in \(C\setminus X\), under the paper's convention for unranked candidates.

The model it lives in

Each ballot type carries rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Use binary variables \(y_c\in\{0,1\}\) for committee membership and require \(\sum_{c\in X}y_c\ge\min(|X|,\ell)\) whenever the mass of the maximal solid coalition over \(X\) is at least \(\alpha\ell/k\). This is a feasibility problem with a discrete committee and a continuous population.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the α-PSC feasibility question in Theorem 2; it does not cover the paper's empirical comparisons, voting-rule computations, or other quantified measures.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does feasibility complexity depend on \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length, particularly for bounded \(\tau\)?
  • Does minimizing the achievable α-PSC value on type-mass input admit useful approximation guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is the lead mirror: Continuous \(\alpha\)-PSC Feasibility, based on Theorem 2. Theorem 2, proved in this paper, says that given a finite election with \(n\) voters, \(m\) candidates, committee size \(k\), top-truncated ordinal ballots, and a threshold \(\alpha<1\), deciding whether any size-\(k\) committee satisfies \(\alpha\)-PSC is NP-complete. Here \(\alpha\)-PSC means that for every \(\ell\in[k]\), each solid coalition of at least \(\alpha\ell n/k\) voters over a candidate set \(C'\) gets at least \(\min(|C'|,\ell)\) seats. The paper proves hardness by reduction from 3-Hitting Set.

The continuous problem has as input a candidate set \(C\), committee size \(k\), a rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), and a society \(\mu\) given as a support of \(\tau\) top-truncated ballot types with positive rational masses summing to one. A type is an exact ballot: its ranking and truncation are the same for every voter of that type. For each \(X\subseteq C\), let
\[ M_\mu(X)=\sum_{t:\,t\text{ ranks every candidate in }X\text{ above every candidate in }C\setminus X}\mu_t \]
be the mass of the maximal solid coalition over \(X\), using the paper’s convention for unranked candidates. The question is whether there exists a committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|=k\), such that for every \(\ell\in[k]\) and every \(X\subseteq C\),
\[ M_\mu(X)\ge \frac{\alpha\ell}{k} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |W\cap X|\ge \min(|X|,\ell). \]
A committee is the witness; otherwise the answer is no. This is a feasibility question, not a committee-utility optimization problem. The population is continuous; the committee remains discrete.

A plausible regime is a large union, municipal, or party election choosing a small committee from a fixed slate: perhaps \(100{,}000\) voters but only a few hundred distinct ranked or truncated ballots. Agents share a type precisely when their ballots match, since this problem uses no other individual attributes. Then \(n\) can greatly exceed \(\tau\), and the mass of a coalition is the natural version of “what fraction of voters supports this solid group?” The paper’s Scottish elections have small candidate slates and millions of ballots across the dataset, though the supplied text does not establish that any individual ward has \(\tau\ll n\).

The authors should recognize this mirror: their measure already scales entitlement by \(n/k\), and the mirror replaces coalition counts by coalition mass without changing either cohesion or representation demands. For any explicit finite profile, aggregating voters by ballot type and setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) gives exactly the same \(\alpha\)-PSC conditions. This is a question about the continuous population, not fractional seats or probabilistic outcomes.

I expect this mirror to be hardness-transferring (Class B). The paper’s discrete NP-hardness transfers: aggregate each explicit voter profile into type counts and normalize them to masses. A size-\(k\) committee satisfies the continuous constraints exactly when it satisfies the original finite-profile constraints. The committee is a polynomial certificate, and feasibility can be checked by considering the maximal solid coalitions induced by ballot prefixes, so the rational-mass version is in NP as well. The reduction’s combinatorics are in the candidate agenda and the hitting-set choices, not in fine-grained control over a huge electorate; continuization has no reason to remove that hardness.

The scope here is only the existence problem for \(\alpha\)-PSC committees in Theorem 2. It does not claim a mirror for the paper’s empirical comparisons, STV computations, or its other quantified axioms.

The weakest point is that the hardness construction grows the candidate set and the number of ballot types; it does not establish hardness when \(\tau\) is small relative to \(n\). For fixed small \(m\), committee enumeration is already feasible, so the mirror may add little computational value in the paper’s typical small-slate setting. Further questions are whether useful algorithms exist for bounded \(\tau\), how the problem behaves under additional structure in the ballot types, and whether minimizing the achievable \(\alpha\) admits approximation guarantees. That limits the regime supported by the hardness result, but it does not undermine the mirror itself: it is the same proportionality feasibility question on a society represented directly by type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make the requested negative case honestly. Theorem 2 is a particularly direct anchor for a continuous mirror: its \(\alpha\)-PSC feasibility question already depends on voter groups only through their sizes. If \(n_t\) voters cast ballot type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). For every candidate set \(X\), the mass of its maximal solid coalition is exactly the coalition’s voter count divided by \(n\). Thus the paper’s threshold \(|N'|\ge \alpha\ell n/k\) becomes \(M_\mu(X)\ge\alpha\ell/k\), with the same committee constraints. Ballot types are complete descriptions for this problem: voter identities, prices, and personal costs play no role.

The strongest adverse point is about demonstrated relevance, not whether the mirror is well posed. The paper does not show that its individual Scottish elections have support size \(\tau\) much smaller than their electorate, and its hardness construction does not establish hardness in that regime. For a fixed small candidate set, enumerating committees is already feasible. So the paper provides little evidence that continuizing would improve computation on its particular dataset. But that does not rule out a large election with many repeated ballot types, or another large electorate choosing from a manageable slate. Those are sensible high-multiplicity regimes for this problem.

The stronger mirror—minimizing the achievable \(\alpha\)-PSC value over committees in a society given by rational type masses—also survives. It follows the paper’s central quantitative measure directly; the threshold constraints remain meaningful at positive mass, and no individual identity is needed. The paper proves a complexity result for finite voter lists, not for society input as a distribution, so I see no continuous-computation collision here.

The negative case therefore falls short of its universal burden. It can question how much practical value the mirror would have for the paper’s particular elections, but it cannot defeat the feasibility mirror or its optimization version across plausible scenarios. On the supplied material, this is a worthwhile question for the programme.
