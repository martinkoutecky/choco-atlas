The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many good types \(I\), per-capita supplies \(b_i\), and \(\tau\) carrier types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), capacities, and values \(v_t(q)\) for each feasible package \(q\in Q_t\), find a welfare-maximizing mass allocation \(\lambda_{tq}\) and nonnegative type-addressed prices \(p_{tq}\), or report that none exist. Require \(\sum_{q\in Q_t}\lambda_{tq}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,q}q_i\lambda_{tq}\le b_i\), every package with \(\lambda_{tq}>0\) to maximize \(v_t(q)-p_{tq}\) for its type, and the allocation to maximize the seller’s revenue at those prices.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity package market with a finite set of good types, \(\tau\) complete carrier types of masses \(\mu_t\), and feasible packages \(q\in Q_t\). The variable \(\lambda_{tq}\) assigns type mass to packages, supply scales per unit of population, and prices \(p_{tq}\) may discriminate by type and package. With the package sets and values explicitly listed, the allocation and supporting prices come from a finite LP and its dual.

The objection that survived

The mass LP’s prices need not reproduce finite-agent VCG marginal payoffs; this limits the mirror to pricing equilibrium, a limitation the proponent acknowledges but does not bridge.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the pricing-equilibrium and primal-dual characterization in Theorem 5.1 for a high-multiplicity package-assignment market. It leaves the finite-agent VCG payoff claims, the submodularity results, and the counterexamples for setup costs and integral assignments without continuous analogues.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do prices in this mass market capture any limit of finite-population VCG marginal payoffs, or only support the continuous pricing equilibrium?
  • Can the LP be solved efficiently from a compact encoding of the paper’s capacities and per-good values when feasible packages are not listed explicitly?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high-multiplicity version of the package-assignment problem, specialized to the paper’s carrier-planning setting. Imagine a large pool of freight carriers, with \(N\) carriers but only \(\tau\) distinct operating types, where \(\tau\ll N\). A type records everything the model uses about a carrier: its capacity and its valuation for each good or package. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the pool with type \(t\). Shipment supplies scale with the pool; a continuum allocation may divide a type’s mass among different packages, while each unit of mass receives a whole package. That fractional allocation is the natural co-modification needed to make population mass meaningful.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5.1, which the paper cites from Bikhchandani and Ostroy [4], rather than proves. It says that, for the package assignment problem, an assignment and prices for each buyer-package pair form a pricing equilibrium if and only if the assignment is optimal for the package assignment problem and the prices, buyer payoffs, and seller payoff together form a dual-optimal solution of its LP relaxation. The paper uses this characterization computationally: it explains that prices and VCG payments can be obtained by solving the assignment problem and then an LP to select the lowest equilibrium prices. This is a formulation-and-computation anchor, not a theorem classifying the problem as polynomial-time solvable.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Mass Package-Assignment Equilibrium. The input is a finite set of goods \(I\), a list of \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a rational supply \(b_i\) of each good per unit of population, and an explicitly listed package value \(v_t(S)\) for each type \(t\) and package \(S\subseteq I\). For the paper’s capacity model, one can obtain these values by letting a type value a package at the best value of any subset it can process within its capacity. A feasible allocation is a set of masses \(\lambda_{tS}\ge 0\), where \(\lambda_{tS}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving package \(S\), with \(\sum_S\lambda_{tS}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:i\in S}\lambda_{tS}\le b_i\) for each good \(i\).

The task is to return a welfare-maximizing feasible allocation and type-addressed package prices \(p_{tS}\), or report that no such equilibrium exists. The prices must make every package receiving positive mass utility-maximizing for its type, under utility \(v_t(S)-p_{tS}\); at those prices, the chosen feasible allocation must maximize the seller’s revenue. Allowing prices to depend on type and package preserves the paper’s price-discrimination issue. Restricting to anonymous item prices would change the problem the authors study.

For explicitly listed package values, I expect this problem to be tractable: the mass allocation is a finite LP, and its primal and dual solutions give the allocation and supporting prices. The expected running time is polynomial in the explicit input length, which includes the package-value table; that is not a claim of polynomial time in \(m\) for a compact valuation encoding. Whether the paper’s compact per-good values and capacities admit a comparably efficient pricing algorithm is a natural next question. The mirror covers the package-assignment pricing result and the paper’s planning subclass; it does not claim to continuize every result in the paper.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s main results, including Theorem 6.2 on submodularity, establish economic structure rather than computational complexity. Theorem 5.1 is borrowed, and its LP characterization does not itself prove a new high-multiplicity complexity result. Also, an individual’s finite-population VCG payoff does not transfer automatically to a nonatomic population. A faithful continuation would need to define how those marginal contributions behave as mass changes. Still, this is a credible positive starting point: the paper already connects assignment, prices, and LP duality, and a large population of repeated carrier types is a plausible regime for its planning problem. The price computation and the continuum analogue of VCG payoffs should be treated as open questions, not assumed results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed anchor is not a computational result of this paper. Theorem 5.1, which the paper attributes to Bikhchandani and Ostroy, characterizes pricing equilibria through primal and dual LP solutions; it does not classify the complexity of a problem. The paper’s own main results establish submodularity and substitutes properties. Its planning problem is already polynomially solvable for a finite set of agents. So the paper offers no computational bottleneck that population continuization would resolve or expose.

The carrier scenario is plausible, and the proposed mass model is a sensible high-multiplicity model. But its computational content depends on the encoding. With all package values listed, its LP has size polynomial in the explicit table, which contains \(\tau 2^m\) values. For the paper’s actual linear capacity model, identical agents can be aggregated directly into an LP with \(O(\tau m)\) allocation variables. Either way, the proposed computation is a standard LP formulation, not a pricing problem whose separation complexity the paper identifies.

There is also a mismatch with Theorem 5.1’s substantive question. That theorem relates finite package assignments and their VCG payoffs to prices and dual solutions. The proposed mass allocation already allows each type’s mass to be split among packages. Its dual prices support the resulting convexified welfare allocation; they do not, by themselves, establish that the finite agents’ VCG payoffs are supported. A better mirror could retain integer type counts, scale supplies with population size, and study exact or approximate VCG support as the counts grow. That is a legitimate research question, but it asks for a new discrete-to-continuum or integrality-gap result. The paper states no complexity claim about that problem. Likewise, a continuous analogue of Theorem 6.2 would be a structural statement about diminishing returns over masses, not the computational question ChoCo is meant to chart.

I would not reject the high-multiplicity regime: repeated carrier types are credible, and existing high-multiplicity work supports treating it seriously. The negative case is that this particular paper supplies no computational result to mirror; turning its equilibrium and submodularity results into one requires adding a new question. If the programme counts such question-building as a worthwhile mirror in its own right, this objection is correspondingly weak.
