What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror does not cover a named computational result; the paper’s equilibrium characterizations, welfare comparisons, and asymptotic result remain analytical results for finite electorates.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is a very large membership organization deciding whether to adopt a reform: its members share a small number of information and preference types, but the population is too large to describe voter by voter. A candidate mirror would use masses \(\mu_R,\mu_B,\mu_P\) for the paper’s common-value signal types \(z_R,z_B\) and private-value type \(z_P\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). If \(x_{t,a}\) is the mass of type \(t\) choosing action \(a\in\{r,b,v\}\), the reform passes when total veto mass is zero and \(x_r>x_b\). This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime with large \(n\) and only three types.

But the paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. The closest results are analytic: Proposition 2, proved here, characterizes a responsive symmetric equilibrium under Veto across signal-precision regions; Theorem 1, proved here, says Veto Full Information Pareto Dominates both Majority and Unanimity, with the stated strict-dominance exceptions; and Theorem 2, proved here, says Veto Interim Pareto Dominates Unanimity for all \(\beta,n\), strictly unless \(\phi_B\ge\phi_4\). Proposition 5 proves asymptotic optimality as \(n\) grows. None classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithmic guarantee, so none is an eligible anchor.

A proposed atomless equilibrium-computation problem would also change the strategic question: an individual has zero mass and cannot be pivotal, while the paper’s equilibrium analysis turns on finite-\(n\) pivotal probabilities. Treating each type as a bloc would preserve influence but change who acts and how private information shapes behavior. Thus the population-continuum idea is plausible, but the paper does not supply the computational anchor needed to make a qualifying mirror. The honest positive case is for a new computational follow-up to its voting model, not a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper states no computational result. Proposition 2 characterizes responsive symmetric equilibria; Theorems 1 and 2 compare welfare across voting systems; Proposition 5 gives an asymptotic result. None asks for an algorithm or classifies complexity. Those results can inspire a new computational project, but there is no computational theorem here for a continuous mirror to anchor.

The large membership organization is nevertheless a credible high-multiplicity setting: the three types are complete descriptions for this model, and many members may share each type. The objection is not that the regime is implausible. It is that the paper’s strategic results depend on an individual voter sometimes being pivotal. In an atomless society, one voter’s action changes no aggregate mass. If \(x_{t,a}\) is the mass of type \(t\) choosing action \(a\), a single voter cannot change whether veto mass is zero or whether \(x_r>x_b\). Individual incentives therefore no longer select the paper’s voting behavior; responsive strategies can become arbitrary equilibria. Treating each type as a bloc restores influence by changing who acts. Keeping finite-\(n\) pivotal events restores the discrete strategic game. Adding a manipulation cost or another intervention objective could create a computational problem, but that would be a new model rather than a mirror of these results.

For Proposition 2, the best computational candidate would be to compute equilibrium strategies or locate the precision thresholds. But those thresholds are defined by finite-\(n\) pivotal probabilities. In the atomless version, the pivotal events that discipline the strategies disappear. Retaining them requires finite-population fluctuations, while treating the three types as blocs changes the strategic unit. Neither yields an algorithmic problem over a continuous society that preserves this result’s question.

For Theorem 1, one could ask whether Veto FIP-dominates the alternatives for a given mass distribution. That is a welfare comparison, not a computational result. A mass-based version also needs a specified information structure: if the realized type masses are available to voters, the continuum changes what they know; if they are not, the mass profile alone does not determine strategic behavior. Giving the problem enough extra structure to resolve this creates a new question, rather than a computational mirror of the theorem.

Theorem 2 has the same gap. Interim Pareto comparison can be restated for type masses, but a representative agent of measure zero still cannot affect the outcome. The paper’s link between individual incentives and the welfare comparison is then missing. Moving to a finite-\(n\) approximation preserves that link only by retaining the discrete voters whose pivotality the continuum was meant to replace.

Finally, Proposition 5 already establishes asymptotic optimality as \(n\) grows, but it is an analytic limit result, not a computational guarantee. It does not provide an algorithmic anchor. And a continuous model that preserves its limiting welfare conclusion would not thereby recover the finite-population equilibrium problem.

So the strongest case against is not that large societies lack a sensible continuous description. They do. It is that, for every proposed anchor, atomlessness removes the pivotal incentives central to the result, while preserving those incentives requires finite voters or a new strategic mechanism. The honest limit of this case is that a separate computational project about voting-rule design might be worthwhile; the paper itself supplies no computational mirror to justify it as part of ChoCo.
