The continuous mirror question

Given a finite bipartite graph of types with positive rational masses summing to \(1\), strict rankings of compatible counterpart types, and rational costs per type pair, represent agents of each type by an atomless cohort. Find a minimum-cost measurable one-to-one matching \(M\) such that for every alternative measurable matching \(N\) on the same population, the mass of agents who strictly prefer their partner type in \(M\) to their partner type in \(N\) is at least the mass who strictly prefer \(N\). Treat being unmatched as last; compare types rather than identities within a type.

The model it lives in

An atomless two-sided market with \(\tau\) types, rational type masses, type-level preferences and costs, and measurable one-to-one assignments. A matching can be represented by its type-pair masses, up to measure-preserving relabelling, while its popularity is evaluated against every challenger realization on that same population.

The objection that survived

A challenger’s best vote margin may depend on how it assigns new partners across incumbent-partner subcohorts, so the proposed ordinary capacitated-matching separation intuition does not establish tractability.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s exact minimum-cost popular-matching problem. It leaves Theorem 4’s semi-popular approximation aside, since a continuum analogue of the fraction of all matchings defeated would require an additional measure on alternatives.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a fixed incumbent type-pair flow, can a challenger’s maximum vote margin be represented and separated in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\)?
  • Can minimum-cost popular type-mass matching be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\), and how does it relate to discrete high-multiplicity instances with masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is Min-Cost Popular Type-Mass Matching: keep the paper’s exact optimization question—find the cheapest matching that no alternative defeats—and replace repeated, exchangeable agents by their type masses.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that, given a bipartite graph on \(n\) vertices with strict preference lists and edge costs, a minimum-cost popular matching can be computed in \(O^*(2^{n/4})\), or about \(O^*(1.19^n)\), time. The paper also gives the sharper running time \(O(2^p\operatorname{poly}(n))\), where \(p\) counts popular-subgraph components of size at least four; this makes the algorithm fixed-parameter tractable in \(p\), and polynomial when \(p=O(\log n)\). The introduction reports earlier NP-hardness for the exact problem, citing [9], but that result is not a numbered theorem proved here. Theorem 2 is therefore the strongest eligible numbered anchor, though it is an algorithmic one.

Here is the continuous problem I would give a prover. An instance is a finite bipartite graph \(H=(A_T\cup B_T,F)\) of agent types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one. Each type has a strict ranking of its compatible opposite-side types, with being unmatched last, and each compatible type pair \(ij\) has a rational assignment cost \(\gamma_{ij}\). A type is complete for this problem: split it whenever its side, preferences, compatibility, or costs differ.

A feasible solution is a mass matching \(x\), with \(x_{ij}\geq 0\), \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq\mu_i\), and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq\mu_j\). To define popularity, realize each type as an atomless cohort and realize \(x_{ij}\) by pairing mass \(x_{ij}\) from type \(i\) with mass \(x_{ij}\) from type \(j\). Every agent is still either matched to one agent or unmatched; only the type-level totals are fractional. For two realized matchings \(M,N\), let
\[ \Delta_\mu(M,N) =\operatorname{mass}\{u:M(u)\succ_u N(u)\} -\operatorname{mass}\{u:N(u)\succ_u M(u)\}. \]
Here \(M(u)\) is the type of \(u\)’s partner, or \(\bot\) if unmatched. Call \(M\) popular when \(\Delta_\mu(M,N)\geq 0\) for every feasible \(N\). The problem is to return a minimum-cost popular mass matching, minimizing \(\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}x_{ij}\). Since agents within each type are exchangeable, the type-pair masses can be the output representation: relabelling members within cohorts maps any realization to an equivalent one and preserves the set of challengers.

A credible regime is a large annual hiring or placement market: many applicants and positions fall into a modest number of profiles, each with the same ranking over counterpart profiles and the same type-pair costs. There might be tens of thousands of agents but only dozens or hundreds of positive-mass types, so \(\tau\ll N\). Type-level assignments can be fractional even though every individual receives an integral assignment. This is precisely the permitted co-modification that makes population mass useful.

I would predict Class A for this mirror, especially when \(\tau\) is moderate. For a fixed matching, finding its strongest challenger looks like a maximum-weight capacitated matching problem, suggesting an LP or separation approach; the paper’s witness characterization and its structure-based algorithm give plausible material to adapt. The question is whether minimizing cost over all popular mass matchings admits a polynomial algorithm in \(\tau\), the type graph size, and the binary input length \(L\), independent of the represented population size. This is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 2. It also opens the right follow-ups: whether type masses alone suffice for a compact popularity formulation; how the discrete problem with proportions \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) relates to the mass optimum; and whether any remaining hardness is driven by the number of types or by individual multiplicity.

This covers Theorem 2’s exact minimum-cost popular matching problem, not the paper’s semi-popular approximation in Theorem 4. The latter measures the fraction of *all matchings* that defeat an output; a continuum of alternatives has no canonical uniform measure without adding another modelling choice.

The weakest point is the preference model. The paper ranks named neighbors strictly; this mirror ranks counterpart types strictly and treats copies within a type as exchangeable. That is plausible for a high-multiplicity market, but it is a real modelling restriction. If the authors require arbitrary individual-specific rankings among named agents, the number of types may grow with the population and this mirror loses much of its force.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a valid anchor: it gives an exact algorithm for min-cost popular matching, with running time \(O(2^p\operatorname{poly}(n))\). My strongest objection is that the proposed type-mass problem does not inherit the paper’s matching model just by replacing headcounts with masses.

In the paper, agents rank named neighbors strictly. To compress many agents into a type while keeping a fixed type graph, the mirror instead has to treat members of a counterpart type as interchangeable—effectively, as ties. If we preserve strict rankings over named people, those rankings can distinguish the very individuals the mass model wants to aggregate. The proposed version may be a sensible capacitated matching problem, but it changes a central feature of the paper’s setting, including its guarantee that a popular matching exists.

There is also a gap in the proposed algorithmic intuition. A mass flow \(x_{ij}\) records how much of type \(i\) is matched to type \(j\); it does not say which members of type \(i\) receive those partners. That matters when popularity compares two matchings person by person. For example, suppose a type is split between two incumbent partner types, and a challenger sends mass to two new partner types. The challenger’s type-pair flow can be fixed while its votes change according to which incumbent subcohort receives which new partner type. To make popularity a property of \(x\), one must quantify over all such realizations. The resulting challenger problem tracks incumbent-partner subcohorts as well as type pairs; it is not automatically the ordinary maximum-weight capacitated matching suggested in the case for.

This does not make the model incoherent. With an explicit worst-case-over-realizations definition, it could be studied, and the extra subcohorts may still be manageable as a function of \(\tau\). But the case for offers no reason to expect the paper’s structural algorithm or a convenient pricing problem to survive. Meanwhile, the tempting existing popular fractional matching results concern lotteries over matchings, which is outcome-space continuity rather than a continuous population; they do not settle this mirror. [Huang and Kavitha’s treatment](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.2020.1063)

So the negative case is that the cleanest type-flow formulation is not yet a faithful mirror of Theorem 2, while a faithful mass-vote formulation needs more state and a new separation analysis. That is a real weakness in the proposed case, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists: the categorical, exchangeable market remains plausible, and its added complexity could itself motivate research. I cannot honestly claim to have defeated this anchor under the stated standard.
