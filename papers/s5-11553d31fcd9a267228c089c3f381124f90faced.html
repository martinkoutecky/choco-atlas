What the mirror covers

The proposal focuses on Theorem 11’s max-min rebate guarantee. It leaves the Shapley and Banzhaf characterizations, the impossibility results, and the auction allocation results without a continuous question.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a qualifying anchor: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result. It mentions in prose after Definition 3 that computing MEV is at least an NP problem, citing [3, 4, 19], then assumes a perfect MEV oracle. Section 6 leaves efficient computation of the rebate operator for future work.

The closest positive result is Theorem 11, proved in the paper. It constructs an operator \(\psi\) with symmetry, marginality, Sybil-proofness, and no deficit. Its worst-case welfare ratio is \(1/2^{n-1}\); together with Proposition 7’s upper bound of \(n/2^{n-1}\) on the best possible ratio, this gives an \(n\)-approximation to the max-min objective. That is a quantitative mechanism-design result, not an algorithmic complexity result. Proposition 14 is also about mechanism properties, rather than the complexity of a problem. Treating either as the requested kind of anchor would overstate the paper’s computational contribution.

If I extend the paper into a new research question, the most plausible scenario is a large order-flow auction epoch with many users submitting standardized swaps. A type would specify everything relevant to the mechanism: the bundle, valuation, costs, and marginal contribution to coalition MEV. The mass \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of claims of type \(t\). Let \(V(x)\) be the surplus generated by executing mass \(x\) of each type. A rebate rule assigns each type a payment rate \(r_t(V,\mu)\). The question would be to find a rule that respects symmetry, strong monotonicity, no deficit, and Sybil-proofness when a fixed mass is split among aliases, while maximizing
\[ \inf_{V:V(\mu)>0}\frac{\sum_t \mu_t r_t(V,\mu)}{V(\mu)}. \]
A solution would give the rule and its optimal worst-case guarantee; a computational version could ask how to evaluate it from a succinct representation of \(V\). I would expect the complexity to depend on that representation and on how the Sybil constraints are encoded; this paper provides no basis for predicting tractability or hardness transfer.

The weakness is central: the paper’s Sybil deviations concern named finite players. In an atomless population, splitting an individual’s mass may leave the aggregate unchanged and make the strategic constraint vacuous. So this is a plausible new direction, but not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that there is no qualifying computational result to anchor a mirror. Theorem 11 proves properties and a worst-case welfare guarantee for \(\psi\); it gives no algorithm, running time, or input representation. Proposition 7 likewise bounds a mechanism’s guarantee, and Proposition 14 is an incentive-compatibility and impossibility result. The paper’s computational claim—that computing MEV is at least an NP problem—is cited background, then set aside by assuming a perfect oracle. Efficiently evaluating the rebate operator is left for future work. Turning any of these into a computational question would add a problem the paper does not solve.

The strongest replacement scenario is a large order-flow auction with many repeated, standardized transactions. That could make high multiplicity sensible. But the paper’s cooperative game is an arbitrary function on *named coalitions*. Each player’s marginal contribution can depend on exactly which other players are present. A type-based population measure does not retain that information unless the game is restricted to exchangeable players, where coalition value depends only on type masses. In that restricted model, splitting a mass among aliases leaves the mass distribution unchanged, so the paper’s Sybil constraint either becomes vacuous or must track the identity partition as extra input. The former changes the strategic question; the latter restores the named-agent structure the continuum was meant to replace.

The proponent’s proposed optimization over a continuous surplus function \(V\) could still be a new research direction. But it needs a specified class and representation of \(V\), plus new definitions of mass-level rebates and Sybil deviations. Its tractability would be a property of those choices, not a continuous counterpart of Theorem 11. The guarantee \(1/2^{n-1}\) also has no stated population-limit interpretation; taking \(n\) to infinity makes that particular guarantee vanish, while finding a useful mass-level replacement would require new theory.

So the case against a *mirror* is strong, though the universal claim is not airtight: repeated order flow is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. What fails is the connection to a computational result in this paper. The continuous optimization the proponent sketches may be worth studying independently, but the paper supplies neither the computational anchor nor the population model that would make it a ChoCo mirror.
