What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation covers the homogeneous assignment in Theorem 3; it leaves the paper’s heterogeneity, identification, and inference results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a high multiplicity version of the paper’s homogeneous social surplus assignment. Call it Mass-Optimal Assignment. A scenario is a large centralized placement market: many candidates and vacancies are matched one-to-one, but match value depends only on a candidate’s and vacancy’s standardized, problem-relevant types. For example, the types could record credentials and specialty on one side, and role and location on the other. Each type represents a fraction of its side of the market. The regime has \(N\) agents per side with \(N\) large and support sizes \(\tau_X,\tau_Y\) much smaller than \(N\). People of the same type are interchangeable for the objective.

An instance consists of finite type supports \(X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_{\tau_X}\}\) and \(Y=\{y_1,\ldots,y_{\tau_Y}\}\), rational masses \(p_i,q_j\) summing to one, and a rational surplus \(\Phi_{ij}\) for each type pair. A solution is a mass assignment \(\pi_{ij}\ge 0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_j \pi_{ij}=p_i,\qquad \sum_i \pi_{ij}=q_j, \]
that maximizes \(\sum_{i,j}\pi_{ij}\Phi_{ij}\). Thus \(\pi_{ij}\) is the fraction of the market matching type \(x_i\) to type \(y_j\); its row or column can split across partner types even though each individual gets one partner. With rational input, the question is to return an optimal assignment and its value, optionally with dual potentials certifying optimality.

The anchor is Theorem 3, “Homogeneous social welfare,” proved in this paper (Appendix B.3). Under assumptions (O) and (DD), it states that \(W_0(\Phi)\) equals both the maximum of \(\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)\Phi(x,y)\) over couplings \(\Pi\in\mathcal M(P,Q)\), and the minimum of \(\sum_x p(x)u(x)+\sum_y q(y)v(y)\) over \(u,v\) satisfying \(u(x)+v(y)\ge\Phi(x,y)\) for every pair. It also states that a matching is optimal exactly when \(u(X)+v(Y)=\Phi(X,Y)\) almost surely under that matching. The primal is the transport LP in the proposed problem; the dual gives a certificate. The theorem itself does not claim a complexity class. The Class A expectation follows from applying standard rational linear programming to this finite formulation, in time polynomial in \(\tau_X\tau_Y\) and the input bit length.

There is an exact bridge to the finite market: if type masses are integer counts divided by \(N\), the transportation polytope has an optimal vertex that scales to an integer type-pair count matrix. That matrix can be realized by pairing named individuals of each type. So this mirror does not change the optimum of the homogeneous finite assignment; it gives a compressed representation whose size depends on the number of types rather than the number of people. I would expect tractability, with further questions about faster algorithms for structured surplus tables, and about how the optimal assignment responds to changes in the type masses.

This case covers the homogeneous, type-based matching objective in Theorem 3. It does not extend to the paper’s results about unobserved heterogeneity, parameter identification, or inference. I do not see a named hardness result in the paper to anchor a hardness-transfer question; the positive case here is the computational use of a named optimization formulation.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper already formulates its matching problem using distributions and couplings over types, so this mirror is very close to the object of Theorem 3. The strongest distinction is the added worst-case complexity question for a binary-encoded, high multiplicity instance, where \(N\) may be enormous but \(\tau_X,\tau_Y\) are small. If the novelty gate requires the paper’s model itself to remain finite-agent rather than already population-level, this may count as a collision. The case for accepting it is that the finite named-agent assignment and the mass problem are distinct representations, and the finite-to-mass identity is a useful bridge; the case is for computationally charting that bridge, not for claiming the population formulation is new.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor fails at the continuization step: Theorem 3 already formulates the population as distributions over types and the matching as a mass assignment. That is the object ChoCo would produce. The paper’s \(P\) and \(Q\) are discrete type distributions, and its feasible \(\Pi\) is a joint distribution with those marginals. The theorem maximizes \(\sum_{x,y}\pi(x,y)\Phi(x,y)\) over exactly those couplings. Discrete characteristics do not mean a finite, individually represented population; the paper explicitly works with population distributions, and its heterogeneity assumption even posits infinitely many individuals of a given observable type.

The stronger placement-market version does not escape this overlap. Credentials and specialties on one side, roles and locations on the other, are simply \(x\) and \(y\); their masses are \(P\) and \(Q\), and pair values are \(\Phi(x,y)\). Encoding those masses as binary rationals, or asking for the type-pair matrix and dual potentials, changes the input format and requested output, not the population model. The proposed finite-to-mass bridge is already built into the paper: it starts with named-agent matchings, represents them by distributions over types, and Theorem 3 gives the mass formulation. Scaling integral type counts to a transportation problem does not supply a new continuous mirror.

There is also no numbered complexity result here for a mirror to continue. Theorem 3 is a welfare and duality characterization, not a complexity classification. A complexity study of this assignment problem might still be useful as a separate project, but its subject would be the paper’s existing population-level model. Calling that study a continuization would mistake an already continuous population formulation for a new one. This objection does not depend on whether the LP is easy or on what its answer is.

The negative case is strongest for mirrors that preserve Theorem 3’s assignment objective and weakest against a broader claim that some computational follow-up could be worthwhile. Adding named identities that matter to the objective either enlarges the type support, potentially to one type per person, or changes the problem beyond the theorem’s assignment model. Such variants may motivate other work; they do not rescue this anchor as a continuous mirror.
