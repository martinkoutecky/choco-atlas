The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of chapter-role types \(T\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), direct-voting probabilities \(p_t\), a fixed delegation map within each repeated chapter template, and a target type \(t^\star\) with \(p_{t^\star}>0\), compute the exact expected voting weight of an active member of \(t^\star\): \(\Phi(t^\star)=1+\sum_{s\ne t^\star}(\mu_s/\mu_{t^\star})r(s,t^\star)\), where \(r(s,t^\star)\) is the probability that a member of role \(s\) delegates along the template path to \(t^\star\), and is \(0\) if no such path exists.

The model it lives in

Types are positions in a finite set of repeated chapter templates, each with a direct-voting probability and a fixed delegate role. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the population fraction in that role; the decision is to evaluate the exact expected voting weight per active member of the target type, using source-to-target path probabilities and mass ratios.

The objection that survived

The chapter template retains the path structure that determines influence, so type masses largely encode repeated copies rather than removing the underlying path calculation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s identity for expected voting weight and first-passage influence. It leaves aside the stationary-weight result, the other delegation claims, and the paper’s broader agenda on adaptation over time.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the bit complexity of exact evaluation of \(\Phi(t^\star)\) from the role graph, rational masses, and rational probabilities?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: Appendix B.4, Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It establishes that for every suspendible delegation profile \(\mathcal D\) and user \(i\), expected voting weight equals first-passage weight:
\[ \phi_i=\mathbb E_{D\sim\mathcal D}[w_i]=\psi_i =1+\sum_{j\in\mathcal N}p(ji), \]
where \(p(ji)\) is the probability that \(j\)’s voting right reaches \(i\) along the delegation path. This is an exact influence-evaluation result, and the paper uses the formulas to calculate and compare influence values. The paper gives no named complexity classification—such as an NP-hardness or polynomial-time theorem—so this is a modest algorithmic anchor, rather than a hardness result.

A credible high-multiplicity regime is a large association with many structurally repeated local chapters. Each chapter follows one of a small set of delegation templates. A type \(t\) records a position in a template, its direct-voting probability \(p_t\), and the position \(d(t)\) to which it delegates. Members in the same type behave identically within their chapter template. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the overall membership in that type. The number of members \(N\) can grow with the number of chapters while the number of types \(\tau\) stays fixed or small. The delegation graph within each chapter retains the paper’s individual links; mass records how many copies of each role occur.

My lead mirror is Continuous Expected Influence. An instance consists of a finite support \(T\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational direct-voting probabilities \(p_t\), the template’s delegation map \(d\), and a target type \(t^\star\) with \(p_{t^\star}>0\). The question is: what is the exact expected voting weight carried by an active member of \(t^\star\), averaged over the repeated chapters? The delegation profile \(d\) is part of the instance; the task evaluates its influence rather than choosing a new profile.

For types \(s\) and \(t^\star\) in the same template, let \(r(s,t^\star)\) be the product of \(1-p_u\) over the roles \(u\) on the delegation path from \(s\) up to, but excluding, \(t^\star\). Set it to zero when that path does not reach \(t^\star\). Then the continuous query returns
\[ \Phi(t^\star) =1+\sum_{\substack{s\in T\\s\ne t^\star}} \frac{\mu_s}{\mu_{t^\star}}\,r(s,t^\star). \]
The mass ratio accounts for the average number of source-role members per target-role member; it can be fractional in the continuous instance. A solution is this exact rational value. The formula is Theorem 1’s influence calculation aggregated over repeated role templates.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: path probabilities and rational mass ratios can be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). It is a Class A evaluation problem. That conclusion is a proposed analysis of the mirror, not a complexity result proved by the paper. It also does not show discrete hardness dissolving: the paper supplies no such hardness anchor. A natural further question is the minimum-cost change to delegation mass needed to cap every type’s influence below a chosen threshold, but that would extend beyond Theorem 1.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their influence question: it keeps direct voting, transitive delegation, and expected voting weight, while making the population distribution the continuous object. The mirror covers Theorem 1 alone, not the paper’s broader claims about adaptive representation over time.

The weakest point is the repeated-template assumption. The paper’s delegation edges point to particular people, whereas this mirror groups people by their role in a repeated chapter structure. When cross-chapter links to named individuals matter, that grouping may fail and \(\tau\) may grow with \(N\). This is a scenario-specific case for a mirror, not a claim that liquid democracy as a whole has a useful continuous limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the paper’s strongest anchor, but it is an identity between two ways of computing expected influence, not a result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its calculation sums the probabilities of delegation paths. The paper poses no search, optimization, or decision problem over those paths. In the proposed mirror, the answer is still obtained by multiplying path probabilities and summing them with rational mass ratios. That is a sensible quantity to calculate, but it does not expose the kind of complexity question ChoCo is set up to study.

There is a deeper modeling tradeoff. The population’s type masses do not determine who delegates to whom, which paths converge on a target, or where cycles occur. Those details are exactly what determine influence in this paper. If types include enough network structure to recover them for a general delegation graph, the number of types can grow with the number of agents, removing the compact high-multiplicity representation. If the graph is instead built from repeated finite chapter templates, as the proponent suggests, a fixed template supplies the missing structure—but then the continuous calculation is just the template’s per-member influence calculation, repeated and averaged across copies. That is a coherent narrow scenario, but it gives no new computational landscape.

The repeated-template case is the hardest point for the negative case: it makes the per-member quantity meaningful and can keep the number of roles small. But it does so by preserving finite, local delegation graphs inside each copy. Aggregate masses neither replace those graphs nor simplify the path calculation. Asking how to change the graph to limit influence could be more computationally ambitious, but it would introduce an intervention problem, its costs, and its feasible changes; the paper does not formulate those. On the evidence here, I would not claim that no useful continuous model of liquid democracy is possible. I would claim that this paper offers little basis for a worthwhile ChoCo mirror: its central result is a path-sum identity, and the most plausible high-multiplicity version either loses the network information the result needs or retains a fixed network template whose influence is already directly computable.
