The continuous mirror question

Given a unit-mass population \(P=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}P_t\) with \(\lambda(P_t)=\mu_t\), additive type valuations \(v_t\), perfect type-level MIV predictions \(p_t\), and an adaptive online stream of indivisible goods whose type valuations are revealed on arrival, assign each good \(g_j\) irrevocably to a measurable set \(S_j\subseteq P\) of mass \(1/N\). What is the largest \(\alpha\) that an online algorithm can guarantee, against every such stream, so that for \(\lambda\)-almost every agent \(a\in P_t\), the resulting integral bundle satisfies \(\alpha\)-PROPX?

The model it lives in

A finite-type, high-multiplicity population with mass vector \(\mu\); indivisible goods routed through measurable recipient sets of mass \(1/N\); integral bundle histories distributed continuously within each type; and the objective of maximizing the worst-case \(\alpha\)-PROPX guarantee for almost every agent under perfect MIV advice.

The objection that survived

The lower bound in Proposition 5.1 targets a named agent and uses allocation-dependent valuations, so a fixed-\(\tau\) typewise model may lose the paper's obstruction unless a new positive-mass construction is found.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Proposition 5.1 as a population-level strong-fairness problem and leaves the paper's greedy impossibilities, random-allocation bounds, MIV algorithm, prediction-error theorem, corollary, and empirical results untreated; the proposed Theorem 4.1 analogue changes the guarantee to a type average.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(\tau\ll N\), does any online algorithm guarantee positive \(\alpha\)-PROPX against adaptive typewise streams with perfect MIV predictions?
  • Can the resulting mass-routing process be solved by a finite configuration-flow or column-generation formulation, with explicit dependence on \(\tau\), \(N\), and \(\min_t\mu_t\)?
  • Can a positive-mass obstruction replace the paper's named-agent construction without allowing effective types to proliferate?

The case FOR (proponent)

I find a credible mirror, with Proposition 5.1 as the lead anchor and Theorem 4.1 as supporting evidence. The mirror continuizes the population, not the goods: goods remain indivisible and arrive irrevocably, but a good is assigned to a measurable mass of agents representing many finite-population recipients.

Let \(P\) be a unit-mass population partitioned into finitely many complete types \(T\), with \(\lambda(P_t)=\mu_t\). Agents in \(P_t\) have the same valuation for every good, the same MIV prediction, and the same other parameters. Let \(N\) be the population scale: a unit good is assigned to a measurable subset \(S_j\subseteq P\) of mass \(1/N\). Thus each individual agent receives an integral bundle, while the type-level allocation is fractional. If \(x_{tj}=\lambda(S_j\cap P_t)/\mu_t\), then \(x_{tj}\in[0,1]\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=1/N\).

This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime for online advertising: \(N\) may be millions of advertisers, \(M=\Theta(N)\) impressions arrive sequentially, and advertisers fall into a small number \(\tau\) of campaign types with identical valuation and prediction profiles. The meaningful regime is \(N\gg\tau\), not a seven-person committee.

My lead anchor is Proposition 5.1, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(n\ge2\) and every \(\alpha>0\), no online algorithm can always return an \(\alpha\)-EF1, \(\alpha\)-MMS, or \(\alpha\)-PROPX allocation, even with perfect MIV predictions. The proposition therefore rules out every positive constant approximation for all three stronger fairness notions under adaptive arrivals.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuous Online Strong Fairness under MIV. Given \(N\), \((T,\mu)\), perfect type-level MIV predictions \(p_t\), and an adaptive stream of goods whose type valuations are revealed on arrival, choose measurable recipient sets \(S_j\) of mass \(1/N\) online and irrevocably. The objective is to maximize the largest \(\alpha\) guaranteed for almost every agent.

For the PROPX variant, the requirement is that for almost every \(a\in P_t\), either \(A(a)=G\), or every good \(g\notin A(a)\) satisfies \(v_t(A(a)\cup\{g\})\ge\alpha V_t/N\), where \(V_t=\sum_jv_t(g_j)\). The EF1 variant compares almost every ordered pair of agents and allows deletion of one good from the other agent’s bundle. The MMS variant requires \(v_t(A(a))\ge\alpha\,\operatorname{MMS}_{t,N}(G)\), where the MMS is defined using \(N\)-partitions of the goods.

This is not merely the finite problem with notation changed. The output is a flow of bundle mass: for each type and each currently possible integral bundle \(B\), the algorithm maintains how much type mass has bundle \(B\), then moves mass from \(B\) to \(B\cup\{g_j\}\) when the next good arrives. The individual bundles remain integral; only their distribution within a type is continuous.

For the unrestricted continuous problem, I expect hardness to transfer: the finite-agent model embeds by taking types of mass \(1/N\), with the continuous allocation sets representing individual agents. The more interesting high-multiplicity restriction, with fixed \(\tau\ll N\), is not settled by Proposition 5.1. Its adversary targets named agents, while a continuum algorithm can rotate goods among a positive-mass cohort. That makes the fixed-\(\tau\) version a genuine boundary question: the original impossibility may dissolve, or a new continuum-specific obstruction may survive. Either outcome would be a meaningful result rather than a restatement.

The supporting anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved here. It says that for every \(\delta\in(0,1)\) and \(n\ge2\), against a non-adaptive adversary, the algorithm Rand—which assigns every arriving good uniformly at random to an agent—produces an \(\alpha\)-PROP1 allocation with probability at least \(1-\delta\), where \(\alpha=\Theta(1/\log(n/\delta))\).

Its continuous counterpart is:

Continuous Random Mass Allocation for PROP1. Given \((T,\mu)\), \(N\), and a fixed non-adaptive stream of goods, choose for each arriving good an independently random measurable subset \(S_j\) of mass \(1/N\). For type \(t\), let \(A(a)\) be the integral bundle of agent \(a\), and require with probability at least \(1-\delta\) that the type-average PROP1 ratio is at least \(\alpha\), namely that

\(\frac{N}{\mu_tV_t}\int_{P_t}\left(v_t(A(a))+\max_{g\notin A(a)}v_t(g)\right)\,d\lambda(a)\ge\alpha\)

for every positive-mass type \(t\).

The action is still an allocation of indivisible goods. The integral is only averaging over many same-type agents. This is precisely the sort of fractional type-level object created by high multiplicity, not outcome-space continuization. The expected classification is Class A: the finite-type version should admit concentration and configuration-flow algorithms, with a nontrivial \(\alpha\) and stronger guarantees when items are small relative to per-capita value. The exact dependence on \(\tau\), \(N\), \(\delta\), and the minimum type mass is an open question generated by the mirror.

The authors should recognise these as their problems. The online sequence, irrevocability, additive valuations, adaptive versus non-adaptive adversaries, MIV predictions, and PROP1/EF1/MMS/PROPX benchmarks all remain. The only structural change is replacing a large population of repeated agents by its type distribution and allowing mass to be routed among identical agents.

There is no novelty collision here. The paper studies finite agents and indivisible goods; it does not formulate a continuous population or a high-multiplicity online allocation problem. Nor is this merely divisible fair division: every unit of population receives whole goods. The continuous object is the society.

The weakest point is individual fairness. The type-average PROP1 problem is weaker than requiring every agent in a type to satisfy PROP1, and an opponent can reasonably argue that averaging erases precisely the obstruction studied by Proposition 5.1. That is why the lead mirror retains the stronger almost-every-agent formulation, while the random-allocation mirror explicitly identifies the type-average version as its high-multiplicity relaxation. The distinction is not hidden; it is itself a substantive question: when many agents share a type, does online fairness become a tractable mass-balancing problem, or do individual bundle constraints preserve hardness?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s two anchors fail for the same structural reason: the paper’s difficulty is individual-level and identity-sensitive, while a genuine high-multiplicity population erases exactly that information. Restoring it either destroys the multiplicity regime or turns the model into a new problem.

Proposition 5.1 is not a transferable continuous impossibility result. Its construction targets a named agent—agent \(1\)—and makes later valuations depend on that agent’s evolving bundle, through conditions such as \(\lvert A_1^t\rvert=1\). This is precisely the sort of singular event that disappears in a high-multiplicity limit. If agent \(1\) is one member of a positive-mass type, then the future goods must have the same value for every member of that type. The adversary can no longer make a good valuable specifically to the individual who happened to receive \(g_1\).

There are only three ways to repair this.

First, give every individual a separate type. That preserves the proposition, but it is the singleton regime \(\tau=N\), with each type having mass \(1/N\). It is merely a continuous notation for the finite instance and offers no population-level compression or high-multiplicity phenomenon.

Second, let future valuations depend on each agent’s allocation history. Then agents who began with the same valuation type split into different effective types after every relevant allocation. After \(m\) goods, the state is a distribution over as many as \(2^m\) bundle histories. The continuous object is no longer a fixed distribution over agent types; it is an exponentially large measure-valued process. That may define a new online configuration problem, but it is not the paper’s finite-agent impossibility carried over to a continuous society.

Third, keep finitely many genuinely identical types and require fairness for almost every agent. Then the bad individual used by Proposition 5.1 has vanishing population mass, and the proposition’s obstruction is gone. To recover a positive-mass obstruction one would need a new adversarial construction, not supplied by this paper. In particular, the proponent concedes that the fixed-\(\tau\) version is unsettled; that unresolved version, rather than Proposition 5.1, would be the actual research problem.

The same problem is sharper for Theorem 4.1. The paper’s guarantee is individual: every one of the \(n\) agents must satisfy PROP1 simultaneously. The proposed type-average condition replaces this by an integral. That is not a population translation of the theorem; it changes the fairness objective from a minimum to a mean. The paper itself emphasizes that its benchmarks are individual-level fairness notions.

The difference is substantive. Consider one valuation type, \(N\) recipient slots, and \(M=kN\) unit-value goods. Under uniform random allocation, an individual receives \(X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(kN,1/N)\) goods. If \(X=0\), their PROP1 value is only \(1\), while the target is \(\alpha k\). Whenever \(\alpha k>1\), that individual fails. A positive fraction of the population has \(X=0\), tending to \(e^{-k}\) in the high-multiplicity limit. Thus Rand does not satisfy the almost-every-agent version, even though it satisfies the proposed type-average version: the average bundle value is exactly \(k\), and the unlucky agents disappear inside the integral.

This also destroys the paper’s matching lower-bound intuition. Proposition 4.2 relies on the event that some named agent receives no goods. After averaging over a positive-mass type, that event is irrelevant unless a positive fraction of the type is bad. Replacing the mean by an essential infimum restores individual fairness, but then the random-allocation theorem fails. Replacing it by a \(1-\eta\) quantile introduces a new parameter and a new fairness notion; it is a plausible research direction, but not a mirror of Theorem 4.1.

The proposed continuous formulation therefore has a fork with no satisfactory anchor. If it preserves the paper’s individual guarantees, identical types prevent the adversary from reproducing the paper’s constructions. If it preserves the paper’s identity-sensitive adversary, the number of effective types grows with the population or with the bundle-history space. If it averages over agents, it removes the very rare-agent obstruction that makes the paper’s theorem nontrivial.

This is not an objection that the answer might become easier, nor an “already studied” objection. A fixed-type, quantile-fair online allocation problem could be worthwhile in its own right. But it would be a new high-multiplicity fair-division programme, not a continuous mirror anchored by Proposition 5.1 or Theorem 4.1. On the anchors actually offered, I would reject the paper as a worthwhile ChoCo continuization candidate.
