The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) binary issues, a feasible set \(C\) represented by implications and promised \(k\)-restrictive for fixed \(k\), and ballot types \(b^1,\ldots,b^\tau\in\{0,1\}^m\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute \(w\in C\) such that no nonempty issue set \(S\) has both \(w^S\in C\) and positive mass of types agreeing with \(1-w\) on every issue in \(S\), with \(F_\mu(w^S)-F_\mu(w)\ge 1/m^2\). Here \(w^S\) flips \(w\) on \(S\), and \(F_\mu(w)=\sum_t\mu_t H_{u_t(w)}\), where \(u_t(w)\) counts the issues on which type \(t\) agrees with \(w\) and \(H_r=\sum_{q=1}^r1/q\), with \(H_0=0\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete binary ballot types. Outcomes remain integral and feasible in \(C\); a move flips a set of issues on which a positive-mass group agrees, and the objective is the mass-weighted PAV score \(F_\mu\). Compute an outcome with no feasible group deviation improving that score by at least \(1/m^2\).

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 16’s local-search computation for binary instances with fixed-\(k\), \(k\)-restrictive constraints. It does not transfer Theorem 17’s proportionality guarantee or the paper’s other results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can this threshold-stable outcome be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the rational-mass encoding length \(L\), for fixed \(k\)?
  • Does Theorem 17’s proportionality guarantee extend to mass-based groups under the same type-distribution model?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the Continuous LS-PAV\(_C\) local-search problem, anchored in Proposition 16, proved in this paper: for election instances whose constraint \(C\) is \(k\)-restrictive, with \(k\) fixed, LS-PAV\(_C\) terminates in polynomial time. The paper offers no comparable named hardness result, so this is the strongest computational anchor I can defend.

Here is the mirror. There are \(m\) binary public issues and a finite set of ballot types \(T=\{b^1,\ldots,b^\tau\}\subseteq\{0,1\}^m\). The input gives each type a positive rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and a nonempty feasible set \(C\) represented by implications and promised to be \(k\)-restrictive for fixed \(k\), as in the paper. The instance also includes the total encoding length \(L\). A type is a complete ballot: agents with that ballot are indistinguishable for the satisfaction and deviation tests used by this rule. The intended regime is a large public consultation—say, many thousands of residents—with a moderate number \(\tau\) of recurring response patterns, so \(\tau\ll n\).

For \(w\in C\), let \(u_t(w)=|\{j:b^t_j=w_j\}|\) and \(H_r=\sum_{q=1}^r1/q\), with \(H_0=0\). The continuous PAV score is
\[ F_\mu(w)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t H_{u_t(w)}. \]
For a nonempty set of issues \(S\), define \(w^S\) by flipping \(w\) on \(S\). A positive-mass group can make that deviation exactly when \(w^S\in C\) and
\[ \sum_{t:\,b^t_j=1-w_j\ \forall j\in S}\mu_t>0. \]
The continuous problem asks for \(w\in C\) such that no such deviation raises \(F_\mu\) by at least \(1/m^2\). The solution is this threshold-stable feasible outcome—not a globally PAV-maximizing outcome, which would change the underlying question.

This keeps the paper’s problem recognizable: it still selects one feasible public decision, evaluates voters by the paper’s issue-agreement PAV score, and permits only feasible group deviations. The population is the continuous object; outcomes remain integral. That is a natural fit for the paper’s municipal decision setting, where the participants are many residents and a ballot type records their positions across the issues.

I expect this mirror to be tractable, in Class A. For a finite electorate with \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), \(F_\mu\) is exactly the discrete PAV score divided by \(n\), and the paper’s improvement threshold \(n/m^2\) becomes \(1/m^2\). The score is at most \(H_m\), so every accepted move increases it by at least \(1/m^2\), giving at most \(m^2H_m\) moves. Proposition 16’s implication-graph method finds the relevant deviations in polynomial time for fixed \(k\); checking their support and computing their scores can use the \(\tau\) listed types and rational masses directly, without expanding them into \(n\) voters. This gives a plausible algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The claim about that mass-input running time is the proposed continuous result; Proposition 16 itself proves polynomial termination on the paper’s discrete instances.

The mirror covers the computation result in Proposition 16 and the LS-PAV\(_C\) rule it analyzes, under the stated binary and compact-constraint regime. It does not claim to continuize all of the paper’s proportionality results. Further questions include whether the representation guarantee of Theorem 17 transfers to mass-based voter groups, and what happens when \(k\) is part of the input or the constraints use a broader representation.

My weakest point is that this is a modest mirror: Proposition 16 already gives a polynomial-time discrete algorithm, and aggregating repeated ballots may look like a straightforward high-multiplicity implementation rather than a new optimization breakthrough. The case rests on the fact that type mass is the honest population-level input for this rule and makes its computation depend on the number of distinct ballots rather than the headcount.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about marginal value, not whether the mirror makes sense. Proposition 16 is a local-search result over a finite set of outcomes. After dividing PAV by \(n\), its improvement threshold is \(1/m^2\), and the score bound gives at most \(m^2H_m\) improving steps. With ballots grouped by type, score calculations use their masses, while deviation eligibility depends on which types have positive mass. So the proposed algorithm appears to be the paper’s algorithm run on a weighted ballot histogram. Every rational distribution can also be represented by a finite electorate using a common denominator. The paper does not prove the claimed \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) bound, but for this rule the aggregation argument looks direct; it may add little new machinery.

That makes this a modest anchor if the programme prioritizes mirrors that expose a new optimization barrier or a surprising tractability gain. But it does not defeat the mirror. A large consultation with recurring ballot patterns is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the resulting support-size and bit-length bound is a real computational question even if its answer is easy. A stronger mirror might also ask whether Theorem 17’s proportionality guarantee extends to mass-based groups; the paper’s model uses no individual information that obviously blocks that translation.

Under the programme’s own criteria, high-multiplicity structure supports the case, and a straightforward answer is still an answer worth recording. I can make a case for lower priority, but I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror survives this anchor.
