The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying computational anchor: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Its central findings are empirical—for example, Table 1 reports that proximity to single-peakedness rises on average for low- and moderate-salience issues, while Table 4 reports larger increases among the high-information groups. Those findings motivate a computational question, but they are not complexity results. So there are no eligible anchors to mirror under your rule.

The strongest positive case I can make is a mirror of the quantity the paper actually measures: continuous proximity to single-peakedness. Call the problem Continuous Proximity-to-Single-Peakedness. An instance consists of alternatives \(X\), a support \(T\) of complete preference rankings, and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). A solution chooses an ordering \(\Omega\) of the alternatives and masses \(y_t\) to retain, with \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t\), and \(y_t=0\) whenever ranking \(t\) is not single-peaked with respect to \(\Omega\). Maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}y_t\), returning the maximum value and a witnessing ordering and retained masses. Equivalently, compute
\[ S_\infty(\mu)=\max_{\Omega}\sum_{t:\,\mathrm{SP}(t,\Omega)}\mu_t. \]

The regime is a large electorate on a policy question: millions of people rank a small set of alternatives, and many share the same ranking. Each type is a complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of the electorate holding it. If a discrete profile has \(n_t\) people of each ranking, then \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and this problem’s value is exactly the paper’s \(S=m/n\). That makes the proposed question a direct high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s measured object. The authors would plausibly recognize it as their proximity statistic applied to a society represented by ranking frequencies. It does not model the process by which deliberation changes preferences.

For fixed \(m=|X|\), enumerating the \(m!\) orderings computes the answer in \(O(m!\tau)\) compatibility checks, plus arithmetic on the rational masses. For variable \(m\), I would provisionally expect hardness to transfer if the corresponding discrete maximum-single-peaked-subprofile problem is hard: an explicit profile maps directly to masses \(1/n\), and the combinatorics lie in ordering the alternatives. That is a forecast, not a result established by this paper. The natural follow-up is whether the weighted problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), or whether it is hard even when masses are rational and the support is small.

The weakness is substantial: the paper neither asks for an algorithm nor gives a numbered computational result to anchor one. Moreover, its core empirical finding concerns how deliberation changes \(S\), while this mirror only computes \(S\) for a given society. It is a credible continuous-population version of the paper’s measured quantity, but it is not a mirror of a qualifying result under the stated anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the anchor rule. The paper reports empirical before-and-after changes in proximity to single-peakedness, including differences by issue salience and respondents’ information levels. It states no computational problem whose complexity it establishes. The proposed \(S_\infty(\mu)\) is a well-defined computation of the paper’s statistic on a distribution, but it does not capture those findings: it returns proximity for one society, while the paper’s main result concerns how that quantity changes after deliberation and across groups.

A more faithful population version could give each type a before-and-after ranking, information level, and treatment status, then compare the resulting values of \(S\). Given those masses, however, this evaluates an empirical estimand. To turn it into a ChoCo optimization problem—such as finding the least costly intervention that raises proximity to a threshold—one must supply intervention choices, costs, and a model linking deliberation to preference changes. Those additions are absent from the paper. They could motivate a new project, but they do not mirror a computational result here.

For a fixed axis, the proposed \(y_t\) variables add no real choice: retain all mass whose rankings fit the axis and none of the rest. The computation is therefore a weighted search over axes. This is mathematically coherent, and computational work already studies distances to single-peakedness, including variants with hardness results; that history makes the proposed problem credible rather than disqualifying. [Erdélyi, Lackner, and Pfandler](https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.2627)

So I would reject this paper as having a qualifying computational anchor. I cannot honestly defend the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario: a large electorate with repeated ranking types makes the weighted proximity problem sensible. The negative case is strong at the stated anchor gate and weak as a universal claim.
