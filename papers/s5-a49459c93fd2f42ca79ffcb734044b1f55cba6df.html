What the mirror covers

Theorem 1's equilibrium prediction and Theorem 2's welfare comparison are analytic results; no named computational result is covered by a continuous mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The population regime is a credible fit: in a large referendum, agents can be grouped by preference \(A\) or \(B\), with masses \(p\) and \(1-p\) (and by cost too if costs vary). The paper’s Poisson electorate is direct evidence that this kind of large, low-support society is sensible.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a mirror on. Theorem 1, proved here, says that for every \(\varepsilon>0\), a sufficiently large assessment group makes it happen with probability at least \(1-\varepsilon\) that all first-round members vote for their preferred alternative, nobody votes in the second round, and \(A\) wins. That is an equilibrium and asymptotic result, not a claim that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. Theorem 2 is a welfare comparison; the other numbered results concern equilibria as well.

So I cannot give a qualifying lead anchor or an anchor-backed continuous problem without misrepresenting the paper. A computational question about equilibrium computation for a society with rational type masses could be a follow-on, but it would be a new question rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The positive case is therefore limited to the plausible high-multiplicity population regime; its weakest point is that the paper studies large electorates analytically and supplies no computational result for the programme to continuize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s population story is credible: the paper studies many exchangeable citizens drawn from just two preference types, with a randomly selected assessment group. That is good evidence that a high-multiplicity regime makes sense. It is a precondition for a mirror, though, not a computational question to mirror.

Theorem 1 is an equilibrium and concentration result: with a sufficiently large assessment group, a specified outcome occurs with high probability. Theorem 2 compares expected welfare across voting procedures. Neither poses or classifies a computational problem. The remaining numbered results concern equilibrium existence, uniqueness, or multiplicity. There is no named algorithmic result about computing a strategic intervention, deciding a winner-robustness question, or optimizing over a compressed society. So there is no result here to anchor a continuous computational mirror.

A stronger follow-up might ask for the smallest assessment-group size that meets a target success probability, given rational type masses and voting costs. But the paper already gives a sufficient size bound as a function of those parameters. Finding a sharper minimum would be a newly posed probability-quantile problem, not a mirror of a computational result in the paper. Likewise, asking for equilibrium computation over a richer type space would add a computational objective the authors do not study.

There is also a modelling tension in that extension. The results rely on finite individual vote counts and the probability that one voter is pivotal. Replacing the electorate with deterministic, atomless masses removes that individual pivotality. Retaining finite random counts may be a sensible stochastic model, but it leaves the programme’s usual continuous-society formulation and still does not supply a computational result to continuize.

The honest limit to this negative case is that the paper’s large-population regime is unusually convincing, and those follow-up questions might be worthwhile research on their own. The case against is narrower: they would extend the paper’s analytic voting model, rather than mirror a computational result it contains.
