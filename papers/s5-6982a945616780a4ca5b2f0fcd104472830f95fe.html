The continuous mirror question

Given click types \(M\), a finite support \(T\) of complete campaign types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-campaign budgets \(b_t\), ROI targets \(r_t\), values \(v_{tj}\), and per-capita click supplies \(S_j\), compute uniform prices \(\alpha_j\), per-campaign allocations \(x_{tj}\), outside-option quantities \(z_t\), and pacing multipliers \(q_t\). Require each type to maximize \(\sum_j (v_{tj}/r_t)x_{tj}+r_tz_t\) subject to \(\sum_j\alpha_jx_{tj}+r_tz_t\le b_t\); aggregate allocations must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_tx_{tj}\le S_j\), with equality whenever \(\alpha_j>0\); and \(q_t=b_t/W_t\), where \(W_t=\sum_j(v_{tj}/r_t)x_{tj}+r_tz_t\).

The model it lives in

Use the paper’s augmented Eisenberg–Gale program over per-campaign allocations: maximize \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t[b_t\log W_t-r_tz_t]\), where \(W_t=\sum_j(v_{tj}/r_t)x_{tj}+r_tz_t\), subject to \(\sum_t\mu_tx_{tj}\le S_j\) and nonnegativity. The supply multipliers give \(\alpha_j\), and \(q_t=b_t/W_t\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 11’s ex-ante pacing computation and the equilibrium outputs it uses; it leaves the separate claims about revenue optimality, price uniqueness, auction equivalence, and time-varying no-arbitrage for further analysis.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give an exact algorithm to compute the equilibrium prices, allocations, and pacing multipliers in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the rational input bit length.

The case FOR (proponent)

My positive case is narrow, but the paper has a named computational anchor: Corollary 11 (Ex-Ante Computability), stated here and supported by Proposition 10 and Theorem 4. It says that, given an optimum \((x^*,z^*)\) of program (2), the pacing multiplier for advertiser \(i\) is \(\mu_i=b_i/W_i^*\), where \(W_i^*=\sum_j u_{ij}x_{ij}^*+r_i z_i^*\); the exchange can compute these multipliers from the program without running an auction. The paper does not have a numbered result classifying this computation as polynomial-time. Section 5.1 gives the polynomial-time basis by citing the DPSV algorithm for Fisher markets. So the positive complexity prediction below is an inference from that algorithm and the paper’s market isomorphism, not a complexity theorem proved by Corollary 11.

The mirror I would put to a prover is Continuum-Campaign HOTW Equilibrium and Pacing. An instance has click types \(j\in M\), a finite set \(T\) of complete campaign types, and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. A campaign type \(t\) specifies its per-campaign budget \(b_t\), ROI target \(r_t\), and exchange-known values \(v_{tj}\), or equivalently \(u_{tj}=v_{tj}/r_t\). It also has click supply \(S_j\) per unit of campaign population. The question is to compute uniform prices \(\alpha_j\), an allocation \(x_{tj}\) and outside-option quantity \(z_t\) per campaign of each type, and a pacing multiplier \(q_t\), so every type chooses a utility-maximizing allocation within budget, click supplies clear at positive prices, and the pacing multipliers implement the allocation.

One precise way to ask for that solution is to optimize

\[ \max_{x,z\geq 0}\ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\left[b_t\log\left(\sum_{j\in M}u_{tj}x_{tj}+r_tz_t\right)-r_tz_t\right] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_{tj}\leq S_j\quad(j\in M). \]

The prices are the multipliers on the supply constraints, and \(q_t=b_t/W_t\), where \(W_t=\sum_j u_{tj}x_{tj}+r_tz_t\). A solution must satisfy the market’s demand and clearing conditions; in particular, \(x_{tj}>0\) implies \(\alpha_j=q_tu_{tj}\). This preserves the paper’s budgets, ROI requirements, exchange-known values, uniform per-click-type prices, and one-shot allocation question. The continuum is over campaigns; divisible clicks and any time indexing are not being offered as the population mirror.

A plausible regime is an exchange with millions of small campaigns using standardized settings: repeated budget tiers and ROI targets, and shared predicted value vectors for campaigns with the same objective and targeting profile. If there are millions of campaigns but only hundreds or thousands of such complete profiles, then \(n\gg\tau\). The type must include all the attributes the model uses, including the entire value vector, budget, and ROI target. Grouping merely similar campaigns would change the problem.

I expect Class A for this mirror. Aggregate each type into one Fisher-market buyer with budget \(\mu_t b_t\), and aggregate its allocation as \(y_{tj}=\mu_t x_{tj}\). The weighted market is then the paper’s finite Fisher market with at most \(\tau\) buyers, the same click types, and the outside option. Thus the cited exact algorithm gives a route polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the rational input bit length, rather than in the number of individual campaigns. The mirror also has a clean discrete bridge: \(n_t\) identical campaigns correspond to mass \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), with supplies normalized per campaign. The expected tractability comes from compression by complete type, not from assuming away budgets or ROI.

The weak point is whether that regime is real at exact type granularity. Advertiser budgets and value vectors can vary enough that \(\tau\) approaches \(n\); then the continuous formulation has little computational advantage. The paper’s emphasis on thick markets and exchange-known values makes a large population plausible, but does not establish repeated complete campaign profiles. Still, this is a credible test of the paper’s own one-shot equilibrium and pacing computation: it asks whether the same HOTW problem can be solved from a distribution over advertiser types, and its answer has a specific, defensible route to tractability.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

My best case against the mirror is that the paper’s “thick market” premise supports many advertisers, but says nothing about a small number of complete advertiser types. A type here must include the whole value vector across click types, budget, ROI target, and every other campaign attribute the model uses. The exchange may standardize campaign settings, but its advertiser-specific conversion estimates can still make those full vectors distinct. Grouping campaigns that are merely similar changes the demand system; the equilibrium can depend on small differences in willingness to pay. So the paper gives a plausible story for \(n\) being large, but not for \(n \gg \tau\).

The stronger continuous version would use exactly the proponent’s finite-support model. But when campaigns really do share a complete type, aggregation is exact: each type becomes one buyer with budget \(\mu_t b_t\), and its aggregate allocation is \(y_{tj}=\mu_t x_{tj}\). The weighted Eisenberg–Gale objective becomes that buyer’s ordinary objective up to an additive constant, with the same prices and pacing multiplier. This makes the mirror a compression of repeated buyer rows. It is useful only where exact profiles recur at scale; the paper offers no evidence that they do. A more general distribution over continuously varying campaign parameters would need a specified finite representation or oracle before its computational problem could even be assessed.

That is the case against Corollary 11 (Ex-Ante Computability): its stated computation takes the pacing multipliers from the equilibrium program, and the proposed mirror inherits that same market computation after exact type aggregation. But this does not establish that the mirror is ill-posed or outside the programme. Corollary 11 is a named computational result, and a finite rational type distribution gives it a clear continuous-population question.

So the negative case is weak at its decisive point. The absence of evidence for repeated complete campaign profiles undercuts the proposed scenario, but does not rule out a credible standardized-campaign regime. If such a regime is accepted, this anchor survives. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
