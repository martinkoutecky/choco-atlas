The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is a large producer population extension of Theorem 2, but it does not meet the programme’s anchor requirement. The paper’s Theorem 2, proved here, says that for every finite market the highest unit price among Pay-as-Bid mixed Nash equilibria is no higher than the highest such price under Pay-as-Clear, and that the reverse comparison does not hold for all markets. That is a named equilibrium comparison, not a result about whether a problem is tractable, hard, or approximable. Theorem 1’s non-domination claim and Theorem 3’s \(\Theta(\log n)\) VCG price gap are also game-theoretic results, not computational-complexity results. So there is no qualifying anchor in this paper.

A concrete *extension* of Theorem 2 would be Worst-Equilibrium Price in a Continuum Procurement Market. Fix a finite type set \(T\); type \(t\) specifies a producer’s marginal cost \(c_t\) and capacity coefficient \(a_t\). An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), the bid limit \(M\), and unit demand, with \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t\ge1\). In a population of \(N\) producers, each type-\(t\) producer has capacity \(a_t/N\), and there are \(N\mu_t\) producers of that type. Each producer independently draws a bid from a type-specific distribution \(\sigma_t\) over \(\{0,\ldots,M\}\). Specify cost-minimizing allocation by bid, with proportional rationing among producers at the marginal accepted bid. The continuum equilibrium is a profile \((\sigma_t)_{t\in T}\) in which each infinitesimal producer’s bids maximize its expected revenue given the aggregate bid distributions. For either PB or PC, the decision problem is: given a rational threshold \(K\), does there exist such an equilibrium whose average unit price is at least \(K\)? A yes-instance can be witnessed by the equilibrium bid distributions. The natural conjecture, motivated by Theorem 2, is that the maximum equilibrium price under PB is no higher than under PC.

This is a plausible many-small-producers scenario: generators sharing technology, marginal cost, and capacity class form types, with population mass representing their share of the fleet. It mirrors the paper’s comparison of equilibrium prices, rather than its experiments or its other theorems. But the conjecture is not a result of the paper, and I would not assign this decision problem to a complexity class based on its results.

The weakest point is fundamental: a producer’s deviation can change the clearing bid in the paper’s finite game, while an infinitesimal producer cannot change the aggregate market in this mirror. That may alter the equilibria the theorem compares. More decisively for ChoCo, even this plausible extension has no named computational-complexity anchor in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the proponent’s strongest candidate: it compares the highest equilibrium unit prices under Pay-as-Bid and Pay-as-Clear. But it proves an equilibrium-price relation, not a computational result. Theorem 1’s non-domination claim and Theorem 3’s VCG price gap have the same limitation. The equilibrium characterizations and Hedge simulations add analysis and experiments; none states a complexity bound or gives an algorithm for a named computational problem. Under ChoCo’s anchor criterion, the paper supplies no computational result to continuize.

The proposed many-producer setting is plausible. Grouping producers by cost and capacity, and scaling each producer’s capacity with population size, gives a coherent continuous society. The threshold question is also mathematically concrete. A stronger version could ask for the complexity of computing the maximum equilibrium price under each mechanism, or of comparing those two maxima. That would still introduce a new computational problem: Theorem 2 compares equilibrium values across finite markets but asks for no computation of those values. Turning its quantity into an algorithmic task makes a worthwhile follow-on question possible; it does not make the paper’s result a computational anchor.

The limit also shifts the strategic object. In the paper, one producer can be pivotal; with capacities scaled as \(1/N\), a single producer’s deviation has vanishing effect on the aggregate market. The resulting nonatomic game may be worth studying, and its equilibrium behavior could be surprising. That observation does not rule out the model or predict its answer. It does show why the proposed extension needs its own equilibrium model and motivation, rather than inheriting the paper’s finite-player question unchanged.

So the strongest negative case is about fit with this programme: a continuous procurement-game project may be valuable, but this paper offers no named computational result for it to mirror. That is a strong case against assigning this paper a ChoCo mirror under the stated criteria, though it cannot establish that no useful related research question exists.
