The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: support membership for maximal lotteries over a high-multiplicity electorate. The anchor is Brandt and Fischer (2008), Theorem 5, which this paper cites in §1.2 rather than proves: deciding whether an action has positive probability in an equilibrium of a symmetric zero-sum game is P-complete. This paper connects maximal lotteries to equilibria of skew-symmetric zero-sum games and explicitly presents its process as an approximate way to compute such an equilibrium. The cited result is P-completeness, not NP-hardness, and it does not imply sequential intractability.

Here is the continuous problem. An instance consists of \(d\) alternatives, a list of \(\tau\) strict-ranking types \(T^+\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a queried alternative \(a\). Define
\[ M_\mu(i,j)=\sum_{t:\,i\succ_t j}\mu_t,\qquad \widetilde M_\mu=M_\mu-M_\mu^\top, \]
and let
\[ \mathcal E(\mu)=\{p\in\Delta_d:\widetilde M_\mu p\le 0\} \]
be the maximal lotteries for this society. The question is whether some \(p\in\mathcal E(\mu)\) has \(p_a>0\). Equivalently, solve the exact LP
\[ \max\{p_a:p\in\Delta_d,\ \widetilde M_\mu p\le0\} \]
and answer yes precisely when its optimum is positive. A yes instance can return a rational maximizing lottery with positive \(a\)-probability; a no instance has optimum zero. The expected classification is polynomial time in \(d,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the encoding length of the masses and rankings.

A plausible regime is a large, recurring audience choosing among a modest catalogue of songs. Many listeners can share exactly the same full ranking, so the audience has \(N\gg\tau\) people but only \(\tau\) distinct preference types; rational masses describe their shares, without identifying listeners individually. This is a population model: the mass is on voter types. It does not rely on making the urn, time, or outcome space continuous. The fit to the authors’ problem is direct: the paper’s collective choice and its equilibrium interpretation depend on pairwise preference shares, and the paper discusses repeated music choices as an application. A query about whether a song appears in an equilibrium lottery is recognizable as their support-membership problem.

This covers only the paper’s computational interpretation of equilibrium support membership. It does not mirror the main urn convergence theorem, the temporal-average corollary, or the continuous ODE theorem: those concern the urn dynamics and their limit, not a continuous voter population. The mirror’s further question is whether P-completeness survives when game matrices must come from a succinct distribution over rankings with support \(\tau\); the cited theorem alone does not establish that lower bound for this restricted input format.

The weak point is substantial: the paper already uses fractional preference profiles in its axiomatic discussion, and the support query is determined entirely by their aggregate comparison matrix. So this may look like attaching a high-multiplicity input encoding to an existing equilibrium problem, rather than uncovering a new complexity phenomenon. The P-completeness result is also external and applies to general game inputs; it is not a hardness result proved here for ranking distributions. I would defend this as a modest, well-grounded mirror of the paper’s explicit computational framing, not as a claim that continuization dissolves a discrete hardness barrier.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor is Brandt and Fischer’s Theorem 5, cited in §1.2; it is not a result of this paper. The paper’s own main results concern convergence of the urn process and its continuous ODE, alongside axiomatic properties. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. That makes the proposed mirror a follow-up to a cited connection, rather than a continuous mirror of a result the authors establish.

The song-listener scenario is plausible: many listeners can share a ranking, and their shares give a sensible high-multiplicity input. But for the proposed support query, the paper already defines the relevant object on fractional preference profiles in §E, and characterizes maximal lotteries by \(\widetilde M_\mu p\le 0\). Given ranked types and rational masses, one can aggregate \(\widetilde M_\mu\) and maximize \(p_a\) over that polytope. So the continuous society is a valid input to an already specified lottery problem; what remains potentially new is whether the cited P-completeness result survives the restriction to ranking-generated matrices. The cited theorem does not settle that restriction.

That restricted-input question could be worth studying on its own. It is the strongest version of the proponent’s case, and I would not reject it because the audience is too small or because its answer might be in P. The negative case is narrower: it is not a complexity result of this paper, and the paper already supplies the continuous population domain and the LP description of the queried object. Establishing a new lower bound for that input restriction would be new work, but would not rescue the proposed anchor as a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
