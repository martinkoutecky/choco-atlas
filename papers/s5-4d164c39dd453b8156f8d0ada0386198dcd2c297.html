The continuous mirror question

Given \(M\) candidates, rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), and a secret-shared support of \(\tau\) valid tied rankings \(Q^i\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), compute and reveal only the Copeland winner set under \(A_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_iQ^i(a,b)\). Determine the exact MPC protocol and its complexity in \(M,\tau\), the input bit length, and the number of talliers, while preserving the paper's honest-majority, output-only privacy goal.

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete tied-ranking types \(Q^i\), each valid under Theorem 4. The talliers validate the supported types, securely compute the weighted pairwise totals \(A_\mu(a,b)\), and output only the candidates maximizing the Copeland score determined by the signs and zero values of those totals.

The objection that survived

The paper receives ballots from registered voters and validates them individually, whereas secret-shared type masses need a trustworthy process to ensure they came from eligible, one-time votes; the proponent does not specify that process.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers tied Copeland ballot validation via Theorem 4 and winner computation from weighted pairwise totals; it leaves the paper's other rules and its complete voter-submission protocol outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the exact MPC communication and computation bounds in \(M,\tau,L,D\) for rational masses and exact zero tests?
  • How can the protocol verify or obtain type masses from eligible one-time ballots without exposing individual rankings?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a private continuous-population Copeland tally with ties. The paper has no named theorem classifying a computational problem as being in P, NP-hard, or similar. My one anchor is therefore a structural result, using the paper’s characterization of valid ballots that its secure protocol actually computes with.

Theorem 4, proved in this paper, characterizes exactly which matrices represent Copeland ballots with ties. An \(M\times M\) matrix \(Q\) is valid iff: (1) \(Q(m,m')\in\{-1,0,1\}\) for \(m<m'\); (2) its diagonal entries are zero; (3) \(Q(m',m)=-Q(m,m')\); (4) whenever \(Q(m',m)=0\), rows \(m\) and \(m'\) are equal; and (5) define \(\eta_m=1\) if \(Q(m',m)\ne0\) for every \(m'<m\), and \(\eta_m=0\) otherwise, and let \(Q_m=\sum_{m'=1}^M\eta_{m'}Q(m,m')\). Then \(Q_m\ne Q_{m'}\) whenever \(m<m'\) and \(\eta_m=\eta_{m'}=1\). The theorem is proved here, and Section 4.2 uses these conditions to validate secret-shared ballots. It is not a complexity classification; it is the named structural result that supports my mirror.

Call the proposed problem Private Copeland Tally over a Continuous Society. An instance has \(M\) candidates, a public \(\alpha\in[0,1]\cap\mathbb Q\), and \(\tau\) secret-shared slots. Slot \(i\) contains a complete weak ranking \(Q^i\) and a positive rational mass \(\mu_i\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). The types and masses are hidden from the talliers; \(\tau\) may be public. The instance asks the talliers to validate each ranking using Theorem 4 and reveal only the Copeland winner set. A solution is a correct protocol that outputs
\[ \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a\in C} \left( \left|\{b\ne a:A_\mu(a,b)>0\}\right| +\alpha\left|\{b\ne a:A_\mu(a,b)=0\}\right| \right), \qquad A_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_iQ^i(a,b), \]
while preserving output-only privacy against the same honest-majority class of tallier coalitions as in the paper. Rational inputs can be encoded by clearing denominators; the protocol must retain enough precision to distinguish a zero pairwise total from a positive or negative one.

The intended regime is a large electorate choosing from a modest candidate slate, with many voters sharing the same complete ranking, including the same ties. Here a type is exactly one such ranking, and its mass is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. A plausible instance has millions of voters but far fewer distinct ranking types. The society is the continuous object: masses replace counts. The paper’s own examples of millions of voters and a small candidate set make this a recognizable regime, though the paper does not establish how small \(\tau\) would be in a particular election.

I expect this problem to be tractable under ordinary MPC assumptions. The tally needs weighted pairwise aggregation, exact sign and zero tests, and score comparisons; Theorem 4 supplies a way to validate the ranking slots. Those operations are polynomial in \(M,\tau\), the mass bit length, and the cryptographic parameters using standard secure arithmetic. But that is a proposed continuation, not a result proved by this paper. In particular, multiplying secret-shared masses by secret-shared ranking entries adds secure arithmetic beyond the paper’s unit-ballot aggregation.

The mirror covers the paper’s tied-ballot Copeland validation and winner computation, not its whole protocol suite. Its high-multiplicity bridge is exact: a finite election with \(n_i\) voters of each type gives \(\mu_i=n_i/N\), and the same pairwise comparisons and winner set result. The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem with aggregate type masses: the rule, legality conditions, privacy goal, and output are retained. The input representation and the secure weighted aggregation are the co-modifications.

My weakest point is that population size is not where this paper’s winner-computation cost lies: its reported tallying time is independent of \(N\), and its per-ballot validation is already repeated independently. Grouping identical rankings therefore does not obviously remove a bottleneck or dissolve a hardness result. This is a credible continuous mirror of the authors’ secure computation problem, but a modest one for the programme’s complexity landscape. Further questions include the precise MPC bounds in \(M,\tau,L,D\), the cost of hiding and validating the support representation, and whether any meaningful continuum-specific complexity boundary appears once the rational masses are treated exactly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the wrong kind of result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. It characterizes whether one ballot matrix encodes a legal weak ranking; it says nothing about a population, an aggregate, or the complexity of a social-choice problem. For a distribution over rankings, its direct extension is simply to check each support type. If the matrices are aggregated first, Theorem 4 no longer tests them: an aggregate can be a mixture of legal ballots without itself representing any ballot. Either way, the theorem’s substance remains at the individual-ballot level.

The strongest salvage is the proposed private weighted Copeland tally. It is mathematically coherent, and a large electorate with many repeated rankings is a plausible regime. But its continuous input raises a different security boundary. In the paper’s voting system, submissions come from registered voters, and the system must accept and validate each ballot. If voters submit individually and are grouped only after secret sharing, the per-voter work remains. If a service instead supplies secret-shared ranking masses, the talliers protect those masses, but the system now trusts a source to have formed them from eligible, one-time votes. That may be a good new protocol model; it is not the paper’s ballot-level validation problem. Treating credentials and identity as part of a complete type preserves that accountability, but removes the multiplicity the mirror was meant to exploit.

So the proposed tally is a credible adjacent extension, not a mirror anchored by Theorem 4. It asks about securely computing from weighted, hidden inputs and about the trust placed in whoever forms those inputs. The paper’s results concern MPC protocols for voter submissions, with cryptographic and communication costs; they do not chart the complexity of problems over continuous societies. I would argue against giving this mirror ChoCo priority on that basis. The case is limited, though: the weighted tally is sensible, and the authors might recognize it as a natural secure-voting variant. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous version exists under any scenario.
