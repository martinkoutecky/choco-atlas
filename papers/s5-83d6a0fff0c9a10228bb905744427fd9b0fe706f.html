What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns Theorem 4.3’s symmetric equilibrium selection and large-population welfare guarantees; it leaves Proposition 3.2’s permutation-equilibrium impossibility and the other results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • In the specified nonatomic game, must every symmetric equilibrium outside the permutation strategies have lower per-capita welfare than truth-telling?
  • As finite populations grow under the same prior construction, do their symmetric equilibria converge to the nonatomic equilibria and preserve the paper’s focality guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible positive case is a continuous-population version of the paper’s equilibrium-selection question for a large peer-elicitation panel. There is a strict limitation: the paper has no named computational-complexity result—no numbered result about an algorithm’s running time, membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or the like. So there is no qualifying anchor under your definition. The closest substantive result is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper, though it is an equilibrium theorem rather than a computational one.

Theorem 4.3 states that for an SNIFE prior, \(n\ge 3\), and the printed condition \(\alpha/\beta<1/(4m)\), the Disagreement Mechanism: (1) is truthful; (2) makes truth-telling symmetric-quasi-focal, meaning permutation equilibria have equal welfare and every other symmetric equilibrium has lower welfare; (3) makes every symmetric equilibrium within \(\gamma_1\) of truth-telling’s welfare \(\tau_1(\gamma_1)\)-close to a permutation equilibrium, with \(\tau_1(\gamma_1)=O(\gamma_1^{1/3})\); and (4) gives an approximate guarantee for general equilibria: no equilibrium beats truth-telling by more than \(\gamma_2(n)=O(m/\sqrt n)\), and profiles close in welfare are \(\tau_2(n)\)-close to a permutation, with \(\tau_2(n)=O((m^2/n)^{1/6})\). The omitted constants depend on the prior’s first two moments.

There is a material caveat in that anchor: the proof at the end of §5.3 requires \(2m\beta/\alpha<1/2\), or \(\beta/\alpha<1/(4m)\), the reverse ratio from the theorem’s printed condition. I would treat the theorem’s parameter condition as unresolved until the authors correct it.

Here is a concrete candidate mirror. Consider a survey or feedback platform with many respondents, a fixed finite signal set \(\Sigma\), and a common prior over signal populations. Represent that prior by a finite-support rational distribution \(\Pi\) over population compositions \(z\in\Delta(\Sigma)\). In each realized society, type \(\sigma\) has mass \(z_\sigma\); agents know \(\Pi\), while the mechanism does not. An agent of type \(\sigma\) has posterior \(\Pi_\sigma(dz)=z_\sigma\Pi(dz)/q(\sigma)\), where \(q(\sigma)=\int z_\sigma\,d\Pi\). This induces peer-signal probabilities \(q(\tau\mid\sigma)=\int z_\sigma z_\tau\,d\Pi/q(\sigma)\). Agents with the same signal and common prior have the same mechanism-relevant information. The regime has a very large respondent population and a fixed number of signal types, with mass rather than headcount as the natural quantity.

Call the proposed problem Continuum Disagreement Equilibrium Gap. Given \(\Sigma,\Pi\), the mechanism parameters and scoring rule, and a rational tolerance \(\varepsilon\), ask whether there is a symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium \(s\), outside the permutation strategies, whose expected per-capita payment is at least \(W_\Pi(T)-\varepsilon\), where \(T\) is truth-telling. Strategies map each signal to a probability distribution over reports \((\hat\sigma,\hat p)\in\Sigma\times\Delta(\Sigma)\); payoffs use the paper’s mechanism with peers sampled from the continuum masses. A YES answer supplies such an equilibrium; a NO answer proves the welfare bound for every non-permutation symmetric equilibrium. This keeps the paper’s signals, reports, disagreement mechanism, and focality question intact while making population mass the continuous object.

The case for plausibility is unusually direct: the paper already fixes the signal set while studying arbitrary \(n\), and its main theorem asks whether truth-telling remains focal as the population grows. I would expect the proposed problem to go in the tractable direction, with proper scoring and information monotonicity as likely tools. The mirror covers the Disagreement Mechanism’s symmetric equilibrium-selection result and its large-population robustness; it does not continuize the report space or turn outcome probabilities into the population model. Further questions include whether the permutation-equilibrium impossibility in Proposition 3.2 persists under this population model, and whether the approximate focality guarantee becomes an exact limit statement.

The weakest point is that this is still a new analytic equilibrium problem, not a computational result supplied by the paper. The paper’s asymptotic analysis gives strong motivation, yet it has already studied large finite populations. Also, representing its priors by \(\Pi\) over population compositions restricts attention to priors compatible with such a continuum model. A finite encoding for equilibrium strategies would be needed before the proposed decision question had a standard complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the anchor is outside ChoCo’s stated target. Theorem 4.3 and Proposition 3.2 are equilibrium and welfare results: neither gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or a computational problem whose input and output can be carried over. The proposed “Continuum Disagreement Equilibrium Gap” would therefore create a new equilibrium-computation problem, rather than continuize a computational result of the paper.

The best version of the mirror does have a credible population story: a large survey panel with finitely many signal types. Agents sharing a signal and common prior are indistinguishable for the game, and aggregate welfare does not depend on individual identities. A distribution over latent population compositions can also preserve the signal correlations the paper needs. So the usual objections about missing multiplicity or identity-dependent objectives do not defeat this mirror. Nor is it already done continuously: the paper studies large finite populations, not a continuum population.

The computational formulation still needs more work than the proponent’s statement suggests. Strategies involve predictions in a simplex and can be mixed; a standard complexity question needs a finite representation of the relevant equilibria. The paper’s Claim 5.11 may help reduce equilibrium strategies to finitely many prediction vectors, and fixing a scoring rule could make a finite-dimensional problem possible. But establishing and using that reduction would be new equilibrium-computation work. It would not follow from the theorem’s population limit, and it would not expose the kind of mass-transfer LP or pricing problem that motivates ChoCo’s continuous-optimization programme. Proposition 3.2’s relabeling argument faces the same issue: it may carry over analytically, but it supplies no computational task.

There is also a parameter-ratio discrepancy in the cited theorem: the theorem states \(\alpha/\beta<1/(4m)\), while the proof later requires \(\beta/\alpha<1/(4m)\). That needs correction before relying on its quantitative guarantee.

I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile mirror. The high-multiplicity regime is persuasive, and a carefully defined continuum equilibrium-computation project could interest the authors. But the case against is strong for ChoCo’s present purpose: this paper offers no named computational result to mirror, and the proposed anchor turns its equilibrium analysis into a different research programme.
