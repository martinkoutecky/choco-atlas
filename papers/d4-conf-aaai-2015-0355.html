What the mirror covers

The proposed cohort optimization would cover load shifting and payment minimization, while leaving out the paper's forecasting procedures, interactive algorithms, axiomatic cost and risk disaggregation, and experiments.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best population mirror is plausible, but there is no qualifying result in this paper to anchor it to. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational result such as tractability or hardness. Algorithms 1 and 2 describe procedures; Equations (5) and (8) give disaggregation formulas; Table 1 reports experimental outcomes. None supplies the required named computational anchor, so I cannot honestly give a one-question-per-anchor case.

The strongest candidate, offered as an unanchored mirror, is a continuous version of the cooperative’s demand-shifting problem. Consider a utility-scale cooperative serving millions of premises through standardized programs. A type \(t\) records a complete program cohort: its expected and real-time baseline load profiles, variance information, and the shifts its members will accept under the cooperative’s price signal. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of subscribers in type \(t\). This regime is credible for large fleets of standardized devices or tariff cohorts, where the number of subscribers \(N\) greatly exceeds the number of distinct types \(\tau\).

Given rational type masses, price forecasts, a baseline day-ahead bid, permitted daily zero-sum shift vectors \(d_{ta}\), and linear aggregate load constraints, choose masses \(x_{ta}\geq 0\) assigned to each type’s permitted shift \(a\), with \(\sum_a x_{ta}=\mu_t\). The resulting aggregate bid and real-time load determine the cooperative’s per-subscriber payment
\[ \sum_{h=1}^{H}\left[B_h p^{DA}_h+(R_h-B_h)p^{RT}_h\right]. \]
The task is to find a feasible \(x\) minimizing that payment and return both the shift allocation and its value. With explicit shift menus and fixed forecasts, this is a linear program, so I would expect this particular formulation to be tractable. It captures the objective behind the paper’s interactive load-modification procedures and permits fractional cohort mass to take each individually feasible action.

The weak point is substantial: the paper studies a forecasting-and-response protocol, not this centralized optimization problem, and reports no complexity result for either. Also, consumer profiles and response behavior may vary enough that \(\tau\) approaches \(N\); the high-multiplicity regime relies on standardized cohorts. So this is a defensible research extension the authors might recognize, not a mirror established by one of their named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that there is no computational result here to mirror. The paper gives forecasting methods, two interactive procedures, cost and risk formulas with axioms, and simulation results. It does not state a theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. The formulas and experiments may motivate new questions, but they do not provide the kind of discrete complexity result whose high-multiplicity counterpart ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed mass-assignment LP is the best rescue, but it changes the problem. In the paper, the cooperative sets its bid using prescribed forecasting rules, consumers make stochastic offers subject to their own financial constraints, and the cooperative ranks and approves offers. The total payment and consumer-level price variation are then measured. The LP instead gives a central planner the power to choose each type’s shifts to minimize payment. That captures the paper’s goal, but replaces its decision process. A more faithful continuous version could represent each exact profile, risk level, and response behavior as a type, with mass taking different offers. Yet executing that protocol is not a complexity result the paper establishes; optimizing over it would require adding a new objective and feasible-choice model. Either route looks like a new cooperative-design problem rather than a mirror of a result in this paper.

A utility-scale population with standardized tariff or device cohorts is a credible high-multiplicity setting, so I would not argue that energy consumers can never be modeled by mass. That setting rescues the *domain*, though, not the missing computational anchor. It also exposes the choice: preserve the paper’s individualized responses and payment comparisons, or simplify them enough to obtain the proposed aggregate planner problem. The case against a worthwhile mirror is therefore strongest at the boundary of the paper’s contribution, not at the boundary of the possible model. It cannot honestly rule out a valuable continuous energy-cooperative problem; it can say that this paper supplies no named computational result, and that the strongest proposed candidate is an extension rather than its mirror.
