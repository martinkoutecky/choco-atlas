What the mirror covers

Theorems 1 and 2 have plausible axiomatic continuations over type distributions, but neither yields a computational mirror; the examples, lemmas, and related-literature discussion are not computational results either.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case for a population-continuum reading is that high multiplicity is already built into the paper’s argument. A type can be the complete preference–endowment pair \(t=(R_t,\omega_t)\). In a large market, mass \(\mu_t\) represents the fraction of consumers of that type, and the outcome assigns each type a consumption bundle \(x_t\), subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=\sum_t\mu_t\omega_t\). A plausible regime has very many consumers but only a few repeated types: the paper’s own example uses two preference–endowment types before integration and three afterward. Its proof explicitly considers replicated economies and uses core convergence. That makes a high-multiplicity model a natural extension of the paper’s setting; the replication results support the regime’s plausibility.

The corresponding continuous question would be whether a rule defined on distributions over types can always select feasible allocations that are Pareto efficient and make every type weakly prefer integration to remaining separate. For the fairness variant, one could also require equal treatment of equals and impose the paper’s coalition veto condition. This is a concrete population-continuum question, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as an axiomatic extension of their model.

But it is not a computational mirror under ChoCo’s criteria. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that no social choice function on the paper’s finite economies satisfies both Efficiency and Integration Monotonicity. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that if, for every population \(I\), the veto family \(\mathcal P(I)\) contains every subset \(J\subseteq I\) with \(|J|\ge \lfloor |I|/3\rfloor-1\), then no rule satisfies Equal Treatment of Equals, Efficiency, and Integration Monotonicity under \(\mathcal P\)-vetoes. These are named impossibility results, but they do not classify a computational problem: neither specifies an algorithmic task or running time, nor asserts tractability or computational hardness. The paper’s lemmas establish implications among axioms and core membership, not complexity results. Theorem 1’s proof uses Debreu’s core-convergence theorem, but that does not turn its conclusion into a computational result.

So the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor, and I would not hand a prover a per-anchor continuous problem as though it were one. The proposed distribution-over-types model is a credible axiomatic continuation, not a ChoCo computational mirror.

The weak point in the positive case is therefore decisive for this programme: the natural question asks whether a global rule with certain properties exists, rather than how to compute an outcome or how hard such computation is. Also, replication is already central to the paper’s proof, so the continuum lens is a natural way to recast its population structure, not a new computational contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is real: a continuous society with types \(t=(R_t,\omega_t)\) and masses \(\mu_t\) is a sensible model here. Consumers with the same preferences and endowments are interchangeable, and the paper’s replication argument supports a high-multiplicity reading. That establishes a plausible population continuum; it does not establish a computational mirror.

Theorem 1 asks whether there exists a social choice function satisfying Efficiency and Integration Monotonicity across all economies. Theorem 2 asks whether such a function can also satisfy Equal Treatment of Equals under the stated veto condition. Both are axiomatic nonexistence results. Neither defines an algorithmic task, an input-output problem, or a complexity measure. The example and lemmas do not supply those missing computational objects either.

The stronger proposed mirror—ask the same existence questions over distributions of types—preserves the paper’s axioms, but remains a global question about a rule defined across economies. Computing an allocation for one distribution would not enforce Integration Monotonicity across separate and joined economies. Restricting the domain to finitely many distributions could make a search problem, but that would require choosing a new finite domain and encoding; the paper defines and studies neither. The same issue applies to Theorem 2’s coalition vetoes, even if their size conditions are restated as mass thresholds.

So I would reject this as a ChoCo computational mirror, not because the continuous model is incoherent or because its answer is known. The negative case is narrower than the requested universal claim: the paper supports an axiomatic continuous extension, but supplies no computational result to mirror. A computational project inspired by exchange economies could still be worthwhile; it would be a new problem rather than a mirror of these results.
