The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^{\tau}\) summing to \(1\), and a rational piecewise-linear value function \(V:[0,\mu]\to\mathbb{Q}\) with \(V(\mathbf{0})=0\), promised to satisfy \(V(q)+V(r)\leq V(q\wedge r)+V(q\vee r)\) whenever \(q\wedge r\neq\mathbf{0}\) and \(q\vee r\neq\mu\), decide whether there exists a type-level payoff-rate vector \(a\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau}\) such that \(\mu\cdot a=V(\mu)\) and \(q\cdot a\geq V(q)\) for every \(q\in[0,\mu]\). Specify an explicit rational piecewise-linear encoding for \(V\), or give oracle complexity when only exact value access is available.

The model it lives in

A society has \(\tau\) interchangeable types with rational masses \(\mu\); a coalition is any mass vector \(q\in[0,\mu]\), and its normalized value is \(V(q)\). The decision variable is a payoff rate \(a_t\) for each type, and feasibility requires grand-coalition efficiency and \(q\cdot a\geq V(q)\) for every coalition mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's core nonemptiness test for crossing-supermodular games. It leaves Theorem 1's nucleolus algorithm, the iterative reduced-game procedure, and the appendix's general base-polytope result unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can continuous analogues of the superadditive covers and the sandwich criterion yield an efficient core-feasibility test?
  • How does the answer relate to core feasibility on increasingly fine finite replications of the same type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor I trust is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that for a crossing-supermodular game \((N,w)\), and any chosen player \(s\), the core is nonempty exactly when
\(\min_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{s\}}\bigl(-\overline{w}(S)-\overline{u}(S)\bigr)\ge 0\), where \(u(S)=w(N\setminus S)-w(N)\) and \(\overline{w}\) and \(\overline{u}\) are their superadditive covers, obtained by maximizing the sum of function values over partitions of \(S\). The theorem also shows that the minimized function is submodular, so the test can be carried out in strongly polynomial time using submodular function minimization. The introduction mentions NP-hardness of nucleolus computation in general, but gives no numbered in-paper hardness result, so I do not use it as an anchor.

A scenario where the population interpretation is natural is a large power cooperative: many small generators or storage units, grouped into a modest number \(\tau\) of technology, location, and contract types. A type records every feature relevant to participation and value, so units in it are interchangeable. There may be millions of units but only dozens of types. A coalition can contain any fraction of each type in the limit; its composition is a mass vector \(q\), rather than a named set of units. Its value can reflect network complementarities and joint dispatch. Transferable profit and crossing-supermodularity are substantive assumptions here, just as they are in the theorem’s finite-game setting.

My proposed mirror is Mass-Core Feasibility. An instance gives positive rational type masses \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}^{\tau}_{>0}\), summing to \(1\), and a rational piecewise-linear value function \(V\) on \([0,\mu]\), available through an exact value oracle. The function is normalized by \(V(\mathbf 0)=0\) and is crossing-supermodular: for \(q,r\in[0,\mu]\) with \(q\wedge r\ne\mathbf 0\) and \(q\vee r\ne\mu\), require \(V(q)+V(r)\le V(q\wedge r)+V(q\vee r)\). Values are normalized by total population, so a coalition of mass \(q\) has total surplus \(nV(q)\) in an \(n\)-unit realization.

The question is whether there is a type-level payoff rate \(a\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau}\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t=V(\mu)\) and \(\sum_t q_t a_t\ge V(q)\) for every coalition mass \(q\in[0,\mu]\). A solution is such a vector \(a\); a no-instance has no feasible vector. This is the same core question as in Theorem 3, with coalitions represented by masses. Requiring equal payoffs within a type does not weaken the question: when agents of a type are interchangeable, averaging any core allocation over them gives a type-symmetric allocation.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, cautiously: the paper’s result suggests that core feasibility may still yield to a structural reduction and submodular optimization in the \(\tau\)-dimensional mass space. For a finite replication, coalition constraints are checked only on the grid of possible mass vectors; the mirror asks for them across the whole box. A concrete route to investigate is a continuous superadditive cover, \(\overline V(q)=\sup\{\sum_j V(q^j):\sum_j q^j=q\}\), alongside the complement function \(U(q)=V(\mu-q)-V(\mu)\). The open questions are whether these covers can be computed and optimized efficiently, whether a continuous sandwich criterion gives the core test, and when core feasibility on increasingly fine finite grids converges to the continuum answer.

The scope here is deliberately Theorem 3’s core-nonemptiness result. I am not claiming a mirror of Theorem 1’s full nucleolus algorithm: lexicographically ordering excesses over finitely many coalitions does not have an obvious canonical extension to a continuum of coalitions. The weakest point in this case is also specific: Theorem 3 chooses a player \(s\) and removes that player to obtain its test. A nonatomic population has no distinguished singleton to remove, and the finite partition argument may not survive replacing sets by divisible masses. That is a real obstacle to transferring the algorithm, but it does not make the underlying mirror unintelligible: the authors’ question—whether any allocation withstands every coalition’s deviation—remains intact when the society is described by type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 3’s computation depends on the population being made of countable atoms. Its proof chooses a player \(s\), eliminates that player’s payoff coordinate using complements, and builds its test from partitions of finite sets. The surrounding least-core procedure also gets its iteration bound from \(n\). In a mass model, there is no singleton of positive measure, and coalitions can be split into arbitrarily many pieces. Replacing sets by mass vectors therefore does not carry over the theorem’s algorithm or its proof structure.

That is a real obstacle to transferring the result, but it does not defeat the mirror. The paper’s question—whether some payoff allocation withstands every coalition’s deviation—has a coherent version for a finite set of interchangeable types with coalition values \(V(q)\) over mass vectors \(q\). Asking for a type-level rate vector that supports every \(q\) is a computational problem, provided the representation of \(V\) is specified. Exact rational piecewise-linear input is one reasonable choice. The missing singleton may force a new characterization; it does not make the question unintelligible or out of scope.

The proponent’s averaging claim needs care. Finite symmetry lets one average a core allocation over permutations within a type. The same argument does not automatically establish that a truly atomless game has an equivalent type-constant allocation. But defining the mirror directly in terms of type-level rates avoids relying on that claim. It remains a plausible high-multiplicity question for standardized units whose coalition values depend on their aggregate mix.

I would therefore reject the universal negative. The atomless version may be a substantially different computational problem, and Theorem 3’s finite partition machinery may offer little guidance. Those are reasons to demand a precise formulation and a new analysis. They do not show that no worthwhile mirror exists. Related work on non-atomic production games studies core deficits as populations grow, which confirms the neighboring analytic setting without supplying the computational result at issue here ([Flåm, Owen, and Saboyá](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165489605000685)). The honest case against this anchor is weak: it exposes a gap in transferring the algorithm, but the proposed mass-core question survives it.
