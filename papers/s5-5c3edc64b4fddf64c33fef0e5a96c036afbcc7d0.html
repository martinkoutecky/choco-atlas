The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result that can anchor the requested case. The closest results are Theorem 1, proved here, which characterizes sovereignty-protecting SPEs by PNE in the message game plus credibility inequalities (5); and Theorem 2, also proved here, which gives conditions under which the cautious commitment rule makes full cooperation the unique sovereignty-protecting SPE. Proposition 3 gives a threshold condition in a symmetric model. These are results about equilibrium characterization and incentives. None asserts tractability, hardness, or an algorithm for a computational problem.

There is a plausible setting for a *new* continuous computational question: many climate jurisdictions grouped by complete payoff and cost types, with \(\mu_t\) the share of jurisdictions of type \(t\), and each type’s announcement and induced carbon price as the decision variables. But asking whether a sustainable cooperative outcome exists, or how to compute the largest such common price, would add a computational objective the paper does not study. It would also need a careful account of how the finite players’ incentives change when represented by a continuum. So the paper offers a natural population-shaped motivation for future work, but no qualifying anchor from which to state the requested mirror problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case starts with the missing anchor: Theorems 1 and 2 and Propositions 1–4 characterize equilibria and give incentive conditions. None states the complexity of a computational problem. The paper specifies neither a finite encoded input nor an algorithmic task with a runtime or hardness result; its payoff functions are largely arbitrary functions over continuous actions. So “find an equilibrium” or “compute the largest sustainable carbon price” would add a computational problem the paper does not study.

The proponent’s best candidate is a population distribution over complete jurisdiction types, such as \((d_i,k_i,\sigma_i)\), with a task like finding a common price or commitment grid that supports cooperation. That is coherent as a new research question, but the continuum creates a basic problem for mirroring this paper’s mechanism. The cautious rule’s staircase argument depends on a particular player raising the minimum announcement and thereby changing the promises made to others. In an atomless population, one jurisdiction’s deviation has zero mass and cannot move the distribution or its essential minimum. If instead all members of a type choose together so that their announcement has positive weight, the type is acting as a coalition player; the population has not been continuized in the paper’s strategic sense.

This is a limit on the paper’s mechanism, not an objection that a continuous model might yield a different answer. Citizens within countries do form large populations, but the paper’s strategic actors are countries, while citizens’ choices are already aggregated into each country’s emissions response. Replacing countries with a continuum of citizens would leave the committee’s strategic population finite.

I cannot honestly rule out every useful adjacent project: a nonatomic climate game or a model of coordinated regional blocs could be worthwhile. The negative case is narrower but stronger: the paper supplies no computational result to mirror, and the most natural continuous-population reconstruction removes the pivotal individual action on which its central equilibrium argument relies.
