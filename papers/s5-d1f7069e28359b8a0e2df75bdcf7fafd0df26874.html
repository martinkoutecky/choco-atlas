The continuous mirror question

Given finite proposer and arm type sets with rational masses, strict type-level preferences, reward means, and the APKP information pattern, run OCA-UCB for a nonatomic population, tracking the distribution of individual learner states and the induced matching flow \(x(t)\). Does there exist a bound \(T(\varepsilon,\eta,r,s,L,\Delta,\lambda)\) such that, for every \(t \geq T\), the probability that \(x(t)\) is more than \(\varepsilon\) in \(L_1\) distance from the set of stable mass matchings is at most \(\eta\)?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic two-sided market with finitely many complete proposer and arm types, each carrying mass. Proposer and arm types retain the paper’s reward distributions, rankings, and APKP information pattern; individuals run OCA-UCB with separate learning histories. Arm-type masses are capacities, and proposals produce a mass matching flow through priority-based acceptance, with nonatomic rationing where capacity cuts through a type. The objective is convergence of that flow to the set of stable mass matchings.

The objection that survived

The aggregate flow alone does not determine its next value because same-type agents can have different learning histories, and the finite-player proof gives no convergence bound uniform in population size.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s OCA-UCB convergence guarantee in the APKP setting. It leaves the APU convergence theorems and the simulation comparisons between PCA-UCB and PCA-TS outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What measure-valued transition rule over individual OCA-UCB states exactly captures proposals, capacity-based acceptance, and learning updates?
  • Does the resulting flow approach the stable mass-matching set, and how does a convergence bound depend on type support, mass bit length, preference gap, and \(\lambda\)?
  • Do finite replicated markets inherit the fluid convergence guarantee uniformly in population size?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: the APKP result in Theorem 3.1 (“SimpleTheorem,” in Section 3). It says that under OCA-UCB’s belief updates, OCA-UCB retains CA-UCB’s guarantee of convergence to stability. The paper proves this transfer in Appendix B, using Lemmas B.1 and B.2 to show that mistaken beliefs about an arm’s preferences are eventually resolved or become irrelevant. The underlying CA-UCB guarantee comes from earlier work cited by the authors. This is a convergence result, not a P-versus-NP classification, but it is a named algorithmic result about reaching a computational social-choice outcome.

A mirror I find credible is Fluid OCA-UCB Stability. Take finite proposer types \(P=\{1,\ldots,r\}\) and arm types \(A=\{1,\ldots,s\}\), with rational masses \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_k\), each side’s masses summing to one. Each type is a complete description for this market: members of proposer type \(i\) have the same reward means and strict ranking over arm types; members of arm type \(k\) have the same ranking over proposer types. Keep the APKP information pattern: proposers learn their rewards and do not know arms’ preferences, while arms know their own preferences. Rewards and delay randomization follow the paper’s OCA-UCB model.

The action at each round is a matching flow \(x_{ik}(t)\): the mass of proposer type \(i\) matched to arm type \(k\). Each infinitesimal participant still receives one match; the flow is fractional because it aggregates many participants. Arm-type mass acts as capacity and accepts incoming proposer mass by its known ranking. Individual members retain their own learning histories and run the OCA-UCB rule against arm types. Thus the society itself is represented by type masses, rather than merely making the matching outcome fractional.

The prover’s question is: for this type-level OCA-UCB process, does the flow approach the set \(\mathcal S\) of stable mass matchings? Here \(y\in\mathcal S\) has row sums \(\alpha_i\) and column sums \(\beta_k\), and has no blocking type pair: for every \((i,k)\), either no positive mass of proposer type \(i\) is assigned to types it likes less than \(k\), or no positive mass of arm type \(k\) is assigned to proposer types it likes less than \(i\). A precise convergence target is that for every \(\varepsilon,\eta>0\), there is a bound \(T(\varepsilon,\eta,r,s,L,\Delta,\lambda)\) such that, at every \(t\ge T\), the probability that \(x(t)\) is farther than \(\varepsilon\) from \(\mathcal S\) is at most \(\eta\). The distance can be the minimum \(L_1\) distance to a flow in \(\mathcal S\); the performance objective is reaching stability, measured by the time to enter its \(\varepsilon\)-neighborhood. A high-multiplicity version should also establish whether the bound is uniform in the number of replicated agents.

The regime could be a large, recurring short-contract or internship market: many workers and openings, but comparatively few skill and role cohorts. Within a cohort, participants share their relevant preferences and reward distributions; the masses say what fraction of the market belongs to each cohort. This is a narrower scenario than arbitrary individualized preferences, but it is a plausible high-multiplicity market. The authors should recognize the mirror’s central question—whether decentralized learning in their two-sided market reaches stability—though they would have to accept type-level preference categories and capacity mass as natural scaling changes.

I would tentatively expect this question to be tractable for fixed or moderate type support and a positive minimum preference gap. The proof’s finite resolution of inconsistent beliefs gives a reason to investigate that direction. It does not establish a rate uniform in population size, however, so a tractability claim would need that new analysis. Further questions include the dependence of \(T\) on support size, mass bit length, and preference gap; whether finite replicated markets inherit the fluid guarantee; and whether the paper’s APU algorithm, PCA-SCA, also admits a stable fluid version.

The weakest point is that Theorem 3.1 is proved for finite, individually identified players and arms. Its reasoning depends on individual conflict feedback, while the proposed mirror has type capacities and a distribution of private learning histories within each type. The theorem therefore motivates this continuous question but does not already solve it. I think the case survives because those changes follow from scaling the population itself, while retaining the paper’s decentralized learning and stability objective.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.1 is a real algorithmic result, so I would not dismiss the anchor on the ground that the paper has no named result. The problem is that it proves qualitative convergence for a prescribed stochastic learning process. It gives no computational task on a society input, no complexity bound for computing a matching, and no explicit convergence-time guarantee to carry into the proposed mirror. The proponent’s question—whether a fluid population approaches stable flows—is therefore a question about the dynamics of learning agents. That is a respectable question, but it sits closer to mean-field analysis than to the computational landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.

The best version of the mirror would retain the agents’ private learning histories and ask whether the *mass* of agents in unstable matches tends to zero, with a rate depending on type support and preference gaps. But the proposed flow \(x_{ik}(t)\) does not contain enough information to determine its own next value: members of one static type accumulate different reward samples, UCB values, and conflict beliefs. An exact aggregate process must therefore track a distribution over learner states, whose support grows with time. That is a substantially different object from a finite-support society described by rational type masses.

This also removes the proof mechanism that motivates the anchor. OCA-UCB resolves finitely many inconsistencies between named players and arms; the proof’s argument does not yield a population-uniform bound when each individual has zero mass and a positive measure of learners may still have atypical histories at any finite time. A measure-level convergence theorem might overcome that obstacle, but it would need new assumptions and new analysis. If it does, its central contribution is a mean-field learning result. With a fixed finite set of known preference types, the stable mass allocation itself is readily found by deferred acceptance with capacities; the difficult part is the stochastic evolution, not a new computational bottleneck in matching.

The negative case is strongest as a scope objection, not as a proof that no useful mirror exists. A sharp sample-complexity theorem for convergence to stable mass flows could be worthwhile, and the authors might recognize it as a natural extension. But that would be a new mean-field bandit project, rather than a computational mirror clearly supported by Theorem 3.1. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario; I would say this anchor does not establish one for ChoCo.
