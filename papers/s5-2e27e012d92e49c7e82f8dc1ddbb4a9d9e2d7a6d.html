The anchor — other

Corollary 5

Every anchor argued

  • Corollary 5 — survived: The opponent disputes its computational character, but the corollary and the stated EADAM procedure together give a way to compute a student-optimal stable matching.

The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), complete student preference rankings, partial school priorities over types, and rational per-capita school capacities \(q_s\), compute a feasible stable assignment of mass to schools and the outside option that is Pareto-undominated among stable assignments. Pareto improvement is evaluated by coupling assignments within each type so that no mass receives a less-preferred assignment and positive mass receives a strictly preferred one.

The model it lives in

Types encode each student's preferences and all priority attributes; the input gives rational masses \(\mu_t\), type-level partial priorities \(\succ_s\), and per-capita capacities \(q_s\). The decision variables \(x_{t,s}\) specify what fraction of each type receives each school or the outside option; individual students still receive one school.

The objection that survived

The opponent's strongest point is that expanding rational masses into individual students may require exponentially many students in the binary input, so the finite EADAM result does not establish a compact mass algorithm.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the student-optimal stable matching characterization in Corollary 5 for partial-order priorities; it leaves the paper's other results on tie-breaking, broader acyclic priorities, and allowable priority violations without a separate mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the exact EADAM characterization in Corollary 5 extend to stable mass assignments with type cohorts split at school capacities?
  • Can a student-optimal stable mass assignment be computed in time polynomial in the number of schools, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\), without expanding masses into individual students?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. The paper has no numbered result asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, or another complexity classification. Its strongest named computationally relevant result is Corollary 5, proved in this paper: for partial-order priority profiles, the set \(f^\succ\) of student-optimal stable matchings is exactly
\[ f^\succ=\bigcup_{\succ'\in E(\succ)}\{\mathrm{EA}(\succ,\succ')\}. \]
That is, every EADAM outcome obtained from a total-order extension is student-optimal for the original priorities, and every student-optimal stable matching is an EADAM outcome for some extension. This is a mechanism characterization, not a runtime result, so it falls short of the requested complexity-anchor standard.

The best mirror I see is Type-Mass Student-Optimal Matching. Consider a large school district with many students but relatively few complete student types: each type specifies a preference ranking and all policy attributes that determine its priorities at every school. Let \(\mu_t\) be the rational fraction of students of type \(t\), and let each school \(s\) have a rational capacity fraction \(q_s\) and a partial order \(\succ_s\) over types. The regime is plausible where policy categories and preference patterns recur across large cohorts; it is not plausible where individual lottery numbers or other personalized priorities make nearly every student a separate type.

A solution is a rational mass assignment \(x_{t,s}\), including assignment to the outside option, with \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\) and school loads at most \(q_s\). It is stable if all assigned schools are acceptable, no positive mass of type \(t\) is assigned below a school with unfilled capacity, and no positive mass of type \(u\) occupies \(s\) while a positive mass of a higher-priority type \(t\), with \(t\succ_s u\), is assigned below \(s\). Call a stable assignment student-optimal if no other stable assignment can be coupled to it so that every student is weakly better off and a positive mass is strictly better off. The problem is: given these data, output a student-optimal stable mass assignment. A proposed route is to extend each type priority order and run the paper’s DA/EADAM process on cohorts, splitting mass when a school’s capacity cuts through a type.

I would expect this problem to be Class A, with a running time polynomial in the number of schools, the support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), if the cohort version of EADAM can be implemented with polynomially many mass operations. Corollary 5 is the anchor for that conjectured route: it says exactly which finite-population matchings the extension-and-EADAM process must recover. The continuous theorem to prove is whether the same representation holds for stable mass assignments and whether the process remains compact when cohorts split.

The mirror covers Corollary 5’s characterization of student-optimal stable matchings under partial-order priorities. It does not cover the paper’s claims about weak-order single tiebreaking, acyclic but nontransitive priorities, or allowable priority violations. The authors could plausibly recognize the proposed problem: it keeps their matching, stability, student-optimality, and EADAM questions while replacing repeated students with mass. Fractional \(x_{t,s}\) records the shares of identical students assigned to each school; every individual still receives an integral assignment.

The weakest point is decisive for grading: Corollary 5 is not a complexity result, and the paper does not establish that the type-mass version is computationally tractable. Even its fidelity depends on priorities genuinely being shared within types; individualized priority features can drive \(\tau\) up to the population size. So this is a credible follow-on question inspired by a named mechanism result, but it cannot honestly be presented as a mirror of a named complexity classification in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 5 is the only proposed anchor, and it does not establish a computational result. It characterizes which finite, indivisible matchings are student-optimal: each is an EADAM outcome for some extension profile. It gives no running time, complexity classification, or compact way to find the relevant extension. In fact, the union ranges over extension profiles that can be numerous. So the paper does not provide the computational result that the proposed continuous problem is supposed to mirror.

The type-mass problem can still be made coherent. A district with recurring preference and policy-priority categories is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and scaled capacities and split cohorts are reasonable model choices. But Corollary 5 does not by itself justify the proposed polynomial-time route. To carry it over, one would need to show that the mass stability and Pareto notions match the finite ones, and that EADAM’s individual-level steps can be compressed when a cohort is split by capacity. For rational masses, expanding each type into sufficiently many identical students may transfer the finite characterization; that expansion can be much larger than the binary input. Proving an algorithm that avoids it would be a new complexity result, not a consequence of Corollary 5.

Priority information sharpens the gap. If the application has individualized lottery numbers or exam ranks, faithfully including them in each type can make the support as large as the population. A small support instead assumes priority relations that are uniform across repeated categories. That assumption may fit some districts, but it restricts the paper’s individual-level priority model; the resulting compact algorithmic question is a follow-on problem.

So I would reject this as an anchored continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The negative case is weaker if the claim is that no worthwhile continuous research problem exists at all: repeated policy and preference categories make the proposed matching problem plausible. That possibility supports a new research lead, but the paper’s Corollary 5 does not make it a computational mirror.
