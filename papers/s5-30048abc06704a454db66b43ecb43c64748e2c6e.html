The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses μ_t summing to \(1\), complete rankings π_t, a party partition, a non-increasing scoring rule \(f\), contact rates β_{st}, and a seed-mass budget \(b\), choose \(x_t\) with \(0 ≤ x_t ≤ μ_t\) and ∑_t x_t ≤ b. For a population of size \(N\), type-\(s\) agents have independent edge probability \(β_{st}/N\) to each type-\(t\) agent. Define \(a_t^0=h_t^0=x_t\), \(h_t^{r+1}=(μ_t-a_t^r)(1-\exp[-∑_s β_{st}h_s^r])\), and \(a_t^{r+1}=a_t^r+h_t^{r+1}\), with limit \(a_t^∞\). Apply the paper's constructive ranking update to each activated type, and score candidate \(c\) by \(q_c(x)=∑_t[(μ_t-a_t^∞)f(π_t(c))+a_t^∞ f(π̃_t^+(c))]\). Rank candidates by these scores with the paper's tie-breaking rule. If \(H_i(x)\) counts party \(C_i\)'s candidates among the \(k\) winners, maximize either \(H_1(x)-H_1(0)\) (CDW) or \(H_1(x)-\max_{i\ne1}H_i(x)-(H_1(0)-\max_{i\ne1}H_i(0))\) (CMEC).

The model it lives in

A sparse stochastic block network with \(Nμ_t\) agents of each type, where a type specifies a common ranking and contact profile. The decision is the seed mass \(x_t\) assigned to each type; the objective is the paper's constructive difference-of-winners or margin-of-victory objective evaluated on mean-field cascade masses and candidate score densities.

The objection that survived

The block model changes the controller's input from a realized graph to type-level contact rates, and the paper gives no evidence that this restricted network regime is central to its original problem.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's constructive CMEC and CDW objectives. It leaves the destructive multi-winner results and the other straight-party objectives outside this proposed question.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a block-population version of the constructive control problem. It preserves the paper’s central ingredients—multi-winner seats, ranking updates, social diffusion, and a campaign budget—while making the population, rather than the graph’s individual nodes, the continuous object.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper by a reduction from deterministic Influence Maximization, is the strongest anchor. It says that, for the paper’s nonconstant non-increasing scoring rules, both constructive Multi-winner Election Control (CMEC), which maximizes margin of victory, and Constructive Difference of Winners (CDW) are NP-hard to approximate within any factor \(\alpha>0\). The hardness holds even with deterministic diffusion and three parties with two candidates each. The reduction uses the known hardness of deterministic Influence Maximization; that underlying hardness is cited from elsewhere.

For the mirror, take a population with finitely many types \(T\). Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), a complete candidate ranking \(\pi_t\), and a block-level contact profile. The profile is given by a matrix \(\beta_{st}\): in a finite population of size \(N\), an individual of type \(s\) has an independent probability \(\beta_{st}/N\) of an edge to each individual of type \(t\). This is a sparse stochastic block version of the paper’s social network. A scenario might be an election with millions of voters but dozens or hundreds of recurring ballot and communication profiles.

The campaign chooses seed masses \(x_t\), with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le b\), where \(b\) is a population share budget. In the mean-field cascade, let \(a_t^r\) be the total active mass of type \(t\) by round \(r\), and \(h_t^r\) its newly active mass in that round. Set \(a_t^0=h_t^0=x_t\), and define

\[ h_t^{r+1}=(\mu_t-a_t^r)\left(1-\exp\left[-\sum_s\beta_{st}h_s^r\right]\right), \qquad a_t^{r+1}=a_t^r+h_t^{r+1}. \]

Let \(a_t^\infty\) be the limiting active mass. Apply the paper’s constructive ranking update to each activated type, writing its resulting ranking as \(\tilde{\pi}_t^+\). The score density of candidate \(c\) is

\[ q_c(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\left[(\mu_t-a_t^\infty)f(\pi_t(c)) +a_t^\infty f(\tilde{\pi}_t^+(c))\right]. \]

Rank candidates by these scores using the paper’s tie-breaking rule. If \(H_i(x)\) is the number of party \(C_i\)’s candidates among the \(k\) winners, then the mirror asks for a feasible seed-mass vector \(x\) maximizing either

\[ H_1(x)-H_1(0) \]

or

\[ H_1(x)-\max_{i\ne1}H_i(x) -\bigl(H_1(0)-\max_{i\ne1}H_i(0)\bigr). \]

Call this Continuous Block Multi-winner Control. Its output is an optimal mass vector \(x\), with the objective specified as CDW or CMEC. My working prediction for variable \(\tau=|T|\) is Class C: the original reduction does not carry over directly, but nonlinear spread across types and discontinuous seat changes could still make the continuous optimization hard. I would first look for tractability when \(\tau\) is fixed or small, and for approximation guarantees when scores lie near winner-switching thresholds.

A distinct second mirror comes from Theorem 3, also proved here. It says that, under the paper’s straight-party-voting relaxation, maximizing constructive and destructive MoV and DoV is NP-hard to approximate within any factor better than \(1-1/e\). The proof reduces from Influence Maximization and invokes its cited approximation-hardness bound. I rely specifically on the constructive Difference of Votes (\(\DOV^{\mathrm{SPV}}\)) result.

For that mirror, use the same type masses, contact matrix, and campaign seed masses, but score parties by aggregating the scores of their candidates. If \(P_1(x)\) is the resulting score density for the target party, the problem is to choose feasible \(x\) maximizing \(P_1(x)-P_1(0)\). Under the paper’s update rule, this objective is a weighted sum of activated type masses: each type’s weight is the gain in target-party score when its voters are activated. I would provisionally put this mirror in Class A for approximation. The paper’s Theorem 5, proved here, already gives a \((1-1/e)-\epsilon\) approximation for constructive and destructive SPV DoV on the discrete graph by reducing to node-weighted Influence Maximization. A continuous version may admit a corresponding optimization over type-level seed masses. Whether the limiting spread objective retains the needed diminishing-returns structure is a central question, not a result I am claiming.

The block model is plausible because campaigns on large platforms can allocate outreach by cohort, ballot profile, and communication community; choosing a share of each cohort is often a more natural decision than targeting individuals one by one. A type includes the attributes the model uses, including its ranking and contact profile, so grouping voters is the high-multiplicity assumption itself. The authors could reasonably recognize both mirrors as social-influence control questions: they retain their cascade, update rule, scoring system, seat or party objective, and budget, while representing the network through type-level contact rates.

The weakest point is that the paper’s arbitrary node-level network is doing real computational work. A block contact matrix suppresses individual topology, so Theorem 1’s reduction from deterministic Influence Maximization is not a hardness proof for the proposed continuum model. The mean-field cascade also replaces random finite-population outcomes with limiting active masses; exact ties and score fluctuations may therefore behave differently. These are substantive changes, but they define a testable mirror rather than a restatement: the open work is to determine which complexity results survive when the population is large and the number of behaviorally distinct types is small. This case covers Theorem 1’s constructive CMEC and CDW, and the constructive SPV DoV entry of Theorem 3; it does not claim mirrors for Theorem 2’s destructive multi-winner results or the paper’s other SPV objectives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to both anchors is that the paper’s computational object is a campaign on a *realized social graph*. A voter’s ranking may be shared by many people, but their position in that graph—and therefore the reach gained by seeding them—is generally individual. With a fixed number of types, ranking plus a block contact profile describes an exchangeable random-graph model. It does not describe the known graph on which the paper’s controller chooses seeds. The block version is coherent, but it changes what the controller knows and chooses.

For Theorem 1, the proposed continuous block model therefore does not mirror the reduction’s hard instances: it discards their arbitrary topology and replaces selecting influential vertices with allocating seed mass among blocks. A stronger attempt would classify voters by their complete network roles in the realized graph. That preserves the seed-selection problem, but on a general graph those roles can be unique, so the number of types grows with the population and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears. Exact repeated neighborhoods or block-generated networks remain possible scenarios, but the paper gives no reason to treat them as its central regime. The objection is that the anchor depends on a substantial network-model substitution, not on a ranking-type continuization alone.

The Theorem 3 mirror inherits the same problem. Straight-party Difference of Votes is a weighted sum of activated voters, and Theorem 5 already reduces its discrete version to node-weighted Influence Maximization. In the block limit, those voter weights become type weights and the question becomes whether to allocate seed mass across contact blocks. That is a legitimate computational question, but it is about influence maximization under a specified random block model, not the paper’s problem on a given network. The theorem’s approximation-hardness result supplies no evidence that its bound or its source of difficulty survives that change. This weakens the anchor; it does not show that the new optimization is uninteresting.

One could preserve more of the graph by taking a sequence of increasingly large networks and representing their positions with a graph-limit or continuum kernel, then choosing a measurable set of seed positions. But that route has the same dilemma: retain arbitrary network structure and the position space encodes the graph rather than compressing it into a moderate number of types; impose a low-complexity kernel and the model becomes a restricted network ensemble. Either way, it may generate worthwhile work, but the paper alone does not establish a population-continuum mirror that both preserves its given-network question and gains the intended high-multiplicity leverage.

That is the best negative case I can make, but it does not defeat the anchors universally. A large election with repeated ballot and communication cohorts, a random block network, and a share-based campaign budget is plausible; the resulting limit keeps the paper’s diffusion, ranking updates, and election objectives. The programme permits such co-modification, and neither an already-known continuous computational treatment nor an invalid outcome-space substitution is apparent here. So the objection is strongest against calling the proposed model a direct mirror of the paper. It is weak against the broader claim that this is a worthwhile, author-recognizable continuous variant.
