The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s concession is decisive under ChoCo’s stated remit: none of the three anchors is a computational result. Theorem 3.1 is a probability identity; Theorems 1.2 and 4.2 are probability bounds; Theorem 1.3 constructs a counterexample to a stability claim. None classifies the complexity of a problem or gives an algorithm to mirror. Turning one into a computation task could start a new project, but it would not mirror a computational result of this paper.

The best version of a mirror for Theorem 3.1 would represent the six rankings by continuous masses and ask for the probability of irrationality. But the theorem’s inputs are functions \(f,g,h\) on the \(n\) individual voters’ preference bits. Its Fourier terms retain which coordinates a function depends on. The paper’s “symmetric” condition only requires invariance under a transitive voter group; it does not make the functions depend solely on ranking counts. A six-mass vector can therefore discard information needed to determine the outcome. Restricting to anonymous rules repairs that mismatch, but an exact mass society then gives deterministic pairwise shares. The theorem’s probability comes from finite-sample fluctuations. Keeping those fluctuations leads back to a stochastic profile question; computing it from a circuit representation of \(f,g,h\) would be a newly posed complexity problem.

Theorem 1.2 has a tempting anonymous version: extend monotone pairwise voting to continuous masses and ask whether the \(3/4\) bound persists. At a fixed mass vector, however, there is no probability of rationality to bound. At the uniform vector, pairwise shares tie, and the finite theorem’s nontrivial probability comes from how random finite profiles fluctuate around that tie. Adding Gaussian fluctuations could yield an asymptotic analogue, but the work would be an analytic limit theorem. Theorem 4.2 has the same issue.

The strongest alternative is Theorem 1.3. For its threshold examples, one could ask for the least relative entropy of a six-type population whose empirical profile produces an irrational outcome. That is a natural large-deviation optimization and a real weakness in the negative case: it could be a worthwhile continuous probabilistic extension. It still does not mirror the theorem’s computational content. With three alternatives, the type space has only six points, and the proposed rate problem concerns rare-sample probabilities, not the complexity of a social-choice operation on a continuous society. Making it into a complexity landscape would require adding a new family of inputs and computational questions.

So the best case against is narrow but strong: these results may inspire continuous probability or optimization work, yet the paper supplies no computational problem for that work to mirror. The case is weakest around the large-deviation extension of Theorem 1.3; under ChoCo’s stated goal of computational mirrors, that extension is a separate project.
