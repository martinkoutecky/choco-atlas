The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), opportunity classes \(J\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), reserve prices \(r_j\), and the Chapter 1 class of strictly decreasing piecewise-linear utility schedules \(u_{tj}(p_j)\) with outside options \(o_t\), compute a bidder-optimal envy-free market-clearing outcome. The outcome has prices \(p_j\ge r_j\) and assignment masses \(x_{tj}\ge 0\), including outside-option mass \(x_{t0}\), satisfying \(\sum_{j\in J\cup\{0\}}x_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}x_{tj}\le q_j\); positive assignment mass goes only to utility-maximizing classes or to the outside option when it is utility-maximizing, and any undersold class has price \(r_j\). Bidder-optimality requires each type’s utility to be at least its utility in every other such equilibrium. Is there an exact algorithm polynomial in \(|J|,|\mathcal T|\), the schedule-description parameters, and input bit length?

The model it lives in

A replicated unit-demand assignment market with a continuum of agents grouped into complete types \(t\), each carrying mass \(\mu_t\), and interchangeable copies of opportunity class \(j\) at per-capita supply \(q_j\). Decision variables are class prices \(p_j\) and assignment masses \(x_{tj}\); the selection objective is a componentwise bidder-optimal type-utility vector among envy-free market-clearing outcomes.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is Mass Bidder-Optimal Assignment. It extends the thesis’s unit-demand assignment market to a population described by type shares. This is a credible mirror because the authors’ underlying problem is already about matching and prices, with bidder-optimal envy-free outcomes; Chapter 1 explicitly motivates it with web auctions, per-click and per-impression valuations, and soft and hard budgets.

For the scenario, imagine a platform allocating a large cohort of campaign opportunities across a season. Each opportunity belongs to one of \(k\) classes, such as a placement or format, and each advertiser can receive at most one. Advertisers with the same preferences, conversion rates, budget schedule, and other relevant parameters form a type. There are \(N\) advertisers but only \(\tau\) such types, with \(N\gg\tau\). Inventory in each class scales with the cohort: class \(j\) has \(Nq_j\) opportunities, for per-capita supply \(q_j\). This scaling is part of the mirror: with fixed inventory and a population growing without bound, per-capita supply would vanish. The type definition preserves the full economic description rather than averaging away individual differences.

My lead question is: given types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and \(k\) opportunity classes \(J\), compute a bidder-optimal envy-free equilibrium for this market. Each type \(t\) has a common outside option \(o_t\) and a utility function \(u_{tj}(p_j)\) for each class \(j\), with the thesis’s strictly decreasing, piecewise-linear utilities, including multiple discontinuities and non-identical slopes. Each class has rational reserve price \(r_j\) and rational per-capita supply \(q_j\).

A solution consists of prices \(p_j\ge r_j\) and assignment masses \(x_{tj}\ge0\), including \(x_{t0}\) for the outside option. They must satisfy
\[ \sum_{j\in J\cup\{0\}}x_{tj}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{tj}\le q_j \quad (j\in J). \]
Let \(U_t=\max\{o_t,\max_{j\in J}u_{tj}(p_j)\}\). Assignment is envy-free if positive mass \(x_{tj}\) goes only to a class attaining this maximum, or to the outside option when \(o_t=U_t\). If a class is undersold, its price must equal its reserve price. The solution is bidder-optimal if, among all such equilibria for the same instance, it gives every type at least as much utility \(U_t\) as any other equilibrium. Fractional \(x_{tj}\) records how the mass of a type is split across opportunities; each actual advertiser still receives at most one indivisible opportunity.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in Chapter 1. It says that the thesis’s mechanism finds a bidder-optimal solution for the finite-bidder problem with piecewise-linear utilities having non-identical slopes and multiple discontinuities. Its running time is
\[ O\!\left((n\min(n,k)+D\min(n,k)+T)\min(n,k)(\min(n,k)^2+k)\right), \]
where \(D=\sum_{i,j}d_{ij}\) is the total number of discontinuities and \(T=\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}\) is the total number of constant-slope intervals. The result is proved in this thesis, not cited from elsewhere. It is an algorithmic anchor rather than a hardness result; the thesis offers no comparable named hardness theorem for this problem.

The proposed continuous question is whether the equilibrium above can be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(k,\tau,D,T,L\), where \(L\) is the bit length of the rational input. That is not what Theorem 1 proves: its runtime depends on the number of individual bidders, and it does not establish compression to support size \(\tau\). But the mirror has a plausible route to tractability. Once agents are grouped by type, the assignment variables form a capacitated mass-allocation problem. The discrete mechanism’s matching and overdemand arguments may have flow or fractional-capacity counterparts, while the same price and demand structure retains the authors’ bidder-optimality objective. I would expect Class A, while treating the price-raising argument across discontinuities as the central obstacle for a prover.

This scope is just the computation of bidder-optimal envy-free assignments in Chapter 1. It does not claim a continuous mirror of the thesis’s equilibrium-selection or learned-payment results. Nor does it claim that Theorem 2’s incentive-compatibility statement automatically survives: adding strategic behavior would raise a separate question about type-level reporting and ties.

The weakest point is the supply co-modification and the change from individual matching to mass assignment. A finite market with \(N\) agents and \(Nq_j\) copies of each class normalizes to this model, but the thesis studies individually labeled bidders and item supplies, and its proof uses alternating trees and discrete matchings. The continuous problem is therefore not a corollary of the theorem. Still, the object being computed remains recognizably the same market-clearing, bidder-optimal assignment problem, and scaling capacity with population is the natural way to make that market nondegenerate at high multiplicity.

Two useful follow-up questions are whether an exact support-size algorithm can preserve bidder-optimality with multiple discontinuities, and how its complexity changes when the number of types \(\tau\) grows rather than remaining moderate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against this mirror is that its useful compression depends on exact repetition of a very rich bidder type. Theorem 1 is a real computational anchor: it gives a polynomial-time algorithm for bidder-optimal envy-free assignment with piecewise-linear utilities, non-identical slopes, and multiple discontinuities. And the proposed mass model is mathematically coherent. If a finite market has rational type proportions and per-capita supplies, scaling by a common denominator realizes it as a market with integer numbers of bidders and item copies.

The difficulty is finding a convincing high-multiplicity regime where the support stays small. A type here must include an advertiser’s utility schedule for every opportunity class: valuations, conversion rates, budgets, slopes, and discontinuities. Those are precisely the features that motivate the thesis’s expressive mechanism, and in real ad markets they are often bidder-specific. Grouping advertisers by campaign format or broad business category would not be enough if their actual schedules differ. A model with one item per class and many interchangeable copies also describes a particular allocation institution; ordinary ad campaigns often seek repeated impressions or placements, not one indivisible opportunity each.

The stronger version of the mirror would use a deliberately standardized cohort: many advertisers with identical complete schedules, competing for copies of a few opportunity classes whose supplies scale with the cohort. That does avoid the objection that prices or costs become type-level averages; by the programme’s definition, the members really are the same type. It also preserves individual unit-demand allocation through mass assignment. But this is where the negative case is weakest: the resulting replica market is sensible, and the thesis’s authors could readily recognize it as their bidder-optimal assignment problem with repeated bidders and capacitated item classes.

So I cannot honestly defeat the anchor on a fundamental modeling ground. The strongest objection is that the thesis motivates rich, heterogeneous bidders, while the support-size benefit requires exact homogeneity across all their economic parameters. That makes the most compelling application narrow, but it does not show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The proposed mirror survives this negative case.
