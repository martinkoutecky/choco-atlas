There is a plausible continuous-population follow-on. Take a monetary economy with many buyer–seller meetings. A type records the relevant preferences, production cost, and liquidity constraint; \(\mu\) gives the fraction of meetings of each buyer–seller type pair. For each pair type, the decision is the trade \((q,y)\). One could ask: given a finite-support rational \(\mu\) and a bargaining rule, compute the type-level trades and aggregate trade and welfare. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no named computational result to mirror. Findings 1–6 report experimental patterns; the equations characterize Nash and Kalai bargaining outcomes. Neither asserts a complexity result about an algorithmic problem. Under the programme’s criteria, that is the strongest objection: there is no computational result in this paper for a continuous mirror to preserve.

The proposed type-distribution extension is the closest candidate, but it changes what the paper studies. In the paper, \(q\) and \(y\) are chosen within a bilateral match; the population does not enter that choice. If types record preferences, costs, and liquidity constraints, each type’s trade can be computed independently and the aggregate outcomes weighted by \(\mu\). That is an economic aggregation exercise, not a computational social choice problem about acting on a society. The paper already has a continuum of agents and aggregate calculations in Appendix C, so this extension is recognizable in the paper’s economics—but supplies no missing computational question.

A stronger attempt might turn the observed bargaining data into a task of selecting between Nash and Kalai, or predicting agreements across a population of subjects. But that would make statistical inference or behavioral prediction the computational target. The paper uses regressions and distributional tests to support its empirical claim; it does not formulate their complexity as a result. Preserving the repeated-round behavior would also require a model of learning or subject histories that the paper does not provide. Either way, the population continuum would not mirror a computational result from the paper.

This is not an airtight claim that no worthwhile computational project could ever be built around bargaining. A new model with cross-match constraints or strategic interactions might produce a genuine computational problem. But those additions would supply the missing problem rather than mirror one of this paper’s results. The negative case is therefore strongest—and weakest—at the same boundary: this paper offers a continuous economic setting and empirical findings, but no computational target for ChoCo’s continuization programme.
