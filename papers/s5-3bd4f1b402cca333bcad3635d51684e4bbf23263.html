The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(T\), each with a strict ranking of \(m\) objects and an institutionally assigned tie-break priority class for each object, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational capacities \(q_o\) summing to \(1\), find a feasible mass assignment \(x_{t,o}\). For object \(o\), order types by Rawlsian claim, with a larger rank number giving a stronger claim, then by tie-break class. Call \(x\) stable if there are no \(t,o\) with \(x_{t,o'}>0\) for some \(o'\) ranked below \(o\) by \(t\), and lower-priority \(s\) with \(x_{s,o}>0\). Determine whether every such instance has a stable assignment that, for each type and rank cutoff, assigns at least as much mass to objects at or above that cutoff as every other stable assignment; compute one if it exists.

The model it lives in

A distribution over ranking and priority-class types, with rational type masses and object capacities; decision variables \(x_{t,o}\) describe mass assigned from type \(t\) to object \(o\). Feasibility and Rawlsian stability define the allowed assignments, and the selection target is typewise stochastic dominance among stable assignments.

The objection that survived

The paper's strict tie-break orders individuals, while the mass model treats people within a priority class as exchangeable; that coarsening changes the resolution of equal claims and needs a setting where class-level priorities are institutionally meaningful.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's existence, stable-allocation structure, and agent-optimal selection. It leaves the incentive, efficiency, and characterization results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every finite rational instance have a typewise stochastic-dominance-maximal stable mass assignment, and can one be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length?
  • How do such assignments relate to deferred-acceptance outcomes in finite replications of the same type distribution?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in the paper. It says that for every preference profile, the Rawlsian equitable allocations form a nonempty lattice under agents’ preferences, and the agent-proposing deferred-acceptance outcome \(DA(P)\) is the agent-optimal Rawlsian equitable allocation. The paper proves this by showing that Rawlsian equity is stability in an auxiliary market whose priorities are built from reported ranks and tie-breaking orders, then applying standard stable-matching results. The theorem gives a constructive algorithmic result, though the paper does not state a complexity class such as \(\mathrm{P}\). It contains no named hardness result to anchor a continuization.

My lead mirror is the Continuous Rawlsian Assignment problem. Consider a large public-housing cohort choosing among \(m\) standardized housing classes. Let \(T\) be the finite set of resident types, where a type records a strict ranking of the classes and its tie-breaking priority class. The instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and rational class capacities \(q_o\) with \(\sum_o q_o=1\). For each class \(o\), types are ordered first by the strength of their Rawlsian claim—lower rank means a stronger claim—and then by the institution’s priority among tied types.

A solution is a mass assignment \(x_{t,o}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_o x_{t,o}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,o}=q_o\). Each unit of mass receives one class; \(x_{t,o}\) records how much of type \(t\) receives \(o\). Require stability: there must be no type \(t\) and class \(o\) for which positive mass of \(t\) is assigned below \(o\) in its ranking while positive mass of lower-priority types holds \(o\). Among stable assignments, ask for one that is typewise agent-optimal: for every type and every rank cutoff, it assigns at least as much mass to objects at or above that cutoff as any other stable assignment does. There is no monetary objective in the paper’s problem; this mirrors Theorem 2’s agent-optimal selection.

The regime is many thousands of residents but a moderate number of distinct preference and priority types, with capacities scaling with the cohort. That is plausible for recurring housing allocations or incoming-student room assignments, especially where institutions use priority bands. The paper itself notes that its construction and deferred-acceptance results extend to objects with capacities, which helps this mirror feel like a variant of the authors’ assignment problem rather than a different application.

I would expect this problem to be Class A, though that is a conjecture, not a result of the paper. With finitely many types and classes, the analogue of deferred acceptance has only finitely many type–class proposal events; a mass-based stability formulation may also admit a polynomial-size optimization formulation. The research questions are whether a typewise agent-optimal stable mass assignment always exists, how to compute it in \((m,\tau,L)\), and whether finite high-multiplicity instances converge to it.

The weak point is the tie-break. In the paper it is a strict order over individuals, and it can determine who receives an object when claims tie. Keeping arbitrary individual tie-break positions in the type would often give nearly every resident a distinct type, defeating the high-multiplicity regime. My mirror instead uses institutionally meaningful priority classes and treats members of a type as exchangeable when the mass is divided. That is a substantive co-modification; the authors might reject it as no longer their precise Rawlsian-equity rule. The case survives only for settings where cohort-level tie-breaking and aggregate assignment are acceptable. It covers Theorem 2’s existence, lattice-selection, and deferred-acceptance result; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s incentive, efficiency, or characterization results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, but its exact fairness test resists the proposed aggregation. Rawlsian equity depends on the strict tie-breaking order over *individuals*. With two agents who share \(a \succ b\), and a tie-break placing agent 1 ahead of agent 2, assigning \(a\) to agent 1 is equitable; swapping their assignments is not. Both outcomes have the same aggregate mass assignment: half the population gets \(a\), half gets \(b\). The mass vector has forgotten the fact that decides equity.

That issue becomes central in a high-multiplicity regime. If type means a complete description relevant to the problem, the tie-break position must be part of the type. A strict tie-break gives each person a distinct position, so the exact type support grows with the population. Grouping people into priority bands restores multiplicity but changes the rule: it no longer says which equal claimant should prevail. A continuum of priority quantiles can preserve the distinction, but then the mirror is a continuum over both preference and priority types, rather than the finite-support mass model the proponent offered.

Even that stronger version has little room for a new existence or selection result. Continuum stable-matching work already studies distributions of types encoding preferences and priorities, proves lattice results for stable outcomes, and gives a deferred-acceptance analogue that finds the extremal outcomes. Arnosti’s framework is a direct precedent for the structural content of Theorem 2, including type distributions and priority information ([*A Continuum Model of Stable Matching with Finite Capacities*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881)). For a fixed reported profile, the paper’s rank-derived priorities can be supplied as the priority field in such a model.

So the proponent’s finite-band mirror either loses the tie-breaking information that defines Rawlsian equity or asks again for a continuum stable-matching result already available in close form. The remaining possible contribution is a precise bit-complexity analysis for compressed rational masses and an exact priority representation. That could be worth studying, but it is a narrower computational question than the claimed mirror, and the paper itself does not formulate a complexity result that would motivate it. The negative case is strongest against this anchor; it cannot rule out every richer formulation of the priority data.
