The continuous mirror question

Given finite firm-type and worker-type supports \(I\) and \(J\), rational type masses \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_j\), rational pair weights \(w_{ij}\), and anonymous choice maps \(C_i:\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{J}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{J}\) and \(D_j:\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{I}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{I}\), find a stable mass assignment maximizing \(\sum_{i\in I,j\in J}w_{ij}x_{ij}\). A mass assignment specifies distributions \(z_{i,b}\) and \(y_{j,d}\) over integral individual bundles, summing to \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_j\), with each assigned bundle individually rational and \(x_{ij}=\sum_b b_jz_{i,b}=\sum_d d_i y_{j,d}\). Stability means there are no bundle patterns \(b,d\) with \(z_{i,b}>0\) and \(y_{j,d}>0\) for which both sides choose an added partner of the other type, meaning \(C_i(b+e_j)_j>b_j\) and \(D_j(d+e_i)_i>d_i\). The choice maps satisfy the paper’s substitutability, consistency, and cardinal-monotonicity conditions, with quota-filling on the worker side; determine the exact maximum value and an optimizing assignment.

The model it lives in

A two-sided society with rational masses of complete firm and worker types, where each type has an anonymous choice map on integral count vectors. Decision variables are masses of agents assigned each integral bundle; they must balance by type pair and satisfy individual rationality and the stated no-blocking condition. Maximize the weighted mass of matched pairs, \(\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}x_{ij}\).

The objection that survived

The paper’s choice functions act on named partners, while a quota-one agent cannot deterministically select one of two same-type partners in a swap-invariant way; the mirror needs count-based choice semantics that induce individual choices and blocking.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 1.5’s weighted stable-matching optimization in the CM-QF setting. It leaves the paper’s structural representation results, polyhedral descriptions, and choice-function counting result outside the proposed question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can this maximum be computed in time polynomial in the type-support sizes, rational input bit length, quota bounds, and choice-map query cost, without expanding the population into individual copies?
  • Do these count-based choice semantics yield a stable mass assignment and a useful analogue of the paper’s rotation-poset structure?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper’s strongest usable anchor is Corollary 1.5, proved here: given a CM-QF market, optimizing a linear function over its stable matchings takes \(O(|F|^3|W|^3\,\text{oracle-call})\) time. Here agents’ choice functions are substitutable, consistent, and cardinal-monotone, with quota-filling choice functions on one side. The paper has no named hardness result for stable-matching optimization. Theorem 6.4 counts how many choice functions are possible; it does not establish computational hardness for this problem.

A plausible regime is a large two-sided job market: many workers and employers, but relatively few complete behavioral types. A worker type records its preferences, acceptable employers, and quota; an employer type records its full hiring choice rule, acceptable workers, and quota. Each type has a rational mass, with the number of agents much larger than the number of types. Employers of a type might share a substitutable team-selection rule, while workers have unit quotas and quota-filling choices. The type definition must include every feature that affects choices or weights. Agents who differ on one of those features belong to different types.

My lead question is Choice-Function Stable Mass Welfare∞. An instance consists of finite supports \(I\) and \(J\) of firm and worker types, rational type masses \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_j\), the types’ choice rules, and a rational pair weight \(w_{ij}\). For a concrete mass model, lift each choice rule to anonymous multisets of counterpart types: a bundle is an integral count vector, so each unit of agent mass still receives an integral bundle. The rule must satisfy the paper’s choice assumptions on individual typed offers, and the worker-side rules must be quota-filling.

For each firm type \(i\), let \(z_{i,b}\) be the mass of agents receiving bundle \(b\); define \(y_{j,d}\) similarly for worker type \(j\). These distributions must sum to the input type masses, use only individually rational bundles, and balance at every type pair:

\[ x_{ij}=\sum_b b_j z_{i,b}=\sum_d d_i y_{j,d}. \]

Require stability at the agent level: there cannot be positive masses of bundle patterns on both sides such that an agent of type \(i\) and an agent of type \(j\) would each choose the other as a fresh partner. The objective is to maximize

\[ \sum_{i\in I,\ j\in J} w_{ij}x_{ij}. \]

A solution is a stable mass assignment and its exact optimal value. This keeps the paper’s underlying question—choosing a maximum-weight stable matching under choice functions—while making the society a distribution over complete agent types. It also allows the type-level averages to be fractional while every agent receives a discrete bundle, as the population model requires.

I expect Class A: a type-level analogue of the paper’s rotation-poset and min-cut machinery, or a configuration formulation with a manageable pricing problem, could yield an algorithm polynomial in the number of types, the rational input bit length, quota bounds, and choice-oracle cost. This is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Corollary 1.5. Clearing the rational denominators produces an explicit finite market, but its size can be exponential in the bit length of the masses; the paper’s algorithm then gives polynomial time in the expanded market, not in the compressed population input. The continuous question is whether repeated agents can be handled without expanding them.

The mirror is recognizable as the authors’ problem because it retains two-sided stability, choice-function behavior, and pair-weight optimization. The paper’s affine representation of the stable-matching lattice is a concrete reason to investigate a compact type-level method. This is a population continuization; fractional pair masses arise from splitting the population across integral individual bundles.

The weakest point is the treatment of counterpart identities. The paper’s choice functions act on sets of named agents. My mirror assumes choices are anonymous within a type and lifts them to typed multisets. That is a genuine model co-modification, so the authors would need to accept anonymous type-level offers as their market. If individual identity affects choice, the type support may grow with the population and remove the compression advantage. The paper’s oracle model also makes choice-query cost part of the running time; a polynomial bound in oracle calls alone does not guarantee a compact encoding of every choice rule. These are material limits on the positive case, but they leave a specific, computationally substantive mass-market question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 1.5 is the one serious anchor: it gives a polynomial algorithm for optimizing arbitrary linear weights over stable matchings in the paper’s CM-QF model. The strongest case against a mirror is that this problem is defined over named pairs. Each choice function acts on sets of named partners, and the objective may assign a different weight to every firm–worker pair. Exact types must therefore preserve each agent’s choices among identities and the values of its incident pairs. In a generic market, that can leave almost no repeated types.

The proposed anonymous lift has a sharper difficulty. Suppose a quota-one agent faces two interchangeable partners of the same type. A deterministic choice rule invariant under swapping those partners can choose only neither or both; quota-filling requires choosing exactly one. A label-based tie-break resolves this in a finite market, but the labels then matter to choices and must be retained in the type. So the proposed type-count rules and bundle distributions require new choice and blocking semantics; they do not directly aggregate the paper’s choice functions over named partners.

Those semantics could define a worthwhile model in their own right. They would, however, restrict pair weights to be constant within type pairs and replace named-partner choice with anonymous typed choice. Corollary 1.5’s rotation-poset algorithm gives no result about whether those stable mass assignments retain a lattice or admit efficient weighted optimization. Existing continuum matching frameworks, such as Azevedo and Leshno’s supply-and-demand model, establish that continuous populations are natural in matching, but their equilibrium analysis does not answer this computational question. ([Azevedo and Leshno, 2016](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/687476))

That is the best negative case: the paper’s algorithmic anchor depends on identity-level choices and values, while the proposed compression changes both. Its weakness is substantial, though. In a market where choices and values truly depend only on a small set of types, the anonymous mass model is coherent and its optimization question remains open. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario; the argument weighs against treating the proponent’s model as a direct mirror, but it does not defeat it as a research direction.
