What the mirror covers

The proposed model addresses matched mass in a stable partial matching. It does not carry over Theorem 1.4’s quantitative random threshold or the paper’s other asymptotic results on stable-matching counts, unmatched agents, and partner ranks.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is an algorithmic one, not a hardness result. Theorem 1.4, proved in this paper, says that if \(p\ge c\log^2 n/n\) and \(c>1+1/(2\Delta)\), then the \(p\)-proposal algorithm’s stable partial matching has more than \(n-\Delta\) pairs with probability tending to \(1\). The theorem further states that for \(c>1\) the number unmatched is bounded in probability, and for \(c>9/4\) the algorithm returns a complete stable matching with high probability. This is a named guarantee about an algorithm’s output on random instances; it does not classify a worst-case problem as being in P or establish hardness. The paper has no named hardness result to anchor a mirror on.

My lead mirror is Type-Compressed Stable Matching. Take two finite sets of types \(A\) and \(B\), with rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b>0\) summing to \(1\) on each side. A type records a complete ranking of the opposite-side types and its compatibility profile; compatibility \(E\subseteq A\times B\) applies to every pair of agents in those two types. The regime is a large hiring or assignment market with many applicants and positions but relatively few recurring preference and eligibility profiles, so headcount \(N\) is much larger than the support size \(\tau=|A|+|B|\).

A solution is a mass flow \(x_{ab}\ge 0\), with \(x_{ab}=0\) outside \(E\), row sums at most \(\mu_a\), and column sums at most \(\nu_b\). Require stability in the mass sense: for every compatible pair \((a,b)\), it cannot be that positive mass of type \(a\) is unmatched or assigned below \(b\) *and* positive mass of type \(b\) is unmatched or assigned below \(a\). Equivalently, define
\[ r_{ab}=\mu_a-\sum_{b':\,b'\succeq_a b}x_{ab'}, \qquad s_{ab}=\nu_b-\sum_{a':\,a'\succeq_b a}x_{a'b}. \]
Stability requires \(r_{ab}=0\) or \(s_{ab}=0\) for every \((a,b)\in E\). The computational question is: given these rational type distributions, compute a stable mass flow and report its matched mass \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\), in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). A perfect result has matched mass \(1\).

I expect this question to be Class A: a mass version of deferred acceptance should be able to make and reject proposals by type, with the work depending on the type support rather than the population. That mirrors the algorithmic shape of Theorem 1.4: the output is a stable partial matching, and the result of interest is how much mass it matches. It also generates a harder follow-up: characterize which type distributions and compatibility patterns force a perfect stable mass matching.

The regime is plausible for the paper’s stable-matching problem: it preserves incomplete acceptability, stability, and unmatched mass, while giving the many agents shared, complete problem-relevant descriptions. But the paper’s headline threshold is not itself carried over. Its admissibility is sampled independently for each named pair; in a random instance, agents with the same ranking will generally have different compatibility profiles. Grouping them by full type can therefore leave little repetition. The continuous mirror instead assumes compatibility is shared by type pairs, a substantive co-modification that the authors might accept as a high-multiplicity stable-matching problem but need not accept as a mirror of their independent-edge threshold theorem.

That is the soft spot: this is a credible mirror of the stable partial matching algorithmic problem, but it does not continuize the paper’s specific random threshold result. If the standard is a named worst-case complexity theorem, this paper offers no qualifying anchor; Theorem 1.4 is the strongest computationally relevant result I can defend.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject Theorem 1.4 as a computational anchor. Its result is a probability statement about the size of the stable matching produced by the \(p\)-proposal algorithm on a particular random market. It does not classify the complexity of computing a matching; the algorithm already terminates after at most \(n^2\) proposals. The continuous question in the case for—compute a stable mass flow for rational type distributions—has no \(p\), random-instance guarantee, or analogue of the theorem’s threshold \(p\asymp \log^2 n/n\). It asks a different question.

The strongest repair would preserve the theorem’s sparse random compatibility and put many agents into each type. But in the paper, admissibility is sampled independently for each named pair. A full type must therefore include an agent’s compatibility profile as well as their preferences, and those profiles are typically distinct. The support then grows with the population. If compatibility is instead shared by type pairs, as in the proposed flow model, one random admissible type pair connects whole blocks of agents. That replaces the paper’s independent sparse graph with a highly correlated block market. Its mass-matching behavior may be worth studying, but it does not mirror the theorem’s random threshold. Averaging individual compatibility into a kernel loses the same structure: at the theorem’s scale, \(p\to0\), while each person still has about \(\log^2 n\) admissible partners.

A finite-type stable marriage model can still be a sensible high-multiplicity problem. That is the honest weakness in this negative case: I cannot establish that no such problem would interest the authors or the programme. The narrower objection is that this paper supplies no named worst-case computational result for it, and Theorem 1.4 does not supply one. On the evidence here, the proposed mass-flow problem is an adjacent research direction, not a continuous mirror of the paper’s result.
