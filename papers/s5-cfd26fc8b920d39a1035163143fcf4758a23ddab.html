The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow population version of the paper’s spectator choice: in a large school district, many residents decide which procedure should assign students in a later cohort. A complete type \(t\) would include the person’s relevant view of fairness, information, and voting cost; for stakeholders, it would also include priority rank and whether they must report preferences sincerely. The mass \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of the electorate. A ballot assigns each type Boston, assortative matching, or abstention. Under the paper’s random-dictator rule, the ballot masses determine the probability that each procedure is selected. For example,
\[ \Pr(\text{Boston})=\sum_{t:\,v_t=\text{Boston}}\mu_t+\tfrac12\sum_{t:\,v_t=\text{abstain}}\mu_t. \]
That gives a concrete, population-continuous account of who wants the Boston mechanism.

The closest evidence is Result 4: in the Experiment 2 Spectator treatment, 45.8% voted for Boston, 37.5% for the assortative matching, and 16.7% abstained. Spectators chose for a later group, so their own matching payoff was not at stake. This is an empirical finding about aggregate procedural preferences, not a computational result. The district-scale scenario is plausible given the paper’s discussion of real-world school choice, but extending the measured preference pattern to a large electorate would require a behavioral model the paper does not provide.

There are no qualifying computational anchors in the paper. Its numbered Results 1–4 report experimental vote patterns; none is a numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a problem’s complexity or giving an algorithmic result. The paper also has no named computational formulation that it solves. Thus I cannot honestly provide one continuous computational problem per anchor or classify an anchor as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard. The ballot-aggregation calculation above would be linear in the number of represented types, but that is a possible extension, not a result this paper proves.

That gap is also the weakest point in the positive case. The experiment uses eight-student groups and a random dictator: as the electorate grows, any one voter’s chance to affect the selected procedure shrinks. A model that instead uses majority voting, or assumes ballot behavior as given, changes or removes part of the studied decision problem. So the paper supports a credible continuous-population research question about procedural preferences, but it supplies no named computational result for a ChoCo complexity mirror to build on.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Result 4 is the best anchor, but it is an empirical vote share, not a computational result. The paper measures how eight spectators choose between two procedures for another eight-person group. Replacing those counts with masses gives a sensible population statistic: the Boston vote share, the assortative-match share, and the abstention share. That may be useful for studying public preferences. It does not create a computational problem the paper poses or solves.

The strongest computational rescue would ask how a district should select a procedure from those population preferences, or how much mass must change its vote to alter the selection. But the paper supplies no aggregation objective beyond its eight-person random-dictator rule, and no type-level preferences, persuasion costs, or intervention model. With ballots fixed, the continuous random-dictator calculation is just a weighted sum. With strategic voting, each person’s chance of affecting the choice tends to zero as the electorate grows while the paper’s voting cost stays positive. Avoiding that limit requires changing the voting rule or adding assumptions about costs or expressive preferences; those might motivate a new study, but they would not be mirrors of Result 4.

A different rescue would continuize the student market itself. That is a plausible direction, but this paper studies a particular eight-student experiment and reports no complexity result about computing or manipulating its matchings. Its numbered Results 1–4 report experimental patterns; the Boston equilibrium discussion does not analyze computational complexity. So the paper could inspire continuous research on procedural preferences or matching, but it offers no computational anchor for ChoCo. The negative case is strongest on that scope distinction; it cannot rule out a worthwhile related project under a broader remit.
