What the mirror covers

The proposed aggregate simulation would concern a new mass version of the allocation algorithm for selected rules; it does not mirror the characterization, incentive theorem, welfare comparisons, or market-specific mechanism results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a possible continuous version of the paper’s local-priority allocation algorithm. In a large market with many agents but relatively few complete types, let type \(t\) have mass \(\mu_t\). A state \(z_{t,r}\) records how much mass of type \(t\) is currently at rank \(r\) in its preference list. A type-symmetric rule chooses amounts of mass to advance to their next choice whenever the aggregate assignment violates constraints such as per-capita school capacities. The outcome is an assignment of an integral object to each individual, represented in aggregate by fractional masses \(x_{t,o}\). The corresponding computational question would be: given the type masses, constraints, and a terminating rule for choosing which masses advance, compute the terminal feasible assignment and its sequence of states. That is a coherent population continuization of their constructive framework.

But it has no qualifying anchor under your stated rule. The closest named result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for a constraint \(C\) and an implementable local compromiser assignment \(\alpha\), if \(\alpha\) satisfies forward and backward consistency, then the induced local priority mechanism \(LP_\alpha\) is group strategy-proof. That is a theorem about incentives, not the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 2 says deferred acceptance for school choice is a local priority mechanism; Proposition 3 says the same of top trading cycles. Neither classifies a problem as tractable or hard. The paper contains no named computational-complexity result to mirror.

The regime also needs care. Agents must share all mechanism-relevant features, not just preferences. In standard school-choice deferred acceptance, strict school priorities can make each student’s priority position unique, so the number of types may grow with the population. A convincing high-multiplicity version would need repeated priority classes or a type-symmetric local-priority rule; that is plausible within the paper’s general framework, but it is an extension rather than a result established here.

So I would present the continuous algorithm as a promising research question, not as a ChoCo mirror anchored in this paper’s results. The paper’s proved contribution is mechanism-theoretic, and its weakest point for this programme is precisely that it supplies no complexity result for a continuous-population computation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1 is its strongest candidate, but it says that consistency conditions on a local compromiser assignment suffice for group strategy-proofness. Propositions 2 and 3 show that deferred acceptance and top trading cycles belong to the local-priority class. None asks whether a computational problem is tractable or hard. That leaves no result here for ChoCo’s complexity programme to mirror.

The best substitute would be to ask how quickly a local-priority algorithm can find its terminal allocation in a large society. But the paper’s general algorithm is not a computational problem until the local compromiser assignment is given a compact representation. In the paper, that assignment can be an arbitrary map over allocations in \(O^n\). A continuous population does not make that map easier to represent or evaluate. For DA and TTC, the paper supplies recognizable structure, but their continuous simulation would be a new algorithmic formulation, not a complexity question arising from one of its results.

There are plausible repeated-type settings for DA, especially with a small number of preference and priority classes. They require a further change to the model: the paper uses strict school priorities, which can distinguish every student and make the number of complete types grow with the population. Replacing those priorities with classes, ties, or a mass-level tie-breaking rule may yield a sensible model, but the paper’s result then provides the mechanism to study, not a computational claim to continuize. TTC has a sharper obstacle: each agent owns a distinct house, and its cycles depend on those ownership links. A large market with replicated house and ownership types can be designed, but the resulting aggregate cycle process would need its own model and results.

One could also adapt Theorem 1 to a continuum and ask whether its consistency conditions guarantee resistance to group deviations. That is a mechanism-theoretic question, not a computational one. Moreover, individual deviations by zero-mass agents may become vacuous; allowing positive-mass coalitions changes the incentive concept and requires a new theorem.

These are possible future projects, but they do not rescue the proposed mirrors as mirrors of this paper. The negative case is strongest on that boundary: there may be worthwhile continuous-population work inspired by local priority mechanisms, yet this paper offers no named computational result to anchor it.
