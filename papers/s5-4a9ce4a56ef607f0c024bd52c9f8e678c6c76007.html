The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a compliant anchor in this paper. Its numbered formal items are Definitions 1–3, which define the Shapley–Shubik index, Banzhaf value, and normalized Banzhaf index. Example 1 and Sections 3.1–3.3 report numerical calculations and observed patterns; none is a named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. So there are no anchor-specific continuous problems or classifications to state honestly.

The strongest prospective mirror I can see is Infinitesimal Council Exit Sensitivity, an unanchored follow-up based on the power-index calculations. Let the types \(t\in T\) be classes of interchangeable, equal-status jurisdictions, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-jurisdiction population weights \(w_t\), and budget contributions \(b_t\). A replicated council has \(k\mu_t\) jurisdictions of type \(t\); a coalition wins if it meets both the member quota \(\alpha\) and the population quota \(\rho\). For an exiting type \(e\), let \(\phi_{s,k}\) be the Shapley–Shubik value of one type-\(s\) jurisdiction before the exit, and \(\phi_{s,k}^{(-e)}\) its value after one type-\(e\) jurisdiction leaves and the quotas are recalculated. The question is whether, and how efficiently, one can compute the limiting vector
\[ E_{e,s}=\lim_{k\to\infty}k\left[\left(1-\frac{b_e}{k\sum_t\mu_t b_t}\right)\phi_{s,k}^{(-e)}-\phi_{s,k}\right], \]
along replications for which all \(k\mu_t\) are integers. This gives the first-order change in each surviving type’s budget-adjusted power from one departure. A solution is the vector of these limits, or an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation to it.

A plausible regime would be a large federation with many equal-status local jurisdictions and a small number of statutory classes sharing the same population weights and budget rules, so \(N\gg\tau\). The continuous object is the distribution over jurisdiction types; population and budget weights are attributes used by the two quotas and the adjustment. For fixed \(\tau\), I would tentatively expect tractable asymptotic formulas away from quota-tie cases, while limit existence and behavior at ties need separate analysis. Further questions include the effect of the blocking-minority rule, multiple departures, and whether the corresponding Banzhaf limits agree.

The weak point is substantial: this replaces a council of a few distinct countries with a large population of interchangeable jurisdictions, and the paper’s single-country departure becomes an infinitesimal perturbation. The authors might recognize the power-index question, but the paper does not establish that this high-multiplicity regime is their problem. Most importantly, this is a plausible research direction, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no anchor to defeat: the numbered items are definitions of the Shapley–Shubik and Banzhaf indices, not results about the complexity of computing them. The paper reports calculations for a council of 28 named states and interprets the pattern after departures; it gives no algorithmic, approximation, or complexity claim. On the requested standard, there is no continuous *mirror of a computational result* here.

The strongest follow-up is the proposed exit-sensitivity question for a large federation of interchangeable jurisdictions. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, but it exposes a basic mismatch: in the continuous quota game, removing one jurisdiction changes no mass, so it cannot change whether a coalition meets either quota. Each atomless jurisdiction has zero marginal contribution. The indices in the paper instead count pivotal players across finite permutations or coalitions. Recovering a nonzero power measure therefore requires retaining finite-population fluctuations and defining a scaled limit; a distribution over types alone does not supply the pivotality the paper measures.

As written, the proposed \(E_{e,s}\) also appears to use too small a scale in regular cases. With \(n\) identical jurisdictions in a simple majority game, an individual’s Shapley–Shubik value is \(1/n\) before an exit and \(1/(n-1)\) after, so the difference is \(O(n^{-2})\); multiplying by \(n\) sends it to zero. With equal budget contributions, the stated budget adjustment makes the two values exactly equal. A type-level aggregate or a stronger rescaling could repair this, but that would ask for a finite-size asymptotic expansion of voting-power indices. It would be a new research question, not a computational result this paper established.

That is the strongest defensible negative case, but it does not prove that no worthwhile project could be built from the paper. A complexity study of power-index computation for a large federation with repeated jurisdiction types might be sensible. The case against is narrower: the paper supplies no computational-complexity result to mirror, and the proponent’s repaired direction needs substantial new asymptotic modeling before it becomes a continuous question.
