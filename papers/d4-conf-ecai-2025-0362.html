Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

Under the stated anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational result. Theorem 2, Theorem 3, and Theorem 4 are proved here, but they establish existence guarantees for approximate MMS allocations; none says the underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The paper’s algorithmization remark is unnumbered and assumes that MMS partitions are supplied. So the formal answer is zero valid anchors.

The strongest positive case I can make uses Theorem 4 as a structural lead, while being clear that it is not a qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 4, proved here, says that for every connected split graph and every set of agents with at most \(2^k\) utility types, there is an allocation giving each agent at least \(\frac{3}{7\cdot 2^k-3}\) of her MMS. Here, agents have the same type precisely when they have the same utility function.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large land-consolidation programme with many farmers but only a few recurring valuation profiles. Parcels are the indivisible goods, adjacency gives the graph of goods, and each profile assigns the same additive value to every parcel for all farmers of that type. For the split-graph case, take a fixed connected split template \(H\), replicate its parcel categories as the population grows, and scale each category’s supply with the number of farmers. Then there are \(N\) farmers, \(N\mu_t\) of type \(t\), and \(Nq_j\) copies of parcel category \(j\). The number of farmers grows while the number of utility types and parcel categories stays fixed. The split-graph geometry is a fairly specific land pattern, but this is a concrete instance regime, not a claim that all land consolidation has that structure.

My lead candidate is Fixed-Template Continuous Approximate MMS Allocation. Its input is \(H\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and additive type utilities \(u_t(j)\), with at most \(2^k\) types. A bundle \(B\) is a finite connected set of parcel copies in the replicated graph; each agent still receives an integral bundle. The continuous MMS benchmark \(M_t\) is the supremum value \(\beta\) such that a unit mass of type-\(t\) agents can be assigned connected bundles, each worth at least \(\beta\), using per-capita supply \(q_j\) of every category \(j\). The task is to maximize \(\alpha\) by finding masses \(x_{tB}\) of type-\(t\) agents receiving bundle \(B\), subject to \(\sum_B x_{tB}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B}x_{tB}|B\cap V_j|=q_j\) for each \(j\), and \(u_t(B)\ge\alpha M_t\) whenever \(x_{tB}>0\). The solution is the mass assignment \(x\) and its guarantee \(\alpha\).

This retains the paper’s problem: agents have additive utilities, goods remain indivisible, bundles must be connected, and fairness is measured against MMS. Only the population becomes continuous. The type masses may be distributed across different integral bundles, but that represents how a continuum of same-type agents is allocated; it does not give any individual a fractional bundle. The per-capita supplies scale with the population, as required for a meaningful large-population regime.

Theorem 4 makes the bound \(\frac{3}{7\cdot 2^k-3}\) a natural conjectured guarantee for this mirror. I would tentatively expect the fixed-template computational problem to be Class A: its allocation formulation is an LP over connected integral bundle patterns, and with fixed \(H\) and \(k\), pricing over those patterns appears to reduce to fixed-dimensional integer optimization. That is a conjecture about a new problem, not a consequence of Theorem 4. Follow-up questions include whether the continuous MMS benchmarks can be computed without supplied partitions, whether the discrete blow-up optima converge to the defined continuous optimum, and what happens when \(H\) is part of the input.

The soft spot is that the paper proves neither this limit identity nor a pricing algorithm; it explicitly identifies supplied MMS partitions as the current algorithmic bottleneck. Also, the template-scaled regime is narrower than all split graphs. The case for the mirror is that the resulting question is still recognizably their connected approximate-MMS problem, with the population—not the goods or outcomes—made continuous. The lack of a named computational anchor limits how strongly this paper can support the programme’s complexity claims.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is decisive at the anchor stage: this paper has no named computational-complexity result to mirror. Theorems 2–4 prove existence guarantees for approximate MMS allocations; they do not classify the complexity of finding one. The paper’s only algorithmic statement is an unnumbered conditional: an allocation can be computed in polynomial time given the agents’ MMS partitions. The authors then identify computing those partitions as the bottleneck. The other analyst acknowledges that Theorem 4 is only a “structural lead,” not a qualifying anchor. So none of the anchors raised meets the programme’s computational criterion.

The fixed-template proposal is the strongest version of their idea, and its population regime is coherent: many farmers can share a finite set of utility types while parcel supplies scale with the population. But its continuous MMS benchmark, mass allocation over connected bundle patterns, and optimization of the guarantee are additional modelling and computational questions. Theorem 4 supplies no algorithm for finding the mass allocation, computing those benchmarks, or pricing connected bundles. The paper’s conditional algorithmization does not fill that gap; it assumes the MMS partitions the paper itself says are hard to compute. A broader version with scaled split graphs rather than a fixed template has the same problem: it may be a worthwhile new project, but it is not a computational result of this paper being continuized.

There is also a mismatch in the stated anchor: Theorem 4 covers at most \(2k\) utility types, not \(2^k\). Its guarantee is \(\frac{3}{7\cdot 2^k-3}\); the paper’s corollary expresses the bound directly in terms of \(p\) types as \(\frac{3}{14p-17}\).

That makes the case against this paper as a ChoCo computational anchor strong. It does not honestly establish the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous question. The land-consolidation regime is plausible, and the resulting algorithmic problem could be interesting in its own right. The negative case is strongest when it says the paper does not anchor that problem with a named computational result, rather than claiming the problem itself has no value.
