Why no mirror

The proposed high-multiplicity population models are sensible and recognisable extensions of the paper's finite-agent fractional models. However, neither anchor is a named computational result under the programme's strict gate: Theorem 5.1 is existential, and Theorem 4.1 is existential/decompositional. Thus the paper yields worthwhile new algorithmic questions, but no computational theorem to mirror.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers matroid-responsive stable-allocation existence and cardinal stable-lottery decomposition over a finite population of types. It leaves the paper's structural equivalences, ordinal ambiguity, incentive impossibilities, applications, and unresolved finite-computation issue without a qualifying computational anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can such an \(x\) be computed in time polynomial in \(|W|+|F|+|E|\) when matroids have efficient independence oracles?
  • Can a finite-support stable lottery preserving the relevant expected utilities be computed with polynomial encoding length?
  • Can rational mass solutions be rounded to \(N\)-agent markets with stability error bounded by \(O(1/N)\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s matroid-responsive matching model. One important qualification comes first: the paper contains no named theorem proving an algorithmic classification such as membership in P, NP-hardness, FPT, or W[1]-hardness. Its polynomial-time remarks occur in surrounding prose, while polynomial-time computation of stable allocations in the general model is explicitly left open. Thus the following are structural anchors, not qualifying computational anchors under the strictest reading of the brief.

The natural regime is a large two-sided market with finitely many worker types \(W\), firm or school types \(F\), and contract types \(E\subseteq W\times F\times L\). A type completely specifies everything relevant: preferences, priorities, matroid constraints, capacities, reserves, and— in the cardinal version—valuations. The input contains worker masses \(\mu_w\) and firm masses \(\nu_f\), with \(N\gg |W|+|F|+|E|\) individual agents represented by these types.

The decision variable is a mass flow \(x\in\mathbb R_+^E\), where \(x_e\) is the mass of contracts of type \(e\). The per-agent bundle of worker type \(w\) is \(p^w_e=x_e/\mu_w\), and that of firm type \(f\) is \(p^f_e=x_e/\nu_f\). Capacities and reserves are per-copy constraints, so aggregate capacity automatically scales as, for example, \(q_f\nu_f\). This is population continuization: \(x\) is not merely a fractional outcome for a fixed list of named agents.

My lead mirror is Continuized Ordinal Doubly-Strong Stability. Given \((W,F,E,\mu,\nu)\), matroid constraints \(r_w,r_f\), and weak orders over contracts, find a mass flow \(x\) such that:

\(p^w\in C_w(p^w)\) and \(p^f\in C_f(p^f)\) for every type;

for every contract type \(e=(w,f,\ell)\), either \(p^w\in C_w(p^w\vee\chi_e)\) or \(p^f\in C_f(p^f\vee\chi_e)\);

and no type-level local exchange violates the three fairness conditions. Concretely, for every \(e\), worker-side alternative \(e'\) or \(\varnothing\), firm-side alternative \(e''\) or \(\varnothing\), and \(\varepsilon>0\), the two exchanges must be matroid-feasible. We prohibit:

  • \(e\sim_w e'\), \(e\succ_f e''\), and \(p^w_e<p^w_{e'}\);
  • \(e\succ_w e'\), \(e\sim_f e''\), and \(p^f_e<p^f_{e''}\);
  • \(e\sim_w e'\), \(e\sim_f e''\), and both \(p^w_e<p^w_{e'}\) and \(p^f_e<p^f_{e''}\).

Here \(\chi_{\varnothing}=0\). The objective is feasibility; a canonical variant would ask for the worker-optimal solution in the aggregate Alkan–Gale order.

The anchor is Theorem 5.1, “Ordinal existence under matroid-responsiveness,” proved in this paper. Its full content is that if every agent has a matroid constraint and matroid-responsive preferences over contracts, then a doubly-strong ex ante stable fractional allocation exists; the set of such allocations depends only on the ordinal preferences and matroid constraints; and, for any consistent cardinal weights, every such allocation can be decomposed into a lottery over deterministic allocations preserving every agent’s expected utility under those weights.

The theorem is a good mirror because the continuous input has exactly the high-multiplicity interpretation the paper’s applications invite: a large cohort of students, workers, or applicants share a finite collection of preference and policy types, while many schools, employers, hospital departments, or branches share a finite collection of capacity and priority types. Type-specific quotas, reserves, soft bounds, EDCR rules, and overlapping reserves remain meaningful after replacing counts by masses. A discrete market with \(N\mu_w\) workers of type \(w\) and \(N\nu_f\) firms of type \(f\) maps directly to the flow \(x\), with \(Nx_e\) contract units.

I would tentatively place this problem in Class A for explicitly represented finite matroids: the aggregate dimension is \(|E|\), the feasibility regions are polymatroids, and fractional choice is computable by greedy or separation methods. But this is a conjectural classification, not a result of the paper. The coupled blocking disjunction and fairness conditions may instead produce continuum-specific difficulty, especially if matroids or type systems are given succinctly. The first questions are whether the Alkan–Gale procedure can be made finite and polynomial in the number of types, whether exact rational solutions have polynomial encoding length, and how a continuous solution rounds back to a finite market while preserving stability up to \(O(1/N)\).

A second, cardinal mirror uses Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. The theorem states: if a fractional allocation \(\pi\) is ex ante stable, then there exists a lottery allocation \(\lambda\) over feasible integral allocations that induces \(\pi\), and every allocation in the support of \(\lambda\) is ex post stable. The proof additionally shows that the lottery can simultaneously attain the concave-closure value for every agent by using the common intersection of the relevant \(M^\natural\)-convex supporting sets.

The corresponding problem is Continuous Stable-Lottery Decomposition. The input is a finite-type cardinal market with \(M^\natural\)-concave valuations, masses \(\mu,\nu\), and an ex ante stable mass flow \(x\). The output is a probability measure \(\Lambda\) over measurable integral matchings of the underlying atomless population such that the expected mass of every contract type is \(x_e\), every realization is ex post stable, and each type \(t\) receives expected utility equal to \(\overline v_t(p^t)\). In a finite-support representation, \(\Lambda\) is a lottery over finitely many integral allocation patterns.

This is probably Class A in the finite-type setting: the paper already points to supporting faces, \(M^\natural\)-convex intersection, Carathéodory decomposition, and weighted matroid intersection. High multiplicity should make exact splitting of mass easier. The interesting boundary question is whether non-responsive ordinal information makes the decomposition representation-dependent, as in the paper’s Examples 5.3 and 5.4.

The main weakness is that the paper already studies fractional allocations and lotteries, so an opponent can argue that this is merely outcome-space continuity. The defence is that the mirror changes the primitive object: the market is specified by distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) over complete agent types, not by named \(I\) and \(J\); capacities and reserves scale with population mass; and the finite market is recovered as a high-multiplicity discretization. Still, because the paper has no named computational result, this is a respectable continuous analogue of its existence and implementation theorems, but a weaker ChoCo case than a paper with a discrete NP-hardness or polynomial-time anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case begins with a decisive scope problem: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 5.1 is an existence theorem; Theorem 4.1 is an existence/decomposition theorem. Neither states a complexity bound, an algorithm for the full problem, or a hardness result. The paper explicitly leaves polynomial-time computation of stable allocations open, while its polynomial-time remarks concern only subroutines such as local choice and matroid decomposition. Thus a computational “mirror” would not be continuizing a computational theorem from the paper. It would be inventing a new algorithmic problem around an existential theory.

The proponent’s best version of the ordinal mirror is nevertheless a coherent high-multiplicity model. Large cohorts of students and firms sharing complete preference, priority, and constraint types are entirely plausible; this is not a valid objection based on lack of multiplicity. But the proposed flow \(x\), and the normalized bundles \(p^w\) and \(p^f\), are simply the paper’s fractional allocation \(\pi\) after quotienting symmetric copies and rescaling by population masses. Matroid choice is already defined on fractional vectors, and the fairness conditions are already fractional local-exchange conditions. The population continuum contributes no computational question stated or solved by Theorem 5.1.

The strongest rescue would be to add a worker-optimal objective and ask for an exact algorithm on the type-level flow. That is a legitimate research problem, but it is a new problem generated by the paper’s open question, not a mirror of its theorem. The Alkan–Gale procedure is only shown to converge; finite termination and polynomial computation in this general domain are explicitly left open. If the type system and matroids are explicitly finite, the resulting object is a finite-dimensional polymatroid/flow problem. If they are succinct, any hardness may come from the representation of the matroids or contracts rather than from population multiplicity. Either way, the paper supplies neither a discrete computational baseline nor a continuous complexity comparison.

There is also a genuine modelling fork. A type-level flow records only aggregate contract marginals, whereas the paper’s cardinal stability and concave-closure arguments concern each agent’s bundle and a common decomposition into integral bundles. In non-responsive settings, the same marginals can have different decompositions and different utilities—the paper’s own motivation. Retaining only \(x\) therefore loses information. Restoring it requires a distribution over type-level bundles together with a coupling across the two sides. That turns the mirror into a measure-valued lottery model, not merely a continuous population model. In the responsive matroid subclass, where marginals suffice, the extra object disappears and one is back to an ordinary fractional matroid-flow formulation.

Theorem 4.1 fares worse as an anchor. Its “continuous stable-lottery decomposition” is already the content of the paper’s fractional allocation theory: \(\pi\) is a mean allocation, and \(\lambda\) is a lottery over integral allocations realizing that mean. Passing to an atomless population merely permits masses to be split exactly among anonymous copies. If individual identities and bundle correlations are retained, one has introduced a new infinite-dimensional measurable-matching problem; if they are discarded, the decomposition is just the existing convex-hull argument over \(M^\natural\)-convex sets. In neither case does the paper provide a computational theorem whose high-multiplicity relaxation can be classified.

So the honest negative verdict is not that high-multiplicity matching is nonsensical. It is sensible, and a future paper could study its algorithms. The stronger point is that this particular paper offers no computational result to mirror: its proposed mirrors either repackage its already-fractional outcome and lottery objects, or create a new open algorithmic programme. Under ChoCo’s stated remit, that is not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
