The case FOR (proponent)

I can make only a narrow positive case, and it does not meet the required anchor test: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its numbered equations state analytical claims instead. For example, equations (7)–(8) show that a polyarchy accepts a larger fraction of projects than a hierarchy; Section IV and Appendix II analyze optimal screening cutoffs. Those results are proved in the paper, but none establishes tractability, hardness, or another complexity classification. So there is no eligible anchor, and no compliant per-anchor mirror to give.

The strongest candidate question I see is an unanchored computational version of the paper’s rational screening model. Imagine a large grant agency or investment system processing \(N\) proposals, with \(N\gg\tau\), where proposals fall into \(\tau\) classes with the same net benefit and observation-error law. An instance would be rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational net benefits \(x_t\), and a common observation error uniform on \([-u,u]\). Here a type is the proposal’s net benefit \(x_t\), and \(\mu_t\) is its share of the proposal pool. For a cutoff \(R\), the probability a proposal of type \(t\) passes a screen is
\[ p_t(R)=\min\!\left\{1,\max\!\left\{0,\frac{x_t+u-R}{2u}\right\}\right\}. \]

For the paper’s two-firm polyarchy, ask for all symmetric pure-strategy cutoff equilibria \(R\), or a report that none exist. A firm using cutoff \(r\) against a rival using \(R\) has expected profit proportional to
\[ U(r,R)=\sum_t \mu_t x_t\,p_t(r)\bigl(2-p_t(R)\bigr). \]
Thus a symmetric equilibrium satisfies \(R\in\arg\max_r U(r,R)\). The resulting accepted mass and expected profit are respectively
\[ \sum_t\mu_t p_t(R)\bigl(2-p_t(R)\bigr) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t\mu_t x_t p_t(R)\bigl(2-p_t(R)\bigr). \]
The individual proposal still receives an integral accept/reject decision; the fractional quantities describe aggregate mass. I would expect this finite-support, uniform-noise problem to be tractable by examining its finitely many cutoff intervals, though the paper does not prove that complexity claim. It corresponds to Section IV’s rational cutoff choices, not to a named complexity result.

The weakness is fundamental for ChoCo: the large population here is a pool of projects, while the paper’s decision makers are just two firms or bureaus. The paper also already represents project values using a distribution \(g(x)\). So this is a plausible computational extension of its screening analysis, but a referee could reasonably judge it to continuize the portfolio rather than the decision-making population. No discrete hardness anchor is present whose fate in a continuum could be classified.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the starting point: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its numbered equations establish analytical claims about acceptance rates, profits, and screening cutoffs. It gives no complexity classification or algorithmic problem with an input encoding and running time. Those results may be interesting, but they are not anchors for ChoCo’s computational programme.

The proponent’s cutoff-equilibrium question is a plausible extension, but it continuizes the portfolio of projects. The paper already describes that portfolio through a distribution \(g(x)\), while the decision makers whose organization defines the paper’s subject are two firms or two bureaus. A support of project-value types, however large, does not make those decision makers a continuous population. The uniform-noise version is also especially tractable for a reason unrelated to continuization: with finitely many project types, the objective is piecewise linear in the cutoff, so one can check finitely many breakpoints. That could be a useful computational exercise, but it is not a complexity mirror of a result in this paper.

A stronger project-side version could ask for optimal cutoffs or architecture comparisons over a richer finite support, with rational masses and values. It still lacks the needed bridge: the paper proves no discrete computational result whose continuous counterpart this would classify. And using projects as “agents” would change their role in the model: they are objects being screened, not decision makers whose preferences, costs, or identities determine the decision.

One could instead try to continuize evaluators or inventors. But the results at issue depend on two specific organizational positions and their information flows. Replacing those positions with a mass of screeners would require a new architecture and new assumptions; the paper offers no computational result about that model to anchor a mirror. The inventors’ symmetric technology choice likewise appears as an analytical extension, not a complexity problem.

So the proponent has not supplied an anchor that survives the paper’s subject matter: the candidate question concerns a continuous portfolio, while a better population-based model would be a new research problem rather than a mirror of a named computational result. This does not prove that no worthwhile computational model could be built from the paper’s ideas. It does make the negative case strong for the task as posed: the paper provides no eligible result from which to launch a continuous computational mirror.
