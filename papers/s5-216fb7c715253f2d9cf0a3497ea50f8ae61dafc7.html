The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow: the paper’s \(\ell_1\) social-welfare objective has a natural high-multiplicity version.

My lead anchor is Theorem 25. It says that the Majoritarian Phantoms algorithm returns an allocation with maximal social welfare, meaning no feasible alternative has smaller total \(\ell_1\) distance from the voters’ ballots. The paper cites this result from Freeman et al. [16, Theorem 6.1]; it does not prove it here. The paper describes computing the mechanism’s parameter by binary search, but gives no named complexity classification.

A mirror is L1-optimal RetroPGF allocation over voter types. An instance consists of \(m\) projects and \(\tau\) voter types. Type \(t\) has a rational ideal allocation \(x^t\in\Delta_m\) and rational population mass \(\mu_t\ge 0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The task is to return any feasible allocation \(a^\star\in\Delta_m\) minimizing
\[ \Phi_\mu(a)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\lVert a-x^t\rVert_1. \]
A solution is such an allocation together with the assurance that no other feasible allocation has lower \(\Phi_\mu\). This is the paper’s underlying welfare-optimization problem over a distribution of ballots, not a claim that the paper studies continuous populations.

The regime would be a large DAO community evaluating a fixed set of projects, with many participants sharing complete ballot types—for example, recurring evaluator cohorts or standardized ballot patterns. Here a type is the full ideal allocation vector, and mass is the fraction of the community holding that vector. The intended regime has \(n\gg\tau\). That scenario extends beyond the paper’s capped badgeholder electorate, but retains its core allocation task.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable: the objective is a weighted sum of absolute values, so it has a linear-program formulation with \(O(m\tau)\) variables and constraints and should be solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the rational input bit length \(L\). This complexity claim belongs to the mirror, not to Theorem 25. It is also an exact high-multiplicity dictionary: if \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), then \(\Phi_\mu\) is the finite electorate’s average \(\ell_1\) welfare loss, so both models have the same optimizers.

The mirror covers Theorem 25’s welfare guarantee only. I would not use Theorems 17–18 on strategyproofness as anchors: for infinitesimal agents, unilateral reports have zero mass and the incentive question risks becoming vacuous. Further questions include whether a moving-phantom implementation extends directly to rational type masses, how it selects among tied welfare optima, and whether any nontrivial incentive property survives in a genuine population limit.

The weakest point is that Theorem 25 establishes welfare optimality, not a named complexity bound, and the paper’s actual badgeholder groups are small. The mass model is recognizable and computationally well-posed, but its LP is a direct weighted reformulation; the case should not be sold as a new complexity transition.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is thin because the weighted-\(\ell_1\) mirror is well-posed. Give each ballot vector \(x^t\) a rational mass \(\mu_t\), and minimizing \(\sum_t \mu_t\lVert a-x^t\rVert_1\) is a legitimate continuous-population question. But I would still argue against making it a ChoCo anchor: it is a new optimization problem prompted by the paper, not a continuization of a computational result the paper establishes.

Theorem 25 says that Majoritarian Phantoms maximizes \(\ell_1\) social welfare. It gives no complexity classification; the result is cited from Freeman et al. The paper’s binary-search description is not an analysis of a computational problem. The proposed mirror therefore adds the task of computing a welfare minimizer over rational type masses. Its direct LP formulation may be useful, but the paper neither poses that task nor identifies an algorithmic bottleneck for it. Calling it a mirror risks treating any welfare guarantee as a computational result.

The proposed type also makes high multiplicity a demanding assumption here. A type is an entire allocation vector over a round’s project set. The paper describes a deliberately small, selected group of badgeholders evaluating particular projects, and emphasizes differences in expertise, affinity, and reputation. In a larger version of that setting, exact repeated ballot vectors are not assured; the support could approach the voter count. Restricting voters to a shared menu of ballot templates could create repeated types, but that changes the voting regime and lacks support in the paper. A more general community-voting scenario is conceivable, yet it is no longer the paper’s capped-badgeholder setting.

So the strongest negative case is about fit and contribution, not impossibility: the mirror can be made coherent, but its central computational question is supplied by the analyst, and the natural high-multiplicity regime is not grounded in this application. I would not claim this proves that no worthwhile scenario exists. The anchor survives the basic modeling test; the case against it is that it seems too externally constructed and too weakly connected to the paper’s actual computational content to warrant ChoCo’s time.
