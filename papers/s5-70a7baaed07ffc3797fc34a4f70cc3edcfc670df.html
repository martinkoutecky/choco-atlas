The continuous mirror question

For a fixed finite type set \(T\) with known masses \(\mu_t\), an unknown binary prior, and unknown type-specific signal channels \(P_t(s\mid\omega)\), suppose each task reveals the exact population report measure \(\nu\) induced by conditionally independent signals and the realized \(\omega\). What is the minimax sample complexity \(N(\varepsilon,\delta)\) for using \(N\) i.i.d. task samples to return \(F\) such that, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), \(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(F(\nu)-f^*(\nu))^2]\le\varepsilon\), where \(f^*(\nu)=P(\omega=1\mid\nu)\)?

The model it lives in

A continuum pool has finitely many expert types \(T\), shares \(\mu_t\), and conditionally independent signal channels \(P_t(\cdot\mid\omega)\). An individual of type \(t\) reports \(r=P(\omega=1\mid s,t)\); the population report measure given outcome \(\omega\) is \(\nu_\omega(B)=\sum_t\mu_t P(r\in B\mid t,\omega)\). From i.i.d. historical pairs \((\nu,\omega)\), learn a rule \(F(\nu)\) whose expected squared difference from \(P(\omega=1\mid\nu)\) is at most \(\varepsilon\) with confidence \(1-\delta\).

The objection that survived

Conditional independence makes the exact population report measure deterministic given ω, removing the within-task variation in finite report vectors; the resulting sample bounds need not transfer, which limits coverage.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the conditionally independent learning problem in Theorem 5.2. It leaves the arbitrary-correlation regime of Theorem 4.1 and the specialized signal conditions in Theorems 5.4 and 5.5 outside its claim.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the tight minimax dependence on \(\varepsilon\) and \(\delta\), including the case where \(\nu_0=\nu_1\)?
  • How does finite-population sampling error change the bound as the type multiplicities grow?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a population version of Bayesian forecast aggregation under conditional independence. Picture a large forecasting pool with a fixed set \(T\) of expert types and population shares \(\mu_t\). A type specifies the expert’s signal experiment and reporting rule; members of the same type are interchangeable, while their signals may differ. For each forecasting task, the event is \(\omega\in\{0,1\}\). The principal observes the population report measure \(\nu\) on \(T\times[0,1]\), where \(\nu(\{t\}\times B)\) is the mass of type \(t\) reporting a value in \(B\), and also observes the event’s realized outcome. In the continuum limit, conditional independence makes this report measure the type-by-type distribution of reports for that outcome. From i.i.d. historical task samples \((\nu,\omega)\), the principal must learn a rule \(F(\nu)\in[0,1]\) whose expected squared prediction error is within \(\varepsilon\) of the Bayesian rule \(f^*(\nu)=\Pr(\omega=1\mid\nu)\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\). The type masses are the decision input; the action is the forecast \(F\).

The anchor is Theorem 5.2, proved in this paper. For \(n\ge2\) experts whose signals are independent conditioned on the event, it proves that the number of historical samples needed to learn an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal aggregator is at least \(\Omega(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(1/\delta))\) and at most \(O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/(\varepsilon\delta)))\). The theorem concerns sample complexity, not runtime or NP-hardness. In the mirror, the exact type-level report measure is the sufficient aggregate of an exchangeable cohort: under conditional independence, the likelihood depends on reports through their type-level frequencies, not the names of individual experts. So this preserves the paper’s Bayesian aggregation question while replacing integer headcounts with masses.

I expect this continuous problem to be tractable. In the ideal population limit, each event value induces a fixed report measure, so the task-level inputs have at most two possible profiles. Learning then amounts to learning the posterior over those profiles; the sample requirement should depend on \(\varepsilon,\delta\), not on the number of people. A natural follow-up is whether the sharp bound is \(O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(1/\delta))\), as in the paper’s lower bound and its more informative special cases, and how finite-population sampling error changes the answer. This case covers Theorem 5.2’s conditionally independent regime; it does not claim to mirror the arbitrary-correlation result of Theorem 4.1.

The soft spot is that the continuum removes within-task sampling noise: the report measure is exact, whereas the paper’s samples contain finite vectors of individual reports. That may make the mirror easier than the authors’ problem, even though it retains the same Bayesian benchmark and aggregation objective. Also, if “computational result” is meant strictly as a runtime or hardness result, the paper has no qualifying anchor: its numbered complexity results concern sample complexity. The positive case is therefore a statistical high-multiplicity mirror, and its fit to a programme focused narrowly on algorithmic complexity is limited.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 5.2, and it has a real population interpretation: a large forecasting pool with many experts sharing a finite set of signal types is plausible. Under conditional independence, the report measure within each type is a sufficient aggregate. I would not reject that mirror on the ground that experts lack multiplicity.

Its weakness for ChoCo is that Theorem 5.2 measures how many historical samples are needed, not the time or resources needed to compute an answer from a society. The paper gives no runtime or algorithmic-complexity result of the kind ChoCo is charting. Its continuous mirror could be valuable in statistical learning, but it does not supply the computational question this programme is set up to classify.

The population version also changes what each historical sample contains. With a fixed type distribution and conditionally independent signals, the exact report measure becomes deterministic given the binary event: there are at most two possible population profiles. If the profiles differ, observing the measure identifies the event; if they coincide, the reports add no information and the predictor is just the prior. The finite-vector aggregation problem has collapsed to learning the labels of at most two profiles, or estimating a binary prior. To retain within-task sampling noise, one must specify finite resolution or another source of population-level variation. That makes a coherent new learning problem, but its data model and computational costs are no longer supplied by Theorem 5.2.

So the case against is strong as a claim about fit to ChoCo’s algorithmic agenda: the best mirror is a statistical-learning question, and its exact continuum version largely removes the report-aggregation problem. It is weak as the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The finite-type forecasting pool is sensible, and if ChoCo includes sample complexity among its targets, this remains a defensible mirror.
