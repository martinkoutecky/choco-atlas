The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for the paper’s coalition-probability calculation, but there is a firm limit: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Table 1 reports that, under the impartial coalition assumption, the two-thirds threshold’s decision probability falls from 1.2% with 51 members to 0.032% with 101 and 0.000002% with 193, while the simple-majority figure stays at 50%. Those are numerical calculations, not a qualifying complexity anchor. So there is no paper result I can honestly name as an anchor or restate as an algorithmic classification.

A plausible, unanchored lead suggested by those calculations is the *High-Multiplicity Coalition-Tail Rate*. An instance consists of a finite set of member types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a rational affirmative-vote probability \(p_t\) for each type, and a quota \(q\). At population scale \(N\), there are \(N\mu_t\) members of type \(t\), each voting independently with probability \(p_t\). The question is to compute the exponential decay rate of the probability that at least a \(q\)-fraction vote yes, and return the type-level support profile that achieves that rate. In the large-membership regime \(N\gg |T|\), the continuous society is \(\mu\). For \(q>\sum_t\mu_t p_t\), the rate is

\[ \min_{\substack{z_t\in[0,1]\\ \sum_t\mu_t z_t\ge q}} \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t D(z_t\|p_t), \qquad D(z\|p)=z\ln\frac{z}{p}+(1-z)\ln\frac{1-z}{1-p}. \]

For a quota below the expected affirmative share, the rate is zero. The vector \(z\) describes the most likely type-level voting fractions among successful coalitions. Under the paper’s impartial-coalition assumption, \(p_t=1/2\); at \(q=2/3\), this becomes a convex optimization problem with a single effective type. I would expect this rate-computation problem to be tractable. Distinguished veto players could be kept as a fixed finite set, with their approval requirement handled separately.

The regime that makes this credible is a very large federation or membership assembly with many comparable voting units and relatively few complete voting-propensity types. The paper’s 193 sovereign states are a poor instance of that regime: each has distinct political circumstances, and the P-5 have special veto status. The proposed question covers only the coalition-probability calculations in Section 3, not the paper’s broader account of path dependence or UNSC reform. Its weakest point is that it changes the paper’s finite probability calculation into an asymptotic rate problem. That is a sensible continuation of the calculation, but the paper neither asks that question nor establishes a computational result about it; under the requested anchor rule, this remains a research lead rather than an anchored mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. It develops a theoretical explanation for institutional inertia and reports numerical decision-probability calculations, especially in Table 1. It gives no theorem or algorithmic classification for a computational problem. So there is no paper result that can serve as a formal anchor; the other analyst also acknowledges this.

The closest candidate is the proposed High-Multiplicity Coalition-Tail Rate. It is coherent, but it changes the object under study: the paper calculates the finite Coleman probability of a random coalition meeting a quota, while the proposed question asks for the large-population exponential rate of a threshold event among repeated voter-propensity types. That is a useful continuation of the calculation, not a mirror of a complexity result in this paper. For the UN itself, the type assumption is especially thin: the states are not a repeated population of interchangeable units, and the ICC assumption deliberately abstracts from their preferences and behavior.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the proposal. A large federation with many voters in a few voting-propensity classes could be a sensible high-multiplicity society; a fixed set of veto players could be modeled separately. In that setting, the rate question would be computational and the continuous population would be central. Calling it uninteresting merely because the model differs from the UN, or because its answer might be tractable, would not be a sound case against it.

So the negative case is strong against an *anchored mirror of this paper*, but weak against the requested universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The rate problem remains a plausible research lead; it just is not a computational result of this paper being continuized.
