The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) supported types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), strict rankings \(\succ_t\) over acceptable partner types, and mutually acceptable pairs, with each type listing at most three partner types, minimize blocking-pair mass over feasible matching masses. For \(t\ne u\), let \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\ge0\) be the mass paired across types, and let \(x_{tt}\ge0\) be the mass of within-type pairs; require \(2x_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}\le\mu_t\). Define \(y_{tv}=x_{tv}\) for \(v\ne t\) and \(y_{tt}=2x_{tt}\), and \(q_{t,u}=\mu_t-\sum_{v:\,v\succeq_t u}y_{tv}\), the mass unmatched or partnered with a type ranked below \(u\). Minimize \(B(x)=\sum_{\{t,u\}:t\ne u}q_{t,u}q_{u,t}+\frac12\sum_{t:\{t,t\}\text{ acceptable}}q_{t,t}^2\), where \(v\succeq_t u\) means \(t\) ranks \(v\) at least as highly as \(u\).

The corrected capacity constraint counts both agents in a same-type pair, and the willingness measure counts only agents unmatched or assigned to a less-preferred type.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 1, and the mirror I would defend is a type-based version of minimizing blocking pairs in the roommates problem.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, states that for every \(\delta\) with \(0<\delta\le 1/10^{16}\), \(\min bp\ 3\text{-sri}\) is NP-hard to approximate within \(1017/1016-\delta\), unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). The proof reduces from \(\max (2,2)\text{-e3-sat}\): for the constructed instance \(I\) and formula \(B\), it establishes \(t(B)+bp(I)=2m\), then uses the gap result cited as [2]. Thus the hardness comes from the variable-clause structure represented in the roommate instance.

Here is a continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Type-Mass Minimum Blocking Roommates with three acceptable types. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of supported types, with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) has a strict ranking of at most three acceptable partner types; acceptability is mutual. Everyone in a type has the same ranking over partner types, and any two people from mutually acceptable types can be paired. A type may also find its own type acceptable.

The population is nonatomic: type \(t\) occupies mass \(\mu_t\). A solution is a symmetric matching matrix \(x\), where \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\ge 0\) is the mass of type-\(t\) people paired with type-\(u\) people. It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}+x_{tt}\le \mu_t. \]
Unmatched mass is allowed. For an acceptable pair of distinct types \(t,u\), let
\[ q_{t,u}(x)=\mu_t-\sum_{v:\,u\succeq_t v}x_{tv}, \]
where \(u\succeq_t v\) means type \(t\) ranks \(u\) at least as highly as \(v\). This is the mass of type \(t\) that is unmatched or would prefer a type-\(u\) partner to its current partner. Define the blocking-pair mass as
\[ B(x)=\sum_{\{t,u\}\in E,\ t\ne u}q_{t,u}(x)q_{u,t}(x) +\frac12\sum_{t:\{t,t\}\in E}q_{t,t}(x)^2. \]
The first term measures mutually willing pairs across two types; the second does the same within a type. The question is to minimize \(B(x)\). Equivalently, given rational \(K\), decide whether a feasible matching exists with \(B(x)\le K\). The matrix is a complete description of the outcome: because the population is nonatomic, every feasible matrix can be realized by pairing the indicated masses.

The scenario is a large residential programme where many residents share a small number \(\tau\) of complete compatibility profiles—say, profiles that determine which other profiles they will room with and how they rank them. The population can be much larger than \(\tau\); mass means the fraction of residents in each profile. The model preserves the paper’s central objective, minimization of instability, while asking whether the optimization changes when agents are interchangeable within types.

My expectation is continuum-specific hardness (Class C), though that is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 1. The finite-dimensional objective above has products of mutually willing masses, so the resulting optimization is nonconvex. The reduction’s clause and variable structure also suggests that combinatorics need not disappear when population mass becomes divisible. The key open question is whether fractional mass assignments dissolve those gadgets or leave a hard core. Further questions include exact complexity, approximation guarantees, and whether fixed \(\tau\) or stronger type restrictions make the problem tractable.

The authors could recognize this as a continuous version of their “minimize blocking pairs” question in a cohort setting: the assignment and the definition of a block remain central, while the outcome is measured over a population distribution. This covers Theorem 1’s optimization objective; it does not mirror the paper’s other results or claim to cover every SRI instance.

My weakest point is substantial: Theorem 1 bounds each person’s list of individuals, whereas this mirror bounds acceptable types. A type can contain many people, so the induced individual-level acceptability graph can be dense. Preferences are also indifferent between members of the same type, unlike the paper’s strict individual rankings. That is plausible for compatibility-profile assignments, but it means Theorem 1’s hardness gap does not automatically transfer, and a referee requiring a literal high-multiplicity relaxation of bounded-list SRI might reject this as too much of a variant. I still think the mirror is worth asking: it keeps the paper’s underlying instability-minimization problem and makes the population, rather than the outcome, the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that Theorem 1’s hardness is carried by a sparse graph of named relationships. Its agents have at most three acceptable partners, and the reduction encodes variable–clause occurrences in specific edges. A distribution over preference types records how much population has each profile; it does not record which particular agents are connected.

That gap matters when the population is replicated. If a type stands for a gadget role, the type-mass model makes an acceptable relationship between two roles available across all members of both types. The lifted graph then has \(\Theta(N^2)\) possible pairs where \(N\) copies of the construction have only \(\Theta(N)\) edges. Those are different matching and blocking-pair problems. Preserving the original wiring requires copy or edge information. Encoding each copy in the type makes the support grow with \(N\); supplying the wiring separately changes the input from a distribution over types to a sparse graph or graphing with its own representation and complexity questions.

The proposed model illustrates the change. Its partners are types, so a resident who accepts one type can accept many individual residents as the population grows. That is a sensible dense, category-based roommate model, but its lists are not bounded by three individuals as in Theorem 1. Its blocking-pair mass, built from products \(q_{t,u}q_{u,t}\), counts cross-type opportunities in that dense population. It does not scale the paper’s sparse blocking-edge count. There is also a feasibility correction for same-type matches: if \(x_{tt}\) measures pairs, capacity requires \(2x_{tt}+\sum_{u\ne t}x_{tu}\le\mu_t\).

The best rescue for a direct mirror would retain a sparse, measured relation between agents while taking population masses. That may be a worthwhile research direction, but the relation and its encoding would then carry the combinatorics that type masses omit. The paper’s reduction gives no reason to expect a compact, fixed-support mass model to preserve those relationships. This is a representational objection to mirroring its bounded-list result; it is not an objection that hardness might survive, or that a different answer would make the question uninteresting.

I cannot honestly make the universal case that no continuous mirror is worthwhile. The cohort model in the proponent’s case remains a plausible computational problem the authors might recognize. My negative case is strongest against treating it as a continuization of Theorem 1: it changes sparse individual-level acceptability into dense type-level acceptability. It is weak against the broader claim that type-based roommate matching deserves continuous complexity analysis.
