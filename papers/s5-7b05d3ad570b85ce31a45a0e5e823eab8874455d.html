The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) over finitely many complete additive utility types \(t\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\) of \(m\) object types, and unit equal incomes, let \(v_t^\star\) be the unique type utilities in the corresponding fractional Eisenberg–Gale market, defined by maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t\log v_t\) subject to \(v_t=\sum_j u_{tj}y_{tj}\), \(\sum_t\mu_t y_{tj}\le q_j\), and \(y_{tj}\ge0\). Decide whether there are masses \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\) such that \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\sum_{B:j\in B}x_{t,B}=q_j\), and \(x_{t,B}>0\) only when \(\sum_{j\in B}u_{tj}=v_t^\star\). Each person receives a whole bundle, while each type’s mass may split among bundles; define the computational version with exact representation and comparison of the benchmark utilities.

The model it lives in

A finite set of utility types carries masses \(\mu_t\); each type has a rational additive utility vector, and object types have per-capita supplies \(q_j\). The fractional equal-income market determines target utilities \(v_t^\star\). The decision variables \(x_{t,B}\) allocate type mass among whole bundles, with exact supply clearing and each allocated bundle attaining its type’s target utility.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5’s existence question for discrete CEEI-FRAC assignments. It leaves Theorems 1–4, Lemma 1, and Corollary 1 outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does exact bundle pricing over bundles satisfying \(\sum_{j\in B}u_{tj}=v_t^\star\) admit a polynomial-time method, or yield hardness for natural utility domains?
  • What exact finite-bit representation of the Eisenberg–Gale utility targets gives the intended complexity measure?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Exact-target CEEI-FRAC mass assignment. It keeps each person’s bundle indivisible while making the population a distribution over complete utility types.

The anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: deciding whether a discrete assignment exists that gives every agent the utility they receive in a fractional CEEI assignment is strongly NP-complete. The paper proves hardness by reduction from strongly NP-complete 3-Partition. Given \(n\) agents and \(3n\) objects with weights \(w_j\) satisfying \(W/4<w_j<W/2\) and \(\sum_j w_j=nW\), every agent has the same additive utility \(u_j=w_j\) for object \(j\). The fractional CEEI utility of each agent is \(W\). Thus a discrete assignment reaches those utilities exactly when the objects can be partitioned into \(n\) groups, each of weight \(W\). The paper’s polynomial-time verification result supplies membership in NP. Theorem 5 is a particularly useful anchor because its hard question is exactly whether indivisible bundles can attain a fractional-market utility benchmark.

Here is the continuous version I would ask a prover to classify. Let an atomless population of total mass \(1\) have a finite support \(T\) of utility types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A type \(t\) is its complete additive utility vector \(u_t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^m\). There are \(m\) individually indivisible objects; object \(j\) has aggregate supply \(q_j\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), interpreted as the fraction of the population that could receive its one copy. For a finite market of \(N\) agents with one copy of an object, the corresponding supply is \(q_j=1/N\).

Let \(v_t^\star\) be the unique type utilities in the fractional equal-income market with unit budgets and these supplies. Equivalently, they are the utilities at the optimum of the Eisenberg–Gale program
\[ \max \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log v_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad v_t=\sum_{j=1}^m u_{tj}y_{tj},\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t y_{tj}\le q_j,\qquad y_{tj}\ge0. \]
The question is whether there are nonnegative masses \(x_{t,B}\), one for each type \(t\) and integral bundle \(B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\), such that
\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t}\sum_{B:j\in B}x_{t,B}=q_j, \]
and \(x_{t,B}>0\) only if
\[ \sum_{j\in B}u_{tj}=v_t^\star. \]
A solution is such a mass assignment: almost every person receives a whole, integral bundle and exactly their type’s fractional-CEEI utility, while the object supplies clear. The population may split across bundles; an individual may not receive a fractional bundle. The problem should also specify how the exact representation of \(v^\star\) is handled.

A plausible regime is a large university course-allocation market. The agents are students, types are repeated complete valuation profiles over the available seats, and the population is much larger than the number \(\tau=|T|\) of types. Equal budgets and indivisible seat assignments are already native to the paper’s market model. The reduction itself has just one utility type, repeated across \(n\) agents, so small support is not an artificial feature of the mirror.

I expect this problem to be continuum-specifically hard (Class C), provisionally. Its configuration LP has a column for each integral bundle attaining \(v_t^\star\); a pricing step would need to optimize a linear cost over bundles satisfying an exact utility equation, a knapsack-like problem. But Theorem 5’s hardness does not establish hardness of this mass version. In its 3-Partition construction, eligible bundles must be triples summing to \(W\). There are only \(O(m^3)\) such bundles to enumerate, after which the continuous question is a fractional perfect-matching LP. Mass splitting can therefore erase the integral partition obstruction that proves the discrete result. Whether the general pricing structure gives a new hardness result, or admits a tractable characterization, is the actual complexity question.

The mirror is plausible because it asks the same fairness question as Theorem 5: can everyone receive their fractional-market target utility while keeping each person’s bundle discrete? It changes the population representation to cohort shares and allows those shares to divide among integral bundles. A useful bridge to prove is that a rational mass solution becomes a finite high-multiplicity allocation whenever all its masses and supplies are scaled to integers; conversely, every such finite allocation gives a mass solution.

The weakest point is that allowing arbitrary mass splits removes the finite counting constraint that drives Theorem 5’s reduction. That is a real change in the computational problem, though it preserves indivisible individual outcomes and the paper’s CEEI-FRAC benchmark. Further questions are whether exact-target bundle pricing is hard for natural utility domains, whether restrictions such as \(0\)-\(1\) utilities yield a polynomial algorithm, and how finite markets approximate a feasible mass solution when the cohort size cannot realize its exact rational masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 5’s reduction does not carry over to the proposed mass assignment. In the 3-Partition construction, every bundle of utility \(W\) contains exactly three objects. The mass version becomes a fractional perfect-matching problem on those triples, which can be written as an explicit LP after enumerating \(O(m^3)\) triples. The theorem’s integral partition argument therefore gives no hardness result for the mirror. The proposed general bundle-pricing problem might still be hard, but that needs its own analysis.

There is also a modeling fork. If the objects remain unique physical items while the population becomes atomless, each item has zero per-capita supply, so it cannot support positive mass receiving a bundle. Preserving per-capita supply requires scaling or replicating goods. That is a meaningful co-modification for settings such as course allocation, but it means the mirror depends on adopting a repeated-goods regime; it does not follow directly from the paper’s one-off object instances.

Those points weaken the claim that Theorem 5’s hardness itself motivates the continuous problem. They do not defeat the proposed question. Its course-allocation setting is plausible, and checking whether a mass of people can receive whole bundles at their fractional-CEEI target remains a computational question about a continuous population. The fractional equilibrium computation alone does not answer it. I cannot make an honest case that no worthwhile mirror exists; the negative case only establishes that this particular paper’s reduction is not evidence that its mirror is hard.
