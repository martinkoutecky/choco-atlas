The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates, committee size \(k\), and a unit-mass distribution \(\mu\) over approval types \(t\subseteq C\), represented by \(\tau\) positive binary-rational masses, can a deterministic anonymous rule compute a size-\(k\) committee in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) and satisfy both of these conditions? First, continuum JR: for every \(x_t\le\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t x_t\ge1/k\), if all types with positive \(x_t\) share an approved candidate, the committee contains a candidate approved by at least one such type. Second, for every decomposition into known masses, a designated focal atom of mass \(\epsilon\) and true type \(T\), and unknown mass \(\rho\), no report \(R\) is both safe across every allocation of the unknown mass and profitable for at least one allocation whenever \(\rho\ge7\epsilon\). A report transfers the focal mass from \(T\) to \(R\), and utility is \(|W\cap T|\).

The model it lives in

The rule receives only the complete type-mass vector and returns a size-\(k\) committee; the focal atom is designated for evaluating incentives, not as a voter identity the rule can use. A deviation transfers \(\epsilon\) mass from approval type \(T\) to \(R\), while unknown mass \(\rho\) ranges over all distributions on approval types. Require continuum JR at quota \(1/k\), and rule out reports that weakly improve utility in every completion and strictly improve it in one when \(\rho\ge7\epsilon\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5.25’s polynomial-time construction and its \(n-7\) RAT guarantee; it leaves the impossibility result, the bounds for GreedyAV, PAV, and MES, the finite construction’s \(n-5\) guarantee, and the immutable-ID result aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a rule with the stated JR and RAT guarantees exist with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) for arbitrary binary-rational masses?
  • Does the \(7\epsilon\) guarantee hold for unrestricted fractional completions, rather than only populations on a common finite grid?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive anchor is Theorem 5.25, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(n\ge1\) and \(1\le k\le m\), the authors construct an anonymous mechanism that, on a list of \(n\) approval ballots, runs in polynomial time, always selects exactly \(k\) candidates, satisfies JR, and has RAT-Degree at least \(n-7\). The paper gives no NP-hardness result to anchor a continuization; I would not use Theorem 3.2 for that purpose, since it is an impossibility theorem rather than a complexity result.

Here is a continuous-population question I would hand to a prover. Call it Compressed-Mass JR with Seven-Unit RAT Resistance. There are \(m\) candidates, committee size \(k\), and a unit-mass society over approval types \(t\subseteq C\). Its distribution is given by the \(\tau\) positive masses \(p_t\), encoded as binary rationals. The mechanism must select a size-\(k\) committee from that distribution. It must satisfy continuum JR: for every vector of masses \(x_t\le p_t\) with \(\sum_t x_t\ge1/k\), if all types with \(x_t>0\) share an approved candidate, the committee must include a candidate approved by at least one of those types.

For the incentive condition, designate one focal voter of mass \(\epsilon>0\) and true approval type \(T\). The remaining mass is split into known type masses and an unknown mass \(\rho\). A report \(R\) moves the focal mass from \(T\) to \(R\); a completion assigns the unknown mass across approval types. The report is safe if, for every completion, it gives the focal voter no fewer approved committee members than truth does. It is profitable if it gives strictly more for at least one completion. The question is whether there is an anonymous deterministic rule computable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) that satisfies continuum JR and admits no safe, profitable report whenever \(\rho\ge7\epsilon\). Here \(L\) is the input bit length. Its output is a committee, and its solution is an algorithm for producing that committee on every permitted mass vector.

The seven-unit threshold is the direct mass analogue of the theorem’s guarantee: when \(\epsilon=1/n\) and all masses lie on the \(1/n\) grid, \(\rho\ge7\epsilon\) means at least seven other voters are unknown. Theorem 5.25 rules out a safe profitable report in that case. In a high-multiplicity regime—many voters, relatively few distinct approval ballots—representing the electorate by type masses is natural, and the paper’s own anonymous mechanism already depends only on the ballot multiset. That makes this a plausible extension of the authors’ problem, rather than a change to fractional outcomes or probabilistic profiles.

I would expect the compressed-mass version to be tractable, though that is a conjectural direction, not a consequence of Theorem 5.25. Its finite construction provides a concrete starting point: the challenge is to preserve its JR and information-robustness guarantees while computing from binary-encoded type masses rather than expanding them into a list of voters. Further questions are whether the guarantee can reach \(n-5\) or the full \(n-1\), whether it survives arbitrary fractional completions rather than just grid-aligned populations, and whether the compressed algorithm can avoid running time polynomial in the expanded population size.

My weakest point is that the theorem preserves *individual* manipulation: the focal voter has mass \(\epsilon\), so her report changes the distribution by that amount. A truly nonatomic voter has mass zero and cannot affect a mechanism determined only by the distribution; strategyproofness would then become vacuous. Keeping a focal atom is a deliberate co-modification that preserves the paper’s strategic question while making the rest of society a mass distribution. The paper’s theorem supports the grid-aligned special case; the unrestricted fractional-mass problem is the proposed mirror, not an already established result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.25 is the only anchor raised, and the strongest objection is that its strategic subject disappears in a genuinely nonatomic society. A unilateral voter has mass zero, so changing that voter’s report leaves the distribution \(\mu\) unchanged. Any rule whose input is only \(\mu\) must return the same committee, making profitable individual manipulation impossible by construction. That would make the theorem’s RAT guarantee vacuous in a pure continuum.

Giving the focal voter mass \(\epsilon>0\) preserves the incentive question, but leaves one atom in the model. If \(\epsilon=1/n\), the theorem’s seven-unknown-voter threshold becomes \(7/n\), which vanishes as the population grows. If \(\epsilon\) stays fixed, the focal voter remains a positive share of society; the model is then an atom interacting with a continuum, rather than a limit in which the population itself becomes nonatomic. A positive-mass bloc would preserve influence too, but would turn unilateral RAT into a coalition-manipulation question.

That objection does not defeat the best mirror, though. Treat \(\epsilon\) as the mass of one focal agent, describe everyone else by rational masses over approval types, and measure uncertainty relative to \(\epsilon\). Ask whether a rule can be computed from the compressed mass representation, satisfy JR at quota \(1/k\), and resist safe profitable reports whenever the unknown mass is at least \(7\epsilon\). On mass vectors with denominator \(n\), this recovers the finite unit-voter scale. Theorem 5.25 does not answer the compressed-input question: polynomial time on an explicit list of \(n\) ballots need not be polynomial in the bit length of rational masses. Finding out whether such a succinct rule exists is a real computational question.

So the negative case narrows the interpretation: there is no nontrivial pure atomless analogue of this individual-manipulation theorem. But the focal-atom version is a plausible high-multiplicity mirror, and its compressed computation is not already settled by the paper. I cannot honestly argue that this anchor fails in every reasonable scenario; the case against it is weak at precisely that boundary.
