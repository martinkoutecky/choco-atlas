The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many complete operator types \(t\in\Theta_+\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), and each type's service-unit and subscriber classes, rates, SLA minima, and concave utility, define \(v(q)\) by the paper's pooling optimization with capacities and subscriber obligations scaled by coalition mass \(q_t\). For rational piecewise-linear concave utilities, compute per-provider payouts \(p_t\) such that \(\sum_t \mu_t p_t=v(\mu)\) and \(\sum_t q_t p_t\ge v(q)\) for every \(q\in[0,\mu]\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named hardness result. Its strongest computational anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved here using techniques from cited work. The theorem says that, when the grand-coalition optimization \(P(N)\) is feasible, the set \(O\) of allocations obtained from optimal solutions of its dual \(D(N)\) is nonempty and lies inside the core \(C\). Specifically, for any optimal dual solution \((\lambda^*,\beta^*,\gamma^*,\tau^*)\), the allocation
\[ x_i^*=f_i(\gamma^*,\tau^*)+ \sum_{j\in B_i}\lambda_j^*+ \sum_{k\in M_i}(\beta_k^*-\tau_k^*m_k) \]
sums to the grand coalition’s revenue, and every coalition \(S\) receives at least \(v(S)\) in total. The discussion following the theorem gives the algorithmic consequence: \(D(N)\) has \(O(B+M)\) variables and \(O(BM)\) constraints, and the authors claim that solving it and producing such an allocation takes time polynomial in \(B,M,n\). The theorem’s statement is about core existence and containment; the polynomial-time claim is made in the surrounding discussion.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a pooling consortium of many small regional wireless providers, community networks, or virtual operators. Some may have repeated network and subscriber templates: the same kinds of service units, subscriber and SLA classes, utility function, and coverage relationships. Let \(\Theta_+\) be the \(\tau\) operator types present, with mass \(\mu_t\) for type \(t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type is a complete per-operator bundle, including its service units, subscriber classes, rates to every other type’s subscriber classes, minimum-rate guarantees, and utility. The intended regime has many providers but relatively few repeated bundles. This is a scenario for the paper’s operator-coalition problem, rather than a claim that a handful of national carriers form a useful continuum.

I would call the mirror Mass-Core Spectrum Pooling. Given the type data and binary-rational masses, a coalition is any measurable subset of providers, described by a mass vector \(q\) with \(0\le q_t\le\mu_t\). Define its value \(v(q)\) by the paper’s pooling optimization at type level. For example, let \(r\) index service-unit classes and \(k\) subscriber classes within each operator type. The variable \(a_{tr,uk}\ge0\) is the aggregate service time assigned from units of class \(r\) owned by type-\(t\) providers to subscribers of class \(k\) owned by type-\(u\) providers. Let \(z_{uk}\) be the aggregate rate delivered to that subscriber class. For a coalition containing mass \(q\), impose
\[ \sum_{u,k}a_{tr,uk}\le q_t,\qquad \sum_{t,r}a_{tr,uk}\le q_u, \]
\[ z_{uk}\le\sum_{t,r}R_{tr,uk}a_{tr,uk},\qquad z_{uk}\ge q_u m_{uk}. \]
Only service units and subscribers in the coalition are included. If each operator of type \(u\) has the same concave utility \(U_u\) over its subscriber-class rates, set
\[ v(q)=\max\sum_u q_u\,U_u\!\left((z_{uk}/q_u)_k\right), \]
with zero-mass terms omitted. This type-level form represents symmetric average rates; concavity lets identical operators be treated by their average allocation.

The question is: given this instance, find a per-provider revenue rate \(p_t\) for each type such that
\[ \sum_t\mu_t p_t=v(\mu) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t q_t p_t\ge v(q)\ \ \text{for every }q\in[0,\mu]. \]
The output is one type-uniform core allocation: each provider of type \(t\) receives \(p_t\), and no coalition of provider mass can improve on its total payout by separating and pooling independently. The continuum creates infinitely many coalition constraints, so the task is to produce a payout vector that satisfies them all. For a concrete exact-complexity version, the utilities can be given as rational, piecewise-linear concave functions.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. The paper’s dual-certificate argument appears to extend: solve the grand-coalition type-level dual and use its per-unit dual contribution as \(p_t\). Restricting that certificate to any coalition mass \(q\) should give the required lower bound on \(\sum_tq_tp_t\). With finitely many supported types and explicitly represented per-type bundles, the piecewise-linear version should reduce to a finite linear program. The further questions are whether this certificate works for the paper’s full utility class, and whether the trust-graph extension of Theorem 4.1 admits the same mass-level treatment.

This mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s core-existence and core-allocation result; it does not attempt to mirror the paper’s results on the Shapley value, SLA selection, or the full core. Its weakest point is the regime assumption. A type must include network and contract details that can make nominally similar providers distinct, and in actual pooling arrangements there may be only a few large operators. Fractional coalition mass also idealizes providers as divisible in the limit. So this is a credible repeated-provider regime, but a narrower one than the paper’s main national-carrier setting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.1 is the one serious anchor: it proves core existence and gives a polynomial-time way to find a core allocation. The objection has to be about whether its players have a sensible high-multiplicity regime.

The theorem indexes coalitions by operators. Subscribers appear inside each operator’s utility and rate constraints; continuizing subscriber mass would leave the theorem’s coalition problem finite. Continuizing operators requires many interchangeable, independently acting firms. A complete type would have to capture each firm’s assets, users, service agreements, utility, and its service rates to every relevant subscriber class. In the paper, those rates depend on locations and channel conditions. Repeated business templates alone therefore do not establish repeated types; the firms’ full network relationships must repeat too. In ordinary telecom settings, there are few license-holding operators. Many small providers tend to serve separate areas, where pooling across areas offers little interaction and the game splits into local games.

The proponent’s standardized consortium is the strongest reply. It is coherent to posit many independent community networks with repeated assets and symmetric cross-network rates. That assumption would make an operator-mass core a recognizable version of the paper’s problem. Its cleanest one-type case, however, scales each coalition’s service capacity and subscriber obligations together. Then the worth scales as \(v(q)=qv(1)\), and the type’s core payout is fixed at \(p=v(1)\). A substantive pooling question needs multiple complementary types, which restores the need for carefully repeated cross-type network relationships. This narrows the natural regime; it does not rule it out.

The computational payoff also looks modest: the paper already computes a core allocation in polynomial time, and its dual proof is structured to assign contributions to operators. A mass version may be a useful limit formulation, though the proposed extension appears close to replacing finite sums with type-mass terms. That observation alone does not disqualify the question; it does make this a less compelling complexity-landscape case than a mirror that could expose a barrier caused by population multiplicity.

So the negative case is strongest on naturalness: the paper’s actual agents are firms whose network interactions are highly specific, while a useful continuum needs many firms with repeated, connected network structures. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile scenario exists. Under the proponent’s consortium assumptions, this mirror survives, and it is the only anchor raised.
