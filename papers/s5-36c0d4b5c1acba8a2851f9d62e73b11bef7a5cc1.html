The continuous mirror question

For rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) and rational \(\epsilon>0\), let \(D_\alpha=[0,\alpha]\times[0,1-\alpha]\), and let \(\mathcal G_\alpha\) be the measurable upward-closed winning regions \(W\subseteq D_\alpha\) that exclude \((0,0)\), include \((\alpha,1-\alpha)\), and make each coordinate relevant. With \(d(W,V)=\lambda_2(W\triangle V)/[\alpha(1-\alpha)]\), determine the minimum size \(N_2(\alpha,\epsilon)\) of a finite family from \(\mathcal G_\alpha\) whose \(\epsilon\)-balls cover \(\mathcal G_\alpha\); when \(\alpha=1/2\), take the cover on the quotient identifying rules that differ by swapping the two types.

The model it lives in

Two agent types have masses \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\); a coalition is represented by its mass pair \((x,y)\), and its binary decision rule is an upward-closed winning region. The computational object is a smallest finite cover of these rules under normalized area disagreement.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow. The paper gives no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or similar. Its best computational anchor is an exact enumeration theorem, which can motivate a continuous counting problem, though that mirror must change exact counting into approximate enumeration.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. For every \(n\ge2\), it gives the number of isomorphism classes of simple games with \(n\) players and exactly two equivalence classes: \(2^{n+1}-\frac{n^2+3n+4}{2}\) when \(n\) is odd, and \(2^{n+1}+2^{n/2}-\frac{n^2+4n+6}{2}\) when \(n\) is even. This is the paper’s strongest anchor because it explicitly counts the objects the authors study. It is an enumeration result, rather than a complexity classification.

My lead mirror would be Two-Type Continuous Simple-Game Entropy. Consider a large atomless membership with two voter types, A and B, of masses \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\), where \(0<\alpha<1\). Members vote yes or no on a proposal. A coalition is represented by its support masses \((x,y)\in D_\alpha=[0,\alpha]\times[0,1-\alpha]\). A game is a measurable upward-closed winning region \(W\subseteq D_\alpha\), with \((0,0)\) losing and \((\alpha,1-\alpha)\) winning. Require both coordinates to matter, so the two types are genuinely distinct in the rule.

For rational \(\alpha\) and rational tolerance \(\epsilon>0\), ask for the minimum number \(N_2(\alpha,\epsilon)\) of such games whose \(\epsilon\)-neighborhoods cover all two-type games, using normalized disagreement area \(d(W,W')=\lambda_2(W\triangle W')/(\alpha(1-\alpha))\). At \(\alpha=1/2\), identify games that differ only by swapping the two types. A solution is an optimal finite family of games, together with a proof that it covers the whole class and that no smaller family suffices. This asks for a finite, approximate catalogue of continuous population rules.

The regime is a large union, cooperative, or membership organization with two constitutional classes. Members within a class have identical voting rights and costs; the population may be very large while the number of types stays at two. The paper itself supports the plausibility of this regime: its subject is already games with two equivalence classes. In the mirror, class masses replace player counts, and a rule’s boundary separates winning from losing support proportions.

I would initially expect the fixed two-type problem to be tractable, perhaps with running time polynomial in \(1/\epsilon\) and the input bit length. Each game has a monotone boundary in two dimensions, so the approximation problem reduces to covering a family of one-dimensional monotone frontiers. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a result of this paper. Further questions include the sharp growth of \(\log N_2(\alpha,\epsilon)\) as \(\epsilon\) shrinks, how it varies with \(\alpha\), and how the finite enumeration in Theorem 2 relates to these covering numbers.

This mirror covers the paper’s enumeration of two-class games. It does not continuize the paper’s other structural statements, and it does not preserve Theorem 2’s exact count: a continuum of regions generally has uncountably many members, so the finite-resolution cover is the natural computational question suggested by that result.

The weakest point is that the cover-number problem is a new formulation prompted by Theorem 2, not a direct limit theorem from the paper. Also, individual members of an atomless population have zero mass, so the paper’s player-by-player equivalence definition becomes degenerate. The mirror instead treats the two population types as the relevant coordinates and requires both to affect the rule. The paper’s authors could recognize this as continuous enumeration of two-type simple games, but they might reasonably regard approximate metric entropy as a different project.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised one anchor: Theorem 2’s exact count of isomorphism classes of finite games with two equivalence classes. That is a real enumeration result, and the setting makes high multiplicity plausible: large bicameral organizations can have many members in each class. The negative case rests on a narrower point. The theorem counts rules as a function of \(n\); it gives no algorithmic task on a society supplied as a distribution.

The strongest continuous version would take two atomless blocs of masses \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\), and represent a game by an upward-closed winning region \(W\subseteq[0,\alpha]\times[0,1-\alpha]\). A closer continuation than the proposed cover number might study the scaling limit of finite two-class games: for example, take \(n_1/n\to\alpha\) and ask for the limiting distribution of normalized minimal-winning boundaries when a finite game is sampled uniformly from the isomorphism classes counted by the theorem. That is a coherent asymptotic question, and it avoids choosing an approximation metric at the outset.

It still turns the paper’s result into a study of the space of rules, rather than a computational problem on a continuous society. Theorem 2 provides no manipulation, control, or robustness task whose input is a population distribution. Exact counting also has no direct continuation: the measurable upward-closed regions form an uncountable class. Replacing the count with metric entropy creates a new objective. In the proponent’s version, area disagreement is a choice the theorem does not motivate. Uniformly sampling coalition masses over the rectangle differs from uniformly sampling finite coalitions, whose normalized masses concentrate near \((\alpha/2,(1-\alpha)/2)\). Other choices of coalition distribution or distance can therefore define different entropy questions.

A large two-bloc organization could supply a reason to approximate a catalogue of rules, and the paper’s subject makes that scenario credible. But it would also need to supply the catalogue’s loss function or sampling distribution. With those added, the result might be worthwhile asymptotic combinatorics or rule design; the paper’s enumeration theorem does not identify it as a continuous-complexity problem for ChoCo. The negative case is weakest on the claim that no such externally motivated question could ever be worthwhile. It is strongest on the narrower conclusion that this paper supplies no non-arbitrary computational mirror of its named result.
