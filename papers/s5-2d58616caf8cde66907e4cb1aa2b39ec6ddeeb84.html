The continuous mirror question

Given a finite goods set \(G\), known desired bundles \(\mathcal{B}\subseteq 2^G\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), a finite-support population distribution \(\mu\) over types \((S,v)\), and a step count \(\gamma\), construct a succinct anonymous direct mechanism. The mechanism observes each agent’s known bundle \(S\) and submitted scalar bid, but not the agent’s true value \(v\); it assigns each unit of mass either the whole desired bundle or nothing, with total allocation of each good \(g\) at most \(q_g\) times total population mass. Require dominant-strategy truthfulness in expectation, participation, the paper’s maximality condition, and a common \(\delta>0\) such that an individual’s winning probability rises by at least \(\delta\) at each of at most \(\gamma\) bid steps unless it reaches \(1\). Require total expected revenue not to increase when any positive-mass cohort is removed and supplies are recalculated at the same per-capita rates. Determine whether such a mechanism can be constructed in time polynomial in \(|G|\), the support size \(\tau\), \(\gamma\), and the input bit length, with \(\delta\) specified by the construction.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic auction for repeated demands over known bundles: types carry mass, each agent still receives an integral bundle or nothing, and per-capita supply yields aggregate capacity \(q_g\) per unit of population. The mechanism uses individual bids for incentive guarantees and aggregate reports for feasibility and cohort-level revenue comparisons.

The objection that survived

In an atomless market, one agent’s report does not change aggregate feasibility, so the finite theorem’s individual step guarantee may no longer express the same capacity trade-off; the proponent preserves the guarantee but does not fully explain its role in the continuum mechanism.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the stepwise mechanism construction and its polynomial-time guarantee in Theorem 5.2, with Theorem 5.1’s maximal-allocation construction as a supporting ingredient. It does not mirror the deterministic impossibility in Theorem 2.1 or the paper’s other characterizations and mechanism results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite maximal-allocation construction be represented and computed in time polynomial in the mass support size \(\tau\)?
  • Can the step guarantee \(\delta\) be bounded in terms of \(\tau\), bundle structure, and per-capita supplies rather than total population size?
  • Does revenue monotonicity under cohort removal remain nontrivial when supplies scale with the remaining population?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a stepwise mass-auction synthesis problem. Its natural regime is a large market for standardized bundles of resources—compute capacity, spectrum blocks, or similar entitlements—with many bidders but relatively few distinct desired bundles. A complete agent type is the desired bundle \(S\subseteq G\) and private value \(v\); the mechanism knows \(S\), as in the paper, but not \(v\). Mass \(\mu_{S,v}\) is the fraction of the population with that type. Many agents share each type, so the support size \(\tau\) can be much smaller than the population.

To keep a continuum of agents from competing for a fixed, vanishingly small inventory, let the supply of each resource class \(g\) scale with population: \(q_g\) units per unit of mass. That is a co-modification of the auction environment, but it is the kind of scaling the continuization programme permits. In each realized allocation, every agent still receives either their whole desired bundle or nothing; only the mass assigned to each outcome can be fractional.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5.2, proved in this paper. It says that, given a known-single-minded bidder setting, goods set \(G\), bidder set \(N\), and step count \(\gamma\), the authors construct a \(\gamma\)-step randomized mechanism in time polynomial in \(|N|\) and \(|G|\). The mechanism is strategyproof in expectation and revenue monotonic, satisfies participation and maximality, and satisfies \((\gamma\text{-step},\delta)\) consumer sovereignty with \(\delta=1/(n^2\gamma)\). Theorem 5.1 supports the construction by showing that an appropriate set of maximal allocations can itself be found in polynomial time. Theorem 2.1 is relevant background—it rules out the corresponding deterministic mechanism under its stated conditions—but it is an impossibility result, not a computational-complexity anchor. The paper offers no comparable NP-hardness result, so the algorithmic anchor is the right one here.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is this:

Stepwise Mass-Auction Synthesis. Given a finite goods set \(G\), a finite family \(\mathcal B\subseteq 2^G\) of publicly known desired bundles, rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), a population distribution \(\mu\) with finite support on types \((S,v)\), and a step count \(\gamma\), construct a succinct anonymous direct mechanism. The mechanism accepts each agent’s scalar bid, maps the resulting report profile to a lottery over measurable assignments and payments, and allocates whole bundles to agents subject to the aggregate supply limits. It must be strategyproof in expectation for each individual agent, satisfy participation and the paper’s maximality condition, and have revenue that does not increase when a positive mass of agents is removed. It must use at most \(\gamma\) bid steps per bundle class and give a common positive \(\delta\): at each step, an agent’s winning probability rises by at least \(\delta\), unless it reaches \(1\). The input masses, supplies, and values are binary rationals; the desired algorithmic bound is polynomial in \(|G|\), \(\tau\), \(\gamma\), and their bit length, with \(\delta\) specified as part of the output.

This asks for the underlying mechanism, not a solution to the paper’s QCLP encoding. It keeps the recognizable core—known single-minded demands, private scalar values, randomized allocations, incentives, and revenue monotonicity—while replacing bidder counts and individual allocations with population masses and aggregate feasibility. Its main new test is whether the construction can depend on the number of supported types rather than the number of agents. The paper’s guarantee \(1/(n^2\gamma)\) decays with population size; the continuous problem asks whether a positive guarantee can instead be expressed in terms of support and resource structure.

For the unrestricted bundle family, I would initially expect the general problem to be continuum-specifically hard, since allocation choices still interact through overlapping bundles, and a pricing step over such packings may be difficult even when the mass input has small support. That is a conjectural direction, not a hardness claim: structured bundle families may admit a tractable LP or column-generation approach. The question also opens natural follow-ups: whether \(\delta\) can be bounded using \(\tau\) rather than total population, which bundle structures make the pricing step tractable, and how solutions relate back to finite high-multiplicity auctions.

The weakest point is the supply scaling. It gives the mass population a nondegenerate allocation problem, but revenue monotonicity then compares markets whose inventories scale with their populations. A referee could reasonably ask whether that is still the authors’ revenue-monotonicity question. I think the mirror remains credible because the strategic and revenue properties are intact and the supply change is explicit, but this is the part most likely to decide whether the authors recognize it as their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 5.2’s guarantees depend on bidders being individual atoms. With \(n\) bidders, removing one changes the participant set; in a population normalized to mass one, the corresponding mass is \(1/n\). In the atomless limit, removing an individual leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, so revenue monotonicity under individual deletion reduces to \(R(\mu)\ge R(\mu)\). The theorem’s step guarantee also weakens with population size: its construction gives \(\delta=1/(n^2\gamma)\).

The proposed mass-auction problem repairs this by requiring monotonicity under removal of a positive-mass cohort and scaling supplies with population. Those are intelligible choices, and the supply change is permitted by the programme. They also change the theorem’s central comparison: the paper removes one bidder while holding the goods universe fixed; the mirror removes a cohort and may change the inventory too. The \(\tau\)-type problem would need its own account of how cohort departures and supply changes interact.

There is a related problem with carrying over consumer sovereignty. For a measure-zero bidder, a unilateral bid has no effect on aggregate feasibility. Individual winning probabilities and payments can therefore be specified around that bidder without the finite auction’s capacity trade-off. If consumer sovereignty is meant to constrain the allocation of a positive mass of agents, it becomes a cohort guarantee rather than the paper’s individual guarantee. Per-capita supply keeps aggregate allocation nondegenerate, but it does not resolve this difference.

Theorem 5.1 has the same limitation as support for the mirror: its polynomial-time construction finds a finite set of maximal allocations for named bidders. A type-mass version would need a representation of fractional assignments and maximality across mass profiles. And although the paper’s algorithm is a genuine computational result, it constructs a mechanism uniformly over bidder and bundle configurations; it does not take a truthful population distribution as its computational input. The proposed synthesis problem would need a finite representation of a mechanism acting on all report profiles and mass-removal scenarios. “Succinct” gestures at that issue without settling it.

These points make the proposed problem a substantial extension rather than a close continuation of the paper’s algorithmic guarantee. They do not establish that the extension is unworthy: repeated-demand markets for spectrum or compute capacity are plausible, and positive-mass revenue monotonicity could be a meaningful question. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The negative case is strongest on fidelity and on the atomless collapse of individual deletion; it remains weak against the proponent’s explicitly cohort-level reformulation.
