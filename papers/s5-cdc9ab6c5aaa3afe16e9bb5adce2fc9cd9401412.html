The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compatible type pairs, and strict rankings over compatible partner types with being unmatched ranked last, decide whether the atomless population \(U=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\) admits a dominant popular matching, assuming it admits a stable matching. Matchings are measurable pairings of individuals, and \(\Delta(M,N)\) is the measure of individuals who prefer their assignment in \(M\) to their assignment in \(N\), minus the measure who prefer \(N\). Popularity requires \(\Delta(M,N)\ge 0\) for every feasible \(N\); dominance additionally requires \(\Delta(M,N)>0\) whenever \(N\) matches strictly more population mass than \(M\). Stability means there are no compatible positive-mass subpopulations that mutually prefer one another to their current assignments.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity roommates market with finitely many complete types: every member of type \(t\) has the same compatible partner types and strict ranking, and type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\). A feasible solution is a measurable pairing of individuals; its objective is not a cost but the existence of a matching \(M\) whose vote margin \(\Delta(M,N)\) meets the popularity and dominance conditions against every alternative \(N\).

The objection that survived

A type-to-type mass flow alone does not capture which agents change partner type between a proposed matching and a challenger, so the finite computational representation may need additional coupling variables; this challenges the algorithmic sketch, not the well-definedness of the question.

fatal: False

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the maximum vote-margin challenger against a proposed mass matching be represented by a finite optimization that captures the joint realization of both pairings?
  • What is the complexity of deciding dominant popular matching existence, parameterized by the number of supported types and the input bit length, when a stable mass matching exists?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror is the paper’s headline question: does a population admit a matching that no alternative matching defeats in a vote by the people being matched? I would put it in a large recurring roommate market—for example, a housing network serving millions of residents—where many residents share a profile of compatibility, schedule, language, and partner-type preferences. There might be millions of residents but only dozens or hundreds of profiles. Each profile is a complete type for this problem: its members have the same eligible partner types and the same strict ranking of those types. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime, though it deliberately abstracts away preferences between named individuals.

My anchors are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved in this paper. Theorem 1 says that, given a roommates instance with strict preference lists, deciding whether it has a popular matching is NP-hard. Theorem 2 says that deciding whether such an instance has a dominant matching is NP-hard, even when the instance admits a stable matching. The paper proves these results using a reduction from 1-in-3 SAT; that source problem’s hardness is cited. Theorem 1 is my lead because it mirrors the headline problem directly. Theorem 2 is the stronger hardness anchor because it retains hardness under the stable-matching restriction. ([The Popular Roommates problem](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.00141))

Call the first mirror Popular Roommates\(_\infty\). An instance consists of a finite support \(T\) of roommate types, a compatibility graph \(E\) on \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and, for each type \(t\), a strict ranking of its compatible partner types and being unmatched, \(\bot\). The society is \(U=\bigsqcup_{t\in T} [0,\mu_t]\). A feasible matching pairs compatible agents, leaves any remaining mass unmatched, and gives each individual either one partner or none. Thus type-level assignments can be fractional, while each unit of mass receives an integral bundle.

The type ranking is strict over partner types; agents are indifferent between two individual partners of the same type. This makes the comparison well-defined from the support-level input. For two feasible matchings \(M,N\) on the same population, let
\[ \Delta(M,N)=\sum_{t\in T}\int_{[0,\mu_t]} \operatorname{sgn}_t\!\bigl(\operatorname{type}(M(i)),\operatorname{type}(N(i))\bigr)\,di, \]
where \(\operatorname{sgn}_t\) is \(+1\) if type \(t\) prefers its partner under \(M\), \(-1\) if it prefers its partner under \(N\), and \(0\) for indifference. Unmatched is ranked last. The question is whether there exists a feasible \(M\) such that \(\Delta(M,N)\ge 0\) for every feasible alternative \(N\). A solution can be given as the masses of type-\(t\) agents assigned to each partner type, with a measurable pairing realizing those masses. The universal comparison is over alternatives on the same population, just as in the paper.

Dominant Roommates\(_\infty\) uses the same instance and asks for a popular \(M\) such that \(\Delta(M,N)>0\) for every feasible \(N\) that matches strictly more population mass than \(M\). The mirror of Theorem 2 is the version promised to have a stable mass matching: no compatible positive-mass subpopulations mutually prefer one another to their current partners. The strict comparison against *any* larger mass is deliberate. In the finite problem “larger” means at least one more edge; in a continuum it can mean an arbitrarily small increase in matched mass.

I expect Popular Roommates\(_\infty\) to be Class A, at least with an FPT-in-\(\tau\) target, where \(\tau=|T|\). For a proposed mass matching, split each type further according to its current partner type. A challenger is then a weighted fractional matching among at most \(O(\tau^2)\) such subpopulations: its edge weights record how much each endpoint prefers the challenger’s partner type to its current one. Maximizing the challenger’s vote margin is a linear program. Its bounded vote weights suggest finite-valued dual certificates; enumerating those certificates and solving the resulting mass constraints is a plausible parameterized route. That is a research target, not a result established here. I tentatively expect the dominant mirror to be tractable by related parametric optimization, but with less confidence: its strict size condition raises a real question about challengers that add only an arbitrarily small mass.

The authors should recognize these as their problems: the feasible object remains a roommate matching, popularity remains a vote between matchings by the matched population, and the question remains existence against every alternative. The scope is specifically the population mirror of Theorems 1 and 2; fractional matching flows arise because mass is continuous, not because the individuals receive fractional partners.

The weak point is that the paper assumes strict rankings over individual neighbors, while this mirror ranks partner *types* and treats members of one partner type as interchangeable. More seriously, the reduction’s variable and clause gadgets have finely differentiated roles. The reduction does not establish hardness when those roles are repeated at high multiplicity, and mass splitting may undo its binary gadget choices. So I would not claim that either NP-hardness result transfers. The case for the mirror is that it preserves the computational question in a plausible many-agent regime and exposes a concrete fractional-matching optimization route; whether that route succeeds is exactly the question to investigate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this mirror changes what a roommate preference is. In the paper, each person strictly ranks named neighbors, and popularity compares who gets the better *individual partner* under two matchings. A type that preserves those lists must encode identities and their incidence in the graph, so its support can grow with the population. To keep support small, the mirror must rank partner *types* instead, making people indifferent among everyone in a type. That is a coherent anonymous roommate model, but it is a substantive restriction on the paper’s problem.

This also weakens the proposed tractability route for Theorem 1. A type-to-type flow does not by itself say which people currently assigned to one partner type get reassigned to another in a challenge; popularity depends on that joint reassignment. One can add variables to record it, and the result may still be a finite optimization problem. But the LP sketch does not yet establish tractability. That is a challenge to the proposed algorithm, not a reason the question is unworthy.

Theorem 2 inherits the same gap. Its stable-instance restriction depends on the named agents and their exact preference lists. In atomless roommate models, stability can behave differently: one existing analysis proves stable matchings in a nonatomic roommate setting under stated conditions, including an example where the corresponding finite instance has no stable matching. That is analytic existence work, not an answer to dominant-popularity complexity, but it makes the theorem’s stable-instance qualifier less obviously informative in the mirror. [Carmona, *Stable matching in large markets with occupational choice*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/TE5915)

There is nearby fractional-popularity machinery too. For example, Huang and Kavitha study lotteries over matchings in a finite roommates graph and give polynomial-time results for popular fractional matchings. That is outcome-space continuity, so it does not settle the population-mass problem; it does mean the mirror must identify what the population interpretation adds. [Huang and Kavitha, *Popularity, Mixed Matchings, and Self-Duality*](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.2020.1063)

This does not defeat either anchor. The paper has two genuine computational theorems, and a large housing cohort whose members rank compatible partner profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The argument against is therefore limited: the mirror is not faithful to arbitrary strict rankings over named neighbors, and its claimed optimization route needs a more precise representation. I cannot honestly turn that into “no worthwhile continuous mirror in any scenario.” The strongest defensible position is to treat it as a new complexity study of anonymous type-based roommate markets, rather than a continuization of the paper’s full named-person model.
