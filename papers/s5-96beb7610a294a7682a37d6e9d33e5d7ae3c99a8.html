The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly give this paper a qualifying computational anchor. The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here while extending a limit analysis by DeMarzo et al.: for a connected graph, if \(|\lambda_2|\ne|\lambda_3|\) and the initial opinions have nonzero projection onto the second eigenvector, the normalized mean-centered opinions converge, up to the paper’s sign convention, to the normalized mean-centered second eigenvector. Corollary 3, derived here, says that any continuous group-based polarization metric then converges to its value on that eigenvector. These are spectral and dynamical results; neither classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. The paper has no named result of the required complexity kind.

The strongest positive foothold is a new, unanchored follow-on question, not a mirror of a computational result in the paper. Let each type \(a\) represent a community and initial-opinion profile shared by many agents; let \(\mu_a\) be its population fraction, and let \(K_{ab}\) be the symmetric contact intensity between types. Given rational \(\mu\), \(K\), initial opinions \(x_a^{(0)}\), and an error tolerance \(\varepsilon\), define
\[ P_{ab}=\frac{\mu_bK_{ab}}{\sum_c\mu_cK_{ac}}, \qquad x^{(t+1)}=Px^{(t)}. \]
One could ask for an interval of width at most \(\varepsilon\) containing the limiting weighted Sarle bimodality of the opinion distribution, under assumptions ensuring the relevant spectral limit exists. This is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large society has few recurring community and opinion profiles, and mass means population fraction. I would expect spectral computation to make the question tractable when the relevant eigenvalue is well separated and the numerical inputs are well conditioned. Further questions would include how the bit complexity depends on the spectral gap and whether changing contact intensities to raise equilibrium bimodality is hard.

That candidate follows the paper’s subject, but it does not meet the requested anchor standard: the authors do not pose or classify this computational problem. The paper’s opinions are continuous-valued, but its society remains a finite graph; its main contribution is analytic, in a tradition the programme explicitly distinguishes from computational complexity. So the paper supplies a credible seed for a population-continuum research question, but no anchored continuous mirror of a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor problem is decisive here: Theorem 1 and Corollary 3 prove spectral convergence on a finite graph. They give no complexity classification for a decision, optimization, approximation, or parameterized problem. Calling either a computational anchor would stretch what the paper establishes.

The strongest direct mirror is to represent communities and initial opinion profiles as types, with masses \(\mu_a\) and a type-to-type contact kernel \(K\). From these, one gets a finite transition matrix \(P\), whose second eigenvector determines the limiting group-based metrics under the paper’s assumptions. For rational inputs with a suitable spectral gap, approximating that eigenvector and evaluating weighted bimodality or local agreement is standard finite-dimensional spectral computation, polynomial in the number of types and input precision. The same kind of computation is already available on the paper’s finite graphs. A type quotient either preserves the relevant dynamics, in which case the computational task is routine, or loses node-level network structure, in which case it is a new mean-field or block-model assumption. Neither route reveals a complexity question supplied by the paper.

The proposed intervention variant is less direct still. At infinite time, changing initial opinions generally leaves the limiting metric unchanged; the theorem makes it depend on the graph, not the starting vector. Changing \(K\) can produce a substantive network-design problem, and the paper itself mentions outside actors modifying the graph. But optimizing a contact kernel is a newly specified problem whose difficulty comes from the chosen intervention model. Its finite-network counterpart can be posed without a continuous population, so it does not rescue an anchored mirror.

That is the strongest negative case I can make: no worthwhile continuous mirror of a named computational result survives, because the paper has no such result to mirror, and its closest computational-looking continuization reduces to routine spectral analysis. The broader claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile new work is weaker. A community-level kernel model may be sensible and useful; it would make a follow-on project, rather than a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
