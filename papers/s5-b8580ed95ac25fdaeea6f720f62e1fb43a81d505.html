The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), a simplicial complex \(K\) specifying feasible type supports, rational type masses \(\mu_t\), weights \(a_t\), and quota \(q\), clone each type to \(r\mu_t\) voters at every admissible scale \(r\), with quota \(rq\). Count critical voter–coalition pairs under the paper’s equal-coalition-counting convention and, for instances where the resulting type-power vector converges, compute its limit to additive error \(\varepsilon\).

The model it lives in

The society is the distribution \(\mu\) over complete voter types. Each type carries a weight and coalition-compatibility role; finite cloned populations use the original threshold and feasible-support rule. Coalition mass profiles receive the multiplicities induced by counting discrete coalitions, and the objective is each type’s limiting share of total Banzhaf power.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a high multiplicity limit of the paper’s weighted Banzhaf calculation. The anchor is Proposition 5.5, proved in this paper. It gives the relative Banzhaf index in a weighted voting game with infeasible coalitions: for each voter \(v_i\), count feasible coalitions \(S\) for which \(v_i\) is critical—\(w(S)<q\leq w(S)+w_i\)—and divide that count by the total number of critical voter–coalition pairs over all voters. The proposition expresses those counts by summing over the relevant simplices of the weighted complex. The paper uses the formulas in its voting-system examples. It does not give a P, NP-hardness, or other complexity classification; this is a computational formula, not a complexity result.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: High Multiplicity Banzhaf Limit. An instance gives a finite set of voter types \(T\), a simplicial complex \(K\) of feasible type supports, rational population fractions \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a rational voting weight \(a_t\) for each type, and a rational per-capita quota \(q\). A type is a complete description for this game: its weight and its coalition-compatibility behavior. For each admissible population scale \(r\), create \(r\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\), each with weight \(a_t\), and set the quota to \(rq\). A coalition is feasible exactly when the types it contains form a face of \(K\).

For type \(t\), let \(c_t(r)\) count all critical pairs \((i,S)\) where \(i\) is a type-\(t\) voter, \(S\) excludes \(i\), \(S\cup\{i\}\) is feasible, and \(w(S)<rq\leq w(S)+a_t\). Set \(B_t(r)=c_t(r)/\sum_s c_s(r)\). The question is: assuming these indices have a limit, compute the limiting type-power vector to additive error \(\varepsilon\). The output is each type’s share of total Banzhaf power in the high multiplicity regime.

The continuous object is the society distribution \(\mu\). Coalition mass profiles are \(x_t=|S\cap N_t|/r\); type \(t\) is pivotal in a thin slice near \(a\cdot x=q\), provided the type support remains feasible. Crucially, this does not replace the paper’s equal-counting convention with an arbitrary uniform measure over fractional coalitions: the number of discrete coalitions giving a mass profile is part of the limit. Its exponential scale is described by the entropy \(H_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t h(x_t/\mu_t)\), where \(h\) is binary entropy. For each type, the relevant continuous optimization is over quota-boundary profiles satisfying \(a\cdot x=q\) and \(\operatorname{supp}(x)\cup\{t\}\in K\). Dominant profiles and their lower-order contributions determine the limiting power shares.

The regime I have in mind is a large federation or assembly with thousands of delegates but only a small number of recurring institutional roles or caucus types—for instance, \(10^4\)–\(10^6\) delegates and a few dozen types. Delegates of a type share weight and coalition compatibility; feasibility depends on which types participate, not on named individuals. The mass \(\mu_t\) is their population share, and \(x_t\) is the share of that type participating in a coalition. This is a credible extension of the paper’s interest in coalition feasibility and its aggregation of electoral blocs and parties. The high multiplicity literature cited by the paper supports the regime; it is not a novelty collision.

I expect a Class A route at fixed \(\tau=|T|\), at least for nondegenerate instances: optimize the concave entropy objective on the quota slice of each allowed type face, then calculate the asymptotic contributions to the normalized indices. The harder follow-up is whether this remains efficient when \(\tau\) varies and dominant profiles tie or lie on boundaries. The paper’s scope covered here is only the weighted Banzhaf calculation in Proposition 5.5; this is not a mirror of Theorem 4.2’s topological characterization or of the Shapley–Shubik formulas.

The main weakness is substantial: the paper calculates power in particular finite games, while this mirror asks about an asymptotic sequence of cloned voters and restricts infeasibility to type-level coalition rules. It also preserves an index’s equal-counting convention, which is a mathematical definition rather than a behavioral model of coalition formation. Still, the underlying object is recognizably theirs: the same weighted threshold, the same feasible-coalition structure, and the same count of critical voters, now expressed as a type-level power question about a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Proposition 5.5 is a counting formula, not a complexity result. It expresses the relative Banzhaf index as critical voter–coalition counts; the paper does not classify the complexity of computing those counts or give an algorithmic theorem. Its introduction says its code enumerates coalitions and points readers to separate work on power-index complexity. So the proponent’s limit problem is better described as a new asymptotic power-index project than as a continuization of a computational result established here.

That objection does not defeat the stronger mirror. Cloning voters by complete type, retaining the same weighted threshold and feasible-coalition structure, and asking for limiting type-level power shares is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem. A large assembly with recurring roles is plausible, and aggregation by type is legitimate under the programme’s definition. Existing work on Banzhaf limits supports the relevance of large electorates; it is not a novelty collision or a reason to dismiss the regime. [Kurz’s study of Penrose–Banzhaf limit results](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03197)

The more fundamental concern is that a continuous society alone does not define Banzhaf power. The index asks whether adding one voter changes the outcome. In an atomless population, one voter has zero mass, so pivotality vanishes away from the quota boundary. The proposed nonzero type shares must therefore be recovered from a finite-population boundary layer: the cloning sequence, the equal-counting measure on discrete coalitions, and how quota and pivotality behave at that scale. Entropy optimization may identify dominant profiles, but ties and lower-order terms can decide the normalized shares. This makes the mirror depend on asymptotic microscopic structure that the mass distribution itself does not contain.

Still, the paper’s equal-counting convention and the finite-scale sequence can reasonably be included in the mirrored model. The proponent has done that, and the resulting computation is well-posed if the limit exists. I can make a strong case that this is not a direct mirror of a complexity theorem in the paper, and a narrower case that the continuum does not intrinsically define its power measure. I cannot honestly show that the strengthened question is unworthy of ChoCo’s time. The negative case is weak at that point: this anchor likely survives as a worthwhile asymptotic computational question, though not as a continuation of a complexity result the paper actually proves.
