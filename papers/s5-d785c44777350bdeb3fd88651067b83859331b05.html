The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational population shares \(\mu_t\), principal values \(x_t\), agent values \(v_t>0\), penalty fraction \(c\), and per-capita supply \(\rho\in(0,1]\) of individually indivisible awards, find allocation probabilities \(q_t\in[0,1]\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_tq_t\), subject to \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tq_t\le\rho\) and \(q_t\ge(1-c)q_s\) for every \(t,s\in T\).

The model it lives in

Use a continuum of applicants with mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete value types and a fixed positive award supply \(\rho\) per applicant. A direct mechanism gives each applicant allocation probability \(q_t\) from their report; the awards remain indivisible, while the aggregate capacity and expected principal value are measured per capita.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named hardness or complexity-class result in this paper. The one possible anchor is Proposition 1, proved here, under the prompt’s exception for a formulation that the paper uses to compute an optimum. It says that the principal’s original mechanism problem, optimizing over stochastic allocation rules \(p\) subject to truthful reporting and allocation feasibility, is equivalent to the reduced-form problem over \(g\): maximize \(\int_a^b xg(x)\,dF(x)\), subject to \(v(x)g(x)\ge v(x)(1-c)\sup_y g(y)\) for every \(x\), and the generalized Matthews–Border feasibility condition. A reduced form \(g\) solves that problem if and only if some optimal allocation rule \(p\) has reduced form \(g\). The paper then solves this formulation. If “formulation” is read narrowly to mean a computational encoding such as an LP or MILP, Proposition 1 would not qualify, and there would be no anchor under the stated rule.

The strongest continuous mirror of that underlying problem is Mass Allocation with Limited Penalties. Consider a large grant competition or hiring pool with a normalized population measure \(\mu\) over social-value types \(x\). Agents of type \(x\) share the same value \(v(x)\) of receiving the prize; the principal’s value from selecting one is \(x\). The prize remains indivisible, and a winner who lied loses a fraction \(c\) of their surplus. The regime is many applicants relative to the number \(\tau\) of standardized value categories, or the corresponding limit with a distribution of values.

Choose a measurable allocation intensity \(g(x)\ge 0\). The measure \(\mu(dx)g(x)\) is the probability that the winner has a type in \(dx\); any remaining probability leaves the prize unallocated. Interpret \(g\) as the large-population limit of \(n\) times an individual’s interim probability of selection. This scaling preserves the paper’s incentive comparison even as each applicant’s unscaled chance of winning tends to zero. The problem is

\[ \max_g \int xg(x)\,d\mu(x) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \int g(x)\,d\mu(x)\le 1,\qquad g(x)\ge (1-c)\sup_y g(y)\ \ \forall x. \]

The incentive condition follows from the paper’s reporting incentives: a truthful type \(x\) gets utility proportional to \(v(x)g(x)\); by lying, they can obtain at most \(v(x)(1-c)\sup_y g(y)\). The objective and constraints retain the paper’s central tradeoff: favor higher social value while giving low types enough chance of selection to deter lying.

For \(\tau\) rationally weighted value categories \(x_j\), this becomes a linear program: maximize \(\sum_j\mu_jx_jg_j\), subject to \(\sum_j\mu_jg_j\le1\) and \(g_j\ge(1-c)g_k\) for every pair \(j,k\). Its solution gives the winner-type lottery, with the prize still going to one agent. For an atomless distribution satisfying the paper’s assumptions, the paper’s Proposition 2 solves the corresponding relaxed problem with a threshold rule: \(g(x)=(1-c)r\) below \(z\), \(g(x)=r\) at or above \(z\), with \(z\) and \(r\) determined by its equations (8) and (9). The paper’s Theorem 1, proved here, supports the population-limit interpretation: beyond some finite \(\bar n\), the optimal finite-agent mechanism is a binary shortlisting procedure. The expected direction is therefore tractable, and in the finite-category version the stated problem is directly an LP. This mirrors only the optimal-allocation problem and its high-population regime, not the paper’s comparative statics or its discussions of other mechanism-design settings.

The weakest point is that the continuous-population problem is not the paper’s stated theorem: its agents are finite in number, even though their values are drawn from a continuous distribution. Proposition 1 is a reduced-form characterization, not a complexity result, and the paper already derives the large-\(n\) structure. Also, the paper assumes an atomless value distribution, whereas a fixed, small set of categories is the cleanest high-multiplicity scenario for ChoCo. Those gaps make this a plausible mirror of the underlying allocation problem, but a modest formulation-based case rather than evidence of a new complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named complexity result to mirror. Proposition 1 is the closest anchor: it shows that the finite-agent mechanism problem can be represented using a reduced-form allocation \(g\). Theorem 1 and Propositions 2–3 characterize optimal mechanisms; none asks for, or establishes the complexity of, an algorithmic problem. Even granting Proposition 1 as an anchor because the paper uses it to solve the optimization, the proposed mirror does not reveal a new computational question.

The proposed LP is the paper’s own relaxed reduced-form problem. Its objective and incentive constraints match the paper’s, and its constraint \(\int g\,d\mu\le 1\) is the relaxed feasibility condition. Proposition 2 solves that problem, while Theorem 1 shows that its solution is attained in the paper’s finite-agent model once \(n\ge\bar n\). So the cleanest version of the proposed continuous question is already the paper’s solved large-pool case, expressed with population language.

There is also a substantive gap between the two models. In the paper, \(F\) is the prior distribution of each of finitely many agents’ private values. It is not a known distribution of types in an atomless society. If \(\mu\) instead describes the actual mass of a continuum population competing for one indivisible prize, a single agent has measure zero. A mechanism that depends only on \(\mu\) cannot register that agent’s unilateral report, so the paper’s individual incentive constraints no longer follow. Allowing the mechanism to respond to that individual’s report makes the mass distribution insufficient to describe the mechanism. The proposed scaling of selection probabilities by \(n\) preserves finite-\(n\) incentive comparisons, but it defines a limit of finite games, not an atomless one-prize mechanism with those same individual incentives.

The better repair is to keep large finite pools, group applicants by complete value types, and retain the finite-\(n\) feasibility constraints. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, but it is not a continuous-population model; its single-prize limit returns to the already-solved relaxation. Scaling the number of prizes with the population could avoid vanishing individual chances, but then the paper’s one-prize problem has become a proportional-supply allocation problem. That may be a worthwhile extension, yet it does not rescue the proposed mirror of Proposition 1 or Theorem 1.

I would not claim this proves that no related continuous model could interest the authors. Large grant competitions with standardized applicant types are plausible, and an algorithmic study of finite high-multiplicity variants might be useful. But the case against this paper having a worthwhile *new computational* mirror is stronger than the case for it: the sole anchor is not a computational result, its proposed LP is already solved in the paper’s large-pool analysis, and the literal atomless one-prize version does not preserve the incentive problem.
