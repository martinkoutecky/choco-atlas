The continuous mirror question

Given a planar subcubic bipartite graph \(G=(S\cup R,E)\), assign each type \(v\in S\cup R\) mass \(\mu_v=1/|V(G)|\). Does there exist a strict preference ranking for each type over opposite-side types and being unmatched such that the graph containing \(sr\) exactly when some stable mass allocation assigns \(x_{sr}>0\) is \(G\)? Allocations satisfy type-mass capacity constraints, and stability requires that for every pair \(sr\), at least one side has zero mass assigned below its partner type, including unmatched mass when that type prefers \(sr\) to being unmatched.

The model it lives in

Each graph vertex represents a cohort type with mass \(\mu_v=1/|V(G)|\) and a strict ranking over opposite-side types and being unmatched. The decision variables are mass assignments \(x_{sr}\ge 0\), with each type’s assigned mass at most its mass. Ask whether there is a preference profile for which the union of positive-mass pairs over all stable allocations has edge set exactly \(E(G)\).

The objection that survived

The mirror tests whether positive mass from two types can mutually prefer one another, rather than whether a particular pair of agents can match; the clone-support dictionary remains unproved.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5.1’s recognition result, including its planar subcubic restriction. It does not cover the graph characterizations, lattice results, or exact recognition algorithms.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the support graph of stable allocations in this type-based model coincide with the support graph in a finite market formed by cloning each type?
  • Is recognition still NP-complete for planar subcubic type graphs under this fractional stability condition?
  • Does a carving-width algorithm extend to this continuous recognition problem?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is Continuous Stable-Pair Graph Recognition. The population is the continuous object: for a planar subcubic bipartite input graph \(G=(S\cup R,E)\), treat each vertex as a preference type and give every type mass \(1/|V(G)|\). In a placement market, for example, a type could be a large cohort of applicants with the same full ranking of program types; on the other side, a type is a cohort of programs with the same ranking of applicant types. The headcount can be far larger than the number of types \(\tau=|V(G)|\); the input records the type graph, not individual agents.

A continuous matching is a matrix \(x_{sr}\geq 0\), where \(x_{sr}\) is the mass of type \(s\) assigned to type \(r\). Each type’s total assigned mass is at most its mass, and the remainder is unmatched. Each type has a strict ranking of the opposite-side types and the unmatched option; a pair can be unacceptable. This keeps the individual decision integral—each infinitesimal unit gets one partner—while the type-level averages \(x_{sr}\) can be fractional.

For precision, let \(u_s\) and \(v_r\) be the unmatched masses. Define \(a_{sr}(x)\) as the mass of type \(s\) assigned below \(r\) in its ranking, including unmatched mass if \(s\) prefers \(r\) to being unmatched; define \(b_{sr}(x)\) symmetrically for type \(r\). Call \(x\) stable if, for every pair \((s,r)\), at least one of \(a_{sr}(x)\) and \(b_{sr}(x)\) is zero. Thus no positive mass on both sides would prefer to pair with each other over their current assignments. Put an edge \(sr\) in the type graph exactly when some stable allocation has \(x_{sr}>0\).

The problem is: does there exist a preference profile for these types such that the graph of type pairs used with positive mass in at least one stable allocation is exactly \(G\)? A solution is such a profile, with equality understood across *all* stable allocations: every edge of \(G\) must occur with positive mass in some stable allocation, and no nonedge may do so. This is the population mirror of the paper’s recognition question; fractional flows are the aggregate matching forced by continuization, not the goal in themselves.

The anchor is Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper. It says that, given a bipartite graph \(G\), deciding whether \(G\) is the graph of stably matchable pairs for some stable-matching instance is NP-complete, even when \(G\) is planar and subcubic. The proof establishes membership in NP using either a preference system or a rotation system as a witness, and proves hardness by reduction from monotone planar 2-in-4-SAT. The result’s hardness is a good anchor because it concerns the exact pair-support graph that the mirror retains.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem translated to large cohorts: the question remains which pairs can occur across the stable matchings of some preference system. The natural setting is a centralized matching market where large cohorts share complete descriptions relevant to the model—preferences, acceptability, and any matching parameters. The high-multiplicity fit is strongest when \(N\), the number of people, is much larger than \(\tau\), the number of distinct cohort types. The reduction’s type count can grow with the formula, but each type can still stand for a large cohort.

My prediction is hardness transfers (Class B): Theorem 5.1’s combinatorics are encoded in the incidence structure of the graph and its small-degree gadgets, not in individually addressable agents. But that is a prediction, not a consequence of the theorem. The critical open step is a finite-to-continuous dictionary showing that allowing fractional mass flows does not change which type-pair edges can occur. In particular, one should test whether rational masses and type preferences can be expanded to a finite cloned market with exactly the same support graph.

The scope here is only Theorem 5.1. I would not stretch this argument to the paper’s lattice characterizations or algorithms. The weakest point is the fractional stability condition: splitting a cohort could create stable allocations using extra type pairs, or defeat the gadget constraints that make the discrete recognition problem hard. If that happens, the paper’s reduction may fail even though the mirror remains a sensible question. Further questions include whether planar subcubic type-graph recognition is NP-complete, whether the clone-support dictionary holds, and whether a carving-width algorithm like the paper’s Theorem 6.2 survives for this continuous model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 5.1’s graph records whether a particular pair of agents can be matched, while the proposed mirror records whether positive mass from two preference types can be matched. Those are different relations. If cohort members rank named counterparts as in the paper, scaling the population can make their preference descriptions distinct. If they rank counterpart types instead, the model introduces indifference among individuals and a new aggregate blocking rule. The proponent’s clone-support dictionary is therefore the central modelling question, not a routine way to carry the theorem over. The paper’s rotation systems and reduction establish nothing about that dictionary.

That objection defeats the claim that Theorem 5.1’s NP-completeness directly transfers. It does not, however, defeat the proposed recognition problem as a worthwhile question in its own right. A market of large cohorts with preferences over partner categories is plausible, and the graph of type-pairs used in some stable allocation is a natural object for it. Computational work on fractional stable matching exists, but the work I found studies allocation objectives rather than recognition of graphs of stably matchable type-pairs ([IJCAI paper](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/13)). That is adjacent work, not a continuous answer to this anchor.

So the negative case is weak at the point it must be strongest: the proposed model may be a new variant rather than a faithful high-multiplicity relaxation, but I cannot honestly rule out that the authors would recognize it as their problem. Theorem 5.1 remains a viable anchor unless that recognition gate fails.
