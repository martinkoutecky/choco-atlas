The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) products \(P\), a recommendation size \(k<n\), rational \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), and a finite set of customer types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\) and rational relevance vectors \(v_t:P\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), find nonnegative masses \(x_{t,A}\) over bundles \(A\subseteq P\) of size \(k\). Require \(\sum_A x_{t,A}=\mu_t\) for each type; for every pair with \(x_{t,A},x_{s,B}>0\), require \(\sum_{p\in A}v_t(p)\ge\sum_{p\in B}v_t(p)-\max_{p\in B}v_t(p)\); and require at least \(\lceil n-\alpha k\rceil\) products to have exposure \(e_p=\sum_{t,A:p\in A}x_{t,A}\ge\alpha k/n\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass customer population has finitely many types, each carrying mass \(\mu_t\) and a complete relevance vector. Decision variables \(x_{t,A}\) assign type mass to integral bundles of \(k\) distinct products; the task is feasibility under pairwise EF1 and the limiting producer exposure guarantee.

What the mirror covers

It mirrors Theorem 7.2's exact bundle size, customer EF1, and limiting producer \(\alpha\)-MMS fraction; the vanishing nonzero-exposure guarantee, experiments, and FairRecPlus results are not covered.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does this feasibility problem admit an algorithm polynomial in \(n\), \(\tau\), \(k\), and input bit length \(L\), with a compact output representation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 7.2, proved in this paper. It is an algorithmic result; the paper gives no named NP-hardness result to anchor a hardness-dissolution question. Theorem 7.2 says that for \(n\) producers and \(m\) customers, when \(k<n\le mk\), FairRec runs in polynomial time and returns an allocation in which every customer gets exactly \(k\) items, the customers are EF1, every producer has nonzero exposure, and at least \(\left(1-\frac{1}{m+1}\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha mk}{n}\right\rfloor\right)\) of the producers receive their \(\alpha\)-MMS exposure guarantee.

I would mirror the theorem’s computational question as Continuous Two-Sided Fair Recommendation. There are \(n\) products \(P\), a recommendation size \(k<n\), and a rational \(\alpha\in(0,1]\). Customers form a unit-mass population with finite support \(T\): each type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a complete relevance vector \(v_t:P\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\). A type means customers have the same relevance for every product and the same recommendation constraints. This is population continuization: each person still receives an indivisible bundle of exactly \(k\) distinct products.

A solution is a finite list of nonnegative masses \(x_{t,A}\), where \(A\subseteq P\) and \(|A|=k\). Here \(x_{t,A}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(A\), and \(\sum_A x_{t,A}=\mu_t\) for every type. Require EF1 across every pair of bundles used with positive mass: whenever \(x_{t,A}>0\) and \(x_{s,B}>0\), require \(\sum_{p\in A}v_t(p)\ge\sum_{p\in B}v_t(p)-\max_{p\in B}v_t(p)\). Thus each infinitesimal customer gets an integral bundle, while the mass assigned to each type–bundle pair may be fractional.

The exposure of product \(p\) is \(e_p=\sum_{t,A:p\in A}x_{t,A}\), so total exposure is \(k\). The equal-share producer MMS benchmark in this unit-mass society is \(k/n\). The question is to compute such an EF1 allocation in which at least \(\lceil n-\alpha k\rceil\) products have exposure at least \(\alpha k/n\). This is the limiting form of the theorem’s \(\alpha\)-MMS fraction guarantee: as \(m\) grows, its guaranteed fraction approaches \(1-\alpha k/n\). I would not carry over “nonzero exposure for every producer” as a substantive continuum guarantee: the discrete guarantee of at least one recommendation becomes only \(1/m\) mass and vanishes in the limit.

The regime is a large batch recommendation service with many customers but relatively few exact relevance profiles: for example, mass promotions to large cohorts whose members receive the same product scores. If a platform scores each customer uniquely, then \(\tau\) may be close to the population size and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears. The mirror is therefore credible for a specific instance regime, not for every recommender system. It keeps products indivisible, the per-customer recommendation size fixed, customer utilities, customer EF1, and producer exposure fairness—the authors’ underlying problem.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, with an algorithm polynomial in \(n,\tau,k\), and the bit length \(L\), but that is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 7.2. The theorem’s round-robin construction gives a plausible route: compress repeated customers into masses of identical types and advance allocations in blocks. The main technical burden is proving that this preserves pairwise EF1 and the producer guarantee while producing a compact bundle-mass description. The paper’s running time is polynomial in \(m\), the number of individual customers; it does not establish that this compression is possible. The number of possible bundles can also be exponential when \(k\) varies, so a polynomial-size output representation is part of the question.

This mirror covers the theorem’s algorithmic fairness guarantees, especially exact bundle size, customer EF1, and the asymptotic producer \(\alpha\)-MMS fraction. It does not claim to mirror the experiments’ utility trade-offs or FairRecPlus’s empirical improvements. Further questions include whether the guarantee can hold for every product, how to optimize total customer relevance subject to these fairness constraints, and whether the finite and continuous versions admit useful approximation or parameterized transfers.

The weakest point is the compression step: exact relevance types can still require many distinct bundles, and EF1 constrains the entire support of the allocation, not just average utility by type. A naïve fractional allocation would lose the paper’s individual fairness question. That caveat does not erase the mirror; it identifies the computational question the continuous version should ask.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 7.2: it gives a polynomial-time algorithm with explicit customer- and producer-side guarantees. The strongest case against mirroring it is that the paper’s computation is built around assigning whole \(k\)-product bundles and comparing each customer’s bundle with every other customer’s. In the proposed continuum, splitting a type’s mass across bundles does not average away those comparisons: every bundle used by positive mass must still satisfy EF1 against every other used bundle. So the mass variables do not immediately yield the kind of tractable LP that makes many ChoCo mirrors attractive. With variable \(k\), even listing the possible bundles takes \(\binom{n}{k}\) terms, and the paper gives no structure for compressing that support or pricing bundles efficiently. The mirror may remain a substantial bundle-selection problem whose main combinatorics lie in products and recommendation size.

There is also a regime concern. The paper’s relevance scores are personalized; for its distance-based and latent-factor models, exact relevance vectors may make nearly every customer a separate type. A more convincing high-multiplicity case is a platform that sends mass promotions to large cohorts scored by shared profiles. But that moves toward segment-based recommendations and away from the individualized setting evaluated in the paper. This weakens the claim that the mirror captures its central use case.

Neither point defeats the stronger version of the mirror. The paper is model-agnostic, explicitly discusses mass promotions, and the proposed model preserves indivisible bundles and individual EF1 while making the population continuous. A segment-based recommendation service could plausibly be one of the authors’ problems. The bundle-support difficulty is a research obstacle, not evidence that the question is meaningless; its answer could itself be valuable. I find no existing continuous treatment in the paper that settles the computational question.

So the negative case is weak. Theorem 7.2 survives as a credible mirror, even though the paper does not show that its algorithm compresses to time polynomial in the number of types. I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
