The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying named computational result. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. Its central claim in Section 5 is a conceptual argument, not a named computational result; the Puppe–Xu ranking is a formula the paper cites, not a result it establishes. So there is no honest anchor for the required one-problem-per-result mirror.

The narrowest positive lead is an unanchored extension: a continuous CAP audit for a large population facing the same pair of choice sets \(A\supset B\). An instance would give a finite support \(T\) of complete preference-and-cares types, rational masses \(\mu_t\), and, for each type, its non-adaptive and adaptive preferences plus the care information needed to test dominated worseness. For each type and preference state, apply the paper’s self-expressive Puppe–Xu comparison to \(A\) and \(B\). The task is to output the exact mass
\[ \Delta=\sum_{t\in T:\,\rho_t^{0}(A,B)\ne \rho_t^{1}(A,B)}\mu_t, \]
the share of society whose freedom comparison changes after preference adaptation. A plausible regime is a large population evaluating a shared public-policy or employment menu, with many people but a moderate number of repeated evaluative profiles. Given the full type data, this looks tractable by evaluating each type’s comparison and summing its mass; under the paper’s stated assumptions, it predicts \(\Delta=0\).

The weak point is substantial: the paper studies each person’s ranking, not a population audit, and it does not establish the required type data can be elicited. The audit adds a computational population question the authors might recognize as an application of their ranking, but it is not a mirror of a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Its formal object is an individual’s ranking of choice sets, and its contribution is a conceptual argument about adaptive preferences and self-expression. The Puppe–Xu formula is a ranking rule the paper uses; it is not a complexity theorem or an algorithmic result established here.

The proposed population audit is the best route toward a continuous version, but its computation is just a typewise check followed by a weighted sum. Given explicit preference and care data, evaluate each type’s two freedom comparisons and compute \(\Delta\). With the paper’s assumptions, those comparisons are meant to agree under adaptation, so the audit mainly checks whether the supplied types satisfy those assumptions. If the care information is not supplied, the hard part becomes eliciting or inferring it—an empirical and philosophical problem the paper discusses, with no computational model for it. In either case, population mass adds aggregation but no substantive computational structure.

A stronger extension would aggregate each type’s rankings across many choice sets, or use them to choose which opportunities a population receives. The first remains a collection of typewise comparisons and weighted sums. The second needs a social aggregation rule, a resource constraint, or another collective objective; the paper supplies none. Those additions might define worthwhile research, but they would introduce a new population-level problem rather than continuize one of this paper’s computational results.

The negative case is strongest against calling this a mirror of the paper: there is no computational result to anchor it, and the proposed audit is computationally thin. It is weaker as a universal claim. A large population repeatedly evaluating common policy or employment menus is plausible, and a richer collective model could be valuable. But its value would depend on modeling choices that go beyond the paper’s individual freedom rankings.
