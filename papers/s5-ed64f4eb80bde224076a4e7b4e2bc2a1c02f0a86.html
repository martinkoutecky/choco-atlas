What the mirror covers

The candidate extends only the §5.6 crowd-size comparison of Ranking and Averaging against delegation; the paper's other aggregation-rule simulations and analytic performance bounds receive no computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

There are no eligible anchors in this paper. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result; its findings are Monte Carlo comparisons of expected portfolio performance. The closest lead is §5.6: in simulations, crowd Ranking begins to outperform delegation to three experts at around \(N=13\), and Averaging at around \(N=15\). Those are unnumbered simulation findings, so they cannot serve as the required anchors.

The strongest plausible mirror would continue that crowd-size question. Imagine an open-innovation platform with many evaluators drawn from a small set of expertise groups. A type is an evaluator’s expertise value \(e\); \(\mu_e\) is that group’s share of the evaluator population. For a slate of \(n\) projects, each evaluator of type \(e\) perceives project \(i\)’s quality as \(q_i+\eta_i(e)\), with the paper’s noise law. In the continuum limit, the population’s Borda score for project \(i\), conditional on project qualities and types, is

\[ B_i=\sum_e \mu_e\sum_{k\ne i}\Pr\!\left(q_i+\eta_i(e)>q_k+\eta_k(e)\right). \]

The organization selects the \(m\) projects with the highest \(B_i\).

A candidate computational problem, Continuum Crowd Portfolio Evaluation, would take the project-generating distributions, \(n,m\), and a finite expertise support with rational masses \(\mu_e\). It would ask for an additive-\(\varepsilon\) approximation to the expected total true quality of the \(m\) projects selected by those continuum Borda scores. An answer is a number within \(\varepsilon\) of that expected performance. For bounded, efficiently sampleable quality distributions, this looks tractable by randomized approximation: sample project slates, compute the pairwise win probabilities and selected portfolio, and average its quality. This is a plausible mirror of the crowd-scaling analysis, not a result proved by the paper.

The regime has some support in the text: §5.6 considers crowds up to \(N=1000\) and discusses open innovation and external evaluators. A large evaluator pool divided among a few stable expertise groups is a credible high-multiplicity setting. I would limit the scope to the crowd-performance results in §5.6; this does not supply a mirror for the paper as a whole, and it does not meet the required named-anchor standard.

The main weakness is substantial: as \(N\) tends to infinity, averaging the paper’s zero-mean perceptions recovers each project’s true quality. Averaging may therefore select the truly best \(m\) projects, washing out the finite-crowd effect that motivates the Ranking comparison. So this is a defensible continuous version of the crowd-size question, but a poor basis for claiming that continuization preserves the paper’s central Ranking result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no eligible anchors to defeat: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary about computational complexity. Its claims about Ranking, Delegation, and crowd size—including the §5.6 thresholds around \(N=13\) and \(N=15\)—come from Monte Carlo comparisons of expected performance. The proponent correctly identifies that gap, but then offers a new candidate problem rather than a mirror of a computational result.

That candidate has a credible population story. An open-innovation platform could draw many evaluators from a small set of expertise groups, with \(\mu_e\) representing each group’s share. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; grouping evaluators by expertise is not a distortion. For a fixed project slate, the continuum Borda scores are weighted pairwise win probabilities, and the selected portfolio is the set with the highest scores.

The problem is what the candidate asks us to compute. Expected portfolio quality is the paper’s performance statistic, estimated by simulation; the paper does not study an algorithmic decision problem over a society given as a distribution. With bounded, efficiently sampleable inputs, the proposed additive approximation is generic Monte Carlo estimation. A more elaborate exact or approximation problem could be defined, but that would be a new stochastic performance-analysis project, not a computational result from this paper. That is not an objection that the answer might be easy: a tractable result can be valuable. It is an objection that the candidate’s apparent algorithmic task comes from the analyst’s chosen sampling-oracle formulation, rather than from a computational question the paper poses.

One could instead ask how much evaluator mass must be shifted to change the selected portfolio. That would create a clearer intervention problem, but it adds adversarial control absent from the paper, which explicitly leaves strategic behavior for future work. It would be a new research direction, not a stronger mirror of one of the paper’s findings.

So the negative case is strongest under the requested anchor standard: none of the paper’s named findings is an eligible computational result, and the proponent’s §5.6 proposal does not supply one. The broader claim that no continuous-population question could ever be worthwhile is less secure. The evaluator-pool scenario is plausible, and a well-defined computational problem could be built around it. The paper alone, however, does not establish that such a problem would be a worthwhile continuous mirror of its contribution.
