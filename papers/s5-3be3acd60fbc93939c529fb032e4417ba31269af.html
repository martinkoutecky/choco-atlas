This paper has no eligible named computational anchor. Its numbered results are analytical bargaining results: Proposition 1 establishes unique equilibrium payoffs and agreement after the election; Proposition 2 bounds when delay can occur; Proposition 3 gives conditions for agreement or delay; Propositions 4–8 characterize gridlock and policy outcomes. None asserts the complexity or algorithmic tractability of a computational problem.

There is a plausible population-level extension: represent voters as finitely many response types, with \(\mu_t\) the share of society of type \(t\), and let policy \(z\) affect electoral support. One could ask for the subgame-perfect equilibrium and the states where the parties delay agreement. A large electorate with relatively few response types makes that a credible high-multiplicity setting, and the bargaining question is close to the paper’s subject.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive issue is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–8 establish bargaining and gridlock properties: unique equilibrium payoffs, bounds on delay, conditions for agreement, and how electoral incentives affect policies. None formulates a computational problem or gives a complexity or algorithmic result. Proposition 2’s reference to bounds being “easy to compute” does not supply one: there is no input representation or running-time analysis. The case for also identifies no eligible anchor, so there is no anchor-specific continuous question to rescue.

The proposed voter-type extension is the strongest route to a population model, but it would add a population variable the paper does not use. The strategic game has two parties; voters appear only through the scalar popularity state \(x_t\) and the reduced-form policy effect \(\xi(x,z)\). If voter types determine that effect, their masses might enter as an aggregate such as \(\xi(x,z)=\sum_t \mu_t\Delta_t(x,z)\). The paper already allows arbitrary continuous \(\xi\), so this type distribution would parameterize a primitive rather than expose a new mass-allocation or other high-multiplicity computational problem. Evaluating such an aggregate is straightforward; computing equilibrium would be a dynamic-game problem whose population support is not the source of its computational structure.

A voter-level account of popularity noise would also need new assumptions. Idiosyncratic vote fluctuations tend to average out as the electorate grows; retaining the paper’s Brownian popularity shocks as common shocks leaves them as a primitive independent of the type masses. Either route could motivate a separate computational bargaining project, perhaps with an explicit behavioral microfoundation. The honest weakness in the negative case is that this project might be worthwhile to the authors. But it would be a new analysis built around added voter behavior, not a continuous mirror of any result in this paper, and the paper itself gives no reason to expect ChoCo’s population-continuization tools to matter.
