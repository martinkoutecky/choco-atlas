What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror covers only per-item aggregation after ratings are supplied; it leaves the cross-item matrix-factorization and regression estimation pipeline aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is the paper’s post-estimation aggregation step. For each item, make a type \(t=(g,r)\), where \(g\in\{E,C\}\) is expert or crowd and \(r\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}\) is the rating. The mass of a type is its fraction within that group. This is a complete type for this step: once the ratings for an item are supplied, the aggregation objective uses group membership and rating, not individual identities. The regime could be a large rating or annotation platform with many crowd raters and a sizable pool of expert reviewers per item. Each group may have millions of members but at most five rating types per item. The groups are normalized separately because the paper measures each group’s satisfaction by its average distance from the proposed grade.

A precise candidate is Kalai–Smorodinsky Mass Aggregation. For each item, the instance gives rational distributions \(\mu^E,\mu^C\) over \(\{1,2,3,4,5\}\). For \(g\in\{E,C\}\), define \(S_g(x)=5-\sum_r\mu^g_r|x-r|\), the group’s satisfaction with grade \(x\in[1,5]\). Let \(x_E\) and \(x_C\) be the lowest grades maximizing expert and crowd satisfaction, respectively. Set \(E_1=S_E(x_E)\), \(E_0=S_E(x_C)\), \(C_1=S_C(x_E)\), and \(C_0=S_C(x_C)\). Choose the smallest \(\lambda\in[0,1]\) satisfying
\[ \frac{\lambda E_1+(1-\lambda)E_0}{\max(E_1,E_0)} = \frac{\lambda C_1+(1-\lambda)C_0}{\max(C_1,C_0)}. \]
The requested solution is the lowest grade \(x\) maximizing \(\lambda S_E(x)+(1-\lambda)S_C(x)\). This carries over the paper’s group satisfaction and its Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining selection, replacing lists of individual ratings with type masses. I would expect it to be tractable: the satisfaction functions are piecewise linear, and their maximizers are weighted medians, so the exact computation is polynomial in the support size and mass bit length. Natural follow-up questions concern the corresponding Nash version and whether the grade-estimation stage can also be represented over a small set of types.

There is, however, no qualifying named anchor in this paper. It has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Equations (7)–(12) formulate the optimization methods, and Section 6 reports experimental comparisons—including that the bargaining methods outperform the tested baselines on the reported datasets—but those are not numbered computational results. So the problem above is a plausible mirror of a modeled and evaluated subproblem, not a continuous problem anchored to a named result as requested.

The mirror is deliberately narrow: it covers aggregation after ratings have been estimated, not the matrix-factorization and regression pipeline. Its weakest point is that the paper’s reported data are sparse per item—often only three crowd ratings—so the high-multiplicity regime fits a different deployment or the post-imputation population better than the study’s observed per-item votes. And even there, the aggregation problem appears routine; it offers little prospect of showing discrete hardness dissolving in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that there is no named computational result to mirror. The paper gives algorithms and optimization formulations, then evaluates them experimentally; it proves no complexity classification or algorithmic guarantee. The proposed mass aggregation therefore mirrors a modeled component, not a result of the kind this exercise asks analysts to anchor on. The matrix-factorization and regression pipeline does not supply a stronger anchor: its evidence is predictive performance on two datasets, and it depends on linking each voter’s ratings across alternatives.

The proposed type space is nevertheless a good fit for the *post-estimation* aggregation step. For each item, the paper’s average-distance objective can be written exactly using masses \(p^g_r\) over group and rating types. A large review platform is a plausible setting, and the authors could recognize that question. The obstacle is that this gives a continuous formulation of one aggregation calculation, while the paper’s wider computational pipeline depends on cross-item voter information that those per-item masses discard. A fuller mirror would have to retain that information in voter types, which under the paper’s sparse, heterogeneous setup means carrying each voter’s response pattern rather than reducing the society to a small set of rating types.

That is a strong reason the paper offers no qualifying anchor and only a narrow candidate mirror. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: the mass-based aggregation problem is a sensible high-multiplicity object. I would not reject it merely because its answer may be easy or because it covers only one component; neither is a valid objection under the programme’s criteria.
