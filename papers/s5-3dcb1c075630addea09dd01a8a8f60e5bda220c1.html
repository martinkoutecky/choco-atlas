The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited at the anchor stage: this paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Equations (1)–(5) define bankruptcy rules; Equations (10)–(14) formulate the Nash allocation model; Tables 3–6 report calculations for the Pakistan instance. Table 6’s finding that the proportional rule has the lowest dispersion in that instance is a numerical comparison, not an eligible complexity anchor. So there is no lead anchor and no per-anchor mirror question to hand a prover.

There is still a plausible, but unanchored, continuous-population problem in the paper’s power-allocation setting. Call it High-Multiplicity Power Bankruptcy Rationing. Types are electricity consumers who share all relevant features: province, demand, minimum acceptable service, bargaining weight, and any transmission-loss parameters. Type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t\), per-person claim \(q_t\), and disagreement allocation \(d_t\). Let \(e\) be available power per person, and let \(y_t\) be the per-person allocation to that type. The instance consists of the finite type list and binary-rational values for these quantities and weights \(w_t\). A feasible allocation satisfies
\[ d_t \le y_t \le q_t,\qquad \sum_t \mu_t y_t \le e. \]
The task is to find an allocation maximizing the weighted Nash product
\[ \prod_t \bigl(\mu_t(y_t-d_t)\bigr)^{w_t}, \qquad \sum_t w_t=1. \]
An exact optimum or a specified-accuracy approximation is a solution. With only the four provincial types, the masses could be the population shares reported in the paper: \((110,48,30,12)/200\). A more realistic regime would split provinces into a moderate number of customer and grid-location types, while representing millions of residents.

The expected direction for this bare model is tractable: it is a separable weighted Nash allocation problem with linear resource and bound constraints. That makes it a clear continuous-optimization question, but not a case where a known discrete hardness result might dissolve. The mirror covers only the paper’s bankruptcy and Nash power-allocation framework; it does not rely on the fact that megawatts are divisible.

The regime is plausible because power-shortage allocation concerns large populations, and the paper itself uses population in setting provincial bargaining weights. The weak point is substantial: the paper’s bargaining agents are four provinces, not their residents. Treating residents as a continuous population changes who bargains, and province-level data do not establish that residents within each type share all relevant demands and constraints. Authors might accept this as a high-multiplicity extension of their allocation problem, but the paper supplies no named computational result for it to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in the paper to anchor a mirror. Its named bankruptcy rules are allocation formulas, and the Nash bargaining model in Equations (10)–(14) is applied to four provinces; the paper gives no complexity theorem or algorithmic analysis. Table 6’s ranking is a calculation for that instance, not a computational result. So the proposed consumer model is an adjacent research question, not a continuous version of a result this paper establishes.

The key change is who bargains. In the paper, provinces are the agents; population helps set a province’s bargaining weight. Residents are beneficiaries of provincial allocations, not individual claimants or negotiators. Treating residents as bargaining agents requires new per-person claims, disagreement points, and a rule for their bargaining power. In the proposed objective, \(\prod_t(\mu_t(y_t-d_t))^{w_t}\), the mass terms are constants in the objective, since \(\prod_t\mu_t^{w_t}\) does not affect its maximizer. One could instead weight each type’s log utility by its population mass, but that makes a substantive new choice about whose bargaining power counts.

The strongest repair is to study repeated customer classes or many local distribution units under a continuous population model. That could be sensible power-allocation research, but it supplies new agents and a new bargaining problem; the four-province study does not motivate or analyze it. My negative case is therefore strong against calling this paper a worthwhile continuous mirror, but not airtight against the broader possibility of a useful continuous power-rationing model.
