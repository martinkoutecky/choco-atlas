The case FOR (proponent)

The honest limitation is that this paper has no named computational-complexity result: it proves structural properties of cooperative games, but does not classify a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or otherwise. So I can make a concrete positive case for a continuous mirror, but I cannot present it as a mirror of discrete hardness that might dissolve.

The closest anchor is Theorem 2, which is reproduced from elsewhere rather than proved in this paper: Slikker et al. (2001), an unpublished working paper, with the authors’ permission. It says that for any newsvendor situation in the paper’s setup, if retailers’ demands are independent and \(X_i\sim\mathrm{Norm}(\mu_i,\sigma_i)\), then the associated newsvendor game is convex. That structural result is the basis for the mirror below.

The setting I find plausible is a large regional retail network whose outlets jointly order a common product. The agents are outlets; a type is a complete demand profile, here a normal demand law \(\mathrm{Norm}(\alpha_t,\sigma_t)\), with the common purchasing and selling prices included. Think many thousands of outlets but only a modest number of recurring demand archetypes. A type’s mass is its share of the network. This is a natural high-multiplicity regime: outlets of the same type are interchangeable in the model’s costs, prices, and demand.

Lead candidate: Gaussian Continuum Newsvendor Convexity. Given a population of size \(M\), rational type shares \(\mu_t\), normal demand parameters \((\alpha_t,\sigma_t)\), and common prices \(p\ge c>0\), consider coalitions with type masses \(a_t\in[0,M\mu_t]\). Define their total demand by \(D_a\sim\mathrm{Norm}(\sum_t a_t\alpha_t,\sqrt{\sum_t a_t\sigma_t^2})\), and their value by \(V(a)=\max_q\mathbb E[-cq+p\min(q,D_a)]\). The question is whether this mass-indexed cooperative game has increasing marginal contributions: for every \(a\le b\), type \(t\), and feasible \(\delta\ge0\), does \(V(a+\delta e_t)-V(a)\le V(b+\delta e_t)-V(b)\)? A solution is a proof that the condition holds for every such coalition, or a violating pair and increment.

This fractional-coalition model has a specific justification: Gaussian demand is infinitely divisible. Aggregating \(a_t\) retailer-equivalents gives mean proportional to mass and variance proportional to mass, so the coalition value remains coherent at fractional masses. For \(y=1-c/p\) and \(K_y=\int_0^y\Phi^{-1}(u)\,du\), the value has the form \(V(a)=p\bigl(y\sum_t a_t\alpha_t+K_y\sqrt{\sum_t a_t\sigma_t^2}\bigr)\). Since \(K_y<0\), its marginal contribution for type \(t\) increases as coalition mass grows. The continuous game is therefore expected to be tractable: the formula directly establishes convexity. It also gives a type-symmetric core allocation, if desired: with \(A_t=M\mu_t\), pay each unit of type \(t\) mass \(\rho_t=p\bigl(y\alpha_t+K_y\sigma_t^2/\sqrt{\sum_s A_s\sigma_s^2}\bigr)\). Further questions include whether useful non-Gaussian demand families admit a coherent fractional-mass model, and whether their continuous games retain convexity.

I would expect this candidate to be Class A, but that should be kept in proportion: it extends an already tractable structural result, rather than showing that continuization removes hardness. The paper’s Theorem 1, proved here, reinforces the underlying direction: independent symmetric unimodal demands give a convex game when \(p=2c\). I would not make it a separate anchor, since it supplies no distinct computational result.

The weak point is the mass extension. Fractional retailer-equivalents are not automatically meaningful for arbitrary demand laws; the Gaussian convolution rule is what makes this particular mirror defensible. That restriction narrows the case, but it still leaves a plausible, precise continuous population problem closely tied to the paper’s central question about convexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Its theorems concern convexity and marginal contributions in cooperative games. Theorem 2 is the closest anchor, but it establishes a structural property; Theorem 1 and the later uniform-demand results do the same. The paper does not classify the complexity of computing coalition values, finding a core allocation, or calculating a solution concept. Those are possible new questions, but they are not the questions its results answer.

The Gaussian population model is nevertheless a credible mathematical mirror. A large retail network can have many outlets of the same demand type, and Gaussian demands give a coherent fractional-mass extension. In the proponent’s notation, \(V(a)=p\bigl(y\sum_t a_t\alpha_t+K_y\sqrt{\sum_t a_t\sigma_t^2}\bigr)\). That lets one ask whether marginal contributions increase with coalition mass. But this is a continuous structural convexity question, rather than a computational one of the kind ChoCo is set up to chart. The same issue applies to a mass version of Theorem 1: narrowing the demand family or fixing \(p=2c\) does not turn a convexity claim into a complexity question.

One could extend the model to ask for core allocations or other solutions in a population game. That may interest cooperative-game theory, but it would be a new problem motivated by the paper. In the Gaussian case, even the proposed type-level core allocation follows directly from the displayed value function. To get a substantive algorithmic question, one would need to add a computational task and specify how general demand distributions are represented; its difficulty would then depend on those choices, not on a computational result in this paper.

I cannot honestly argue that no plausible continuous population model exists. The retail-network example is sensible, and Gaussian infinite divisibility handles the fractional-mass concern. The negative case is narrower: under ChoCo’s computational scope, that plausible mirror does not yield a computational question anchored in the paper’s results. If the programme also wants continuous structural results about cooperative games, this paper becomes a stronger candidate.
