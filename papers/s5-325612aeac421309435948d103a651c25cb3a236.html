The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many complete advertiser types \(a\), each with rational mass \(\mu_a\), values \(v_{aj}\), and maximum prices \(m_{aj}\), and slot classes \(j\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\) and reserves \(r_j\), does a feasible envy-free mass assignment and price vector always exist, and can one be computed in time polynomial in the number of types, slot classes, and input bit length? The variables are assignment masses \(x_{aj}\ge 0\) and class prices \(p_j\), with \(\sum_j x_{aj}=\mu_a\), \(\sum_a x_{aj}\le q_j\), and unlimited dummy supply; each positive assignment must be to a reserve-feasible class giving its type maximum utility, with utility \(v_{aj}-p_j\) when \(p_j<m_{aj}\) and \(-\infty\) otherwise. For the bidder-optimal version, require each type's utility to be at least its utility in every other feasible envy-free mass outcome.

The model it lives in

A capacitated matching market with a continuum of advertiser mass divided among finitely many complete types. Slot-class supplies scale with total population, prices are common within each class, and \(x_{aj}\) records the mass of type \(a\) assigned to class \(j\); the dummy option absorbs unassigned mass.

The objection that survived

The one-shot assignment model may fit sponsored search less closely than markets where an advertiser's budget spans repeated impressions; the proposed slot scaling does not establish that the new market captures the paper's motivating application.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's feasible envy-free outcome and running-time result, and Theorem 2's bidder-optimality result. It leaves the incentive-compatibility results, the general-position condition, and the generalized-utility extension unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can feasible envy-free mass outcomes and a type-wise bidder-optimal outcome be computed exactly in time polynomial in the support size and input bit length?
  • Does the type-utility vector remain well-defined and attainable when slot copies are represented by common-price classes?
  • How do these outcomes relate to finite markets obtained by replicating type masses and slot supplies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a large sponsored-search clearing market with repeated slot classes. Advertisers and slot inventory both grow with the market: if the population has size \(N\), class \(j\) has \(Nq_j\) interchangeable slots. That supply scaling is necessary for a population of mass one to be assigned; it is also the kind of co-modification the programme allows.

A type \(a\) is a complete advertiser profile: its values \(v_{aj}\) and maximum prices \(m_{aj}\) for every slot class, together with any other parameter the market uses. Let \(\mu_a\) be the fraction of advertisers of type \(a\), with \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\). The classes have reserves \(r_j\) and per-capita supplies \(q_j\). All these inputs are binary rationals. The dummy option has value and price zero and unlimited supply. Thus a large market might have millions of advertisers but only dozens of types and a modest number of slot classes; advertisers who differ in even one relevant value or cap belong to different types.

The paper’s Theorem 1, proved here, says that for explicitly listed bidders and items—with valuations, item reserves, and bidder–item maximum prices—the Modified Hungarian Method finds a feasible, envy-free matching in time \(O(nk^3)\). Feasibility means every bidder has nonnegative utility, prices are nonnegative, and matched items meet their reserves. Envy-freeness means no bidder could obtain higher utility from another item at its price. A matched bidder’s utility is \(v_{ij}-p_j\) when \(p_j<m_{ij}\), and \(-\infty\) when \(p_j\ge m_{ij}\). The paper proves this theorem; it is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result.

Its continuous counterpart is Mass-EF Matching with Budgets. Given the type masses, values, caps, reserves, and slot supplies above, return rational prices \(p_j\) and assignment masses \(x_{aj}\ge0\). Require \(\sum_j x_{aj}=\mu_a\) for every type and \(\sum_a x_{aj}\le q_j\) for every real slot class; the dummy absorbs any mass assigned to the outside option. An assignment to class \(j\) is allowed only if \(p_j\ge r_j\) and that class gives type \(a\) maximum utility, where its utility is \(v_{aj}-p_j\) if \(p_j<m_{aj}\), and \(-\infty\) otherwise. The question is whether such an assignment and prices can be found in time polynomial in the number of types, slot classes, and input bit length. The expected direction is tractable, Class A: the assignment side is a rational-capacity matching problem, and the paper’s price-adjustment method gives a plausible starting point for handling reserves and budget cutoffs.

The stronger anchor is the paper’s Theorem 2, also proved here: the Modified Hungarian Method finds a *bidder-optimal* outcome. In the paper’s definition, every bidder’s utility in that outcome is at least their utility in every other envy-free outcome. The theorem itself states the method’s correctness; Theorem 1 supplies its polynomial running-time bound.

The corresponding lead question is Mass-Bidder-Optimal Matching with Budgets. It has the same instance and feasibility conditions as Mass-EF Matching. A solution is an assignment \(x\), prices \(p\), and resulting type utilities \(U_a\), with the additional requirement that \(U_a\ge U'_a\) for every type \(a\) in every other feasible, envy-free mass assignment and price vector for that instance. The objective is componentwise utility optimality across types, not aggregate welfare maximization. I expect this to be tractable, Class A, by adapting the price updates and matching structure to rational type masses and capacities. This is my strongest anchor: it mirrors the paper’s central equilibrium-quality result, while Theorem 1 anchors the weaker task of finding any feasible envy-free mass outcome.

The regime is plausible in a large advertising exchange clearing many comparable searches: advertiser cohorts can share value and payment-cap profiles, while repeated placements supply each slot class at a rate proportional to the cohort size. The paper’s prices, budget cutoffs, reserves, and envy-free condition remain central. Replicating each rational type mass and slot supply by a common denominator gives a finite matching-market interpretation. For fixed prices, fractional feasible assignments can be integralized in the corresponding bipartite flow network. This supports the model’s connection to the paper’s problem; it does not establish the desired compressed-input running time. The paper has no numbered NP-hardness theorem, so these are algorithmic anchors; there is no discrete hardness claim here whose disappearance in the continuum could be tested.

The further questions are whether bidder-optimal type utilities and prices can be computed exactly in time polynomial in the compressed input; whether the type-utility vector is unique even when assignments are not; and how the result relates to the finite market obtained by replication. The paper’s \(O(nk^3)\) bound does not answer the first question: it processes explicitly listed bidders, and a common denominator can be exponentially large in the rational input’s bit length.

That is the weakest point in the case. The market must scale item supply along with population, and the paper’s proof does not directly give a running-time bound in the number of types and bit length. Still, this is a credible mirror: it preserves the underlying matching equilibrium with reserves, hard payment caps, and envy-freeness, while asking whether its Hungarian-method structure survives when equivalent agents and repeated slots are represented by masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to both anchors is that the proposed mirror needs a substantial market change before it becomes a nondegenerate population limit. In the paper, bidders and items form a one-to-one matching market. If only the bidder population grows while the item set stays fixed, the mass receiving real items tends to zero. The proponent avoids that collapse by scaling slot supply with population and assigning a common price to each slot class.

That is a coherent capacitated market, but its plausibility depends on the application. In sponsored search, an advertiser usually competes for many impressions and faces a budget across them. The paper instead assigns each bidder at most one item. Scaling up one-shot assignments preserves the paper’s matching structure; modeling repeated impressions would require a different consumption and budget model. This gives a reason to doubt that the proposed mirror captures the paper’s central application, though it does not rule out a large market of one-time assignments.

For Theorem 1, the best version of the mirror is a rational-capacity matching market with one type per complete value-and-cap profile, and envy-freeness defined over mass assignments. That is a meaningful question. But the paper’s \(O(nk^3)\) bound does not answer whether it can be solved in time polynomial in the support size and the bit length: replicating rational masses may require exponentially many bidders. The theorem therefore supplies a starting point, not a result about the compressed problem. That gap is real, but it is precisely a computational question the programme could investigate.

The same objection applies to Theorem 2, with an additional modeling choice: bidder optimality in the paper compares each bidder’s utility across all envy-free outcomes. The mass version compares utilities by type, while common prices for slot classes restrict which outcomes count. One could instead allow prices to vary across identical slot copies, or use a capacitated market directly. Either choice leaves a well-posed question about type utilities and optimality, even if neither inherits the paper’s theorem automatically.

So the negative case is weak. The paper’s most realistic sponsored-search interpretation may not fit the proposed one-shot, supply-scaled market, and neither theorem transfers a compressed-input algorithm. But type cohorts and supply that scales with a large market are plausible, and the resulting questions preserve the paper’s equilibrium concepts. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; the available objections show that the mirror needs careful modeling and fresh complexity results, not that it fails the programme’s test.
