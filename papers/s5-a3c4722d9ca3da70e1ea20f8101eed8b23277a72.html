What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous-population extension of this paper’s no-regret migration policy, but there is no named computational result here to anchor it in the sense your instructions require. Propositions 1–5 concern comparative statics, welfare, and decentralization. The paper numerically computes allocations and subsidy rates, but gives no named complexity result or computational formulation theorem.

The closest result is Proposition 5, proved in this paper. It says the no-regret allocation \(\bar m\) can be decentralized using country-specific subsidies \(\bar p_j\) and lump-sum taxes \(\bar q_j\), with \(\bar p_j\bar m_j=\bar q_j\), and

\[ \frac{\bar p_j}{n_j}u'(\bar c_j) -\beta\frac{\mu_0}{\bar l_0} \left(\frac{f(\bar l_0)}{\bar l_0}-f'(\bar l_0)\right) u'(\bar c_0) \sum_{k\ne j}\frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_j} =\frac{1}{\lambda_j}, \]

where the multipliers \(\lambda_j\) solve the first-order conditions in Equation (9). Section 3.4 computes these rates. That is a useful analytical characterization, but it is not a result that an allocation problem is in P, nor a computational formulation theorem of the kind that qualifies as an anchor under the stated rule.

If carrying the mechanism into ChoCo anyway, the most credible scenario I see is a large set of host regions with many residents but few complete voter types: residents of each type share the relevant income, immigration preferences, and policy environment. The regions could be subnational labor markets in rich democracies, each setting its own admissions rate. Mass \(\mu_t\) is type \(t\)’s share of the resident population; \(x_t\) is its per-capita admissions rate. Given a baseline nationalist rate \(\tilde x_t\), type payoff \(V_t(x_t,M)\), and \(M=\sum_t\mu_t x_t\), the candidate problem is

\[ \text{maximize } M \quad\text{over }x_t\ge 0 \quad\text{subject to}\quad V_t(x_t,M)\ge V_t(\tilde x_t,\tilde M) \ \text{for every supported type }t. \]

A solution is the admissions schedule \(x_t\); a version mirroring Proposition 5 would also return type-specific subsidies and lump-sum taxes that implement the schedule while balancing the fund. This covers only the no-regret allocation and its decentralization, not the paper’s other comparative statics or its calibration exercise.

My expectation is Class A for a restricted version where the welfare constraints are convex and the functions have an explicit, efficiently evaluable representation; then the problem is a finite-dimensional continuous optimization problem in \(\tau\). The paper does not establish those conditions for its full model, so the general version remains open. Further questions include whether the calibrated functional forms yield convex constraints, how to compute an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal schedule in \(m,\tau,L,\varepsilon\), and which type information must be retained for the schedule to remain faithful.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s decision-makers are a finite set of countries, each represented by a homogeneous citizen, whereas this mirror treats a large population of residents and moves the policy units to regions. That is a plausible extension of the coordination mechanism, but I can’t claim it is an anchored computational mirror of a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 5 is not a computational result. It characterizes subsidies and taxes that decentralize the no-regret allocation; it gives no complexity bound or algorithmic theorem. The paper’s computations calibrate and solve its model for a handful of country sets. Its underlying optimization already chooses continuous migration quantities \(m_j\). So computing \(\bar m\) or the subsidy schedule may be a worthwhile question in its own right, but it is not a computational result about a discrete population that this paper establishes and a continuous mirror can sharpen.

The best candidate mirror would keep the paper’s actual decision makers: treat many host jurisdictions as a distribution over types \((n_j,\mu_j,\gamma_j)\), then compute type-specific admissions and subsidies subject to each type’s no-regret constraint. That is more faithful than making residents the decision makers. But it continuizes the *jurisdictions*, not the population of residents. In the paper, countries choose \(m_j\); their citizens enter through a representative utility, and \(n_j\) already scales the model’s per-capita quantities. Replacing residents with a continuum therefore leaves the number of policy choices and participation constraints at \(J\). If the jurisdictions themselves are replicated, multiplicity is doing the work; a large set of interchangeable treaty participants is a new regime that needs its own case.

The proposed regional version runs into the same distinction. If each region has one admissions decision and one regional no-regret constraint, resident types do not create new decision variables. If each resident type instead gets its own \(V_t\) constraint, the problem has changed: Proposition 5 constrains each country’s representative citizen, not every resident class. Subnational governments would also need authority to admit migrants and join the fund—institutions the paper does not model.

That leaves a possible project on computation for many migration-policy jurisdictions, but it does not rescue a continuous-population mirror of a named computational result here. This is the strongest honest negative case; it is a mismatch of object and contribution, not a proof that no related model could be worth studying.
