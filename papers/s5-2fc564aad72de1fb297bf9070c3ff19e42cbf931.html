The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a valid anchor-backed positive case under your rules: this paper has no named computational result asserting a complexity classification or algorithmic bound. Theorems 2.1 and 2.2 are named, but they establish statistical properties. Theorem 2.2 says a weighted average of reliable forecasts is marginally consistent; if it gives positive weight to two forecasts that differ with positive probability, it is unreliable, and its reliable version has greater variance. It also bounds the average’s variance by the largest individual variance, with equality only in the stated case where the average and revealed aggregator coincide with a maximum-variance forecast. None of that is a result about computational complexity.

The nearest possible lead is Section 3’s unnumbered optimization procedure. In a large panel with many forecasters drawn from a few identical forecasting pipelines, let each type \(t\) have mass \(\rho_t\) and a forecast vector \(x_t\) over \(K\) recurring questions. One could fit type weights \(q_t\), an extremization factor \(\alpha\), and a pivot \(b\) to minimize \(\sum_{k=1}^K [\alpha(\sum_t q_t x_{tk}-b)+b-y_k]^2\), subject to \(q_t\ge0\), \(\sum_t q_t=1\), and \(\alpha\ge0\). This is the paper’s fitted-aggregation problem expressed over types, and its reparameterization yields a convex quadratic program. But it is not an eligible anchor. With unrestricted type weights, the population masses can be absorbed into those weights; making mass materially constrain influence would add a modeling assumption the paper does not supply. The paper therefore supports, at most, a tentative high-multiplicity forecasting extension—not a sound anchor-based continuous mirror for this programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent supplies no eligible anchor. Theorem 2.1 and Theorem 2.2 concern reliability, variance, and information; neither states a computational result. That is a strong reason to reject an anchor-backed mirror of this paper.

The closest candidate is Section 3’s fitted aggregation problem. Suppose forecasters fall into \(\tau\) types, each with the same forecast vector across the \(K\) questions. The fitted weights on individuals then collapse to one total weight per type. Because the paper lets weights vary freely over a simplex, any total weight is possible regardless of the type’s population mass. Multiplicity disappears from the optimization. Fixing weights to equal the population shares, or constraining them according to those shares, would make mass matter, but it changes the aggregation rule the paper studies. The resulting problem is still an ordinary convex quadratic program, not a new computational bottleneck.

A more convincing type definition must also preserve the information the paper’s results concern. Forecasters who truly have identical forecasts and information add no new information when duplicated. If their information or realized forecasts differ, grouping them under a broad label such as “same model” can hide the very heterogeneity that the revealed aggregator uses. One can imagine a large panel with repeated forecasting pipelines, but the paper gives no computational question about assigning influence to such types, and adding one would require extra modeling choices.

So the strongest negative case is that there is no worthwhile *anchor-based* continuous mirror here: the named results are statistical, and the nearest algorithmic procedure already reduces to a standard convex program without a meaningful role for population mass. I cannot establish the universal claim that no useful scenario could ever be built; that part of the negative case is necessarily weaker, since a mass-constrained forecasting problem could be sensible as a new question.
