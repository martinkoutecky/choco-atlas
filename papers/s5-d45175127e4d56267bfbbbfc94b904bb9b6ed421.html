The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\), a population scale \(N\), a finite legal type set \(\mathcal X\subseteq\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^m\), an approximation target \(\rho>0\), and an audit budget \(B\), consider societies \(\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal X)\) with \(N\mu_t\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\). Does there exist a mechanism truthful for each individual and each legal report profile that achieves welfare at least \(\rho\) times optimum on every legal society while using at most \(B\) expected individual audits on truthful societies? For the Theorem 6.1 witness, take \(\mathcal X=\{\mathbf e_j,\delta\mathbf e_j:j\in[m]\}\).

The model it lives in

Each type \(t\) has a complete utility vector \(x_t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^m\), and the society is the mass distribution \(\mu\), with \(N\mu_t\) individuals of each type. The aggregate outcome weights are \(w=N\sum_t\mu_t x_t\); the mechanism chooses a lottery \(q\in\Delta([m])\), audits individual reports, and excludes detected liars. The objective is expected utilitarian welfare, with the audit budget measured in individuals.

What the mirror covers

Covers the verification-resource lower bound and Partial Power construction in Theorems 6.1 and 8.1; leaves the facility-location results, Theorem 7.1, and the other mechanism variants outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the minimum audit budget depend on \(m\), support size \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and population scale \(N\)?
  • Does a model auditing positive population mass admit nonvacuous individual incentives in an atomless limit?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity, mass-encoded version of utilitarian voting. The population—not the outcome space—is the continuous object. Keep the paper’s \(m\) alternatives discrete, and keep its output as a lottery over them. A lottery is already part of the original model; it is not the reason this is a population mirror.

Let a type \(t\) be a complete utility vector \(x_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^m\), together with any report or verification attributes the mechanism uses. A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over these types, where \(\mu_t\) is the population fraction. To preserve the paper’s individual incentives and audit operation, include a population scale \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) an integer: there are \(N\mu_t\) individuals of type \(t\), each with the same utility and audit conditions. The aggregate weight of outcome \(j\) is
\[ w_j=N\sum_t\mu_t x_t(j). \]
The mechanism chooses a lottery \(q\) and a set of individual reports to verify; detected liars can be excluded as in the paper. Its objective is expected utilitarian welfare, \(N\sum_t\mu_t x_t\cdot q\), while the resource being minimized is the expected number of individuals verified on truthful reports. Thus \(\mu\) is the compact society description, and \(N\) retains the individual granularity the audit model needs.

A plausible regime is a large public-service or public-project decision with \(m\) discrete alternatives and many residents sharing a small number \(\tau\) of complete, certifiable benefit profiles. The paper’s own lower-bound construction uses at most \(2m\) types and \(N=m\nu\) people, so the number of people can greatly exceed the number of types. It also relies on rare high-value subtypes; that is a real feature of the witness, not something to smooth away. The scenario is most convincing where reported utility types can be checked against records. The authors should recognize this as their utilitarian-voting problem: agents have the same cardinal utilities, the mechanism chooses among the same alternatives, and verification still targets individual reports.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper. It states that any randomized truthful mechanism achieving a constant approximation ratio for every number of agents and every number \(m\) of outcomes must use expected verification \(\Omega(\log m)\). The paper’s witness has \(m\) groups, each with many agents who value only their group’s outcome, at either value \(1\) or a very small \(\delta\). The maximum welfare across groups is \(\Theta(\log m)\) on the hard family. Truthfulness forces verification when an agent’s report could raise an outcome’s probability, yielding the logarithmic audit lower bound.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Mass-Coded Minimum Verification. Its input is \(m\), \(N\), a finite legal type set \(\mathcal X\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^m\), a target constant approximation ratio \(\rho>0\), and an audit budget \(B\). Each legal society is a rational \(\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal X)\) with \(N\mu_t\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\). The question is whether there exists a randomized selective-verification mechanism, truthful for every individual and every legal report profile, whose expected welfare is at least \(\rho\) times optimum and whose worst-case expected verification on truthful societies is at most \(B\). A solution is such a mechanism; a negative answer needs an impossibility proof. For the paper’s witness, take \(\mathcal X=\{\mathbf e_j,\delta\mathbf e_j:j\in[m]\}\), with \(\mathbf e_j\) the unit utility vector for outcome \(j\). The theorem’s lower bound transfers to these mass-coded profiles. I expect this audit-resource lower-bound question to be Class B in the narrow sense that the discrete lower bound transfers. This is a verification-resource result, not an NP-hardness or running-time classification.

A complementary anchor is Theorem 8.1, also proved here. For every \(\epsilon>0\), it establishes parameters for which Partial Power is truthful and robust, achieves a \((1-\epsilon)\)-approximation to social welfare, and verifies at most \(O(\ln m/\epsilon^2)\) agents on truthful inputs. It may output a null outcome, valued at zero by everyone. The corresponding question is Mass-Coded Partial-Power Construction: given the supported utility types, their rational masses, \(N\), and \(\epsilon\), construct an exact-truthful mechanism with that welfare guarantee and audit bound. Its solution is the Partial Power protocol applied to the aggregate type weights, with sampled individuals audited as in the paper. This is the strongest companion to Theorem 6.1: for any fixed \(\rho<1\), the two results give matching \(\Theta(\log m)\) audit growth. I expect the construction side to be tractable as a mechanism-construction and audit-resource question. The paper says its mechanisms are implementable in polynomial time in \(n,m\); whether the protocol can be implemented directly from \((m,\tau,L,\log N)\) without expanding the population is a further computational question.

These anchors cover only the general utilitarian-voting results on verification and approximation, not every result in the paper. Useful next questions are how the minimum audit budget depends on \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\); whether support restrictions improve the logarithmic lower bound; and whether a formulation that audits positive population mass has a nontrivial analogue.

The weak point is substantial: a strictly atomless population does not preserve the paper’s individual incentive problem. One person has zero mass and cannot change aggregate reports, while finitely many audits remove zero mass and cannot eliminate a positive-mass group of liars. The defensible mirror therefore keeps individual audit granularity \(1/N\) alongside the continuous mass description. Also, neither anchor is a standard \(P\)-versus-\(NP\) result. The positive case rests on a genuine high-multiplicity population representation and a sharp audit-resource frontier; it should not be presented as a new atomless mechanism-design theory or as classical computational hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The difficulty for both anchors is the unit that the paper audits. A report belongs to a named individual, truthfulness compares that person’s truthful and false reports, and a caught liar is removed before the mechanism runs again. The population’s size \(N\) therefore matters in a way that its type distribution \(\mu\) alone does not capture.

For Theorem 6.1, the best mass-coded version retains \(N\), requires \(N\mu_t\) to be integral, and counts audits of individuals. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity finite model, but it keeps exactly the granularity that makes the theorem work. Normalize audits as population mass and the lower bound becomes \(\Omega(\log m/N)\), which vanishes as \(N\) grows. Drop \(N\) and use an atomless population, and a single agent’s report has no effect on \(\mu\): the individual truthfulness constraint becomes vacuous, while auditing a measure-zero set cannot change the society or the mechanism’s recursive step. A positive-mass audit would define a different resource and require a different incentive model. Thus the proposed lower bound is either a restatement on a finite grid or a quantity that disappears in the continuum.

Theorem 8.1 has the same problem. Its welfare rule can be evaluated from aggregate type masses, but its claim of truthfulness depends on auditing individuals and excluding detected liars. In an atomless model, excluding a sampled individual leaves \(\mu\) unchanged; auditing no mass makes individual deviations irrelevant, while auditing positive mass changes the mechanism and its resource bound. The partial allocation and lottery are not the obstacle—the population-level welfare calculation survives. The theorem’s audit and incentive guarantees do not survive as stated. Its mechanism is also a direct construction, not a computational-complexity result about a population intervention.

A large public-service population with many residents sharing certifiable utility profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s results support treating that regime seriously. But the proposed mirrors preserve the finite audit unit. Keeping it gives a useful finite-granularity mechanism-design question, not a genuinely continuous-population version of these theorems; removing it changes or trivializes their central guarantees.

That is the strongest negative case, and its limit is clear: I cannot honestly rule out the finite-granularity, high-multiplicity question as worthwhile ChoCo work. The case against is strong for a strict continuous mirror of Theorems 6.1 and 8.1, but weak as a universal claim against any high-multiplicity reformulation.
