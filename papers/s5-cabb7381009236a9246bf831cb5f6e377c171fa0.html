What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s top-layer channel assignment, not of the whole sensing framework.

My anchor is Proposition 8: with the network parameters fixed, the proposed top-layer coalition-formation algorithm converges in at most \(N^M\) switches. The paper says the proof is in the authors’ earlier CISS 2015 paper [14], so this is cited here rather than proved here. It is a named algorithmic result, but it is only a finite termination bound: it is exponential in the number of SUs, not a polynomial-time result. The paper’s separate, unnumbered complexity expression does not strengthen it into one.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large, mostly stationary campus or industrial cognitive-radio network. It might contain thousands of SU transmitter–receiver pairs but only a few dozen complete radio/link types: pairs with the same sensing quality on each channel, data rates, traffic parameters, and other attributes the game uses. The type is that full channel-by-channel profile; its mass is the fraction of SU pairs of that type. This makes the paper’s repeated low-mobility setting the relevant scenario, rather than assuming that arbitrary mobile users are interchangeable.

I would call the mirror Continuous Two-layer Nash-stable Channel Assignment. An instance has a finite channel set \(\mathcal N\), finite type support \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), the channel-dependent sensing and link parameters for each type, and the paper’s sensing and access parameters. A feasible decision is an allocation \(x_{tn}\geq 0\), where \(\sum_n x_{tn}=\mu_t\): \(x_{tn}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to channel \(n\).

For a concrete version, fix the paper’s AND-rule and 0/X access model. Let \(\kappa\) be the number-equivalent SU density per unit mass, so channel \(n\) has \(q_n=\kappa\sum_t x_{tn}\) SU-equivalents. Extend the paper’s sensing and payoff formulas to these masses: the type-level false-alarm rate \(p_{tn}(x)\) uses the paper’s AND-rule formula with channel population \(q_n\); the grand-coalition false-alarm probability is the product integral
\[ P_n(x)=\prod_{t\in T}p_{tn}(x)^{\kappa x_{tn}}, \]
and its access value is \(U_n(x)=\beta_n(1-P_n(x))\). The type’s per-agent access payoff \(a_{tn}(x)\) is the corresponding continuous extension of the paper’s Nash-bargaining allocation, and its expected-rate utility is \(u_{tn}(x)=a_{tn}(x)R_{tn}\). Thus the model preserves the paper’s two-layer structure: a sensing/access payoff on each channel, then channel choice based on individual rate and the combined payoff of the two affected channels.

A solution is an allocation \(x\) with no profitable type-mass switch. Specifically, for any occupied source channel \(n\), destination \(\tilde n\), type \(t\), and transferable mass \(0<\delta\leq x_{tn}\), let \(x'\) be the allocation after moving \(\delta\) from \(x_{tn}\) to \(x_{t\tilde n}\). The switch is profitable if both
\[ u_{t\tilde n}(x')>u_{tn}(x) \quad\text{and}\quad U_n(x')+U_{\tilde n}(x')>U_n(x)+U_{\tilde n}(x). \]
The requested output is an allocation with no such switch; an algorithmic version could ask for an \(\varepsilon\)-stable allocation, meaning no switch improves both quantities by more than \(\varepsilon\). Inputs can be rational and the \(Q\)-function quantities evaluated to the requested precision.

I think authors could recognize this as their channel-partition question: the same complete SU attributes determine payoffs, and the same individual-plus-social preference governs movement. It also asks a computational question about computing a stable assignment, rather than treating continuity of sensing probabilities or spectrum outcomes as the objective. Its scope is only Proposition 8’s top layer; the sensing and payoff equations supply the model, but I am not claiming a mirror of every proposition.

For the stated approximate problem, my tentative expectation is Class A when \(\tau=|T|\) is moderate: the decision has \(\tau N\) mass variables, and continuous optimization may avoid enumerating individual SU moves. That is a research bet, not a consequence of Proposition 8. A key follow-up is whether the aggregate channel-payoff objective has enough concavity or other structure for efficient optimization; if not, the mass-level problem could instead be continuum-specifically hard. This anchor gives no basis for claiming hardness transfers from the discrete problem.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s \(N^M\) bound is about a finite sequence of unilateral SU switches. In an atomless population, one infinitesimal agent has no effect on the channels’ combined payoff, so a literal Wardrop-style deviation would erase half of the paper’s preference test. The proposed mirror therefore lets positive mass of identical SUs move together. That is a defensible fluid adjustment model for repeated SU classes, but it is also the modeling choice the authors or a referee are most likely to challenge.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole anchor is Proposition 8, and the strongest objection is that its result is about a finite-player switching process: from an initial partition, one SU moves at a time, and the process terminates within \(N^M\) switches. It does not give a polynomial-time algorithm for finding a stable assignment, or a complexity classification for doing so. The paper’s separate complexity expression includes the convergence time as an input to the bound; it does not establish that the process converges quickly. So a continuous question about computing stable channel loads would be a new computational problem, not a direct continuation of the result Proposition 8 proves.

There is also a real modeling gap in the proposed instance regime. A complete type would have to retain each SU pair’s sensing and link-quality profile across channels. In the paper’s randomly positioned network, such profiles will generally be distinct. A network with many exact repetitions is possible, but needs a special repeated geometry; grouping merely similar profiles into types changes the payoffs and sensing probabilities. That makes the proposed “few dozen types” scenario less natural for this paper’s own model than the proponent suggests.

The deeper issue is stability. In a normalized atomless society, one SU has zero mass, so its move leaves the aggregate channel payoffs \(U_n\) unchanged. The social-improvement condition in (3) then cannot be strictly satisfied. Allowing a positive mass \(\delta\) to move restores that condition, but turns a unilateral SU switch into a coordinated bloc deviation. Taking the limit of a one-SU move, \(\delta=1/M\), instead requires defining a derivative-based stability test. Either repair may yield a coherent load-assignment problem, but neither preserves the finite-player process or its termination argument. The proposed single \(\varepsilon\) for both rate utility and probability-valued social utility also needs separate tolerances, though that is readily repairable.

I would therefore reject the proposed mirror as a mirror of Proposition 8: it trades the paper’s individual switching dynamics for a new continuous stability concept. But this does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. A mass-flow channel-assignment model for a deliberately repeated network could be meaningful, and the authors might recognize it as their population-level channel-choice problem. The negative case is weakest exactly there: ruling out that broader mirror would take more than showing that the original convergence theorem does not carry over.
