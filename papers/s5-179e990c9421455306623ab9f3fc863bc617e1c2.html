What the mirror covers

The candidate extension concerns Proposition 6's sufficient condition for comparing allocations; the other propositions also provide axiomatic welfare results, not computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it has no named computational result of the kind the programme requires as an anchor. Propositions 1–7 are proved here, but they establish welfare comparisons from axioms; none classifies a problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally. The paper’s talk of a “tractable criterion” is about deriving a usable welfare ordering, not an algorithm or complexity result.

The closest lead is Proposition 6 (proved here). It says that if a social ordering satisfies Weak Pareto, Independence, and Pigou-Dalton, then allocation \(x_N\) is strictly preferred to \(x_N'\) whenever the minimum lower healthy-equivalent income in \(x_N\) exceeds the minimum upper healthy-equivalent income in \(x_N'\).

A plausible *near-miss* mirror would concern a large health-policy population grouped into a small number of recurring preference types. Let \(T\) be those types, with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\), and let \(P_t^*\) be type \(t\)’s incomplete preference relation. Given two feasible policies assigning health-consumption bundles \(x_t\) and \(y_t\) to each type, ask whether

\[ \operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{t\sim\mu} E_{\inf}(x_t,P_t^*) > \operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{t\sim\mu} E_{\sup}(y_t,P_t^*). \]

For finite support, a solution would be a yes/no answer, with the types attaining the minima as witnesses. This makes the population continuous and gives a concrete comparison question. But it is not an admissible anchor: the paper does not formulate or compute this decision problem. And if the healthy-equivalent-income bounds are supplied as input, the comparison is just a minimum calculation; the paper gives no computational result about deriving those bounds from preferences. Moreover, under this maximin test the masses matter only through which types have positive mass.

So the health-policy scenario is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and Proposition 6 is the paper’s closest substantive connection. The weakest point is decisive: the paper’s results are axiomatic welfare results, while the programme asks for computational questions anchored in named computational results. A continuous-population extension of Proposition 6 would be a new question, not a computational mirror of a result established in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 6 is the proposed anchor, and it fails the programme’s computational gate. It says that certain axioms force one social comparison when the minimum lower healthy-equivalent income in one allocation exceeds the minimum upper income in another. Like Propositions 1–5 and 7, it is an axiomatic welfare result: the paper gives no computational problem, input representation, algorithm, or complexity classification. “Tractable criterion” here means a usable welfare criterion, not a claim about computational tractability.

The health-policy setting is plausible, and its best continuous version is easy to state. Let types \(t\) have masses \(\mu_t\), incomplete preferences \(P_t^*\), and policy bundles \(x_t\) and \(y_t\). The natural population analogue replaces the finite minima in Proposition 6 with essential infima over the type distribution. That could be a sensible extension of the welfare framework. But if the healthy-equivalent-income bounds are supplied, checking the resulting comparison on finite support takes \(O(\tau)\) time; the masses matter only through which types have positive mass. The proposition does not characterize the full social ordering, so this check certifies a sufficient condition, rather than deciding whether one allocation is socially preferred under the paper’s axioms.

A stronger computational proposal would optimize redistribution or policy subject to a resource constraint, perhaps maximizing the worst-off type’s lower bound. That may be worthwhile as a new problem, but the paper specifies no feasible policy set, resource constraints, input model for preferences, or method for computing the bounds from choice data. Those choices would determine the computational problem and its complexity. The paper’s concluding remarks do point to extending empirical estimation of equivalent incomes to incomplete preferences; that is a plausible research direction, but it is not a computational result here to mirror.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s stated scope: no proposed anchor survives as a mirror of a named computational result. It does not rest on denying that repeated preference types are plausible, or that a continuous welfare extension could be valuable. The broader claim that no scenario could ever yield worthwhile computational work is harder to defend; an independently specified policy-optimization problem might. It would, however, need its own computational foundation rather than inherit one from this paper.
