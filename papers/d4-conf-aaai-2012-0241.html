The case FOR (proponent)

Under the anchor rule, this paper has no eligible named computational result: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic classification. Its convex objective and iterative algorithm are substantive contributions, but they are presented in unnumbered prose and equations; the experiments are empirical results. So I cannot honestly offer a mirror anchored to a result of the required kind.

The strongest positive candidate is a continuous version of the paper’s central task: learning a base classifier from noisy labels supplied by heterogeneous workers. Consider a population of total worker mass \(M\), with finitely many types \(T\) and rational proportions \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type specifies workers with the same annotation protocol, feature assignment pattern, and response behavior. For each type, the input gives a rational finite measure \(\nu_t\) over feature-label pairs \((x,y)\); it records the annotations per unit of worker mass. Given \(\lambda,\eta>0\), find the base parameter \(w_0\) and one personal parameter \(w_t\) per type that minimize

\[ F(w_0,(w_t)_{t\in T}) = M\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\left[ \int \bigl(-y\log \sigma(w_t^\top x) -(1-y)\log(1-\sigma(w_t^\top x))\bigr)\,d\nu_t(x,y) +\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert w_t-w_0\rVert^2 \right] +\frac{\eta}{2}\lVert w_0\rVert^2. \]

The solution is the fitted base classifier \(w_0\), together with the type-level personal classifiers. This preserves the paper’s main modeling choice: fit worker models linked to a shared base model, and use the base model for prediction. With positive masses and regularization, the objective is strongly convex, so the minimizer is unique. The question is whether it can be computed to a specified accuracy with resource use governed by feature dimension, number of types, input bit length, and accuracy, rather than total worker mass.

A plausible regime is a large annotation operation for a recurring task, with many workers drawn from a small number of stable skill and response profiles and similar assignment patterns. There, \(M\) could be very large while \(|T|\) remains modest. The paper’s sum over workers makes this aggregation natural, and its explicit separation between a base classifier and personal classifiers gives the authors a reason to recognize the type-level problem. I would expect this version to be tractable through convex optimization, subject to explicit input bounds and an approximation model.

That is a candidate mirror, not an anchored case. It covers the core base-classifier estimation problem; it does not cover active learning, inference of latent true labels, or the paper’s comparisons with other methods. It also raises questions about whether the type-level fit agrees with the limit of individual-worker fits, how assignment patterns affect the number of types, and what complexity bounds hold for an \(\epsilon\)-accurate solution.

The weakest point is that the paper gives each worker a separate parameter \(w_j\), with a Gaussian shrinkage relation to \(w_0\). Pooling workers into shared type parameters is a homogeneous-population specialization; the paper does not analyze it, and its experiments do not establish a large-worker, few-type regime. The sum-decomposable objective and the central role of the base classifier make the specialization plausible, but the missing numbered anchor prevents this from satisfying the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Under the requested anchor standard, there is no named computational result here to mirror. The paper gives a convex objective and describes an iterative optimization method, but it states no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary with a complexity or algorithmic guarantee. The method’s runtime is not analyzed. A complexity classification for a continuous version would therefore be a new result about a new problem, not a mirror anchored to a result of this paper.

The proponent’s type-level estimator is the strongest candidate, but its two ways of relating it to the paper both have a cost. To preserve the paper’s separate personal classifier \(w_j\) for each worker, a type must capture that worker’s assignment pattern and observed labels closely enough to preserve their individual loss. Grouping workers with identical records is exact, but noisy responses make such groups unlikely to stay small as the crowd grows. If types instead represent broad skill profiles and \(\nu_t\) pools their responses, the model has one \(w_t\) per profile. That replaces the paper’s worker-level personal models with a tier-level model. It may be useful, but it does not establish a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s central formulation.

There is a more fundamental scope problem: the paper studies statistical prediction from noisy annotations. Its output is a classifier for future instances, not a collective choice, allocation, or winner. A large annotator pool makes a continuous-population learning problem plausible, but it does not make that problem computational social choice. Making the annotators into voters or the classifier into a social choice rule would change the task rather than produce a mirror.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo: no eligible anchor, and the most plausible population model is either an uncompressed restatement of worker-level data or a different learning model outside the programme’s scope. It is weaker as a universal claim about research value: a type-level crowdsourcing model could be worthwhile in machine learning. I would reject it as a ChoCo mirror, not claim that no useful continuous crowdsourcing model can exist.
