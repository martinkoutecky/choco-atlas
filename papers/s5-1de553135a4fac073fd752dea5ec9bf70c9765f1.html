The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no named complexity-theoretic result of the kind your anchor rule asks for: no numbered result classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its closest useful anchors are named results that compute or optimize limiting welfare. The positive case below rests on those, and treats them as analytic optimization results rather than claiming they establish a complexity class.

The lead anchor is Proposition 11, proved here. In a balanced large market with independent uniform preferences and one offer per hospital, fix an expected number \(x\) of applications per doctor. Among distributions of application quotas with that mean, welfare is maximized by assigning \(\lfloor x\rfloor\) applications to some doctors and \(\lceil x\rceil\) to the rest, in proportions that give mean \(x\). This is a direct result about how to allocate a finite per-person action across a large population.

A supporting anchor is Proposition 6, also proved here. In the same balanced, uniform-preference setting, when every doctor applies to \(k\) hospitals and each hospital selects one applicant, the limiting social welfare is
\[ 1-\left(1-\frac{1-e^{-k}}{k}\right)^k. \]
This gives a concrete welfare objective for the quota-allocation question.

The continuous mirror I would put to a prover is Fixed-Mean Fluid Quota Design. The instance is a unit mass of applicants and a unit mass of hospitals, a positive rational mean quota \(x\), and the paper’s one-offer, independent-uniform-preference rule. An applicant type is a quota \(\ell\) together with the common preference-generating law; let \(\alpha_\ell\) be the mass assigned quota \(\ell\). Choose nonnegative rational masses satisfying \(\sum_\ell\alpha_\ell=1\) and \(\sum_\ell \ell\alpha_\ell=x\). Each applicant makes \(\ell\) applications, hospitals select one applicant per received application pool, and an applicant with offers accepts one. The task is to return a mass allocation maximizing the expected matched mass. In the aggregate limit, a random application becomes an offer with probability \(p=(1-e^{-x})/x\), so the objective is
\[ \sum_\ell \alpha_\ell\bigl(1-(1-p)^\ell\bigr). \]
Proposition 11’s solution is to put mass \(\lceil x\rceil-x\) on quota \(\lfloor x\rfloor\) and mass \(x-\lfloor x\rfloor\) on quota \(\lceil x\rceil\); if \(x\) is integral, put all mass there. The expected behavior is tractable: this version reduces to a closed-form objective and the adjacent-quota solution.

The second, narrower mirror is Homogeneous-Quota Fluid Welfare: the same society and mechanism, but with all applicant mass at quota \(k\); compute the expected matched mass. Its solution is Proposition 6’s formula. It is also tractable, and supplies the objective used in the quota-design problem.

The regime is a very large recurring market—such as a national application cycle—with many applicants in each quota cohort and only a few quota types. The model makes population fractions the decisions: a policy can assign, for example, a fraction of the cohort one extra application. This is a plausible question for the authors because it preserves their central ingredients: limited applications and interviews, independent random preferences, deferred acceptance in the one-offer case, and expected matching size. It covers the single-offer quota-allocation results, not the paper’s general multi-interview analysis or its correlated-preference simulations. Further questions include whether analogous quota allocations optimize welfare with multiple interviews, heterogeneous applicant cohorts, or hospital-specific interview limits.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s preferences are independently realized rankings, while this mirror’s compact types share a preference *law*, not the same realized preferences. If ChoCo requires types to include each agent’s complete realized ranking, the support may grow with the population and the high-multiplicity compression disappears. Also, the paper already takes a large-market limit and computes these welfare expressions. So this is a credible population-mass formulation of its quota-allocation question, but a weak source of a new complexity-theoretic landscape: the two anchors are already answered analytically, and extending them into a richer computational problem would go beyond what those propositions establish.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I don’t see a worthwhile ChoCo complexity mirror in either anchor. Proposition 6 and Proposition 11 are already limiting-population results: the paper takes \(n\to\infty\), computes the limiting welfare, and, for quota allocation, identifies an optimum. Its named results give formulas and optimality statements; they do not pose computational problems with an input and an algorithmic output.

For Proposition 6, the proposed unit-mass model reproduces the same calculation. With \(p=(1-e^{-k})/k\), the matched mass is \(1-(1-p)^k\). Calling the applicants a continuum leaves no new computation to classify; the paper has already evaluated this large-market limit.

Proposition 11 is an even closer collision. At fixed mean quota \(x\), let \(\alpha_\ell\) be the mass assigned quota \(\ell\). The fluid objective is \(\sum_\ell \alpha_\ell(1-p)^\ell\) subtracted from \(1\), with \(p=(1-e^{-x})/x\), and the paper’s argument already shows that adjacent quotas maximize it. Theorem 10 supplies the resulting welfare formula. Theorem 13 also treats multiple interviews and gives a bound tight for adjacent quotas. Replacing cohort counts by mass fractions would restate these limiting results.

There is a real modelling snag behind the proponent’s “quota plus preference law” types. Deferred acceptance uses realized rankings over the opposing individuals, and hospitals distinguish applicants by those rankings. A shared distribution over rankings does not make two applicants identical in every respect the mechanism uses. If types include realized rankings, the type space changes with market size and rankings are typically almost all distinct. If types instead record only preference-generating laws, the model is a stochastic mean-field description; under the paper’s symmetric assumptions, that is precisely the kind of large-market behavior its formulas already analyze.

A recurring market with heterogeneous preference cohorts or interview policies could motivate a new optimization problem. But it would add structure the paper does not specify, and its computational question would need to be formulated independently. That makes the negative case strongest for the named anchors, rather than a proof that no useful extension could ever be devised. The source paper already does the relevant continuization and reports its answer; it leaves no anchored computational landscape for ChoCo to chart.
