The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), group labels \(g(t)\), eligibility sets \(A_t\), and per-capita resource capacities \(\kappa_j\), compute a maximum-throughput feasible flow \(y_{tj}\) satisfying \(\sum_{j\in A_t} y_{tj}\le \mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:j\in A_t} y_{tj}\le \kappa_j\), with group totals \(x_c=\sum_{t:g(t)=c}\sum_{j\in A_t}y_{tj}\) equalized as \(x_c/r(c)\), where \(r(c)\) is the maximum flow using only group \(c\).

The model it lives in

A hiring or resettlement society is a distribution over complete group-and-eligibility types; mass can be assigned by flow to eligible resource categories with per-capita capacities, and the objective is to maximize total assigned mass under opportunity fairness.

The objection that survived

The typed flow may reproduce the paper's existing fractional matching polytope, leaving little new support-sensitive structure to study; this limits the mirror's novelty but not its status as a continuous-population computational question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers typed bipartite matching, maximum opportunity-fair flow, and the corresponding price characterization; it leaves the paper's adversarial bounds, parametric bounds, and random-graph analyses unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the paper's polymatroid characterization and exact price computation extend to rational type masses with running time polynomial in the support size, resource categories, and input bit length?
  • As market size grows, can maximum fair typed flows be rounded to integral assignments with fairness and welfare guarantees analogous to the paper's finite-market rounding result?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible, deliberately narrow mirror here: the paper’s transversal matroid case, viewed as a large hiring or resettlement market with many applicants and relatively few distinct eligibility profiles.

A limitation up front: the paper has no numbered theorem stating that a problem is NP-hard, in P, or in a parameterized complexity class. The closest computational anchors are its named characterizations, which the paper uses to analyze fair allocations and the Price of Fairness. Proposition 3.2 (printed that way in the main text; Appendix E.3 calls it Theorem 3.2) says that the feasible group-allocation polytope is exactly
\[ \mathcal M=\left\{x\in\mathbb R_+^C:\sum_{c\in\Lambda}x_c\le r(\Lambda)\text{ for every }\Lambda\subseteq[C]\right\}, \]
where \(r(\Lambda)\) is the rank of the agents in those groups. Corollary 3.3, proved in the paper, then gives
\[ \operatorname{PoF}(\mathcal M) =\frac{r([C])}{\sum_{c\in[C]}r(c)} \max_{\Lambda\subseteq[C]} \frac{\sum_{c\in\Lambda}r(c)}{r(\Lambda)}. \]
These are structural results, not complexity-class claims; the paper computes with them, and says maximum-size fair fractional allocations are efficiently computable with a polynomial-time independence oracle.

Lead mirror, corresponding to Proposition 3.2: Typed Opportunity-Fair Matching. The instance has groups \([C]\), a finite list \(T\) of supported applicant types, and resource categories \(J\). Each type \(t\) has rational population mass \(\mu_t\), group \(g(t)\), and eligibility set \(A_t\subseteq J\). Each resource category \(j\) has rational per-capita capacity \(\kappa_j\). A type completely describes the relevant features: applicants of that type share their group and eligible resource categories. For example, a large hiring platform could have hundreds of thousands of applicants but only hundreds of supported group-and-qualification profiles, with job capacity growing with market size.

A solution is a nonnegative flow \(y_{tj}\) for eligible pairs, subject to
\[ \sum_{j\in A_t}y_{tj}\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t:j\in A_t}y_{tj}\le\kappa_j. \]
Let \(x_c=\sum_{t:g(t)=c}\sum_{j\in A_t}y_{tj}\), and let \(r(\Lambda)\) be the maximum flow when only types in groups \(\Lambda\) are present. The problem is to find a feasible flow maximizing \(\sum_c x_c\), subject to \(x_c/r(c)\) being equal across groups; assume \(r(c)>0\) for every group. Its solution is an explicit typed assignment flow. I expect this to be Class A, tractable: it is a rational flow or linear program, with runtime polynomial in the supported types, resource categories, and input bit length. Its aggregate feasible group allocations have the polymatroid description of Proposition 3.2.

Second mirror, corresponding to Corollary 3.3: Typed Opportunity-Fairness Price. On the same instance, compute the exact ratio of maximum total flow without fairness to maximum total flow with opportunity fairness, and return a group subset \(\Lambda\) attaining the displayed maximum. This is the underlying price-of-fairness question, not a question about an encoding. I expect Class A, tractable as well: subset ranks are max-flow values, and the ratio characterization can be approached with rank-oracle and parametric submodular optimization. This mirrors the paper’s stated polynomial-time computability for matroids with a polynomial-time independence oracle; the typed flow setting gives a natural rank oracle.

The population interpretation is plausible for these anchors. The paper itself motivates large markets, studies matching, and uses fractional allocations. Here \(\mu_t\) is the society: it says what fraction of applicants have each relevant type. The variables \(y_{tj}\) specify how that population is allocated. Scaling job capacities with market size is a natural co-modification of a large-market model. The mirror covers typed bipartite matching, its maximum perfectly opportunity-fair allocation, and its price. It does not claim to continuize every matroid instance or the paper’s random-graph asymptotics.

The weakest point is that the paper already studies fractional group allocations and large markets, so a referee could see this as a re-expression of its matching model. Also, the paper’s headline contribution is bounds on the price of fairness, not a complexity classification, and it supplies no hardness anchor from which to ask whether hardness dissolves. The positive case rests instead on a change in the input object—from individually specified applicants to a distribution over complete eligibility types—and on computational questions that become natural for that high-multiplicity market. Further questions include how far this typed flow formulation extends to other matroid classes, how approximate ex-post integral allocations behave as capacities grow, and what breaks when agents with the same eligibility profile have different individual weights.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that the paper’s named results are not complexity results. Proposition 3.2 characterizes the feasible group-allocation polytope, and Corollary 3.3 gives a formula for the price of fairness. The numbered results that follow bound that price or analyze its behavior under random inputs; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper does say that fair fractional allocations are efficiently computable, and that the price can be computed with a polynomial-time independence oracle. So a mirror would need to introduce a new, support-sensitive computational question rather than continue a complexity result the paper established.

The typed matching proposal is a sensible way to pose that question: eligibility profiles are complete types, and masses and capacities give a natural large-market flow model. But in this case the paper already studies fractional matching allocations and characterizes their aggregate feasible region as a polymatroid. The typed flow is a compact representation of that same fractional allocation structure. It gives an explicit flow algorithm parameterized by the number of types and resource categories, but the step from individual applicants to type masses does not expose a new structural problem for Proposition 3.2; it recasts its feasible set in flow coordinates. That leaves a potentially useful encoding result, but a narrow one.

Corollary 3.3 is a weaker anchor still. In the typed model, each rank \(r(\Lambda)\) is a maximum-flow value, and the price formula is the same subset-ratio characterization. The paper already states that the price is polynomial-time computable given a polynomial-time independence oracle. A support-sensitive analysis in \(\tau\), the resource categories, and the rational bit length could sharpen that claim, but the proposed mirror has not identified a missing computational question beyond this refinement.

This case against has a firm limit. The paper itself motivates large markets, studies matching, and uses fractional allocations. Nothing makes repeated eligibility types implausible, and treating a type-level average as fractional while each unit of mass receives an integral assignment is a natural model choice. I therefore cannot make the universal negative case convincingly: the typed matching mirror survives as a sensible high-multiplicity question, even if these two anchors offer little new complexity structure and the likely flow formulation is direct.
