The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: the paper contains no qualifying named computational result. It has empirical findings in Section 1.1 and formal definitions of fairness, but no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. So there are no paper-result anchors to restate, and I can’t supply one continuous problem per anchor. What follows is a plausible mirror of the allocation problem the paper formalizes, not a mirror of a computational result it proves.

My lead candidate is High-Multiplicity Equitability Allocation. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete additive valuation types over goods \(M\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Let \(q_g\in[0,1]\) be the per-capita supply of each good \(g\). Choose \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving the whole bundle \(S\subseteq M\), so that
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni g}x_{t,S}=q_g. \]
Each person still receives an integral bundle; only the mass assigned to each type-bundle pair is fractional. The objective is to minimize the range of utilities actually received,
\[ \Delta(x)= \max_{x_{t,S}>0}v_t(S) - \min_{x_{t,S}>0}v_t(S). \]
A solution is a feasible mass assignment attaining the minimum. Exact supply constraints keep the paper’s equitability objective from being vacuously satisfied by giving nothing to everyone; they fit its main-text partition model, though the appendix also allows disposal.

A plausible regime is a large public allocation of standardized goods to a very large population whose members fall into a modest number of genuinely identical valuation profiles—for example, cohorts defined by standardized need and valuation schedules. There, population size could be in the millions while \(\tau=|T|\) is in the tens or hundreds. The model uses type as the full valuation vector, not a loose demographic label. Per-capita supplies are the co-modification that keeps allocation meaningful as the population grows.

This is a credible computational analogue of the paper’s formal question of minimizing inequality while allocating indivisible goods. It does not mirror the paper’s main scientific claim about whether LLM responses align with human preferences: those experiments use a few named individuals and small instances, and study model behavior rather than algorithmic complexity. The authors might recognize the allocation problem as theirs, but not the empirical alignment claim as answered by it.

For this standalone candidate, my tentative expectation is Class C, as a research hypothesis rather than a result: fractional masses relax population counts, but each person’s bundle remains a subset of goods, and checking which bundles fit a utility interval can create knapsack-like pricing questions. Hardness does not automatically transfer from the discrete setting, since a type’s mass can be split across bundles; nor does an LP formulation by itself give an efficient algorithm if pricing is difficult. Natural follow-ups are whether the pricing problem is tractable, whether bounded \(m\) or restricted valuations yield algorithms, and how solutions relate to discrete high-multiplicity instances as \(N\) grows.

The soft spot is decisive: this paper supplies no named computational anchor, and its central contribution is empirical. The candidate makes the best case for a useful continuous problem underneath the paper’s allocation model, but it cannot honestly be presented as continuizing one of the paper’s proved computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is decisive at the paper level: there is no computational result to continuize. The paper reports empirical response patterns and formalizes fairness criteria; it proves no algorithmic or complexity theorem. Its human sample and repeated LLM queries are observations about decision makers, not a large population receiving goods. Treating those observations as masses would continuize the sample or response distribution, which is outside ChoCo’s population scope.

The proposed High-Multiplicity Equitability Allocation is mathematically coherent, yet it changes the task. The paper studies what humans and LLMs choose when asked for a fair allocation. The proposed problem asks an optimizer to find an allocation minimizing inequality. Equitability is one criterion used to assess responses; computing its optimum is not the paper’s computational question. The model also moves the population axis from decision makers to recipients. A more faithful continuous version of the paper’s question would aggregate LLM or human choices, which makes the choices—not the society—continuous. The self-serving-bias experiments make the mismatch especially clear: the decision maker’s assigned role is part of what the paper measures.

A stronger standalone allocation model could keep complete valuation types, per-capita supplies, integral bundles for each person, and mass assignments across bundles; it could also include envy-freeness and maximin. A public program allocating standardized goods to large cohorts is a plausible setting for that model. It would still study a new fair-division optimization problem, rather than computationally analyze the LLM–human alignment question in this paper. The authors may recognize the topic, but the paper gives no reason to treat the optimizer’s problem as their own.

So the negative case is strong against this being a continuous mirror of the paper, and the proposed anchor does not survive that test. The broader claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous problem is weaker: a large cohort with repeated valuation profiles is plausible, and the supplied material cannot rule it out. I would call that a worthwhile adjacent research direction, while rejecting it as a mirror of this paper’s results.
