The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are Definitions 1–5 (EF, EF1, PROP, PROP1, and MMS), not computational complexity results. The paper’s findings are experimental and statistical. Its statement that EF1 allocations exist and can be computed in polynomial time is attributed to Conitzer et al. (2017), so it is prior work, not a named computational result established here.

A possible follow-on would model a large allocation population by masses of agents with each additive valuation type, then ask for an allocation rule that maximizes the mass reporting their bundle acceptable. But that makes computational allocation the object of study; this paper studies reported perceptions in small experiments. It would be a new question, not a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s named results. So I can’t honestly state a per-anchor continuous problem or predict its complexity class.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Definitions 1–5 define EF, EF1, PROP, PROP1, and MMS; they are not complexity theorems. Hypotheses 1–3 and the findings about fairness, information, and framing are experimental claims about responses. The polynomial-time EF1 statement is attributed to Conitzer et al. (2017), not established here. So there is no paper-authored algorithmic or complexity result whose population version this study leaves open.

The proponent’s best proposed rescue is to give complete respondent types masses \(\mu_t\), let \(p_t(A,z)\) describe type \(t\)’s response to allocation \(A\) under treatment \(z\), and ask for an allocation maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t p_t(A,z)\). With \(A\) fixed, this is an aggregate response rate—the statistical quantity the experiment estimates. Optimizing over \(A\) would be a different allocation-design problem: this paper selects allocations by fairness property and measures how people judge them; it does not seek allocations that maximize acceptance. And the response policy \(p_t\) is the empirical object the paper investigates, not an input it provides. Supplying it assumes away the learning question; estimating it adds a new one.

The same issue applies to the three hypotheses. A mass of respondents could estimate how acceptance varies with a bundle’s value (H1), compare reactions to threshold and envy notions (H2), or measure treatment effects (H3). Those are sensible population-level statistical estimands. They do not, by themselves, define computational problems about allocating a continuous society. In the experiment, respondents judge scenarios involving four agents and ten goods; the respondents are not the agents receiving those goods. Treating respondents as the population to be allocated across changes the paper’s object and objective.

This makes the negative case strong against a paper-anchored computational mirror. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could ever be inspired by the paper: maximizing acceptance in a large housing, computing, or resource-allocation population could be useful if these authors adopted it as their problem. That would be a new research direction, though, not a continuization of a result this paper establishes.
