What the mirror covers

The proposed mass-based analogue concerns strong stability like Proposition 5.4; it supplies no computational mirror for that proposition or the Nash-existence results in Propositions 5.2 and 6.1.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that group formation is a natural setting for continuization. Imagine a large political or civic organization whose members form groups around policies or projects. Let \(T\) be the finite set of complete member types—preferences, utility from group size, and any characteristics affecting feasible alternatives—and let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s share of the population. A group is a measurable subpopulation, represented by its type masses; it chooses an alternative feasible for that group. A strong-stability question asks whether there is a partition of the population into such groups and choices that no subpopulation can leave together for a feasible alternative that strictly benefits every member. This keeps the paper’s core question: who groups with whom, and what do their groups choose? In this regime the number of people can be vastly larger than the number of types.

But there is no named computational result in the paper that qualifies as an anchor under your criteria. The closest substantive result is Proposition 5.4, proved here: within \(E''_{\mathrm{IR}}\), a strong Nash equilibrium exists. That is, with a finite one-dimensional alternative set, increasing feasible sets, and Euclidean preferences—utility given by minus distance from an agent’s ideal point, plus the common strictly increasing group-size benefit when the alternative is feasible—a strongly stable group structure exists. It is a useful existence theorem for a continuous-population analogue, but it says nothing about an algorithm or its complexity.

Propositions 5.2 and 6.1 are similar: they prove existence of a Nash equilibrium under, respectively, separable preferences with an anonymous increasing-returns feasibility correspondence, and separable preferences with decreasing returns. They do not establish tractability, hardness, or another computational classification. The paper gives no input encoding, running-time bound, or complexity reduction. So these propositions cannot honestly be presented as the computational anchors your brief requires.

The mass-partition problem above is a plausible follow-on question, but it would be new and unanchored here. Without a finite representation for utilities and feasibility, even its computational input is underspecified; this paper provides no basis for predicting whether the encoded version is tractable, inherits discrete hardness, or is continuum-specifically hard. Thus I can defend the model fit and a credible high-multiplicity regime, but I cannot supply a compliant per-anchor prover question for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to continue. Propositions 5.4, 5.2 and 6.1 prove existence of strong or ordinary Nash equilibria under different assumptions. Proposition 3.4 relates strong Nash equilibrium to the core. None defines a computational problem, gives an algorithm, or classifies its complexity. The paper’s questions are whether stable structures exist and how assumptions on preferences, feasibility and dimension affect existence. Asking how quickly one can find such a structure is a natural follow-on, but it adds a computational agenda the paper does not pursue.

For Proposition 5.4, the best candidate would be: given finitely many voter types with ideal points, increasing feasible sets, and a payoff bonus depending on group mass, compute a strongly stable partition of the continuous population. That is a sensible model of a large organization. Yet it is a new game specification. The paper’s theorem covers finite-player partitions with coalition-specific feasibility; a type-mass model needs feasibility and payoffs to be determined by type composition, plus a representation of those rules as input. The theorem’s existence proof gives no computational result for that model.

The same gap affects the Nash-existence results. Proposition 5.2 uses an anonymous feasibility correspondence and a potential over individual strategy profiles; Proposition 6.1 uses a different potential under decreasing returns. Their continuous counterparts could ask for an equilibrium among masses assigned to alternatives. To make those algorithmic questions precise, one must choose how \(h(|S|)\) becomes a function of group mass, how feasibility is encoded, and how a possibly fractional type population is partitioned. With finite support and an explicit encoding, those choices may yield worthwhile complexity problems. They are still newly formulated problems, rather than continuous versions of computational results in the paper.

The proposed large party or civic organization scenario is plausible, and finite types could preserve the distinctions among preferences and other relevant characteristics. That makes the underlying population regime credible; it does not supply the missing computational anchor. This negative case is therefore strong as a judgment about the paper’s fit for a programme specifically charting computational complexity. It is weak as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: a finite-type, mass-based equilibrium-computation project could be valuable, and the paper’s authors would likely recognize its subject. I can argue that it is a new project only loosely anchored here; I cannot honestly rule out that it is worth doing.
