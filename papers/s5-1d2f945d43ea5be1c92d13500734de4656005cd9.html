The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(E\) of contract types, rational available masses \(u_e\), complete transitive preference orders \(\succ_S\) for \(S\in\{D,H\}\), and rational polymatroid capacity regions \(\mathcal P_S\) (given by explicit partition or laminar constraints, or polynomial-time separation oracles), decide whether there is and output an allocation \(x\in\mathcal P_D\cap\mathcal P_H\) with \(0\le x_e\le u_e\) such that, for every \(e\) with \(x_e<u_e\), at least one side \(S\) has no \(y\in\mathcal P_S\) satisfying \(0\le y_f\le u_f\), \(y_e>x_e\), and \(y_f\ge x_f\) for all \(f\succ_S e\).

The model it lives in

A society of repeated applicant and employer profile types, represented by rational contract masses \(x_e\) and available masses \(u_e\). Each individual receives at most one integral contract; the mass vectors satisfy both sides' polymatroid capacities, and an unsaturated contract is blocked only when both sides can accept more of it without giving up mass on contracts they strictly prefer.

The objection that survived

The one-copy correspondence does not establish that the proposed polymatroid feasibility sets and side-specific willingness tests describe the limit of replicated finite markets at interior mass allocations.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the existence-and-search question for super-stable allocations in a profile-aggregated mass market. It does not transfer the finite fundamental-circuit algorithm or cover every arbitrary matroid-oracle instance; related continuum matching with finite capacities is nearby work, but does not settle this ties-and-matroids question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can existence and search be solved in time polynomial in the number of supported contract types and the rational input bit length for explicit partition or laminar capacities?
  • Do the proposed mass feasibility and stability conditions arise as the high-multiplicity limit of finite markets formed by replicating applicant and employer profile types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is Mass Super-Stable Common-Independent Allocation. Its setting is a large graduate or internship placement market: many applicants share a small number of complete profiles, and many employers or programs share a small number of preference and capacity profiles. A profile includes every feature the allocation uses—preferences, eligibility, and the constraints it consumes. Thus the number of people \(N\) can be far larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct contract types. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and it sits close to the student-project allocation problem that the paper identifies in Appendix A as a special case.

Here a type \(e\) is an acceptable contract between two profile classes. The population is represented by rational masses, and \(x_e\) is the mass assigned that contract. A partition or laminar capacity on applicants, projects, and employers is a natural example of the two sides’ constraints. More generally, let \(u_e\) be the available mass of contract type \(e\), and let each side \(S\in\{D,H\}\) have a rational polymatroid capacity function \(\rho_S\). Its feasible mass vectors are
\(\mathcal P_S=\{z\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^E:\sum_{e\in A}z_e\le\rho_S(A)\text{ for every }A\subseteq E\}\).
These are continuous counterparts of matroid independence polytopes. The feasible allocations are \(x\in\mathcal P_D\cap\mathcal P_H\) with \(x_e\le u_e\). Each infinitesimal person still receives at most one whole contract; \(x_e\) records the mass of people receiving it, rather than a fractional contract assigned to an individual.

Use the paper’s complete, transitive preference orders on contract types. Say side \(S\) is willing to accept more mass of \(e\) at \(x\) if there is a one-sided feasible vector \(y\in\mathcal P_S\), with \(y_f\le u_f\), such that \(y_e>x_e\) and \(y_f\ge x_f\) for every \(f\succ_S e\). In words, that side can take some more of \(e\) without giving up any mass on contracts it strictly prefers to \(e\); it may give up mass on tied or worse contracts. The named continuous problem is: decide whether a feasible \(x\) exists such that, for every \(e\) with \(x_e<u_e\), at least one side is unwilling to accept more mass of \(e\), and output such an \(x\) if it exists. Input masses and capacities are rational; a first computational version can use explicit partition/laminar constraints or polynomial-time separation oracles.

This preserves the paper’s stability condition rather than merely asking for a fractional matching. In the one-copy finite case, take \(u_e=1/N\), \(\rho_S=r_{M_S}/N\), and \(x=\mathbf 1_I/N\). Then willingness by side \(S\) to increase an omitted \(e\) is equivalent to being able to add \(e\) while retaining every selected contract strictly preferred to it. The matroid circuit exchange condition says this is exactly when \(e\notin\mathrm{dom}_S(I)\). Thus the continuous condition reduces to \(E\setminus I=\mathrm{dom}_D(I)\cup\mathrm{dom}_H(I)\), the paper’s super-stability definition. In the many-copy regime, the masses and rank capacities scale with cohort size, and type-level average allocations can be fractional even though each person’s assignment remains integral.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: “Algorithm 4 can solve the super-stable common independent set problem.” Fully stated, given two matroids on the same finite ground set \(E\), with every singleton independent, complete transitive preference relations on \(E\), and polynomial-time independence oracles, the algorithm decides whether a super-stable common independent set exists and outputs one when it does. The paper proves the algorithm runs in polynomial time. This is the only computational anchor I would use; the paper gives no comparable named hardness result. The mirror therefore extends an existence-and-search algorithm to continuous high-multiplicity societies. It is not a claim that discrete hardness dissolves.

I would provisionally expect the mass problem to be tractable. For a fixed allocation \(x\), each side’s willingness test is a linear optimization problem over its polymatroid. The paper’s result also gives a structural reason to look for a continuous algorithm based on matroid exchange. But constructing \(x\) is harder than running those tests: each unsaturated contract needs at least one side to reject it, creating a disjunction across contracts. A prover would need to show that the exchange structure resolves those choices efficiently. Further questions include whether the algorithm can run in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the rational bit length \(L\), independent of population size; whether finite replicated instances converge to this mass problem; and when a continuous super-stable allocation can be rounded to finite super-stable matchings.

The weakest point is that the paper proves no such extension. Its elements are individual contracts, whereas this mirror groups repeated agents into types and replaces their finite matroid constraints with mass capacities. Showing that those aggregate capacities faithfully represent a particular clone market—while retaining the relevant preferences—is part of the promised discrete-to-continuous dictionary, not something Theorem 1 already supplies. Still, the mirror is plausible: it keeps the paper’s contract structure and super-stability test, names a concrete many-agent regime, and makes population mass the object being allocated.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only computational anchor is Theorem 1, and it is a genuine one: Algorithm 4 decides whether a super-stable common independent set exists and finds one in polynomial time.

The strongest case against the mirror is that it changes the theorem’s central object. Algorithm 4 reasons about individual elements and their fundamental circuits in two finite matroids. The proposed mass model replaces that geometry with rational polymatroid constraints and defines a block through possible increases in contract mass. At a fractional allocation, there is no corresponding fundamental circuit; an increase may depend on a direction along a face involving several contract types. The one-copy scaling shows that the proposed condition agrees with the paper at particular integral points. It does not establish that it is the right limit for replicated markets, or that the circuit-based algorithm carries over. Preserving arbitrary matroid structure may require retaining distinctions among individual contracts; compressing to type-level partition or laminar constraints gives a narrower, newly formulated market problem.

There is also nearby continuous matching work: Arnosti studies mass-distributed student types, fractional matchings, and a deferred-acceptance analogue for finite-capacity schools. That makes the standard capacity case less novel, but it does not settle super-stability with ties under general matroid constraints, so it is not a collision with this anchor. [Arnosti, *A Continuum Model of Stable Matching with Finite Capacities*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881)

This objection still does not defeat the stronger mirror. A market with repeated applicant and employer profiles and explicit laminar capacities is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s student-project special case gives the authors a clear point of contact. I cannot honestly argue that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror. The negative case is that the manuscript does not yet establish the proposed population model’s fidelity or computational value; under the stated criterion, Theorem 1 survives.
