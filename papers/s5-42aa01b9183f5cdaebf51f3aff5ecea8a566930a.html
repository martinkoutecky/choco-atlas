The continuous mirror question

Given finite rider types \(r\) and driver types \(d\), rational masses \(\alpha_r\) and supplies \(\beta_d\), and pairwise scores \(w_{rd}\) and acceptance probabilities \(p_{rd}\), each rider assigned a configuration \(\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{\mathcal D}\) receives the paper's BA expected score \(V^{BA}_r(\mathbf{k})\). Choose masses \(x_{r,\mathbf{k}}\ge 0\) to maximize \(\sum_{r,\mathbf{k}}x_{r,\mathbf{k}}V^{BA}_r(\mathbf{k})\), subject to \(\sum_{\mathbf{k}}x_{r,\mathbf{k}}=\alpha_r\) and \(\sum_{r,\mathbf{k}}k_d x_{r,\mathbf{k}}\le\beta_d\) for each type. Can an additive-\(\varepsilon N_R\) approximation be found in time polynomial in the type support, input bit length, and \(1/\varepsilon\), where \(N_R=\sum_r\alpha_r\)?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity, two-sided dispatch model: rider types and driver types carry mass; each rider in a configuration receives an integer bundle of drivers by type; BA acceptance remains independent with the type-pair probabilities; the objective is total expected score and driver-type supplies constrain total mass assigned.

The objection that survived

Theorem 2.1's 3-PARTITION proof relies on indivisible assignments to force equal loads; mass can split across configurations, so that hardness mechanism does not carry over directly.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the BA branch of Theorem 2.1's single-cycle welfare problem. It leaves the FA results, dynamic marketplace effects, and joint optimization of match time and quality aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the configuration LP pricing problem, maximizing \(V^{BA}_r(\mathbf{k})-\sum_d\lambda_d k_d\) over integer count vectors \(\mathbf{k}\), be solved or approximated in time polynomial in type support, input bit length, and \(1/\varepsilon\)?
  • How does the mass optimum relate to finite discrete instances as the multiplicity of each type grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s Best Acceptance (BA) welfare problem. The population becomes continuous; each representative rider still receives an ordinary finite bundle of notifications and experiences the paper’s same stochastic acceptance process.

The lead anchor is Theorem 2.1, proved in this paper by a reduction from 3-PARTITION in Appendix A. It says that welfare maximization under either FA or BA is strongly NP-hard, even when all riders have identical pair parameters, every match weight is \(1\), and each acceptance probability is dyadic of the form \(p_{ij}=1-2^{-a_j}\) for an integer \(a_j\ge1\). Consequently, the general problem has no FPTAS unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). I would mirror its BA branch: it is a strong, clean anchor, and its restricted instances already have a natural type interpretation.

Call the mirror High-Multiplicity BA Notification Welfare. Let \(\mathcal R\) be a finite set of rider types and \(\mathcal D\) a finite set of driver types. A rider type \(r\) has mass \(\alpha_r\), a driver type \(d\) has supply \(\beta_d\), and each pair of types has a binary-rational score \(w_{rd}\in[0,1]\) and acceptance probability \(p_{rd}\in[0,1]\). A type is complete for this model: its members share the same relevant pair parameters against every type on the other side. Assume the total driver supply \(N_D=\sum_d\beta_d\) is an integer.

A notification configuration is a vector \(\mathbf k\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\mathcal D}\) with \(\sum_d k_d\le N_D\). It means that one rider receives \(k_d\) distinct drivers of each type \(d\). Let \(V^{BA}_r(\mathbf k)\) be that rider’s expected BA score: each notified driver accepts independently with probability \(p_{rd}\), and the highest-scoring acceptor wins, with score zero if no one accepts. Choose masses \(x_{r,\mathbf k}\ge0\), where \(x_{r,\mathbf k}\) is the mass of type-\(r\) riders assigned configuration \(\mathbf k\), to maximize
\[ \sum_{r\in\mathcal R}\sum_{\mathbf k} x_{r,\mathbf k}V^{BA}_r(\mathbf k) \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{\mathbf k}x_{r,\mathbf k}=\alpha_r \quad\text{for every }r, \qquad \sum_{r,\mathbf k}k_d x_{r,\mathbf k}\le\beta_d \quad\text{for every }d. \]
A solution is a finite list of configurations and their assigned masses. The computational question is whether one can find an additive-\(\varepsilon N_R\) approximation, where \(N_R=\sum_r\alpha_r\), in time polynomial in the support sizes, the input bit length, and \(1/\varepsilon\); exact optimization is a further question.

This is a plausible dispatch regime: a large city or service area over a cycle can have many riders and drivers but relatively few operational profiles, formed from service zone, vehicle class, rider constraints, distance band, and the platform’s predicted score and acceptance profile. The regime requires genuine repetition within those profiles; if every prediction is idiosyncratic, the type support grows with the population and the mirror loses its high-multiplicity advantage. Scaling the restricted Theorem 2.1 populations by a common factor gives the intended regime: many members per type while preserving the same type-level acceptance structure.

The authors should recognize the mirror. It preserves their single-cycle BA objective, the same acceptance uncertainty, and the exclusivity constraint on drivers. The co-modification is on the population side: finite supplies become type masses, and a continuum of riders can be divided across configurations. The paper’s configuration LP is relevant machinery, but in its finite-agent setting it is a relaxation; here, assigning mass to multiple configurations is itself a feasible population allocation. The mirror leaves each individual notification bundle discrete, so the expected-reward calculation still matches their protocol.

My forecast is Class A for approximation in a repeated, low-support BA regime: making rider mass divisible removes the need to round one integral configuration per named rider, leaving a configuration LP as the central object. The restriction in that forecast matters. The LP may have many configurations, and an algorithm polynomial in the number of named drivers would not establish a high-multiplicity result when masses are encoded in binary. The key generated question is whether its pricing step can be handled in time polynomial in type support and bit length. If not, continuum-specific hardness remains possible. A second bridge question is whether the discrete welfare optimum approaches this mass optimum as multiplicities grow, and with what error.

This mirror covers the BA branch of Theorem 2.1’s single-cycle welfare problem. It says nothing about FA, the paper’s dynamic companion setting, or joint optimization of match time and quality.

The weakest point is that a realistic platform may have too many distinct score and acceptance profiles for low support to be credible. And the mass relaxation does not erase the discrete choice a representative rider faces among driver types; pricing could preserve substantial combinatorics. The positive case rests on a specific repeated-profile regime and a concrete question about whether mass divisibility makes the welfare problem tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 2.1’s hardness does not carry over to the proposed high-multiplicity model in the way the proponent suggests. Its 3-PARTITION reduction encodes each item \(a_j\) in a driver’s probability \(p_j=1-2^{-a_j}\). Exact driver types therefore retain the item identities: multiplying every supply by \(q\) creates many copies of each type, but does not reduce the number of types carrying the reduction’s combinatorics. And when rider mass can be split across configurations, the equal-load argument no longer forces an integral partition of the original items. The theorem motivates asking what fractionality changes; it does not establish hardness for the mirror.

There is a further gap between the paper’s algorithms and the proposed complexity target. Its BA demand oracle runs in time polynomial in the number \(n\) of named drivers. That does not give an algorithm polynomial in the bit length of binary-encoded type supplies: \(n\) could be enormous relative to that encoding. The mirror’s real technical question is whether pricing over integer notification-count vectors can be solved efficiently in the type support and bit length. That is a substantive question, but it remains unanswered by the paper’s results.

The practical case is also narrower than the proponent’s description suggests. A driver type must specify the score and acceptance probability against every rider type, and a rider type must specify them against every driver type. With pair-specific geography and predictions, exact profiles may be nearly unique. Binning profiles could produce a low-support model, but then the mirror needs an approximation argument showing that the binned model represents the original dispatch problem well.

Those points make this a weaker ChoCo candidate than Theorem 2.1’s headline might imply. The paper studies a platform’s stochastic matching and notification problem, not preference aggregation by a social choice rule. Unless ChoCo intends to include two-sided platform allocation, the more natural home for the mirror is stochastic matching and assortment optimization.

Still, I cannot honestly claim that these objections defeat every version. A repeated-profile market with discrete service zones and vehicle classes is plausible, and the proposed mass allocation over finite notification bundles is coherent. The paper’s configuration LP makes the resulting pricing question particularly clear. My negative case can challenge whether that question fits ChoCo’s priorities and whether it applies to realistic low-support dispatch data; it cannot show that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proponent’s BA anchor survives the stipulated test.
