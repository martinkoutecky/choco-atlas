What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a valid ChoCo anchor in this paper: it contains no named computational result about complexity or algorithms. The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here. It characterizes the Pareto-efficient and envy-free allocations for two agents with monotonic preferences: the whole interval must be divided at a cut \(\alpha\); if the agents’ balanced cuts coincide, \(\alpha\) must equal that cut; if one balanced cut is smaller than the other, the lower-cut agent gets the left piece and \(\alpha\) may be any point between the two cuts. Theorem 2, also proved here, says no allocation rule on the paper’s two-agent domain can satisfy Pareto efficiency, envy-freeness, and strategy-proofness simultaneously. Both are structural or axiomatic results, not claims that a computational problem is tractable or hard.

The strongest plausible population story is two large land-owning cooperatives. Each cooperative is a claimant whose members share a complete preference over contiguous plots, and it receives a plot for collective use. Its type is that shared preference; its mass is its fraction of the resident population. Given the two types and their masses, the question would be to find a cut \(\alpha\) and assignment of the two pieces that is Pareto efficient and envy-free. Theorem 1 supplies the answer through the balanced cuts. For a mechanism question, one could ask whether a rule choosing among those cuts is strategy-proof; Theorem 2 says no.

That is the best affirmative analogy I can make, but it fails the programme’s gate. The mass of each cooperative does no work in either theorem: the original claimants are still two agents, and the allocation and axioms are unchanged. Treating the communities as collective claimants gives a plausible setting for the paper’s model, but does not turn its result into a computational question about a continuous population. Nor does the paper specify a finite encoding or oracle model for its arbitrary ordinal preferences, so even “compute the balanced cut” lacks a computational input model.

So I would offer the cooperative interpretation as a possible application of the paper, not as a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result. A genuine mirror would require new modeling choices—such as how individual residents receive allocations and how their mass affects feasibility or fairness—and new results. The paper’s continuous object is the divisible resource and its preference domain; its society remains two agents.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest reason to reject a ChoCo mirror is that the paper proves no computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes Pareto-efficient, envy-free divisions for two agents; Theorem 2 gives an axiomatic impossibility for rules on that same two-agent domain. Neither asks for an algorithm or a complexity classification. That matters here: the paper’s resource and bundles are continuous, but its society is not. Continuizing the land or the outcome space would repeat a feature already in the model, while the programme’s target is a computational problem over a continuous population.

The best computational question suggested by Theorem 1 would be to find the balanced cut \(q_i^f\) from reported preferences, then select a fair division. But the paper gives no finite input representation for its arbitrary continuous ordinal preferences. A comparison oracle, a utility formula, and a piecewise-linear representation would define different computational problems. Choosing one could yield a legitimate new algorithmic study, but the theorem itself supplies neither an encoded input nor a complexity claim to mirror. The same gap applies to Theorem 2: its strategy-proofness condition tracks how one of two named agents fares when that person changes their report. It does not specify a computational task over a distribution of types.

The cooperative interpretation does not repair that gap. If two large cooperatives are the claimants, the model still has two strategic agents, and their population masses do no work in the paper’s efficiency or envy-freeness conditions. If instead each resident is an agent who must receive a contiguous piece of the single unit interval, at most countably many can receive positive-length pieces. Almost every person in an atomless population would receive quantity zero, and the paper explicitly makes all such bundles indifferent. The balanced comparison between complementary, positive-length pieces would then cease to describe the typical person’s allocation. Giving each group a collective bundle, or comparing groups by average member welfare, avoids that problem only by adding a new allocation and fairness model. The resulting computational questions could be worthwhile, but they would not be mirrors of Theorems 1 or 2.

I would therefore argue against a worthwhile continuous mirror *of this paper* for ChoCo’s purpose. The case is strongest on the programme’s stated gate: there is no named computational result to anchor, and the obvious population reinterpretations either leave the two-agent problem intact or require enough new structure to define a different problem. The universal claim that no useful computational extension could ever be built from this model is weaker; one could certainly formulate one. But that possibility does not give this paper a continuous-population complexity question to mirror.
