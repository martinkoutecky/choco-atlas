The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a rubric-compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational-complexity result. Its propositions analyze equilibria and learning; none establishes that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest named results are still analytic. Proposition 9, proved here, says that with common signal precision \(q\in(1/2,1)\) in the discrete-action model, the unique ABEE has the first agent follow their signal and every later agent imitate them. Proposition 10, proved here, says that for every finite \(T\), a symmetric ABEE exists when signal precisions are drawn from a density on \([1/2,1]\), with equilibrium accuracy \(\beta_T^*\in(1/2,1)\). Proposition 11, proved here, says there is no ABEE sequence whose accuracy converges to \(1\) as \(T\to\infty\). These could motivate studying equilibrium computation, but they do not report its complexity.

A plausible *scenario* for a population-continuous extension is a large cohort of sequential forecasters grouped into a few shared signal-precision classes. One could ask for the state-contingent mass choosing each action in a self-consistent ABEE. But that would be a new question, not a mirror anchored by a computational result in this paper. The model’s evidence accumulates once per observed predecessor, and Proposition 9 makes the first individual pivotal; replacing individuals by mass requires deciding how those features scale. The paper does not supply that continuization.

The continuous action space in Sections 2–6 is outcome-space continuity, and the unbounded signal precision in Section 7 is a signal model. Neither makes the population continuous. So the strongest honest verdict for the requested positive case is: no qualifying anchor, and no prover-ready continuous problem per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that Propositions 9–11 do not state computational results. Proposition 9 characterizes an equilibrium; Proposition 10 proves one exists; Proposition 11 rules out a particular asymptotic learning outcome. None asks for an algorithm or establishes a complexity bound. One could pose new tasks—compute the ABEE in Proposition 9, find an equilibrium for Proposition 10, or determine convergence rates for Proposition 11—but those would be computational questions added to the paper, not mirrors of its results.

The population structure also makes the most direct continuization a poor fit. In Proposition 9, agents share signal precision, but they do not share a complete type: each position has a different information set and a different set of predecessors. Grouping them by precision loses that role; distinguishing them by position leaves one type per agent. Proposition 11’s \(T\to\infty\) limit grows the sequence of decision makers, but it does not create repeated, interchangeable agents within a role. The paper’s continuous action space and its distribution of signal precisions are continuizations of outcomes and information, respectively—not of society.

The best repair is a large-cohort version: many agents with the same precision classes act simultaneously at each stage, observe earlier cohorts, and generate state-contingent masses of actions. Section 6.4 makes that modification plausible. It could support a question about computing a self-consistent ABEE. But it changes the model’s unit of decision and shifts the task to equilibrium computation in a sequential learning game. The paper supplies no complexity result for that task, and the resulting mass-action process is not a collective-choice rule over voters, candidates, or allocations. It may be worthwhile work in algorithmic game theory; that possibility does not make it a computational-social-choice mirror of any of these propositions.

So the case against is strong under ChoCo’s stated anchor requirement: none of the three proposed anchors survives as a named computational result. It is weaker as the absolute claim that no worthwhile continuous-population question could ever arise from the paper. The cohort model shows a plausible new direction; what the paper does not provide is a reason to treat that direction as a mirror of its results.
