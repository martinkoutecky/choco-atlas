The continuous mirror question

Given a finite weighted compatibility graph \(H=(R,F)\) on student profiles and rational per-capita supplies \(\beta_r\), consider a mass-one population of identical advisors. Each advisor receives an integral bundle \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{R}\), with matching utility \(u_H(z)\). Does there exist a polynomial-time algorithm, measured in the graph size and binary input length, that outputs a rational finite-support distribution \(x_z\ge 0\), with \(\sum_z x_z=1\) and \(\sum_z x_z z_r=\beta_r\), such that every pair of bundles in its support satisfies EF1 and \(\sum_z x_z u_H(z)\ge (2/3)\mathrm{SW}^*\), where \(\mathrm{SW}^*\) is the maximum welfare without EF1?

The model it lives in

A mass-one population of identical advisor types, each receiving an integral bundle \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{R}\) of student profiles from a finite weighted compatibility graph. Choose bundle masses \(x_z\) matching the rational per-capita supplies \(\beta_r\); require EF1 across support bundles under \(u_H\), and maximize \(\sum_z x_z u_H(z)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.8's homogeneous-agent EF1 welfare guarantee. It does not cover the MMS results or the heterogeneous-agent EF1 results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is there a polynomial-time algorithm for the stated \(2/3\)-approximation in the continuous advisor population model?
  • Does every such instance admit an EF1 allocation with welfare equal to \(\mathrm{SW}^*\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is for the paper’s homogeneous-agent EF1 welfare result. I would use one anchor: Theorem 4.8, proved in this paper. It says that for every homogeneous instance, Algorithm \(\ref{alg:EF1:n}\) runs in polynomial time and returns an EF1 allocation whose social welfare is at least \(\left(2/3+2/(9n-3)\right)\mathrm{sw}^*(\mathcal I)\). The guarantee approaches \(2/3\) as the number of agents grows.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Homogeneous Matching-EF1 Welfare. An instance has a finite graph \(H=(R,F)\) of student profiles, rational nonnegative edge weights \(w\), and a rational per-capita supply \(\beta_r\) of students of each profile \(r\in R\). There is a mass-one population of identical advisors. Their type is the complete weight table \(w\), with mass \(\mu_w=1\). A bundle is an integral count vector \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{R}\): an individual advisor receives an integer number of students of each profile. Write \(u(z)\) for the weight of a maximum matching among those students, using \(H\) to specify compatibility and weight between profile copies.

An allocation is a finite-support distribution \(x_z\ge0\), where \(x_z\) is the mass of advisors receiving bundle \(z\), satisfying
\[ \sum_z x_z=1,\qquad \sum_z x_z z_r=\beta_r\quad\text{for every }r\in R. \]
Thus each unit of advisor mass receives an integral bundle, while its type-level average bundle may be fractional. For any two bundles \(z,y\) in the support, require EF1: if \(y\ne0\), some profile \(r\) with \(y_r>0\) satisfies \(u(z)\ge u(y-e_r)\), where \(e_r\) removes one student of profile \(r\). Define welfare by \(\mathrm{SW}(x)=\sum_z x_z u(z)\), and let \(\mathrm{SW}^*\) be its maximum over feasible allocations without the EF1 condition. The computational question is whether a polynomial-time algorithm, measured in the graph and binary input length, can return a rational finite-support EF1 allocation with welfare at least \((2/3)\mathrm{SW}^*\).

The regime is a large advising system with many advisors but few advisor types: all advisors share the same full matching valuation, so \(\tau=1\), while student supply grows with the advisor population. Students of a profile must be indistinguishable in every way relevant to matching value; the profile graph records their compatibility. This is a plausible cohort version of the paper’s advisor and peer-instruction example. The model keeps each advisor’s bundle integral and makes the population the continuous object. Scaling student supply per advisor is the co-modification needed to avoid a fixed finite resource set being spread over an ever larger population.

I expect this problem to be Class A, though Theorem 4.8 does not establish that. Its maximum-matching structure and finite EF1 construction give a credible starting point for a compressed algorithm over bundle types; the continuous version would need to preserve EF1 across the support while computing with per-capita supplies. The mirror covers only homogeneous-agent EF1 welfare. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s MMS results or its heterogeneous-agent EF1 results. I would not use the heterogeneous MMS impossibility as an anchor here: its two-agent obstruction has no established lift to a positive-mass population of types.

The weakest point is the profile-graph assumption. If student identities determine compatibility in ways that cannot be captured by finitely many complete profiles, aggregating students into roles changes the matching problem. And even with valid profiles, the paper’s finite-\(n\) theorem supplies a useful anchor, not a continuous algorithm or a complexity classification. The next questions are whether the \(2/3\) guarantee is attainable in this mass model, whether an EF1 allocation can always attain \(\mathrm{SW}^*\), and how the problem changes when several advisor types have positive mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I don’t see a sound way to defeat Theorem 4.8 as an anchor. It is a named, polynomial-time computational result, and its homogeneous case removes the need to preserve advisor identities. EF1 also remains meaningful: each advisor receives an integral bundle, even though the population is represented by masses. The guarantee’s limiting value, \(2/3\), does not vanish.

The strongest objection is to the proposed profile graph. The paper allows an arbitrary resource graph, where compatibility can depend on particular pairs of vertices. The mirror assumes students fall into finitely many profiles whose pairwise matching relations are uniform. In many graphs, encoding each student’s full compatibility pattern would create as many types as students, defeating the intended compression. The mirror therefore relies on a structured cohort model that the theorem itself does not require.

But that objection narrows the mirror; it does not rule it out. Large advising systems with repeated student profiles and compatibility determined by profile pairs are plausible, and scaling student supply with advisor population is a natural co-modification. The authors could recognize that as their matching-EF1 problem in a high-multiplicity regime. I would ask for that scenario to be made explicit, but under ChoCo’s criteria the anchor survives. The negative case is weak.
