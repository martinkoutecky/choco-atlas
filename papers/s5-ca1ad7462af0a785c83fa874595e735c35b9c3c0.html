What the mirror covers

No continuous mirror is anchored to a named computational result; the proposed population-game extension leaves the paper’s two-player equilibrium analysis uncovered.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Lemma 3.1 says that when Alice moves the price above or below \(1/2\), her rational vote is respectively \(1\) or \(0\), regardless of Bob’s vote. Lemma 3.2 gives thresholds \(p_L\) and \(p_H\) for Bob’s rational vote under mild assumptions. Both are equilibrium-analysis results, not complexity results about a computational problem. The paper’s main LPP/HPP equilibrium result is also not a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition. So I cannot honestly give a continuous problem “per anchor” or classify one of these results as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

The best positive foothold is a plausible extension: a large shareholder vote or membership referendum coupled to a prediction market, with many participants in a small number of shared-information and shared-role classes. A type could specify an agent’s private signal, trading role, and relevant beliefs; its mass would be that class’s share of participants. Type-level strategies would specify trading and voting behavior, the aggregate vote would determine the market’s payoff, and the question could be to find an equilibrium for a rational distribution over types. That keeps the paper’s distinctive coupling between market trades and outcome-influencing votes. But it is a proposed population-game extension, not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The weak point is central: the paper deliberately studies two strategic players because each can influence the outcome substantially. In a large population of individual voters, a person’s influence usually vanishes; making large classes act as unified blocs preserves influence only by changing who the strategic agents are. And even if the authors accepted that extension, its main question would be equilibrium computation, not the computational-complexity landscape that ChoCo targets. This paper therefore offers an interesting population-continuum direction, but no anchored positive case under the stated criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative begins with the missing anchor: the paper names no computational problem and proves no complexity result. Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2 characterize rational votes in a two-player game; the LPP/HPP result describes its equilibria. The parameters \(q_0\) and \(\pi\) describe beliefs and participation in that game. The prior over \(\tau\) is uncertainty about the event, not a distribution over a population. So there is no result here whose computational question can be mirrored over a society distribution.

The obstacle to rescuing the paper’s central result is more than its choice to study two players. Alice and Bob are individually pivotal: their votes determine \(v=(v_A+v_B)/2\), and their market actions shape incentives around that outcome. In an atomless population, one participant’s vote has zero effect on \(v\); the manipulation incentives behind the vote lemmas and the LPP/HPP distinction disappear. If a type’s entire mass instead coordinates its trades and votes, that type acts as a bloc that internalizes its mass. The strategic players are then blocs, not many interchangeable participants.

The proponent’s strongest repair—a large shareholder vote or referendum with shared-information classes—runs into that same choice. Independent class members lose individual influence in the continuum. Giving each class a binding, coordinated action preserves influence but adds a collective decision maker, which the paper’s two-player model does not supply. Preserving the sequential market also makes trading position and price history relevant to a participant’s role; replacing the sequence with aggregate mass flows would define a new game. Either could motivate separate research, but neither is a continuous-population version of a computational result in this paper.

I therefore find no surviving anchored mirror. A mean-field study of a newly specified prediction-market game could be worthwhile, but that possibility does not establish a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper. The negative case is strongest as a diagnosis of the mismatch, not as proof that no related population model could ever be valuable.
