The continuous mirror question

Given a Boolean domain \(\{0,1\}^n\), a distribution \((\Psi_i,\mu_i)_{i=1}^{\tau}\) over RPF preference types, two partial alternatives \(\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1,?\}^n\), and a rational threshold \(q\in[0,1]\), is \(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\,\mathbf{1}[\exists\,\widehat{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Comp}(\alpha),\,\widehat{\beta}\in\mathrm{Comp}(\beta):\widehat{\alpha}\succ_{\Psi_i}\widehat{\beta}]\ge q\)? Each type’s possible-dominance test uses its own completion witnesses.

The model it lives in

A society is a binary-rational distribution over \(\tau\) complete preference types, each specified by an RPF over the same Boolean attributes. For a fixed pair of partial alternatives, the quantity of interest is the population mass of types for which the first can strictly outrank the second under some completion; the decision output is whether that mass reaches \(q\).

The objection that survived

Because the alternatives are common listings, separate existential witnesses may rely on mutually incompatible completions; the query measures the share of types for whom dominance is possible, not the share who prefer the first listing in one shared completed state.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 2’s NP-completeness result for P-DOM on RPFs. It leaves Theorems 1–3, Proposition 1, Propositions 3–4, and the paper’s other discussion unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding the threshold query as a function of the number of attributes, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length?
  • For which restricted RPFs can the possible-dominance mass be computed or approximated efficiently?
  • How does the complexity change if one common completion must witness the population threshold?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a population-level version of the paper’s possible dominance for RPFs. Imagine a large marketplace considering the same two incompletely specified listings. Buyers use one of a small number of shared preference rules—for example, preference formulas built into shopping assistants. A type is one buyer’s complete RPF over the listings’ Boolean attributes. Many buyers can share that exact rule; the society is a distribution over the \(\tau\) rules, with mass \(\mu_i\) on type \(i\), where \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\) and the masses are binary rationals. The regime is many buyers and relatively few shared rules, \(\tau\ll N\).

Call the decision problem \(\textsc{P-DOM-Share}_{\infty}\). An instance gives a common domain \(\{0,1\}^n\), the support \((\Psi_i,\mu_i)_{i=1}^{\tau}\), two partial alternatives \(\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1,?\}^n\), and a rational threshold \(q\in[0,1]\). Define

\[ M_P=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\, \mathbf{1}\!\left[ \exists\,\widehat{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Comp}(\alpha),\ \widehat{\beta}\in\mathrm{Comp}(\beta): \widehat{\alpha}\succ_{\Psi_i}\widehat{\beta} \right], \]

where \(\mathrm{Comp}\) fills in the unknown bits and \(\succ_{\Psi_i}\) is the strict preference relation induced by the RPF. The question is whether \(M_P\ge q\): does at least a \(q\) fraction of the population have a completion under which the first listing dominates the second? The answer is yes or no; there is no intervention or optimization variable in the paper’s underlying problem, so this mirror keeps its pairwise decision question and asks for its prevalence in a society.

The anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper: \(\textsc{P-DOM(RPF)}\) is NP-complete. Its input is an RPF and two partial alternatives over a binary combinatorial domain; it asks whether there are completions under which the first alternative is strictly preferred to the second. The paper proves membership by guessing completions and computing their ranks. For hardness it reduces SAT: given \(\Phi\), it builds an RPF whose first rank tests \(\Phi\land x_{k+1}\), whose second tests \(\neg x_{k+1}\), and whose third is a tautology. The two partial alternatives leave the first \(k\) attributes unknown and fix \(x_{k+1}\) to \(1\) and \(0\), respectively. A satisfying assignment gives the first alternative rank 1 while the second has rank 2; if \(\Phi\) is unsatisfiable, the first alternative can reach only rank 3. Thus the P-DOM instance is positive exactly when \(\Phi\) is satisfiable.

This mirror is NP-complete as well. It is in NP: a certificate can identify enough types to reach mass \(q\) and give the witnessing completions for each. Hardness follows by taking \(\tau=1\), \(\mu_1=q=1\), which recovers Proposition 2 exactly. I expect hardness to transfer (Class B), since the SAT encoding lies in the RPF over attributes and remains present with a single population type. The population mass does not smooth away that combinatorial structure.

I think the authors could recognize this as a continuous-population extension of their question: the comparison for each type has exactly their P-DOM semantics, and the output says how prevalent that judgment is in a population. The mirror continuizes the population only. It leaves the alternative domain finite and the missing-attribute semantics intact. Its scope is Proposition 2; I would not claim it mirrors the paper’s other results.

The weakest point is that the paper studies one preference relation at a time, while this mirror asks for the share of a population satisfying that relation. It is an aggregation of their problem, not a population model they formulate themselves. The high-multiplicity scenario is most convincing when many buyers use a small library of exact preference rules, as in deployed assistant configurations; it is less convincing if “type” means only a loose demographic segment. Further questions include the complexity of computing \(M_P\) exactly, whether the share can be approximated efficiently for structured RPFs, and which restrictions on the formulas make the population query tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is a genuine computational anchor: the paper proves that P-DOM for RPFs is NP-complete. The strongest objection is not that a population of people sharing RPFs is impossible. A fixed catalogue of preference rules could make that a sensible high-multiplicity regime.

The problem is what the population adds. In the paper, P-DOM asks whether one preference model admits *some* completion of one pair of partial alternatives under which the first is strictly preferred. The proposed share applies that existential test separately to each type, then sums the yes answers. If the missing attributes belong to the same two listings, those attributes have common values for everyone. Separate existential witnesses can count types that favor the first listing under mutually incompatible completions. The result is a prevalence of “could prefer” judgments, not the share of buyers who prefer it in any one possible state of the listings.

A more coherent group question would use one completion for everyone, for example
\[ \exists\,\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\beta}\quad \sum_i \mu_i\,\mathbf{1}[\widehat{\alpha}\succ_{\Psi_i}\widehat{\beta}] \ge q. \]
That is a plausible question about possible population support. But it adds a collective threshold and a shared-world uncertainty model; the paper supplies neither. If instead each buyer has different missing information, the types need to describe that information too. Either repair may be worthwhile, but each makes the population-level problem an extension of the paper’s individual comparison problem, rather than a direct mirror of Proposition 2.

For the stated share query, each type is still tested independently and the population contributes only a weighted tally. The singleton case recovers the original proposition; larger supports batch its instances. That is formally a continuous-population input, but the population does not shape the dominance question or its proof. I would therefore resist treating this anchor as strong evidence for ChoCo’s programme. Still, the marketplace scenario could persuade the authors that prevalence of possible judgments is their problem. The negative case is weak at that point: I can argue that the proposed mirror is thin and under-motivated, but not honestly rule out every worthwhile population formulation.
