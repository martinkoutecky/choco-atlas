What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a narrow high-multiplicity batch of the paper’s two-person problem. Imagine a large service handling independent, standardized divisions—such as repeated cases in which each pair of claimants must split the same catalogue of assets. A case type \(t\) records the complete strict rankings of both claimants, \((\succ^A_t,\succ^B_t)\). Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of cases with that profile. The number of cases can be enormous while the number \(\tau\) of distinct profile types stays small. Each case has its own copy of the catalogue, so assets remain indivisible and each pair still receives equal numbers of items.

The strongest anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It says that, for an even number \(n\) of items, two strict rankings admit a complete envy-free allocation if and only if, for every odd \(k\) from \(1\) to \(n\), the two players’ sets of top \(k\) items differ. In the paper’s notation, Condition \(C(k)\) says those two top-\(k\) sets are equal; Proposition 2 says a complete envy-free allocation exists exactly when \(C(k)\) fails for every odd \(k\). The paper proves necessity directly and proves sufficiency using AL. This is a named structural decision result, though the proposition does not state a complexity class or a running-time bound.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: Complete-EF Batch Division. An instance consists of an even-sized catalogue \(G\), a finite support \(T\) of paired-ranking types, and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Each unit of population is an independent pair of claimants of some type \(t\), with its own copy of \(G\). Does there exist a type-wise rule assigning each supported type a partition \((S_A(t),S_B(t))\) of \(G\), with \(|S_A(t)|=|S_B(t)|=n/2\), such that both claimants have envy-free pairwise matchings between their bundles? A yes-solution is the map \(t\mapsto(S_A(t),S_B(t))\); a no-solution can identify a supported type and an odd \(k\) for which the top-\(k\) sets coincide.

I expect this question to be tractable. Proposition 2 gives a polynomial-time check of the criterion for each supported type, and the AL construction supplies allocations when the criterion holds. Thus the relevant input scale is the catalogue size \(n\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\); the population’s total case count need not be expanded. The continuous batch version is feasible exactly when every positive-mass type satisfies Condition D. A useful follow-on is to ask for the greatest mass of cases that can receive complete envy-free splits; by Proposition 2, that mass is \(\sum_{t:\,D(t)}\mu_t\). The paper contains no named hardness result, so this anchor does not make a case about discrete hardness disappearing in the continuum.

I think the authors would recognize this as a high-multiplicity extension of their problem: each case preserves the two players, rankings, indivisible items, equal bundle sizes, and the paper’s definition of envy. The scenario is a batch of repeated bilateral divisions, rather than a single division with many competing claimants. That distinction is also the weakest point. Here mass counts independent cases, not agents sharing one inventory; a referee could regard the model as batching their problem rather than continuizing its population. The mirror therefore covers Proposition 2 and its constructive consequence, not the paper’s strategic-manipulation results, random-ranking probability calculations, or contested-pile follow-on procedures.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the strongest anchor, and it is a genuine existence result: it characterizes when two rankings admit a complete envy-free allocation. But the proposed mirror changes the unit of the problem. Each type is a *pair* of claimants, and each unit of its mass gets a separate copy of the catalogue. That is a distribution over independent two-person instances, not a continuous population taking part in one allocation. The masses weight how many cases have each profile; they do not couple the choices or change what any pair can receive. The follow-on question about the greatest mass receiving complete splits has the same limitation: it aggregates case outcomes, not agents’ claims on common resources.

The better-looking alternatives do not preserve the paper’s problem either. Cloning the two claimants many times still leaves only two rankings and one bilateral division; the clones add no population dimension to that decision. Giving many individuals a common catalogue would require a new rule for allocating it among them, including how to define envy across more than two people. Giving each pair its own catalogue avoids that problem but returns to a batch of independent instances. Scaling a shared inventory with the population is a possible new model, but Proposition 2’s condition on a pair’s top-\(k\) sets would not characterize it.

So the batch scenario may be useful for studying a service that processes many similar disputes. Its weak point as a ChoCo mirror is that it continuizes the *number of cases*, while the programme continuizes the population within a social-choice problem. That scope objection is the strongest negative case; it is not airtight if ChoCo chooses to include distributions of independent allocation instances. If it does, the proponent’s mirror survives as a batch extension, though it says little about a shared society of many claimants.
