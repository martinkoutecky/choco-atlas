The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a positive case that meets your anchor requirement: this paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result, and it makes no complexity classification. Equations (19)–(23) formulate the matching optimization model, and Section 4 computes a six-by-six example, but neither is a named result of the required kind. I won’t present either as a theorem.

The most plausible *unanchored* mirror is a mass-matching problem for a large property platform. A seller type would include all seller and property data used by the model; a buyer type would include all buyer preferences and terms; and each type would have a rational mass. The decision variable \(x_{sb}\geq 0\) would be the mass of seller type \(s\) matched to buyer type \(b\), bounded by the available mass on each side. Using the paper’s pairwise dominance scores, the objective would be to find a feasible flow that maximizes the parties’ aggregate dominance according to the model’s dictionary order, then minimizes aggregate intermediary moral-hazard cost, such as \(\sum_{s,b} p_{sb}(1-\lambda_{sb}p_{sb}/b)x_{sb}\), with \(\lambda_{sb}\) the effort coefficient and \(b\) the cost parameter.

A plausible regime is a high-volume platform matching recurring cohorts of standardized property listings and buyer requests: many transactions, but far fewer distinct combinations of property attributes, acceptable prices, and evaluation criteria. This is a reasonable aggregate question if matching is assessed across a market period. With explicit pairwise data, the continuous formulation has \(|S||B|\) flow variables and linear constraints, so it appears tractable by linear programming. That would be a Class A candidate, but it would be a new analysis of the paper’s model, not a result established by the paper. The paper does not establish discrete hardness that might dissolve.

The weak point is that the paper’s agents and pairwise intermediary effort coefficients can be highly individualized. A type must preserve every distinction the model uses; if those distinctions are common, the number of types may grow nearly as fast as the number of agents. The aggregate regime is plausible for standardized, repeated platform transactions, but the paper’s case study does not establish that it applies there.

So the paper suggests a potentially sensible continuous matching question, but it offers no qualifying anchor for it. Under the requested standard, there are zero anchors and therefore no per-anchor continuous problems to state.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no anchors to defeat. The paper states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity or algorithms. It proposes a matching model and solves a six-seller, six-buyer example with LINGO; it does not establish a complexity result. Under the requested standard, that leaves no paper result to carry into ChoCo’s continuous-complexity programme.

The strongest possible mirror would start from the model in Eqs. (19)–(23): give seller and buyer types masses, preserve every attribute and price used in the dominance scores, and replace binary \(x_{ij}\) with matched mass. Repeated, standardized listings and requests on a high-volume platform make that regime plausible. I cannot honestly dismiss it by saying the agents are necessarily unique: if two agents are identical in everything the model uses, aggregation is legitimate.

But that would be a new analysis of the paper’s optimization model, not a mirror of a computational result the paper proves. The paper already supplies pairwise data for its finite matching problem and reports one numerical solution; it gives no complexity claim or broader algorithmic result for a continuous version to test. A mass-flow LP might be worth studying as operations research, but its value to this programme would have to come from the new problem one formulates, not from charting how a result in this paper changes under continuization. The paper’s fuzzy and interval-valued inputs also describe attribute information; they do not make the population continuous.

There is a further limitation, though it is not decisive: the model uses effort coefficients \(\beta_{ij}\) that vary by seller–buyer pair. If those remain idiosyncratic at scale, grouping agents by their individual attributes will not compress the pair data much. A repeated type-pair regime could avoid that, so this does not rule out every scenario. The proposed mirror’s illustrative hazard-cost expression also differs from Eq. (22), which uses \(\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}(1-\beta_{ij}/b)x_{ij}\); that is repairable, not a fundamental objection.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit with ChoCo: this paper offers no named computational result to mirror, and the best alternative is a new continuous matching study. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be built from the model. If “mirror” is allowed to include a fresh problem inspired by an application paper, the mass-matching question remains plausible.
