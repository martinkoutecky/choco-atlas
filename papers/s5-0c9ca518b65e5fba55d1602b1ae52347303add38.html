The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I can make is for a continuous version of the paper’s multi-attribute winner determination problem (MWDP). There is one qualification: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. It says in the introduction that MWDP is NP-complete, citing [9], and relates it in Section II to the NP-complete combinatorial allocation problem. Those are unnumbered, externally attributed claims, not eligible anchors under your rule. The paper’s own results are its greedy heuristics and their experimental comparisons in Sections IV–V. So I cannot honestly give an anchor-based mirror or claim that a theorem here establishes the hardness.

As a prospective mirror, I would study Mass-MWDP in the paper’s unconstrained experimental regime. A type is a complete buyer or seller order: buyers with identical XOR lists, valuations, time windows, slot requirements and quality thresholds; sellers with identical OR lists, reservation prices, time windows and quality attributes. The instance gives each type a binary-rational mass, with the masses summing to one. Consider, for example, a large grid market with many repeated workload templates and standardized provider offers: the number of traders is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct complete order types.

The task is to maximize total surplus. A solution assigns nonnegative mass \(z_{b,a}\) of each buyer type \(b\) to each complete service plan \(a\). A plan either rejects the buyer or selects at most one bid from that buyer’s XOR order and supplies its full requested bundle for the required number of slots within its time window, meeting its quality thresholds. It includes a routing of that service to compatible seller types and offers. The constraints require \(\sum_a z_{b,a}\le\mu_b\) for each buyer type, and require aggregate routed supply from each seller type and offer to stay within that type’s mass-scaled availability. The objective sums buyers’ valuations over the served mass and subtracts the corresponding seller reservation costs. As in the paper’s experiments, this version omits coupling and co-allocation constraints; it preserves complete individual service plans, so no individual is assigned a fractional bundle. Only a cohort is divided across plans.

This is a plausible Class A question: with no coupling or co-allocation, aggregate supply, demand, and surplus constraints are linear, so the natural formulation is an LP over type-level flows or service plans. The algorithmic issue is whether the implicit plans admit efficient separation or pricing as the numbers of goods and time slots grow. That keeps the mirror on the paper’s market-clearing problem rather than on divisible outcomes generally. It also gives a clean bridge back to high multiplicity: rational masses encode cohort proportions, and finite markets with repeated order types yield such masses.

The scope is deliberately limited to the underlying MWDP and the market regime exercised by the experiments; it does not mirror a numbered result, because none is available to anchor it. Further questions are how pricing scales with goods, attributes, and time slots; whether coupling or co-allocation preserves tractability; and what approximation or rounding guarantees relate the mass solution to a finite market.

The weak point is the regime evidence. The paper tests only 20–120 bundle bids generated for experiments, not a market with many traders and few repeated *complete* order types. In a real grid, differences in prices, quality requirements, and schedules could make \(\tau\) large. Still, that is a weakness in the case for this scenario, not a reason to treat the population mirror as meaningless: standardized recurring workloads and provider packages make it a plausible instance regime for the same allocation problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed anchor is not one of this paper’s computational results. The paper gives no numbered theorem or lemma about MWDP complexity or algorithm performance. Its NP-completeness claim is attributed to earlier work; its own contribution is an empirical comparison of greedy heuristics on finite, randomly generated markets. Mass-MWDP therefore opens a new question about the underlying allocation problem. It does not mirror a result established here.

That distinction matters for the paper’s actual findings. The reported advantage of the 3/3 heuristic depends on ordering individual bids and time slots, then comparing the resulting finite allocations with a time-limited MILP benchmark. The experiments draw bids and attributes from distributions, so exact complete order types will generally be distinct. A continuous model of repeated types could compare aggregate welfare or study a limiting greedy procedure, but it would need a new definition of that procedure and would not by itself explain the reported experimental results.

This objection does not defeat the best version of Mass-MWDP. Repeated workload templates and standardized provider offers are a plausible regime, and a model with full service plans could retain XOR choices, time windows, quality requirements, and even coupling and co-allocation constraints. Dividing a cohort among plans is compatible with the programme’s definition of a continuous society. I cannot honestly claim that such a question is meaningless or unworthy of study.

So the negative case is strong only in a narrower sense: this paper offers no formal computational result for a continuous mirror to carry across, and its experiments do not establish a high-multiplicity regime. The universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror remains weak; the proposed Mass-MWDP is a credible new computational question, even though it is not a mirror of the paper’s own reported result.
