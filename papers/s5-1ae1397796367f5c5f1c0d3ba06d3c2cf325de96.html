The continuous mirror question

Given types \(S=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_s>0\) summing to \(1\), feasible deployments \(x^1,\ldots,x^k\in X_\alpha\), rational utilities \(u_s(x^j)\in[0,1]\), and a supplied rational lottery \(p\in\Delta_k\), define \(\bar u_s(p)=\sum_j p_j u_s(x^j)\) and \(\Phi_\mu(p)=\sum_{s,s'}\mu_s\mu_{s'}|\bar u_s(p)-\bar u_{s'}(p)|\). Given \(\epsilon>0\) and a horizon \(T\ge\lceil L/\epsilon\rceil\), where \(L\) is a Lipschitz constant of \(\Phi_\mu\) in \(p\) under the coordinatewise maximum norm, construct counts \(q_j\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), \(\sum_j q_j=T\), such that \(\Phi_\mu(q/T)\le\Phi_\mu(p)+\epsilon\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of residents grouped into complete utility-profile types, with mass \(\mu_s\) for type \(s\); each period selects one feasible deployment, and unfairness is the mass-weighted pairwise disparity of types’ average utilities. The decision lottery \(p\) is over deployments, separate from the population distribution \(\mu\).

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 4.4’s guarantee for realizing a supplied relaxed lottery as a finite schedule under population-weighted pairwise disparity. It does not cover optimizing the lottery over all feasible deployments or the paper’s other formulations and aggregation functions.

Open questions for a prover

  • For particular deployment or routing problems with implicit \(X_\alpha\), can the population-weighted relaxed lottery be optimized efficiently, for example using column generation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper (Section 4; proof in Appendix B.4). It says: take an unfairness function \(\phi\) and a distributional aggregation function \(\tilde\vartheta\), with real extension \(\hat\vartheta\). If the resulting objective—applying \(\phi\) to the stakeholders’ aggregated values—is \(L\)-Lipschitz, then any solution of the probabilistic relaxation with value \(\hat\phi\) yields, in polynomial time, a feasible \(T\)-period decision sequence with unfairness at most \(\hat\phi+\epsilon\), for any \(T\ge\lceil L/\epsilon\rceil\). This is a conditional algorithmic result: it constructs a schedule from a relaxed solution; it does not show that finding that solution is easy. The paper gives no comparable named hardness result, so I would use this one anchor rather than add weaker ones.

A plausible population regime is repeated municipal emergency-service deployment. The stakeholders are residents, grouped by their stable service-utility profiles under feasible deployment decisions: for example, residents with the same response utility for every candidate ambulance placement. There may be millions of residents but only \(\tau\) such profiles. A type is one of those complete profiles, and its mass \(\mu_s\) is the fraction of residents with that profile. Each period the municipality selects one \(\alpha\)-efficient deployment \(x\in X_\alpha\); residents of type \(s\) receive the same time-invariant utility \(u_s(x)\). That is recognizably the paper’s repeated, system-level decision problem, with a credible high-multiplicity population.

Here is the continuous analogue of Theorem 4.4, which I would call Population-FOT Lottery Realization. Its instance consists of types \(S=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_s>0\) summing to \(1\), a finite set of feasible deployment decisions \(x^1,\ldots,x^k\in X_\alpha\), and utilities \(u_s(x^j)\in[0,1]\). A supplied relaxed solution is a lottery \(p\in\Delta_k\) over those decisions. With average utility over time, type \(s\)’s utility under the lottery is \(\bar u_s(p)=\sum_j p_j u_s(x^j)\). Use population pairwise disparity as unfairness:
\[ \Phi_\mu(p)=\sum_{s,s'\in S}\mu_s\mu_{s'}\left|\bar u_s(p)-\bar u_{s'}(p)\right|. \]
Given \(\epsilon>0\) and a horizon \(T\), the question is whether one can produce a schedule of \(T\) feasible deployments, drawn from the supplied decisions, whose population disparity is at most \(\Phi_\mu(p)+\epsilon\). A solution can be given as counts \(q_j\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\) summing to \(T\), with the schedule using \(x^j\) exactly \(q_j\) times. This unfairness measure is the population limit of a symmetric finite-stakeholder measure: if type \(s\) is replicated in proportion \(\mu_s\), the normalized sum of pairwise utility gaps is exactly the displayed expression.

For this realization problem, I expect tractability (Class A), with the stated scope caveat. If \(\Phi_\mu\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz in the lottery probabilities under the coordinatewise maximum norm, round the probabilities to multiples of \(1/T\), distributing the leftover periods among the actions. Each coordinate changes by at most \(1/T\), so the disparity rises by at most \(L/T\). Thus \(T\ge\lceil L/\epsilon\rceil\) gives the desired schedule. The type masses enter directly in the objective, while the number of residents does not determine the problem’s dimension. This is the population counterpart of the paper’s rounding argument; applying it to the weighted population objective is the proposed mirror, not a theorem the paper already states verbatim.

This mirror covers Theorem 4.4’s relaxed-solution-to-finite-schedule guarantee. It does not claim that optimizing over all feasible deployments is in P: finding the best lottery can still inherit the difficulty of \(X_\alpha\), and implicit decision sets may call for column generation or another optimization method. Follow-up questions include whether the relaxed population problem can be solved efficiently for particular deployment or routing problems, and how the horizon bound depends on the decision support and utility range.

The weakest point is that the paper’s probabilities are over decision periods, whereas the continuous object here is the population. The mirror keeps those roles distinct: \(\mu\) represents residents, while \(p\) is a time-lottery used to build a schedule. A second judgment is needed to accept the population-weighted pairwise disparity as the authors’ fairness objective. I think it is plausible because it is a symmetric, perfect-fairness measure on finite populations and has a direct high-multiplicity interpretation, but Theorem 4.4 alone supports only the narrow realization result—not a complexity classification of the full fairness-over-time optimization problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about the value of this particular anchor. Theorem 4.4 studies rounding a lottery over decision periods into a finite schedule. In the proposed population version, the resident masses enter the unfairness measure, but the rounding still acts only on the lottery over deployments. With finitely many deployment options and utility types, the population variant is a weighted finite-stakeholder problem; its schedule construction follows the paper’s generic Lipschitz argument. That makes the stated mirror look like a compact reparameterization of the theorem, rather than a new complexity boundary.

That objection does not defeat the stronger mirror the brief requires me to consider. One can optimize the deployment lottery itself against population disparity,
\[ \Phi_\mu(p)=\sum_{s,s'}\mu_s\mu_{s'}\left|\sum_j p_j\bigl(u_s(x^j)-u_{s'}(x^j)\bigr)\right|. \]
For an explicit deployment set, this is a linear-programming formulation with pairwise slack variables; for an implicit set, its pricing problem may be a substantive algorithmic question. Grouping residents with identical utility profiles is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s lottery over decisions does not already make the population continuous. The ambulance example supplies a plausible setting, rather than a contrived one.

So the negative case is weak. The anchor is a genuine computational result, identity does not prevent grouping residents by complete utility profile, and neither the continuum nor the setting rules out a useful mirror. I can argue that Theorem 4.4’s population adaptation may be modest, but I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
