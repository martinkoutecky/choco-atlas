The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is Theorem 12, “Optimal ABO-compatible exchange,” proved in this paper. It is an optimization result, though not a complexity classification: the paper gives no named theorem saying a problem is in P or NP-hard.

Theorem 12 says that, under Assumptions 1–3 and ABO-compatible exchange, each patient group’s steady-state waiting time is determined by a sequence of bottleneck groups. Let \(\mathcal U\) contain the single-patient and underdemanded-pair categories, and \(\mathcal O\) the deceased-donor and overdemanded-pair categories. For each demand set \(V\subseteq\mathcal U\), let \(C_V(P)\) be the supply categories in \(P\subseteq\mathcal O\) compatible with at least one group in \(V\), and let \(r^d_V(P)\) be their total supply rate divided by the total arrival rate for \(V\). The theorem constructs \(V_1,P_1\) by minimizing this ratio over \(V\subseteq\mathcal U\), with \(P_1=C_{V_1}(\mathcal O)\); it repeats the minimization after removing the groups and supplies already selected. If group \(i\) lies in \(V_\ell\), its waiting time conditional on survival is \(F^{-1}(1-r^d_{V_\ell}(P_\ell))\). The proof uses the minimum-cut/maximum-flow theorem.

A concrete continuous problem from that anchor is: Steady-State ABO-Compatible Kidney Exchange Allocation. An instance gives rational arrival rates for each single-patient blood type and patient–donor blood-type pair, deceased-donor rates, the ABO feasibility graph, the common tissue-incompatibility probability \(\theta\), and a rational piecewise-linear survival distribution \(F\). Assume the paper’s conditions on organ shortage and reciprocal-pair arrival rates. A type is a complete ex-ante category for this model: single or paired status, patient and donor blood types, and the shared crossmatch-risk class. Mass is the normalized flow of arrivals per unit time. The decision variables \(x_{ij}\) are flows of donor or pair mass to compatible patient groups, respecting supply, demand, and exchange feasibility. The task is to compute the FIFO-optimal steady-state allocation: return its group waiting times and a feasible flow that realizes them. The bottleneck partition and waiting times provide a certificate of the solution.

The regime is plausible within the paper’s assumptions: a large national clearinghouse can see many pairs in a year but only a small set of ABO pair categories. A mass of, say, \(12\%\) of arrivals of one pair type is a natural description of the pool. The continuum also lets the model represent random tissue compatibility and steady-state patient flows directly. The authors would recognize this problem immediately, since it is their model’s own optimization question.

I expect the stated four-blood-type version to be tractable: its category universe is fixed, and the proof reduces allocation to flow and cut calculations. This yields further questions about the bit complexity of computing outcomes from estimated rates, extending the result to larger compatibility graphs, and bounding the error between a finite kidney-exchange pool and its mass-flow limit. This mirror covers Theorem 12 only; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s entire welfare, equity, insurance, or platform-participation analysis.

The soft spot is also decisive for ChoCo: the paper already makes the population a continuum and studies mass flows and steady-state waiting times. This is strong evidence that population continuization is a natural object for kidney exchange, but a computational mirror of Theorem 12 would largely restate a model the paper already has. I would present it as a compelling positive precedent, while conceding that it is a novelty collision rather than a fresh continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 12 is the wrong place to claim a new continuous mirror: the paper already formulates the society as a continuum. Section 2 measures patient and donor arrivals by Lebesgue mass, and Theorem 12 characterizes the resulting steady-state allocation. Its proof constructs the bottleneck partition using minimum cuts and maximum flows. The proposed task—compute the group waiting times and an allocation realizing them—is therefore the theorem’s existing continuous problem, not a discrete problem newly continuized. The paper is strong evidence that high-multiplicity kidney exchange is a sensible regime; for this anchor, that same fact makes the novelty collision direct.

There is no stated complexity classification, but formalizing this particular computation does not reveal much of a gap. The ABO model has a fixed set of blood types and pair categories, so its feasibility graph has fixed size. With rational rates and a piecewise-linear \(F\), the partition can be found by a constant number of finite-graph operations, and a realizing flow can be recovered by max flow. That makes bit-complexity bookkeeping possible, but it would formalize the constructive result the paper already gives, rather than chart a new landscape in \(m,\tau,L\).

A stronger proposal would let types encode detailed tissue compatibility, sensitization, and other clinical attributes, then study the problem as \(\tau\) grows. That could be worthwhile, but it leaves Theorem 12’s shared crossmatch-risk assumption and fixed ABO category system behind. If the extension remains an ordinary compatibility-flow problem, it generalizes the existing allocation model; if it adds multiway exchange constraints, it asks a different optimization question. Neither is a new continuous mirror of this theorem. Likewise, finite-pool error bounds would extend the paper’s random-matching bridge, rather than continuize its allocation result.

So the proponent’s sole anchor does not survive as a fresh mirror. The negative case is weaker against the broader universal claim: richer clinical type spaces or new policy-intervention questions could be valuable high-multiplicity research. But those possibilities do not rescue the proposed mirror of Theorem 12.
