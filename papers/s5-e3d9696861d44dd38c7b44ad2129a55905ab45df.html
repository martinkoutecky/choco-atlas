The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of roommate types \(T\), rational shares \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and each type's preferences over acceptable roommate types, decide whether there are nonnegative matching masses \(x_{tu}\) and single masses \(s_t\) such that \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\) for \(t\ne u\), \(\sum_u x_{tu}+s_t=\mu_t\), and no mutually acceptable type pair has positive mass on both sides who are single or strictly prefer each other to their assigned type; if so, construct such a mass assignment.

The model it lives in

A roommate market with finitely many complete preference-profile types, rational population shares \(\mu_t\), symmetric pair-mass variables \(x_{tu}\), and single masses \(s_t\); the task is to find a feasible assignment with no positive mass of mutually willing blocking pairs, or report that none exists.

The objection that survived

The paper proves encoding correctness for finite instances but gives no complexity guarantee for the mass problem; this limits what the paper contributes about the mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the stable-matching computation represented by Theorem 1. It leaves the fairness objectives, Almost-SR optimization, ASP formulation details, and empirical comparisons alone; the cited Almost-SR hardness motivates a related question but is not an anchor proved by this paper.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the decision and construction complexity of type-mass stable roommates in the number of types and input bit length?
  • Does the mass problem admit a polynomial-time algorithm, and how does its solution relate to stable matchings in high-multiplicity finite instances?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is for a type-mass version of Almost Stable Roommates. The paper’s complexity table reports: “Almost SR (and thus SRT (weak))” is NP-hard, citing Theorem 1 of Abraham et al. (2005). That hardness is cited, not proved by this paper. Almost SR asks for a matching that minimizes the number of blocking pairs; the table also notes that hardness applies to the weak-stability version with ties.

Here is a continuous version of that underlying problem. Let \(T\) be a finite set of roommate types. A type describes everything relevant to the assignment, including a resident’s compatibility profile and ranking of the available roommate profiles. Members of a type are interchangeable in the model. The input gives each type a rational population share \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and its preference order over types.

Let \(x_{tu}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to type \(u\), and \(s_t\) the mass left single. Require \(x_{tu}\geq 0\), \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\) for \(t\neq u\), and \(\sum_u x_{tu}+s_t=\mu_t\). For an acceptable type \(u\), let \(D_{tu}\) be the mass of type \(t\) residents who are single or strictly prefer \(u\) to their assigned roommate type. Then define the blocking-pair density
\[ \Phi(x)=\sum_{t<u}D_{tu}D_{ut}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_t D_{tt}^{2}. \]
The products count the measure of pairs whose two members would both prefer one another; the factor \(1/2\) handles pairs within a single type. Almost-Stable-Roommates\(_\infty\) asks, given a rational threshold \(K\), whether a feasible assignment exists with \(\Phi(x)\leq K\); an optimizer returns an assignment minimizing \(\Phi\). This is a normalized high-multiplicity version of counting blocking pairs: for \(N\) residents, the pair count scales as \(N^2\).

A second, supporting anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for an SRI instance, each answer set of the ASP program encodes exactly one stable matching, and each stable matching gives exactly one answer set. That is a correctness result for the framework, not a complexity classification. Its continuous counterpart is Stable-Roommates-Mass\(_\infty\): given type shares and preferences, find a feasible mass assignment with no blocking type pair, or report that none exists. In the notation above, this means \(D_{tu}=0\) or \(D_{ut}=0\) for every mutually acceptable pair \(t,u\), with \(D_{tt}=0\) for \(t=u\). The paper also says its formulation applies to weakly stable SRTI instances, which makes the type-level indifferences a natural fit.

The regime I have in mind is a large shared-housing program: many residents, but relatively few relevant profiles and preference patterns—say, people distinguished by sleep schedule, smoking, accessibility needs, or language, and ranking those profiles rather than named individuals. Then \(N\) is much larger than the support size \(\tau=|T|\), and each \(\mu_t\) is the share of residents of type \(t\). This is an honest high-multiplicity setting if those profiles capture the preferences the assignment actually uses.

For Stable-Roommates-Mass\(_\infty\), I would initially expect Class A: fractional assignment can split cohorts across roommate types, which may smooth out discrete obstructions, and the finite stable-roommates problem is polynomial-time solvable. That is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 1. For Almost-Stable-Roommates\(_\infty\), I would expect Class C, continuum-specific hardness: the pair-density objective is quadratic in the assigned masses, and global minimization over the symmetric matching constraints is nonconvex. The cited discrete NP-hardness motivates the question, but fractional assignments mean it does not by itself prove hardness transfers.

The weakest point is that roommate preferences can be personal and directed at named people. This mirror assumes that relevant preferences are genuinely about roommate profiles, so everyone in a type can be treated as interchangeable. That is plausible for some large, profile-based housing systems, but it would not describe every roommates application. The mirror covers the paper’s stable-matching semantics and its cited Almost-SR/SRT hardness result; it does not continuize the ASP encoding, the experiments, or the paper’s other fairness objectives.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper as a ChoCo target is about what the paper contributes. Its only numbered theorem proves that the ASP encoding corresponds exactly to stable matchings. It gives no complexity classification or algorithmic bound. The proposed Stable-Roommates-Mass\(_\infty\) problem is meaningful, but its complexity is a new question; it is not the continuous counterpart of a complexity result in that theorem.

The Almost-SR anchor has a similar attribution problem. The paper reports NP-hardness in its complexity table, but cites Abraham et al. (2005) for it; the paper’s own contribution is a general ASP framework and empirical evaluation. So the proposed mass-density optimization does not continuize a hardness result established by this paper. That weakens the paper-specific case, though prior work does not make the continuous question unworthy.

I cannot honestly defeat the stronger Almost-SR mirror on its substance. In a large housing system where residents rank roommate profiles, profile-based types are plausible, and the proposed \(\Phi(x)\) is a coherent normalized limit of the finite blocking-pair count. It is a genuine continuous-population computational problem. The stable-mass anchor is also well-defined, though it extends a correctness theorem rather than mirroring a complexity claim.

So the negative case is limited: this paper is a weak fit if ChoCo requires a paper’s own computational result to mirror. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The profile-based Almost-SR model is the point where that case fails.
