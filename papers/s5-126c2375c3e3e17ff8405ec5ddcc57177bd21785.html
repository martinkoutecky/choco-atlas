The case FOR (proponent)

A credible population regime is reserve procurement from a large fleet of small batteries or flexible loads. A type would include everything the auction uses: grid location, reserve product, capacity, availability, and cost and bid curves. Mass would be the fraction of resources of that type. The operator would choose accepted supply per resource, with capacities and procurement requirements scaled per capita. For example, a finite-support instance could specify rational type masses \(\mu_t\), per-resource capacities \(\bar X_t\), and cost curves \(b_t\); its procurement cost would aggregate as \(\sum_t \mu_t b_t(q_t)\), subject to per-capita market constraints. This makes the *population* continuous; the paper’s already-continuous goods are not the proposed mirror.

But the paper has no qualifying named computational-complexity anchor. Its closest named results are structural or mechanism-theoretic:

  • Theorem 2 (proved here) says that, for any bid profile and participating bidder set, the VCG outcome is in the core if and only if the auction objective \(J\) is supermodular.
  • Theorem 5 (proved here) says that \(J\) is supermodular when the bidder costs are increasing and convex and the procurement requirement \(M\) is supermodular and nondecreasing.
  • Theorem 6 (proved here) says that a core-selecting mechanism prevents losing bidders from profiting by joint deviation and prevents multiple identities from benefiting beyond the truthful VCG utility.

These results could motivate a population version of core-selecting payment computation. But none classifies a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized. Section 4 discusses computational difficulty and presents Algorithm 1; its convergence claim cites an external theorem, and the paper gives no named complexity bound for that algorithm. I would not present Theorem 5 or 6 as a complexity anchor. Under the requested rule, there are therefore zero anchors and no per-anchor prover questions to state.

The soft spot in the population idea is that continuum coalitions and utility *densities* need a careful definition: a single agent has zero mass, so the paper’s finite-agent VCG utility from removing that agent does not carry over automatically. That is a real modeling task. Still, it does not fix the missing anchor: the paper may support a plausible population extension of its auction model, but it supplies no qualifying named computational result for a complexity mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is narrower than the claim I was asked to defend. The proponent is right that Theorems 2, 5, and 6 are not complexity results. The paper gives no polynomial-time or hardness classification, and Algorithm 1’s convergence claim relies on an external theorem; the reported running times are experiments, not complexity bounds. That leaves no named computational-complexity result here to anchor a ChoCo mirror.

That disposes of those three results as *computational anchors*, but not as possible continuous questions. The best mirror of Theorem 2 would replace bidder subsets by submeasures of a type distribution and ask whether supermodularity of the resulting cost function characterizes core VCG outcomes for positive-mass coalitions. A single agent has zero mass in an atomless population, so its VCG externality vanishes; utility densities and positive-mass coalitions would be needed. Those are substantial modeling choices, but they do not rule out the mirror. They do show that it would be a new cooperative-game-theoretic characterization, not a complexity result from this paper.

For Theorem 5, the strongest version aggregates identical suppliers by type and scales their capacities and procurement requirements with mass. With convex per-supplier costs, the aggregate cost has the perspective form \(\mu_t c_t(q_t/\mu_t)\). The supermodularity condition might then extend, perhaps through a finite-dimensional convex program when the type support is finite. That is mathematically plausible. The objection is again one of scope: the theorem supplies structural sufficient conditions, not an algorithmic classification, and a computational question built on top of it would be new work rather than a mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The closest route to a computational mirror is Theorem 6 and Algorithm 1: compute core-selecting payments when the population is represented by finitely many types. A type-level version would need to handle every possible submass coalition, and its separation problem could be difficult; the paper gives no bound showing that its finite-bidder constraint-generation method carries over. The false-name part of Theorem 6 has an additional gap: splitting one participant’s bid across several identities depends on ownership links that an anonymous mass distribution does not encode. A population model could still study blocking by positive-mass coalitions, but that would capture only part of the theorem.

So my negative case succeeds at the anchor audit: none of the named results is a computational-complexity anchor. It does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The proposed fleet of small, similar suppliers is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and a computational study of type-level core payment calculation could be worthwhile. The honest case against is that such a study would extend the paper’s computational agenda; it would not mirror a complexity result the paper actually proves.
