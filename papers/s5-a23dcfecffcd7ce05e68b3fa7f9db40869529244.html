The continuous mirror question

Given registries with rational pair-type masses \(\mu_t\), type-determined compatibility and match-quality weights, and cycle bounds \(b_k\) and \(B\) with \(B\geq\max_k b_k\), compute each registry’s standalone-optimal allocation and its resulting transplant mass \(r_k\). Let \(\mathcal C\) be the allowable discrete cycle patterns, including repeated types when sufficient mass is available; for each pattern let \(a_{tC}\) count its uses of type \(t\), \(a_{kC}\) count its participants from registry \(k\), and \(q_C\) be its total quality. Find cycle masses \(x_C\geq 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{C\in\mathcal C}q_Cx_C\), subject to \(\sum_C a_{tC}x_C\leq\mu_t\) for every type and \(\sum_C a_{kC}x_C\geq r_k\) for every registry.

The model it lives in

A continuum of pairs represented by rational masses over a small set of complete, match-relevant types. Each individual still receives an integral exchange cycle; the decision variables \(x_C\) aggregate mass assigned to allowable discrete cycle patterns, with type supplies, registry-level IR guarantees, and quality maximization expressed as a linear program.

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 1’s round-level transplant-count guarantee and the associated one-round quality optimization when the IR shortfall is zero. It leaves aside cumulative deficits, Shapley credits, stochastic arrivals and dropouts, and the paper’s multi-year simulation findings.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can realistic large exchange pools be represented by a small support of types that fully determines compatibility and match quality?
  • When the cycle bound \(B\) is part of the input rather than fixed, is there an efficient pricing or separation algorithm for the cycle-pattern LP?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or similar. The strongest named anchor for a positive case is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It says that in every round there is a pooled allocation giving every registry at least its standalone transplant count: take each registry’s standalone-optimal allocation and combine them. The proof uses the model’s pooled feasibility constraints.

My lead is Continuous Round-Level IR Kidney Exchange (C-IR-KEP). Its setting is a large multi-hospital or national exchange, where each registry has many pairs but those pairs fall into a relatively small number \(\tau\) of complete, match-relevant types. A type includes the registry and everything the model uses about a pair: compatibility and match-quality relationships, along with applicable cycle limits. For instance, a model with blood-group compatibility and a finite set of clinical score categories could have many pairs but comparatively few such types. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the total pool in type \(t\); it represents many individual pairs, each of whom still participates in an integral exchange cycle.

An instance consists of registries, rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational arc-quality weights, and the cycle limits \(b_k\) and \(B\). For each registry \(k\), it also includes a standalone-optimal reference allocation \(z^k\), with a fixed tie-breaking rule if needed, and its transplant count \(r_k\). Let \(\mathcal C\) be the allowable cycle patterns on the types, including patterns that use a type more than once when enough mass is available. For each pattern \(C\), let \(a_{tC}\) count its uses of type \(t\), \(a_{kC}\) count its participants from registry \(k\), and \(q_C\) be its total match-quality weight. The question is to return a mass allocation \(x_C\ge 0\) that maximizes \(\sum_{C\in\mathcal C}q_Cx_C\), subject to
\[ \sum_{C\in\mathcal C}a_{tC}x_C\le\mu_t\quad\text{for every type }t, \qquad \sum_{C\in\mathcal C}a_{kC}x_C\ge r_k\quad\text{for every registry }k. \]
Each pattern must respect the registry-specific and pooled cycle limits. A solution is the vector of cycle masses \(x_C\), not an assignment of fractional kidneys to people.

The continuous counterpart of Proposition 1 is that C-IR-KEP has a feasible solution: combine the standalone reference allocations, now expressed as mass over cycle patterns. Optimizing quality among those IR-feasible allocations is a linear program. For fixed maximum cycle length \(B\), the type-level cycle patterns can be enumerated in polynomially many variables in \(\tau\), so I expect this version to be tractable. With \(B\) as part of the input, the pattern count can grow as \(\tau^B\); whether a useful pricing or separation algorithm exists then is a further question. I make no claim that Proposition 1 establishes a complexity classification.

This is a plausible mirror of the paper’s one-round, count-based IR allocation: it retains registries, exchange compatibility, cycle limits, transplant-count guarantees, and match-quality optimization. Its scope is that IR allocation result and the associated per-round optimization. It does not mirror the paper’s six-year stochastic analysis, dropout and arrival dynamics, or Shapley-credit mechanism.

The weakest point is support compression. If the full HLA and quality profile makes nearly every pair a distinct type, then \(\tau\) approaches the number of pairs and the continuous formulation offers little computational gain. The paper’s simulations are small, while my case relies on a larger finite-profile regime. There is also a condition missing from Proposition 1 as printed: the union argument requires \(B\ge\max_k b_k\), so every standalone cycle remains allowed in the pooled model. The paper’s reported parameter settings satisfy that condition; I would restrict the mirror to that regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the wrong anchor for a computational mirror. It proves feasibility by taking each registry’s standalone allocation and combining them. Its continuous counterpart has the same immediate witness. The proposition says nothing about complexity or an algorithm. The proposed C-IR-KEP becomes computationally interesting when it adds quality maximization over mass allocations, but that is a new problem extracted from the paper’s integer program, not a continuization of the result Proposition 1 establishes. On the programme’s stated test, this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror.

The stronger alternative is to continuize the full lexicographic IR allocation, perhaps including arrivals and dropout over time. That is harder to dismiss: a large exchange could have many pairs sharing a finite set of relevant profiles. Yet an exact type must preserve the features that determine compatibility and match quality, as well as registry and waiting state. With detailed crossmatch and clinical scores, the number of such types can approach the number of pairs; coarser categories make support smaller by simplifying the model. The paper’s own simulations, with two registries and modest arrival rates, do not establish a high multiplicity regime.

That limitation still does not defeat every scenario. A large exchange model with a deliberately finite compatibility and quality profile is plausible, and the paper’s blood group formulation gives it a foothold. Its mass allocation problem looks like a natural continuous mirror, even if it is not anchored by Proposition 1 as a complexity result. So the negative case defeats the analyst’s stated anchor, but I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
