The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition in the supplied paper that asserts a computational result. The closest thing is the unnumbered “Problem Complexity” statement in Section IV: the problem in Definition 2—given a tie-aware ranking \(\pi\), find a fair ranking \(\pi^*\) minimizing generalized Kendall–Tau distance \(G(\pi,\pi^*)\)—“is NP-Hard.” The text supplied gives no proof or citation for that assertion at that point. I cannot honestly supply the numbered anchor your criteria require. The exact algorithm and heuristic are described, but the paper gives no numbered complexity theorem for either.

Still, that unnumbered NP-hardness claim is the paper’s strongest candidate for a continuous mirror. The mirror I would argue for is Continuous Closest Fair Ranking of an Applicant Population.

Consider a large hiring or talent-search pool. Applicants share a protected-group label and a score band in an existing ranking; suppose these are the only features used by the post-processing problem. A type is \(t=(g,r)\), where \(g\in\{G_1,G_2\}\) is the group and \(r\) is the applicant’s input rank bucket. The instance gives the positive-support types and rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The number of applicants may be very large while the number of group-by-score-band types, \(\tau\), is small. This is plausible when a ranking system presents many applicants in a relatively small number of score bands. It is a regime claim about such pools, not about rankings in general.

A solution is a measurable output rank \(q(x)\in[0,1]\) for each unit \(x\) of candidate mass; smaller values rank higher, and equal values are ties. Applicants of the same type may be split across output ranks, while each individual unit still receives one rank. Minimize the population’s pairwise disagreement with the input ranking: the loss for a pair is one if the output reverses their input order, or if one ranking ties them and the other strictly orders them, and zero otherwise. Require pairwise statistical parity: the absolute difference between the two groups’ probabilities of outranking one another, for independently sampled applicants from each group, must be at most \(\epsilon\). A solution is a fair measurable ranking attaining the minimum disagreement.

This preserves the problem in Definition 2: the objective is still closeness to the supplied tie-aware ranking under the paper’s generalized Kendall–Tau distance, and the constraint is still its pairwise parity notion. It does not replace the question with fair exposure maximization or with an encoding problem. A real implementation of the continuous question would also need a representation theorem—such as showing that an optimum can be described by a finite ordered set of output buckets and type-mass allocations. That is an important part of the research question, not something established by the paper.

I would provisionally expect this mirror to be Class C, or at least unresolved. Both the pairwise disagreement objective and parity constraint couple pairs of masses, so the mirror does not immediately inherit the programme’s usual linear-programming form. The discrete NP-hardness does not automatically transfer: continuous mass can be split across output ranks. Conversely, if \(\tau\) grows with the number of applicants, the ranking’s combinatorics may remain substantial. Useful next questions are whether optimal rankings admit a finite representation, what the complexity is in \(\tau\) and the mass bit length, and whether a discrete-to-continuous approximation or hardness transfer can be proved.

The original authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: it keeps their input ranking, distance measure, fairness condition, and post-processing objective. The weakest point is that the paper ranks items; the mirror treats a large pool of applicants as the population. That is credible for recruitment, but the type compression and within-type mass splitting are substantive modelling choices. They deserve scrutiny because the paper’s named-candidate setting does not itself establish that the continuous regime is sensible.

The mirror covers only the single-input-ranking closest-fair-ranking problem of Definition 2. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s heuristic, its multiple-ranking extension, or fair rank aggregation generally. The positive case is therefore a concrete research question suggested by the paper’s central optimization problem, not a case anchored in a qualifying numbered result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only plausible anchor is the paper’s unnumbered claim that the closest-fair-ranking problem is NP-hard. It does not meet the requested standard of a named computational result: the manuscript gives no numbered theorem or proof for that claim, and its exact ILP and heuristic are described through formulations and experiments rather than a complexity result. That is the strongest case against treating this as a continuization of a paper result.

The proposed applicant-pool mirror itself is harder to dismiss. A type can be a protected group and an input-rank bucket. If many applicants share those features, the paper’s objective and parity constraint depend on their masses and pairwise relations, not on their names. Splitting a type across output buckets is a coherent mass version of assigning different ranks to otherwise interchangeable applicants. Normalizing the pairwise loss gives a nontrivial population objective; neither the parity condition nor the optimization obviously disappears in the limit. The paper’s own setup makes this regime plausible, since it explicitly allows ties and treats candidates as items.

So I would argue against prioritizing this as a mirror of a named result, but I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The applicant-pool formulation appears to be a credible computational question, though it requires its own representation and complexity analysis. The negative case is strong on the missing theorem anchor and weak on the universal claim.
