The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s max-variant with \(k\) facilities. Imagine a long city corridor where a very large resident population shares a modest number \(\tau\) of home-location types. The city chooses \(k\) facility slots from residents’ reported locations; a resident’s cost is the distance to the farthest facility, and social cost is the population average of those costs.

Represent the profile by \(N\) equal-mass residents and a distribution \(\mu\) over rational location types, where \(N\mu_t\) residents report location \(t\). Here \(N\) may be large while \(\tau=|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\) stays modest. A mechanism returns \(k\) slots assigned to distinct residents’ reports, so slots may share a coordinate when backed by different residents, as in the paper. A resident’s deviation from \(x\) to \(y\) changes the distribution to \(\mu-\frac1N\delta_x+\frac1N\delta_y\). Truthfulness is the paper’s individual strategyproofness condition under that deviation. The objective is to characterize the best worst-case ratio
\[ \frac{\sum_t\mu_t\max_{f\in F}|t-f|}{\operatorname{OPT}_k(\mu)} \]
achievable by deterministic truthful mechanisms as \(N\) grows, with \(\tau\) bounded. This keeps the individual incentive and the report-dependent candidate locations, while making the society’s aggregate distribution the compact input.

The anchor is Theorem 4.5, proved in this paper: for the max-variant with \(k\) facilities, every deterministic strategyproof mechanism has approximation ratio at least \(k+1\). Its witness has \(k+1\) agents: one at \(0\), \(k-1\) at \(1\), and one at \(2\). Since there are only \(k-1\) reports at \(1\), some facility must be placed at an endpoint. Move the endpoint resident at \(2\) to \(1\). Strategyproofness forces a facility to remain at \(0\); the resulting social cost is \(k+1\), while placing all facilities at \(1\) has cost \(1\). That is the theorem’s lower-bound content in full.

The continuous question is whether this obstruction survives when the profile is encoded by rational masses and \(N\) is large: does the best deterministic truthful ratio still have a lower bound like \(k+1\), or can high multiplicity improve it? I expect the lower-bound phenomenon to persist, in the sense of hardness transferring, because a resident’s utility does not shrink with their mass: even a \(1/N\) report change can constrain the mechanism, and a rare report can still create or remove a candidate location. But the theorem’s exact \(k+1\) witness does not transfer automatically: adding residents changes how many facility slots can use a shared coordinate. Establishing the asymptotic ratio, and a succinct mechanism attaining it, is the work this mirror asks for.

The mirror covers only Theorem 4.5’s deterministic max-variant result, not the paper’s other variants. It also turns an approximation lower bound into a high-multiplicity mechanism-design question; it is not an NP-hardness claim.

My weakest point is the mass granularity needed to preserve individual strategyproofness. A purely nonatomic distribution forgets the identity of a single resident, making their deviation vacuous or forcing a different, cohort-level incentive notion. Keeping the \(1/N\) quantum avoids that change and gives a natural high-multiplicity representation, but it means proving that the \(k+1\) obstruction survives as \(N\to\infty\) is essential, not a corollary of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 4.5, and its result is a lower bound on the approximation ratio of deterministic strategyproof mechanisms for the max-variant. That is a result about the efficiency cost of truthfulness, not about the running time or complexity of a computational problem. The paper gives no complexity claim for a mechanism-design problem that a continuous population could make tractable. A mirror could be made computational by adding a new question, such as the complexity of synthesizing truthful mechanisms from rational mass profiles, but that would be an adjacent project rather than a continuation of this theorem.

The more fundamental difficulty is preserving the theorem’s strategic unit. In a nonatomic society, one person’s report changes no mass. If mechanisms depend only on the distribution, individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous. If feasibility still depends on the set of reports—including reports from zero-mass individuals—that set is not determined by the distribution. Replacing individual deviations with shifts of positive mass gives a coherent cohort-incentive model, but changes who is allowed to strategize and therefore changes the paper’s question.

The proponent’s finite-\(N\) version preserves individual incentives, but it does so by retaining the \(1/N\) quantum and the number of residents at each report. Those counts affect which facility placements are feasible. The theorem’s witness makes this especially clear: the deviation changes the number of reports at \(1\) from \(k-1\) to \(k\), and the proof turns on that threshold. A limiting distribution forgets any \(O(1)\) count because its mass vanishes. Restricting attention to types with positive limiting mass avoids that loss only by excluding rare reports, including reports created by an individual deviation. Keeping all counts instead makes the proposed object a sequence of finite games, not a continuum mechanism defined by the mass profile alone.

This is not an objection that the theorem’s bound might change in the limit; the answer is not the issue. It is a problem of defining a continuous mirror that retains both the paper’s feasible outcomes and its individual incentive constraints. The cited high-multiplicity work supports studying large populations with few types, so I would not use it as a novelty collision. It does not resolve the gap between a mass distribution and the finite counts that this paper’s mechanism uses.

My negative case is strongest for ChoCo’s stated computational programme: this anchor supplies no complexity result, and preserving its incentives appears to require retaining the finite granularity that a distributional limit discards. The broader claim that no version could ever be worthwhile is weaker. A finite-\(N\) high-multiplicity study could be valuable mechanism-design research in its own right, but the paper does not give a computational theorem for that programme to continuize.
