The continuous mirror question

Given a rational piecewise-constant probability density \(g\) on a rational polyhedral partition of the simplex \(\Delta(S_3)\) of masses over the six complete rankings, compute exactly the probability that \(\operatorname{IRV}(\mu)\) is a Borda loser for \(\mu\sim g\), using a fixed tie-breaking rule.

The model it lives in

A society is a mass vector \(\mu\in\Delta(S_3)\) over six complete-ranking types. The input density \(g\) specifies a probability law over such societies; the quantity sought is the exact probability that IRV elects a candidate with minimum Borda score.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result establishing a complexity classification such as polynomial time, NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. Its closest named computational anchor is Proposition 2(1), proved here: under impartial anonymous culture (IAC), for three-candidate elections with complete rankings, the limiting probability as \(V\to\infty\) that IRV elects the Borda loser is \(4301/241920\), or about \(1.78\%\). The authors obtain it by describing the relevant regions with inequalities and computing their volumes. I would use this as an exact probability-computation anchor, not present it as a complexity theorem.

A natural regime is a large election with only three candidates and complete rankings: there are \(N\) voters but at most six types, one for each ranking, so \(N\gg\tau\le6\). A type is a complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of the electorate holding that ranking. For \(\mu\in\Delta(S_3)\), IRV transfers the mass of eliminated candidates’ supporters to their highest-ranked surviving candidate. The Borda score is
\[ \mathrm{BSc}_\mu(c)=2\sum_{r:\,c\text{ is first in }r}\mu_r+\sum_{r:\,c\text{ is second in }r}\mu_r. \]
The question is how often the resulting IRV winner is also the candidate with the lowest Borda score.

I would pose Continuous Weak-Winner Probability as follows. An instance gives a rational, piecewise-constant probability density \(g\) over a rational polyhedral partition of \(\Delta(S_3)\). Compute exactly
\[ \Pr_{\mu\sim g}\!\left[\operatorname{IRV}(\mu)=\arg\min_c\mathrm{BSc}_\mu(c)\right]. \]
Fix any tie-breaking rule; for an absolutely continuous density, ties occur on measure-zero sets. The solution is the exact probability. The uniform density is the limiting IAC case in Proposition 2(1), with answer \(4301/241920\).

This is a plausible continuation of the authors’ question about how often IRV elects a weak candidate: the society is now a vector of population shares, and the output remains their election rule’s winner. For three candidates, I expect the density-input version to be tractable: the event is a union of a constant number of polyhedral regions in the fixed five-dimensional simplex, so its probability can be computed by intersecting those regions with the input partition and calculating rational volumes. This prediction concerns the proposed problem; Proposition 2 establishes only the uniform IAC case. Variable numbers of candidates would raise further questions about exact versus approximate probability computation and how complexity grows with the number of ranking types.

The scope is just Proposition 2(1), not the paper’s spatial simulations or empirical results. The weakest point is that the most direct continuous instance—the uniform IAC limit—is already essentially computed in the paper itself, which explicitly uses simplex volumes. The more general density input is a credible extension, but it makes the case less clearly a missing continuous mirror. And with only three candidates, the extension’s expected tractability is a modest result for a programme focused on complexity landscapes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The stated anchor is already the continuous limit of the paper’s own IAC calculation. Its six ballot counts, divided by \(V\), form a point in a five-dimensional simplex; as \(V\to\infty\), the IAC calculation becomes a volume calculation over that simplex. Proposition 2(1) gives the resulting probability, \(4301/241920\). So asking for that same continuous-population probability would add no missing computation. This is a narrow objection: the paper’s discrete-to-continuous limit is a real result, not a reason to dismiss continuization generally.

The strongest repair is the proposed input density \(g\), asking for the probability that IRV elects the Borda loser under a broader distribution over continuous societies. That is a coherent extension, but \(g\) supplies a new layer of uncertainty over societies. A particular continuous society already determines both the IRV winner and the Borda loser; the probability question needs an additional law over societies. That makes the extension less directly about what changes when the population becomes continuous.

I can’t make that objection decisive. The paper itself asks how often the outcome occurs under different population models, so a broader law over continuous societies could be something its authors recognize as their problem. And a large electorate with six ranking types is a particularly clear high-multiplicity regime. The honest negative case is therefore limited: the exact IAC anchor is already answered, but its generalized version survives. I cannot rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario.
