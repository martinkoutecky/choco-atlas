The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over approval types \(t\subseteq C\), listed with their positive rational masses, compute the \(k\)-candidate committee produced by MMS. For each nonempty \(D\subseteq C\), calculate \(v_\mu(D)\) by maximizing \(s\) subject to \(\sum_{t:c\in t} y_{t,c}\ge s\) for every \(c\in D\), \(\sum_{c\in t\cap D} y_{t,c}=\mu_t\) for every type with \(t\cap D\ne\varnothing\), and \(y_{t,c}\ge0\); then iteratively add the unelected candidate maximizing \(v_\mu(W\cup\{c\})\), using the paper’s fixed priority for ties.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over approval types, with \(\tau\) positive-mass types. The committee remains a discrete set of \(k\) candidates; for each candidate set considered by the greedy rule, \(y_{t,c}\) allocates type \(t\)’s mass among approved candidates to maximize the least support. Aggregating by type gives \(O(m\tau)\) allocation variables per support LP and a running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The case FOR (proponent)

Theorem 2 is my lead anchor. Proved in this paper, it states that the maximin support method (MMS) can be computed in polynomial time. MMS chooses \(k\) candidates sequentially: for each unelected candidate, it considers the set formed by adding that candidate to the winners chosen so far, and finds a support distribution that maximizes the least-supported candidate’s support in that set. It then adds the candidate with the greatest resulting support. The paper formulates each support calculation as a linear program: each voter’s unit of support is divided among the approved candidates in the set, and the objective is to maximize the minimum total support. Theorem 1, also proved here, ensures that the candidate’s support in an optimal distribution equals that maximum minimum support.

Here is the continuous-population mirror, which I would call \(\mathrm{MMS}_\infty\). An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\) of size \(m\), a committee size \(k\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over approval types \(t\subseteq C\). The input lists the \(\tau\) types with positive mass, with each \(\mu_t\) a binary rational and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For any nonempty set \(D\subseteq C\), define \(v_\mu(D)\) by the linear program
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{maximize } & s\\ \text{subject to }& \sum_{t:\,c\in t} y_{t,c}\ge s &&\text{for every }c\in D,\\ &\sum_{c\in t\cap D}y_{t,c}=\mu_t &&\text{for every }t\text{ with }t\cap D\ne\varnothing,\\ &y_{t,c}\ge 0 &&\text{for }c\in t\cap D. \end{aligned} \]
The variable \(y_{t,c}\) is the amount of type \(t\)’s population mass credited to candidate \(c\). Starting from \(W_0=\varnothing\), at step \(j\) choose an unelected candidate \(c\) maximizing \(v_\mu(W_j\cup\{c\})\), and set \(W_{j+1}=W_j\cup\{c\}\). Use the paper’s fixed candidate priority to break ties. The solution is the resulting committee \(W_k\), an integral set of exactly \(k\) candidates; the fractional variables describe how approval mass is credited when calculating support.

The regime is a large electorate choosing \(k\) candidates from a fixed slate—for example, a citywide election with many residents but a relatively small number of distinct approval ballots. A type is the complete approval set used by the rule, so agents sharing a type are indistinguishable for this problem. It is plausible to have \(n\) in the hundreds of thousands or millions while \(\tau\) is in the hundreds or low thousands. The population is the continuous object; the committee remains discrete.

The mirror has an exact high-multiplicity dictionary. A discrete election with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\) gives \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); its support values are simply scaled by \(1/n\), so the candidate choices and tie-breaking are unchanged. Conversely, any rational \(\mu\) can be represented by a finite electorate using a common denominator. Aggregating the paper’s voter-indexed LP by approval type gives at most \(O(m\tau)\) allocation variables per LP. Since the method needs only polynomially many LP calls, this mirror is in Class A, with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the encoding length of the masses. The result it covers is specifically Theorem 2’s computation of MMS; it does not extend the claim to the paper’s axiomatic results or to other multiwinner rules.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: their rule depends on approval ballots and support distributions, and their computation already reduces to LPs. Grouping identical ballots and replacing counts by rational proportions preserves exactly the information the rule uses. Further questions include how sharply the running time can depend on \(\tau\), and how much mass must move between approval types to change the winning committee.

The weak point is that this continuization does not change the answer on rational instances: it is exactly a rescaled finite election. Its contribution is the compressed population representation and a corresponding complexity bound when \(n\) is huge and \(\tau\) is moderate, rather than a new outcome or a hardness result that disappears in the continuum. That makes this a narrower case than a hardness-dissolution story, but a credible continuous mirror of the paper’s named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection to Theorem 2’s mirror is that it appears to add almost no new computation. The paper’s support LP can already be aggregated by approval type: for each type \(t\), combine the voter-level variables into totals \(y_{t,c}\), with \(\sum_{c\in t\cap D} y_{t,c}=n_t\). That gives the proposed LP directly, with integer counts in place of masses. Replacing \(n_t\) by rational masses \(\mu_t\) just normalizes the same constraints. Every rational instance can be scaled to a finite election, and scaling all masses leaves every candidate comparison unchanged. So this mirror is an exact re-encoding of the paper’s computation, not a new algorithmic phenomenon.

That objection also applies to a stronger version: aggregate the paper’s LPs, then analyze the running time in \(m,\tau,L\). This is useful for elections with huge multiplicities and few ballot types, but the aggregation itself is immediate from the paper’s formulation. The result would sharpen the input-size analysis of Theorem 2; it would not reveal a new tractability mechanism or a new continuous-specific boundary.

Still, that does not defeat the mirror under ChoCo’s stated standard. The population remains finite in the paper, even though support can be divided fractionally among candidates. The society is not already a distribution over types, and the output committee being discrete is no objection. Moreover, a citywide election with many voters sharing a relatively small number of approval ballots is a convincing high-multiplicity regime. The population mass is meaningful there, and the proposed theorem asks a computational question about that population representation.

So the negative case is weak. It shows that this particular mirror may be a straightforward high-multiplicity corollary of Theorem 2, rather than a technically surprising continuization. But the programme explicitly treats high-multiplicity analysis as worthwhile, and the equivalence of answers does not disqualify a mirror. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists for this anchor.
