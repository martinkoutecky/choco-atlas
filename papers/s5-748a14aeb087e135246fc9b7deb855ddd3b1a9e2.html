The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a single mirror of the paper’s division problem under single-peaked preferences. It is a genuine high-multiplicity mirror, although the paper itself contains no named complexity result.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states:

If a social choice function \(f\) is \(H\)-joint monotonic, \(H\)-respectful, and defined on an \(H\)-rich domain, then \(f\) is \(H\)-strategy-proof if and only if it is \(H\)-weakly group strategy-proof.

Here \(H\)-strategy-proof means that no agent in \(H\) can obtain a strictly better consequence by misreporting. \(H\)-weak group strategy-proofness means that no coalition \(C\subseteq H\) can jointly misreport so that every member of \(C\) is strictly better off. \(H\)-richness requires the domain to contain suitable intermediate preferences: given two admissible preferences and consequences \(a,b\) with \(b\) strictly preferred to \(a\), one can construct an admissible preference whose upper and lower contour sets at \(b\) lie appropriately between the two original preferences while leaving the contour sets at \(a\) unchanged. \(H\)-joint monotonicity says that when the members of \(C\) alter their preferences so that their original consequences become more attractive in the relevant contour-set sense, each member must receive a consequence at least as good as before. \(H\)-respectfulness says that a preference change which leaves the changing agent indifferent at her original consequence cannot alter the utilities of the other agents in \(H\).

This theorem is proved here, not cited. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), FPT, or any other computational-complexity result. Thus this is an incentive-theoretic anchor, not a computational one.

The mirror I would propose is Continuum Uniform-Division Manipulation. Let the population have unit mass and finitely many preference types \(T\). Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and all agents of type \(t\) have the same complete single-peaked preference \(R_t\) over individual shares of a common divisible task. The type includes the full preference, not merely its peak. Assume a per-capita task supply \(r\), so an allocation gives each type a share \(x_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=r\). This scaling is the natural high-multiplicity version of a finite division problem with \(n r\) units of the task.

The mechanism is the continuum uniform rule. If \(p_t\) is the reported peak of type \(t\), it selects a cutoff \(\lambda\) and assigns \(x_t=\min(p_t,\lambda)\) when reported demand exceeds supply, and \(x_t=\max(p_t,\lambda)\) when it falls short, always satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=r\).

A coalition is now a measurable positive-mass set of agents. More concretely, let \(z_{tq}\) be the mass of true type \(t\) reporting peak \(q\), with \(\sum_q z_{tq}\le\mu_t\). The resulting report distribution is the truthful remainder together with these transferred masses. The question is whether there exists a nonzero transfer pattern and new cutoff \(\lambda'\) such that every deviating mass \(z_{tq}>0\) receives \(x_{\lambda'}(q)\) strictly preferred under \(R_t\) to its truthful allocation \(x_t\). A solution is the finite-support mass-transfer certificate \((z_{tq},q,\lambda')\); the decision question is whether the maximum feasible benefiting mass is positive.

The regime is plausible: a large population of households, workers, or units sharing a divisible task, with only finitely many recurring preference classes. The relevant comparison is \(n\gg\tau\), where \(\tau=|T|\), and a finite election with \(n_t\) agents of type \(t\) corresponds to \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). Agents are not artificially identified: agents of the same type are indistinguishable in preferences and reports, exactly as required by the high-multiplicity interpretation.

The authors should recognise this as their problem. The finite vector of agents becomes a measure over preference types, a coalition becomes positive mass, and the uniform rule becomes a weighted cutoff rule. The task is already divisible in the original paper, but that is not what is being continuized; the essential change is that the population is now the continuous object. Scaling the total task with population size merely prevents individual shares from collapsing in the limit.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A. Computing the allocation requires only a weighted threshold, and the manipulation question should reduce to a finite piecewise-linear feasibility problem over the type masses. More importantly, the paper’s argument suggests that, for the continuum uniform rule, individual strategy-proofness should lift to positive-mass group strategy-proofness through an integral version of Theorem 1. The natural follow-up questions are whether the finite proof’s sequential replacement argument extends to nonatomic coalitions, whether type masses may split across several reports, and whether the same construction works for continuum deferred acceptance and house allocation.

The weakest point is substantial: this paper is axiomatic rather than computational, and its Theorem 1 does not itself provide an algorithm or complexity classification. Also, the finite proof explicitly orders coalition members one by one, whereas a continuum coalition may be atomless and split a type across many reports. A formal measure-theoretic lift is therefore still needed. Nonetheless, the underlying problem is a credible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s own division model, rather than a fractional-outcome reformulation with the population left finite.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor is not admissible under the programme’s gate. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic equivalence theorem about social choice functions. It defines no computational input, output, instance size, optimization problem, or complexity claim. The proposed “Continuum Uniform-Division Manipulation” is therefore not a continuization of a computational result in the paper; it is a new computational problem invented around an axiomatic theorem. The paper’s remarks that the uniform rule is strategy-proof and group strategy-proof do not repair this: they are imported facts about a mechanism, not computational results proved here.

Even setting that gate aside, the division mirror has almost no population-level computational content. With finite types and peaks \(p_t\), the continuum uniform rule is just weighted water-filling:

\[ \sum_t \mu_t \min(p_t,\lambda)=r \]

in the excess-demand case, with the analogous \(\max\) equation in the shortage case. Sorting the finitely many peaks and locating the weighted cutoff computes the outcome. For rational masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), this is exactly the finite uniform rule on \(n_t\) cloned agents. The continuum has not exposed a hidden optimization problem, removed an integrality obstruction, or created a pricing/separation question. It has replaced repeated counts by weights in a one-dimensional threshold computation.

The proposed coalition formulation does not add much. Since agents of a given type have the same preference and receive the same truthful share, splitting that type into measurable submasses does not create new strategic structure. A positive-mass coalition is simply a collection of type fragments whose aggregate reports alter one cutoff. If the question is merely whether some coalition benefits, atomlessness makes it almost local: any beneficial type-level deviation can be carried out by an arbitrarily small positive mass. If instead one asks for the maximum benefiting mass, that is a new coalition-capacity optimization problem, not the group-strategy-proofness theorem being mirrored. For the uniform rule, its uncompromising single-peaked structure is precisely what already rules out such deviations.

The proponent’s scaling of the total task by population size is a legitimate repair of the fixed-supply degeneracy, but it also reveals the weakness of the mirror. Without scaling, each individual’s share vanishes as population grows. With scaling, one has simply replicated the original per-capita division economy and normalized the electorate. That is a sensible high-multiplicity representation, but not a worthwhile computational continuization of this paper’s contribution.

The stronger possible rescue—use the paper’s matching, house-allocation, auction, or cost-sharing applications—does not improve the case. Keeping houses, firms, or the single good individually named destroys high multiplicity: agents’ consequences depend on identities that occur only once. Replicating objects and capacities produces aggregate matching or market-clearing models, but then the new complexity lies in contracts, object types, capacities, or the rule itself, not in population multiplicity. With finitely many types these models reduce to finite flows, weighted order statistics, or threshold mechanisms. Likewise, cost sharing and the other single-peaked rules remain weighted cutoff procedures. These may be legitimate new models, but they are not computational mirrors of results established in this paper.

One could manufacture a difficult continuum problem by adding rich contracts, eligibility constraints, geographic structure, indivisible endowments, or an artificially complicated rule. That would make the problem worth studying, but the source of the difficulty would be the added combinatorial structure. Nothing in the paper’s Theorem 1 supplies or motivates it, and it would no longer be a continuization of the paper’s actual contribution.

Thus the negative case is strong as a programme verdict, though not as a literal impossibility theorem about every imaginable extension. A high-multiplicity division model can certainly be written down, and it is mathematically coherent. But the only proposed anchor is non-computational, while the natural population mirror is an exact weighted restatement of an already elementary cutoff rule. A worthwhile ChoCo mirror would have to introduce a new computational problem whose substance comes from elsewhere; that is insufficient to rescue this paper.
