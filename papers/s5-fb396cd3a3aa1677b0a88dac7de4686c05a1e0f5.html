What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror; the paper’s solution-set relations, axiomatizations, and dividend-distribution characterizations remain unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive opening is a high multiplicity version of cooperative TU allocation. Imagine a large population of interchangeable claimants or liable parties, grouped into \(K\) types by the characteristics that determine their contribution to coalition worth. Let \(\mu_t\) be the mass of type \(t\), and let \(V(z)\) give the worth of a coalition containing mass \(z_t\) of each type. A continuous core question would be: find per-unit payoffs \(x_t\) such that
\[ \sum_t \mu_t x_t=V(\mu) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t z_t x_t\ge V(z) \]
for every feasible coalition mass vector \(z\). The solution is the payoff vector \(x\). This keeps the paper’s concern with stable surplus allocations while making the population itself continuous.

But that is a proposed research extension, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. The paper contains no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. Propositions 5 and 6 characterize solution sets; Proposition 10 relates a graph restriction to the core. Those are mathematical characterizations, not algorithmic results. Treating them as computational anchors—or inferring an algorithm from the core inequalities—would overstate what the paper establishes.

So there are no eligible anchors and no honest “one continuous problem per anchor” to provide. The weakness is decisive for this assignment: a plausible continuous-population cooperative game can be formulated, but the paper offers no named computational result whose complexity we could ask whether continuization changes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is also the proponent’s own concession: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 2–10 characterize relationships among the core, Weber, Shapley, and Harsanyi sets, or axiomatize those sets. They establish no algorithmic problem, input representation, or complexity bound. The proposed continuous core is therefore the best candidate, but it is not a continuous version of a computational result in the paper.

The mass formulation also needs substantial new structure. The paper allows an arbitrary worth function \(v:2^N\to\mathbb{R}\). A type-based continuous core instead needs a worth function \(V(z)\) on coalition-mass vectors \(z\), and aggregation by type is valid only when coalition worth depends on type counts. To ask a computational question, one would also have to specify how \(V\) is represented and whether the task is to test feasibility, find a core allocation, or do something else. Those choices define a new computational model; the paper’s characterizations do not settle them. Lifting its axioms to a continuum would remain an axiomatic extension, outside ChoCo’s computational target.

That is a strong reason to reject this paper as an anchored mirror, not proof that no useful continuous cooperative-game project could be devised. Structured high-multiplicity games might support one, but it would be a new research direction inspired by the paper, not a mirror of its results. The universal claim that no scenario could make such a project worthwhile is therefore weaker than the paper-specific negative verdict.
