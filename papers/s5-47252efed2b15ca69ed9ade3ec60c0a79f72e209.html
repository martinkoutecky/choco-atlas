The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, Propositions 2–4, and the corollaries characterize rules by axioms; none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. The deferred-acceptance and greedy procedures are defined, but the paper does not analyze their computational complexity. So there are no anchor-backed continuous questions to list under your criteria.

The closest result is Corollary 1, derived in the paper from Theorem 1. It says that the deferred-acceptance rule based on responsive school choice rules is characterized by non-wastefulness, no justified envy, individual rationality, and strategy-proofness. The underlying finite characterization is credited to Alcalde and Barberà (1994), with the paper deriving this formulation as a corollary. This is a useful foothold, but it is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result.

A plausible follow-up, clearly unanchored, is Type-compressed applicant-proposing DA. An instance has schools \(I\); a finite support \(T\) of student types; rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); a strict ranking over schools and the outside option for each type; a priority order over types at each school; and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_i\). A type includes every preference and priority feature relevant to this matching problem. The task is to output the rational allocation masses \(x_{ti}\) and \(x_{t\varnothing}\) produced by applicant-proposing DA: each type proposes its currently unassigned mass to its next acceptable school, and each school holds up to capacity in priority order, splitting a type’s mass at a cutoff if necessary. A solution is the resulting allocation, with each type’s mass accounted for and each school’s capacity respected.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: large school cohorts with repeated preference lists and priority categories, where the number of students \(N\) is much larger than the number of supported types \(\tau\). Capacities scale with the cohort, so mass means a fraction of students and \(\kappa_i\) is seats per cohort member. Authors working on school assignment would likely recognize it as a continuous-population version of their matching setting. I would expect the problem to be tractable in \(|I|,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), using mass-valued DA. The scope would be the responsive-rule specialization in Corollary 1, not the paper’s Chilean reserve rule or its matroid applications.

The main weakness is that this candidate does not preserve the full result’s incentive claim: an individual of zero mass cannot affect the outcome, so individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous in the continuum. Requiring truthfulness against positive-mass groups would change the question. And since the paper supplies no computational anchor, this is a credible research direction prompted by its model—not a continuous counterpart of a named complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that this paper gives no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 and Corollaries 1–4 characterize matching rules by axioms; Propositions 2–4 characterize choice rules. The paper defines DA and its variants, but proves no complexity bound or algorithmic result about them. So Corollary 1—the proponent’s closest anchor—is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational problem whose continuous complexity remains open.

The strongest version of their follow-up is sensible: model a large school cohort by masses of students who share preferences, priority categories, and relevant traits; scale capacities with the cohort; and let DA split mass at a cutoff. That produces a recognizable continuous-population matching problem. But it does not preserve the result’s central incentive claim. In an atomless population, one student’s report has zero effect, so individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. If types instead include strict priority ranks, as in the paper’s linear priority orders, those ranks distinguish individuals and can eliminate the hoped-for type compression. Using shared priority categories and fractional cutoffs avoids that problem, but replacing individual truthfulness with truthfulness of an entire positive-mass type is a different incentive question. The paper’s characterization relies on individual strategy-proofness, so that change does not mirror Corollary 1.

Computing the mass DA outcome may still be a useful adjacent project. On the proposed finite type support, however, the direct procedure advances each type down its school list and applies each school’s priority cutoff; it needs no apparent continuous-optimization machinery. More importantly, it is not a computational question asserted or characterized by this paper. Harder questions about mass manipulation or robustness would likewise introduce new objectives rather than continuize a result here.

So the negative case is strongest as a scope judgment: this paper offers no named computational anchor for ChoCo. It is weaker as the literal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could be devised—the mass DA formulation is plausible—but that formulation is a new direction, not a continuous mirror of the paper’s results.
