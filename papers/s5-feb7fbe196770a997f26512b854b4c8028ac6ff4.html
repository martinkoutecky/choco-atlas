The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its numbered results characterize cooperative-game values axiomatically; none establishes that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class.

The closest result is Theorem 4, proved here: the PS value is the unique value on public service games satisfying either (i) Efficiency, Independence of activities from obligations, Monotonicity, and Restricted equal treatment of equals, or (ii) Efficiency, Independence of activities from obligations, and Restricted balanced contributions. That is an axiomatic uniqueness theorem, not a computational result. Theorem 1 is the cited Myerson characterization of the Shapley value; Theorems 2 and 3 are cited Young and Pintér characterizations. Propositions 1 and 2 prove uniqueness from axiom sets, and Proposition 3 is cited from Béal et al. on priority values. None supplies a qualifying computational anchor.

The best positive opening is a regulator allocating costs across a large portfolio of local service units. For a postal operator, many routes or service territories might share a small number of complete cost-response types: the type records how a unit’s costs and interactions with other units enter the operator’s cost function. The mass of a type is its share of the portfolio. This is most plausible where the number of units is large and the number \(\tau\) of distinct types is small. It offers a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s allocation setting, though the paper’s own example has only three activities and two obligations.

A possible follow-on problem, not an anchor-respecting mirror of a named computational result, is to compute a continuous PS allocation. An instance consists of activity and obligation types with rational masses \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), summing to one, and a coordinatewise-monotone common-cost function \(V(x,y)\), where \(V(0,0)=0\). The input represents \(V\) by an explicit rational polyhedral partition with an affine cost formula on each cell. Here \(x\) and \(y\) are the masses included from each type. The task is to return the per-unit allocation for each type:
\[ p_i=\int_0^1 \partial_i V(t\alpha,0)\,dt,\qquad q_j=\int_0^1 \partial_j V(\alpha,t\beta)\,dt. \]
The corresponding total allocation to a type is its mass times its per-unit allocation. These formulas extend the paper’s split between the Shapley allocation on the activity game and the Shapley allocation on the incremental obligation game. With the stated explicit input, I would expect this version to be tractable: the integrals reduce to finitely many rational segments along two paths through the partition. Further questions would include finite-to-continuum convergence rates, uniqueness under continuum versions of the paper’s axioms, and complexity under more succinct representations of \(V\). That tractability comes from the chosen representation, not from a result in this paper.

The soft spot is whether the authors would regard a portfolio of many interchangeable local units as their problem. Their model treats activities and obligations as players in a coalition game, while the illustration treats each activity and obligation as an atomic category. A continuous cost-allocation extension may be a worthwhile new project, but this paper provides no named computational hardness result whose dissolution in the continuum could be tested.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to mirror. Theorem 4 characterizes the PS value by axioms; Propositions 1 and 2 establish uniqueness, and the other numbered results are axiomatic or imported from prior work. None states a complexity bound or gives an algorithm for a computational problem. The proponent is right that Theorem 4 is the closest result, and right that it is not a computational anchor. On the programme’s stated criterion, no anchor survives.

The proposed portfolio of local service units is the strongest way to build a follow-on. If units have complete cost-response types and the operator’s cost is given by a function \(V(x,y)\) of type masses, the suggested path integrals are a coherent continuous allocation. But that changes what the paper’s players are: here they are operator-level activities and legal obligations, with coalition costs defined for counterfactual combinations of those choices. Recasting them as many route-level or service-level units creates a new cost-allocation model. It may be worth studying, but it does not continuize a computational question posed or answered in this paper.

The proposed computation also depends on how the new cost function is represented. With an explicit polyhedral partition, the path integrals can be computed by traversing the segments through its cells; with a succinct representation, the complexity becomes a question about that representation. The paper supplies neither computational problem. Its contribution is the axiomatic choice of an allocation rule given coalition costs.

So the negative case is strong for “no continuous mirror of this paper” and weaker for the broader claim that no adjacent project could be worthwhile. A continuous cost-allocation study could make sense; the material here does not give the programme a computational result to continuize or a complexity question to inherit.
