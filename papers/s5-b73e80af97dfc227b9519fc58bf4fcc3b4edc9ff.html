The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify an eligible anchor. The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Its closest result is the unnumbered procedure in Section 3: an exact envy-free chore division for finitely many players. Section 4 says it terminates in finitely many steps, but the number of steps is not bounded, even for fixed \(n\). That is an algorithmic claim, but it is not a named result of the kind your anchor rule requires.

The tempting mirror would take types to be additive chore-disutility measures, \(\mu_t\) to be each type’s population share, and the decision to be a chore bundle for each type, subject to envy-freeness. A large workforce with repeated disutility profiles could supply a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But I can’t make that into a convincing mirror of this paper’s result: if chores scale per capita, giving every type the same bundle makes envy-freeness trivial; with a fixed finite cake and a nonatomic population, individual allocations risk becoming degenerate. The paper’s construction instead works with finitely many players and a shared divisible cake. Divisibility is continuity of the outcome, not of the population.

So the honest positive case stops here: the paper suggests a possible question about chore division across a population of valuation types, but it supplies no named anchor for that question, and its exact finite-player procedure does not establish that the continuous-population version would be one the authors recognize as their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no eligible anchors to defeat. The paper gives an exact procedure for finitely many named players, but states no numbered result about computational complexity. It even says the procedure takes finitely many steps while leaving open whether the number can be bounded, for fixed \(n\). The proponent’s three proposed choices therefore do not become mirrors of named results in the paper.

The strongest version of their possible mirror would use finitely many chore-valuation types, population masses, and per-capita chore supplies, then ask how to compute an envy-free allocation across types. That can be a sensible high-multiplicity problem. But it changes the object: the paper’s procedure assigns distinct pieces to individual players and maintains pairwise envy and “irrevocable advantage” relations between them. A type-level allocation instead needs a new representation of per-capita bundles and weighted resource constraints, plus an input model for the valuation measures. The paper provides no computational claim that this new model would continue or refine.

I would not claim that no useful continuous chore-division question can be formulated; the high-multiplicity setting is plausible. The negative case is narrower: this paper offers no named computational result to anchor such a mirror, and making the proposed mirror well-defined requires supplying substantial modeling and computational structure of its own.
