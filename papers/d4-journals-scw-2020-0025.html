The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), a committee \(W\subseteq C\) of size \(k\), and the positive-mass approval types \(\mathcal A\) with rational masses \(\mu_A\) summing to \(1\), decide whether for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and every \(S\subseteq\mathcal A\), \(\sum_{A\in S}\mu_A\ge \ell/k\) and \(\left|\bigcap_{A\in S} A\right|\ge\ell\) imply \(\left|W\cap\bigcup_{A\in S}A\right|\ge\ell\).

The model it lives in

A population distributed over approval-set types, represented by the \(\tau\) positive-mass sets \(A\subseteq C\) and their binary rational masses \(\mu_A\). The computational task is to decide whether a proposed committee satisfies continuous PJR; a violation is specified by \(\ell\) and a set of types \(S\).

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover dichotomous generalized-PSC/PJR verification and EAR committee computation. They leave the paper's other proportional-representation and monotonicity claims over strict and weak orders without a stated continuous mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the parameterized complexity of continuous PJR verification in support size \(\tau\), beyond the direct \(2^\tau\) enumeration, and which restrictions on approval types yield sharper algorithms?
  • Does type-aggregated EAR have a polynomial exact bit-complexity bound in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length for rational masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s dichotomous case. Imagine a large union, professional association, or municipal election for a \(k\)-member board, where many people submit the same approval ballot. A type is an approval set \(A\subseteq C\); its mass \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of the electorate with that ballot. There may be many more voters than positive-mass types, and the input is the \(\tau\) supported approval sets and their binary rational masses. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: the ballot, not a person’s name, completely describes how the paper’s rule treats them.

My lead anchor is Proposition 13, proved in the paper by using its equivalence between generalized Hare-PSC and PJR under dichotomous preferences, together with coNP-completeness of PJR testing cited from earlier work. Its full claim is: *under dichotomous preferences, testing whether a committee satisfies generalized PSC is coNP-complete.* The paper proves the relevant equivalence; the underlying PJR hardness is cited from Aziz and Huang (2016) and Aziz et al. (2018). [The paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1708.07580)

The continuous question is Continuous PJR Verification. An instance is \(C\), a committee \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|=k\), and a rational distribution \((\mu_A)_{A\in\mathcal A}\) over the \(\tau\) supported approval types. Ask whether \(W\) satisfies this condition: for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) and every set \(S\subseteq\mathcal A\), if
\[ \sum_{A\in S}\mu_A\geq \frac{\ell}{k} \quad\text{and}\quad \left|\bigcap_{A\in S}A\right|\geq\ell, \]
then
\[ \left|W\cap\bigcup_{A\in S}A\right|\geq\ell. \]
Here a coalition can consist of any chosen mass from the types in \(S\). The test only needs to consider which types have positive mass in that coalition: their common approvals and union of approvals determine the condition, and their available mass determines whether they can meet the quota.

I expect this problem to be coNP-complete, with hardness transferring. A violated condition has a short certificate: \(\ell\) and a set \(S\) of types. For hardness, normalize any discrete PJR instance with \(n_A\) voters of ballot \(A\) to \(\mu_A=n_A/n\). The mass threshold \(\ell/k\) then matches the discrete quota \(\ell n/k\), so the PJR test is preserved. Replicating each voter arbitrarily many times leaves the distribution unchanged, while making each type occur many times in the electorate. The hardness does require \(\tau\) to grow across the hard family; it does not establish hardness for fixed \(\tau\). Since there are only \(2^\tau\) type sets to check, enumerating them also gives an immediate FPT algorithm parameterized by \(\tau\).

A second, tractable mirror comes from Proposition 6, proved in the paper: *EAR runs in polynomial time \(O((n+m)^2)\).* Its continuous version is Rational-Type EAR Computation. Given a distribution over \(\tau\) weak-order types, a committee size \(k\), and a quota fraction \(\rho\in(1/(k+1),1/k]\), return the \(k\)-candidate committee produced by EAR. Initialize each type’s remaining weight at its mass. At approval depth \(j\), a candidate’s support is the total remaining mass of types that approve it at that depth. When EAR selects a candidate with support \(T\), multiply the remaining mass of each supporting type by \((T-\rho)/T\); advance \(j\) when no candidate meets the quota. Use the paper’s rank-maximal priority order, computed from type masses, with a fixed tie-break. This is the same rule with voter counts and quota divided by population size, so the authors should recognize it as their population-level EAR. I expect it to be tractable in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length, though a prover should establish polynomial growth of the exact rational weights; Proposition 6’s stated running time does not itself settle that bit-complexity issue.

The mirror’s scope is narrow: it covers dichotomous axiom verification and EAR committee computation. It keeps the committee discrete and continuizes the electorate, with the quota expressed per capita. It does not mirror the paper’s other axiomatic results, such as its monotonicity claims.

My weakest point is that the PJR test is nearly scale-invariant: normalizing a discrete profile gives the same coalition test, so the continuous version may add little algorithmically beyond a support-based formulation. That does not make it a collision—the paper’s model is a finite voter profile, whereas this question is explicitly posed over population masses—but it makes the result a strong Class B mirror, not evidence that continuity removes the hardness. The further questions are whether PJR verification admits sharper algorithms under restrictions on \(\tau\) or the approval types, and whether type-aggregated EAR has a polynomial exact bit-complexity bound while retaining its proportionality guarantee.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that both mirrors may amount to routine high-multiplicity reformulations rather than opening a new line of continuous optimization. The paper already aggregates voter support by preference and, for EAR, updates weights fractionally. A continuous analysis may therefore expose little new structure. That is a reason to question the programme’s priority, though it does not show that either question is ill-posed.

The PJR-testing anchor is the harder one to dismiss. For approval types with masses \(\mu_A\), a coalition can be represented by the types from which it takes positive mass. Its quota feasibility is determined by their total mass, and its common approvals and represented winners by their intersection and union. Clearing denominators turns any rational-mass instance into a finite profile with the same test. That makes the proposed continuous test a direct high-multiplicity mirror of a named coNP-completeness result. Its strongest limitation is that this hardness does not settle the low-\(\tau\) regime: enumerating type subsets gives an immediate \(2^\tau\) algorithm. But the programme explicitly treats hardness boundaries and parameterized algorithms as worthwhile results. Calling the mirror a restatement would not defeat it under the stated standard.

The EAR anchor also has a natural population-level formulation. Divide the quota by population size, initialize type weights at their masses, and apply the same proportional reweighting. The rank priority order is preserved when voter counts are normalized, and authors already allow a range of quotas. The main open issue is exact bit complexity: the paper’s \(O((n+m)^2)\) bound does not establish a bound polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) for rational type masses, since repeated rational updates may enlarge representations. That gap could make the mirror less straightforward, yet it also supplies a concrete computational question the discrete running-time claim leaves unanswered.

So the honest negative case is weak. The best skepticism is that these questions may yield little beyond support compression for PJR and aggregate simulation for EAR. The paper nevertheless has a computational testing result and a polynomial rule-computation result, and approval elections with many voters sharing ballots are a credible high-multiplicity regime. I cannot make a sound case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; in particular, the PJR-testing mirror survives the objections available here.
