The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational-complexity anchor in this paper: none of its numbered results classifies a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its results characterize optimal mechanisms and incentive compatibility. The closest positive anchor is Theorem 1, proved here: in the paper’s binary-policy model, with independent private types, costly verification, no transfers, and the stated alignment of preferences, a voting-with-evidence mechanism maximizes the principal’s expected payoff. In that mechanism, favorable reports below a cutoff and unfavorable reports above a cutoff are bunched at baseline weights; reports outside those regions contribute net types \(t_i-c_i\) or \(t_i+c_i\). The policy is adopted when the weights sum to a positive amount, and a report is verified exactly when its agent is decisive. That is a substantial optimization result, but it is not a complexity result.

The strongest positive mirror I can make is a Continuous Constituency Voting-with-Evidence problem. Consider a policy decision affecting many people organized into \(\tau\) constituencies. A constituency type \(g\) fully specifies its per-capita policy value, the representative’s preferences, the distribution \(F_g\) of the representative’s private claim, and the cost \(c_g\) of checking that constituency’s documentation. Constituency \(g\) has rational population mass \(\mu_g\), with \(\sum_g\mu_g=1\); typically there are many affected people but relatively few distinct constituency types. This is plausible in settings where a firm division, industry association, or other organized group submits evidence on behalf of a large, relatively homogeneous constituency, and checking the group’s shared documentation has a cost that does not grow person by person.

Here is the candidate problem precisely. The input gives the finite type support \(G\), rational masses \(\mu_g\), finite rational claim supports \(Q_g\) and priors \(F_g\), utility signs, audit costs \(c_g\), and a rational target \(K\). Independently draw each representative’s private state \(q_g\sim F_g\). Choose a direct mechanism: for each report profile \(r\in\prod_g Q_g\), a policy decision \(d(r)\in\{0,1\}\), verification probabilities \(a_g(r)\in[0,1]\), and penalties after a lie is detected. The mechanism must make truthful reporting a Bayesian Nash equilibrium for each representative. Its objective is to maximize

\[ \mathbb E\!\left[d(r)\sum_g\mu_g q_g-\sum_g a_g(r)c_g\right]. \]

The solution is an optimal truthful mechanism and its expected value—or, in the decision version, an answer to whether that value is at least \(K\). This mirrors Theorem 1’s underlying mechanism-design problem, rather than treating the mechanism’s description as the problem. It covers the paper’s perfect-verification benchmark only; it does not claim to mirror the imperfect-verification extension or the BIC–EPIC equivalence result.

For this constituency regime, I would tentatively expect the tractable side of the trichotomy: Theorem 1 suggests that the unrestricted mechanism problem may reduce to a small set of type-specific cutoffs and weights. But that is a prediction, not a result of the paper. The paper neither proves an algorithm for finding those parameters with a stated running time nor establishes that this weighted extension has the same structure. The immediate follow-up questions are whether the cutoffs can be optimized in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the total claim-support size, and the input bit length; whether there is a value-preserving dictionary to discrete populations with integer constituency sizes; and which assumptions on representatives and shared evidence are needed for the theorem to survive.

The weak point is fundamental. In this proposed mirror, the strategic agents are constituency representatives, while the continuous mass counts the people they represent. That can be a sensible application, but it is not the same as making the paper’s individual strategic agents nonatomic. If every person reports independently in an atomless population, one person’s report has zero effect on the policy decision. Decisive reports and the verification incentives that sustain Theorem 1 can then disappear. So the positive case depends on a real institutional feature—a common, consequential representative or reporting channel—not merely on there being many similar people. And even if that scenario passes the plausibility test, the paper still supplies no computational-complexity anchor for ChoCo to classify.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only anchor, and it exposes the main difficulty: it characterizes an optimal mechanism, but the paper never poses a computational problem or gives a running-time result. Turning its optimization into “compute the best truthful mechanism” is a possible follow-up, but that question comes from the analyst’s chosen encoding. It is not a computational result of the paper whose population axis can simply be continuized.

The constituency proposal makes the choice concrete, but its strategic game still has one report per representative. Increasing a constituency’s mass changes the coefficient on its value in the principal’s objective; it does not add agents or reports. The resulting task is a weighted finite-agent mechanism-design problem, with complexity governed by the representatives and their claim supports. A firm or industry that submits shared evidence could be a credible setting for that problem. It does not, by itself, make the residents a high-multiplicity population in the mechanism studied by the paper.

A stronger attempt would give every resident an individual report and place the residents in a continuum. Then an individual has measure zero and cannot change an aggregate policy decision. The decisive reports that make verification useful in Theorem 1 disappear, and truth-telling no longer needs to be enforced through individual audits. If a whole positive-mass cohort can report together, that restores influence by making the cohort a strategic bloc—effectively the representative model again. The paper’s existing continuum of possible private values, through each named agent’s distribution \(F_i\), does not resolve this: its number of strategic agents remains finite.

So the negative case is strongest against a *ChoCo population mirror*: preserving the paper’s strategic incentives seems to require finite representatives or positive-mass blocs, while making the individuals themselves nonatomic removes the mechanism’s pivotality. The case is not airtight. A shared-evidence constituency model may be worthwhile in its own right, and the paper’s optimal-mechanism theorem gives it a natural starting point. Whether that counts as a continuous mirror depends on treating blocs as the population’s agents; it does not establish a computational question about a continuous population of the paper’s individual agents.
