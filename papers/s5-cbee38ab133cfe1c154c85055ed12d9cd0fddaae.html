The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) chores of supply one and a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\lambda_t>0\), \(\sum_t\lambda_t=1\), disutilities \(d_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^m\), and budgets \(b_t>0\), can an SGR-type algorithm return prices \(p_j>0\) and per-capita allocations \(x_{tj}\ge0\) in ~\(O(m^3(τ+m)/ε^3)\) arithmetic operations so that \((1-ε)b_t\le p^\top x_t\le b_t/(1-ε)\), \((1-ε)d_t^\top x_t\le d_t^\top y\) for every \(y\ge0\) with \(p^\top y\ge p^\top x_t\), and \(1-ε\le\sum_t\lambda_t x_{tj}\le1/(1-ε)\) for each chore \(j\)?

The model it lives in

A chores market with a continuum of workers represented by finitely many types: type \(t\) has mass \(\lambda_t\), disutility vector \(d_t\), and budget \(b_t\); the decision variables are prices \(p\) and per-capita allocations \(x_t\). The DC and SGR sums weight each type by \(\lambda_t b_t\), while supply clearance weights allocations by \(\lambda_t\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the CE formulation, DCA convergence, and SGR approximate-CE guarantee over finite-support worker types. It does not separately recast the error-bound and KŁ results or the numerical experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove the support-sensitive SGR arithmetic bound for rational type data, including its dependence on disutility and budget ranges.
  • Determine the bit complexity of producing an ε-CE from the finite-support input.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a competitive equilibrium for a mass of chore workers, where the population is continuous but the chore market remains the one in the paper. The paper’s numbered results are algorithmic and equilibrium-characterization results; it contains no new named hardness result to anchor a continuum-hardness claim. The best anchor is therefore its approximation theorem. [Chen, Jiang, and So, *Computing Competitive Equilibrium for Chores*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04036)

Take \(m\) divisible chores, each with supply one, and a finite set \(T\) of worker types. Type \(t\) has mass \(\lambda_t\), with \(\sum_t\lambda_t=1\), a disutility vector \(d_t\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^m\), and a budget \(b_t>0\). A type is the complete market-relevant description \((d_t,b_t)\); agents of that type are interchangeable. The input lists only the \(\tau\) positive-mass types, with rational masses and parameters. The regime could be a large staffing system with standardized job classes: thousands or millions of workers, but only dozens or hundreds of distinct disutility-and-budget types.

Let \(x_{tj}\) be the type’s allocation per unit of population mass. A mass-market equilibrium asks for prices \(p_j>0\) and allocations \(x_{tj}\ge0\) such that each type earns its budget, \(p^\top x_t=b_t\); chooses a minimum-disutility bundle at that income, so \(d_t^\top x_t\le d_t^\top y\) whenever \(y\ge0\) and \(p^\top y\ge p^\top x_t\); and clears supply, \(\sum_t\lambda_t x_{tj}=1\). This is a population mirror, not a claim that divisible chores are novel. For a finite market with \(n_t=n\lambda_t\) agents of type \(t\), the continuum variables use the corresponding population-normalized allocations; prices and allocations can be rescaled so the market-clearing and budget equations agree.

Theorem 3.1, proved in the paper, is the bridge to this model. In the paper’s finite-agent formulation, a stationary point of (DC), together with subgradients \(v_i\in\partial h_i\) satisfying \(\sum_j e^{z_j}=\sum_i B_i\) and \(\sum_i v_{ij}=e^{z_j}\) for every chore \(j\), gives a CE with \(p_j=e^{z_j}\) and \(x_{ij}=v_{ij}/p_j\). The proof also establishes the reverse correspondence. In the mass version, aggregate identical agents: set \(h_t(z)=\lambda_t b_t\max_j\{z_j-\log d_{tj}\}\), choose \(v_t\in\partial h_t(z)\), and use \(W=\sum_t\lambda_t b_t\). The corresponding conditions are \(\sum_j e^{z_j}=W\) and \(\sum_t v_{tj}=e^{z_j}\); they recover \(p_j=e^{z_j}\) and \(x_{tj}=v_{tj}/(\lambda_t p_j)\).

The associated problem, Exact Mass Chore CE, is: given those finite-support rational type data, return prices and type allocations satisfying the three equilibrium conditions. Theorem 3.1 validates the continuous formulation; it does not itself give a complexity bound. I expect this problem to be tractable by continuous optimization, but its strongest algorithmic support comes from the next two results.

Theorem 4.1, proved in the paper, says that the DCA iterates converge \(R\)-linearly to a stationary point of (DC), and that the normalized residuals \(\lvert u_j^k/q_j(z^k)\rvert\) also converge \(R\)-linearly to zero, where \(u^k=\nabla g(z^{k-1})-\nabla g(z^k)\) and \(q_j(z)=\bigl(\sum_iB_i\bigr)e^{z_j}/\sum_\ell e^{z_\ell}\). By Theorem 3.1 the limit is an exact CE. Its mirror, Linearly Convergent Mass-CE Computation, asks for a DCA sequence for the finite-support mass market whose limit is an exact equilibrium and whose normalized market residuals decay \(R\)-linearly. Duplicate types can be aggregated in the same sums, replacing individual-agent data by type-weighted data. I expect this to be tractable as an iterative computation; the theorem gives convergence to an exact limit, not a finite-iteration exact answer or a bit-complexity bound.

The lead is Theorem 5.1, proved in the paper. It states that for \(0<\epsilon<1/2\), with \(\delta=\epsilon/(1.3+\log(m-1))\) and \(\eta=\frac{\gamma}{\sum_iB_i}\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}\) for \(0<\gamma<1\), SGR finds an \(\epsilon\)-CE in at most \(\tilde{\mathcal O}(m^2/\epsilon^3)\) iterations and total complexity at most \(\tilde{\mathcal O}(m^3(n+m)/\epsilon^3)\). Its mass mirror, Approximate Mass Chore CE, has the same input and asks for prices and allocations satisfying the paper’s multiplicative \(\epsilon\)-CE conditions: each type’s payment is between \((1-\epsilon)b_t\) and \(b_t/(1-\epsilon)\); its disutility is at most \(1/(1-\epsilon)\) times the minimum disutility among bundles earning at least its payment; and each chore’s aggregate allocation is between \(1-\epsilon\) and \(1/(1-\epsilon)\). The direct type-aggregated version weights each type by \(\lambda_t b_t\), so gradient evaluation scales with \(m\tau\) rather than \(mn\); the stated total bound correspondingly becomes \(\tilde{\mathcal O}(m^3(\tau+m)/\epsilon^3)\) in the same arithmetic-operation accounting. This is the strongest mirror because it is a finite approximation guarantee whose dependence on population support has a clear interpretation. I expect Class A.

The scope is the paper’s chore-equilibrium computation: exact CE characterization, linearly convergent computation, and approximate CE. The main follow-up questions are whether the support-size bounds persist with full bit complexity for rational inputs, how the guarantees depend on the disutility and budget ranges, and whether type aggregation supports faster algorithms.

The weak point is that the paper’s stated instances have named agents and potentially distinct disutilities and budgets; it does not analyze \(\tau\) or prove the mass-market bounds. Aggregation is an exact and plausible extension when types really do share those parameters, but the support-size runtime is not a theorem of this paper. Also, Theorem 4.1’s asymptotic linear convergence is weaker as a finite computational guarantee than Theorem 5.1. That leaves a solid positive case for a continuous-population mirror, led by approximate equilibrium computation, but not a claim that the paper has already proved its high-multiplicity complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the three anchors are mostly one result repeated across three formulations. The DC objective depends on agents through weighted sums. Group identical agents, and its terms become \(\sum_t w_t\max_j\{z_j-\log d_{tj}\}\) and \(-(\sum_t w_t)\log\sum_j e^{z_j}\), where \(w_t\) is the type’s total budget weight. In the mass model, \(w_t=\lambda_t b_t\). The stationary conditions, DCA updates, and SGR gradients therefore carry over by replacing individual-agent sums with type-weighted sums. Rational masses can also be represented by finite replications after scaling. This makes the mirror look like a direct aggregation corollary, rather than a new algorithmic framework. ([Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04036))

That is a real limit on the novelty claim, but the programme explicitly treats high multiplicity as support for a mirror, not a collision. The exact CE characterization in Theorem 3.1 is not itself a complexity bound, and its mass version follows by weighting the same conditions. Still, the mass-market equilibrium is a coherent question: clearing supply integrates each type’s per-capita allocation against its mass. Nothing in the paper suggests the equilibrium disappears or becomes meaningless in that formulation.

Theorem 4.1 gives a weaker computational anchor than its headline might suggest. Linear convergence is asymptotic, and the paper gives no uniform rate in the input bit length or type support; each DCA step also requires solving a subproblem. So it does not establish a finite, bit-complexity guarantee for exact mass-market CE. But asking whether that convergence guarantee is uniform over finite-support populations remains well-posed.

The hardest anchor to defeat is Theorem 5.1. Its arithmetic cost can be aggregated over \(\tau\) types, giving the proposed support-sensitive bound in place of \(n\). The theorem does not supply a bit-complexity analysis for rational input, and the reported experiments use data that need not contain repeated types. Those points narrow its demonstrated reach. They do not rule out a plausible regime with many workers in a modest number of standardized classes; the type can include both disutility and budget, so mass preserves everything the model uses.

So the honest negative case is about likely incremental novelty: the mass algorithms appear to follow directly from the paper’s weighted-sum structure, and the strongest stated bound uses arithmetic operations rather than a full bit model. That does not defeat the mirror under the programme’s standard. The paper has computational results, the type-level model is meaningful, and approximate mass-market computation has a natural support-size question. I cannot make a defensible case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
