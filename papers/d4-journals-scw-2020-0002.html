What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered; Theorem 3.7 and Corollary 3.8 provide axiomatic characterizations, while the other results introduce or analyze axioms and prove those characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest positive case that meets ChoCo’s requirements: this paper contains no named computational result—no theorem that a problem is hard, tractable, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity.

The closest anchors are axiomatic. Theorem 3.7 characterizes classes of TU-values: with efficiency, multiweighted Shapley values are characterized by null-game, coalitional differential dependence, and marginality; the Harsanyi set by null-game, coalitional differential dependence, and weakly strong monotonicity; random order values by null-game, coalitional differential dependence, and strong monotonicity; weighted Shapley values by mutual dependence and strong monotonicity; and positively weighted Shapley values by mutual dependence and strictly strong monotonicity. Corollary 3.8 gives related characterizations. Neither result asks a computational question.

One could imagine a continuum of participants in a cooperative setting, with types grouping agents who have identical roles in the game and mass recording each type’s population share. But asking which value operators satisfy continuum versions of these axioms would be a new axiomatic question. The paper fixes a finite player set, and its results classify value operators; they do not analyze a continuous population or computation. Random-order probabilities and fractional sharing weights do not make the society continuous.

So there is no valid anchor here, and I cannot state a continuous computational problem per anchor without inventing one that the authors’ results do not support.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a mirror is strongest at the level of the results themselves. Theorem 3.7 and Corollary 3.8 characterize classes of TU-values by axioms; neither states a computational problem or a complexity result. They give no instance encoding, input or output, or algorithm to continuize. The paper therefore offers no computational result to anchor a mirror.

The most plausible repair is to study high-multiplicity cooperative games and ask for each type’s payoff under one of these values. But that adds the computational problem. The paper classifies value operators across all games; it does not study evaluating them on a game, recognizing a value operator, or optimizing an allocation. The complexity of any such task would depend on choices the paper never makes, especially how games are represented. With an explicit table of coalition worths, evaluation may be routine; with a succinct representation, its difficulty depends on that representation. Neither question is a continuous version of Theorem 3.7 or Corollary 3.8.

The population model is also a poor fit to the paper’s domain. A TU-game assigns worths to every coalition, and the paper allows those worths to depend on player identities in arbitrary ways. Players can share a complete type only when their roles are indistinguishable across all those coalition contexts. Restricting worths to depend only on counts of each type could make a high-multiplicity setting sensible, but it is a new, narrower class of games—not the domain on which these theorems characterize values. If types instead encode all the ways players differ in coalition interactions, their number can grow with the population, defeating the proposed compression.

A continuous limit would also need substantive new choices. Marginality and the monotonicity axioms compare a player’s contributions across finite coalitions; coalitional differential dependence perturbs a specific coalition using a unanimity game. In an atomless population, one individual has zero mass, while a specific finite coalition has no direct mass-space counterpart. Derivative-based versions or mass-threshold games might be reasonable models, but they would supply a new axiomatic framework rather than translate the paper’s results. Corollary 3.8 adds no computational anchor: it gives another parallel axiomatic characterization.

There is a limit to this negative case: one could study algorithms for symmetric, high-multiplicity cooperative games, and that could be worthwhile work in its own right. But it would introduce the game representation, computational task, and continuous axioms that this paper does not provide. On the evidence here, that is an adjacent research programme, not a continuous computational mirror of this paper.
