The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many single types \(s\) with masses \(a_s\), couple types \(c\) with masses \(b_c\), hospitals with rational per-capita capacities \(q_h\), strict hospital priorities over acceptable doctor-role types, and complete responsive couple rankings \(P_c\), decide whether a stable mass allocation exists and compute one if it does. Require \(\sum_s a_s+2\sum_c b_c=1\); let \(y_{s,h}\) be single mass assigned to \(h\), and \(x_{c,h,h'}\) couple mass assigned with its two members at \(h,h'\), where \(h,h'\in H\cup\{\lambda\}\). Enforce type-mass conservation and hospital capacities. Require the mass-scaled extreme-altruism condition at every hospital with \(q_h<1\): if \(h'P_fh\), \(h'R_fh''\), and \(h''R_m\lambda\), then \((h',h'')P_c(h,h)\), with the symmetric condition for the other member. Stability means no positive mass of a single or couple type prefers a feasible alternative hospital or hospital pair that the relevant hospital or hospitals can accommodate by replacing lower-priority assigned mass and strictly improve their priority-based choice.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity placement market with finitely many complete single and couple types carrying rational mass, per-capita hospital capacities, joint couple assignments \(x_{c,h,h'}\), single assignments \(y_{s,h}\), and responsive hospital choices induced by priorities over doctor-role types.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s constructive sufficient direction under type-priority hospital choices. It leaves the continuous necessity direction open, since the paper’s finite nonexistence construction may not extend to divisible mass, and does not cover Theorem 2’s hospital-side characterization.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a deferred acceptance procedure over type masses always compute a stable allocation in polynomial time in the number of types, hospitals, and input bit length?
  • Is extreme altruism necessary for a universal stability guarantee in this divisible-mass model, or does the paper’s finite nonexistence construction fail when mass can be split?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s couple-side existence result. My anchor would be Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that a fixed responsive couple-preference profile satisfying extreme altruism has a stable matching under every responsive extension of the hospitals’ preferences; if extreme altruism fails, there is an extension with no stable matching. The proof uses the doctor-proposing deferred acceptance algorithm to construct a stable matching in the sufficient case. The theorem itself is an existence characterization, not a result stating that a problem is in P or is hard, and the paper gives no named complexity classification. The algorithm makes it an algorithmic lead, but that distinction matters.

A credible high-multiplicity setting is a large centralized placement market for doctors or trainees, with many applicants sharing a small number of complete preference types. Some applicants are couples seeking two positions jointly; others apply as singles. An exact type must include everything the matching problem uses: a single’s hospital ranking, or both members’ rankings and the couple’s complete ranking over hospital pairs. The regime is plausible for large cohorts with standardized specialties and locations, provided the number \(\tau\) of distinct complete types stays much smaller than the number of applicants.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover: Extreme-Altruism Stable Mass Matching. Normalize total doctor mass to \(1\). Let single type \(s\) have mass \(a_s\), and couple type \(c\) have mass \(b_c\), with
\[ \sum_s a_s+2\sum_c b_c=1. \]
Hospitals have rational per-capita capacities \(q_h\). For each couple type, let \(x_{c,h,h'}\) be the mass assigned with its first member at \(h\) and second at \(h'\); for each single type, let \(y_{s,h}\) be its mass assigned to \(h\). The dummy hospital \(\lambda\) represents unemployment. Require \(\sum_h y_{s,h}=a_s\), \(\sum_{h,h'}x_{c,h,h'}=b_c\), and, for each real hospital \(h\),
\[ \sum_s y_{s,h}+ \sum_c\sum_{h'}\bigl(x_{c,h,h'}+x_{c,h',h}\bigr)\le q_h. \]
A solution is such an allocation with no positive mass of a single or couple type able to move to a preferred hospital or hospital pair while the relevant hospital or hospitals can accept them by replacing assigned mass and strictly improve. If both members move to the same hospital, it must be able to accept both.

The continuous analogue of extreme altruism imposes the paper’s couple-ranking condition on each couple type at every hospital with \(q_h<1\). In particular, if \(h'\) is better than \(h\) for the first member, \(h'\) is at least as good as \(h''\) for that member, and \(h''\) is at least as good as unemployment for the second, the couple must prefer \((h',h'')\) to \((h,h)\); impose the symmetric condition for the other member. The threshold reflects the paper’s \(\kappa_h\le |D|-2\): in a large society, a hospital with capacity strictly below the whole population is the nondegenerate case.

For a finite computational input, I would initially give each hospital a strict priority over the finite doctor-role types, so it chooses its highest-priority acceptable mass up to capacity. The question is: given rational type masses, capacities, rankings, and couple preferences satisfying this condition, compute a stable mass allocation or report that none exists. This covers Theorem 1’s constructive side. I would expect a Class A result for this version: a deferred-acceptance process that moves masses by type rather than enumerating applicants could plausibly run in time polynomial in the number of hospitals, \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). That would matter for high-multiplicity inputs, whose population counts are written in binary. The paper’s Theorem 1 also motivates the further question of whether violating the condition can still be characterized as failure of a universal stability guarantee in the continuum.

The weak point is that this mirror does not preserve every hospital preference the paper allows. Its responsive preferences compare arbitrary sets of doctors, including comparisons that a simple priority over types cannot express. Also, a fractional population admits arbitrarily small blocking masses, and the paper’s nonexistence construction uses finite counts; its converse may not survive divisibility. So the most defensible positive case is for a compressed, type-based algorithmic version of Theorem 1’s sufficient side. The source paper does not establish a continuous complexity result, and I would not present this as one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 1 characterizes when stable matching is guaranteed, rather than classifying the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s algorithmic content is the doctor-proposing deferred acceptance procedure. A continuous version that computes a stable allocation would therefore add a high-multiplicity algorithmic question; it would not continuize a complexity result the paper establishes.

The proposed mirror also narrows the theorem. Theorem 1 ranges over responsive hospital preferences, while the proposal gives hospitals strict priorities over types. It keeps the sufficient extreme-altruism condition but does not mirror the theorem’s full characterization. And the paper ranks finite sets of named doctors; those rankings do not by themselves specify how a hospital compares fractional bundles of doctor mass. Type priorities make a workable mass model, but they make it a new model choice. In that restricted model, the natural computational task is to aggregate deferred acceptance by type.

That is a limited case against the mirror’s payoff, not a decisive objection. A better version could define a mass-based stability notion explicitly, retain couple types as joint types with their complete pair preferences, and ask whether the existence characterization survives for responsive mass-choice rules. The example constructions might also scale to positive masses. A large placement cohort with repeated applicant and couple types is plausible, and identity need not matter once the complete type records the preferences relevant to matching.

So I would argue that this is a lower-priority mirror for a programme focused on complexity landscapes: its anchor is an existence characterization, and the clean computational version may amount to aggregating a familiar algorithm. But I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The stronger, better-specified mass-matching question survives; my negative case is weak beyond questioning its priority.
