Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 4.13 — survived: The \(K_{m,m}\) instance has two exchangeable populations and yields a concrete free-energy barrier problem over their opinion masses; the missing link to the mixing exponent limits the theorem-level transfer but does not erase the mirror.

The continuous mirror question

Given two types of equal mass, each with belief \(1/2\), and symmetric cross-type interaction \(J_{AB}=J_{BA}=2\), let \(F_\beta\) be the stated mean-field free energy. Given rational \(\beta\), rational boxes isolating two distinct local minima \(q^-,q^+\in[0,1]^2\), and additive error \(\epsilon>0\), compute an additive-\(\epsilon\) approximation to the minimum, over continuous paths joining those minima, of the maximum \(F_\beta\) along the path minus the lower endpoint value.

The model it lives in

A two-type population with masses \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\), binary individual opinions, and normalized cross-type interactions; the decision variables are the fractions \(q_A,q_B\) choosing opinion \(1\), and the objective is the minimax path height of the entropy-corrected potential \(F_\beta\).

The objection that survived

The paper proves a finite-chain mixing lower bound, but does not establish that the proposed free-energy barrier determines the chain's asymptotic mixing exponent.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the two-cohort complete-bipartite slow-mixing regime of Theorem 4.13. It leaves the paper's best-response results, general-topology bounds, and other mixing-time results alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • For the scaled finite \(K_{m,m}\) logit chain, does the normalized logarithm of its mixing time converge to the stated barrier between the corresponding free-energy minima?
  • How does the barrier-approximation complexity change when the number of types \(\tau\) is part of the input?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the slow-mixing result for the complete bipartite graph. Its appeal is that the population really does have two repeated roles, so the continuous model can preserve the paper’s opinion game and its network interactions.

Anchor: Theorem 4.13, proved in this paper. It states that on \(K_{m,m}\), there are choices of internal beliefs and edge weights for which, for every \(\beta=\Omega(1/m)\), the logit dynamics has mixing time at least \(e^{\beta\Theta(\mathrm{CW})}/n\), where \(\mathrm{CW}\) is the graph’s cutwidth and \(n=2m\). In the proof’s instance, all beliefs are \(1/2\) and all edges have equal unit weight. Since the cutwidth is \(\Theta(m^2)\), taking \(\beta=c/m\) for a sufficiently large constant \(c\) gives an exponential lower bound in \(m\), up to the polynomial factor \(n\).

My proposed continuous problem is Mean-Field Logit Barrier. There are two types, \(A\) and \(B\), each with mass \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\). Type records an agent’s belief and network role: each \(A\)-agent interacts uniformly with the \(B\) population, and vice versa. All agents have belief \(1/2\) and two actions, opinions \(0\) and \(1\). Let \(q_A,q_B\in[0,1]\) be the fractions of each type playing \(1\). For \(N=2m\) agents, give each cross-type edge weight \(1/m=2/N\), so each agent’s total interaction weight stays bounded as the population grows. The type masses remain fixed while \(N\) grows.

More generally, for finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), beliefs \(b_t\), and a symmetric nonnegative interaction matrix \(J\), define \(q_t\) as the fraction of type \(t\) playing \(1\), and set
\[ \phi(q)= \sum_t\mu_t\bigl((1-q_t)b_t^2+q_t(1-b_t)^2\bigr) +\frac12\sum_{t,u}\mu_t\mu_uJ_{tu} \bigl(q_t(1-q_u)+(1-q_t)q_u\bigr), \]
\[ F_\beta(q)=\phi(q)-\frac1\beta\sum_t\mu_t h(q_t), \qquad h(z)=-z\log z-(1-z)\log(1-z). \]
Here \(\phi\) is the limiting potential per agent, and the entropy term accounts for the number of individual action profiles represented by each type-level mass vector. For the \(K_{m,m}\) instance above, \(T=\{A,B\}\), \(b_A=b_B=1/2\), and \(J_{AB}=J_{BA}=2\).

Given rational input for this model, a rational \(\beta\), and two rational boxes promised to isolate distinct local minima \(q^-\) and \(q^+\) of \(F_\beta\), the problem asks for an additive-\(\epsilon\) approximation to
\[ B_\beta(q^-,q^+)= \inf_{\substack{\gamma\in C([0,1],[0,1]^\tau)\\ \gamma(0)=q^-,\ \gamma(1)=q^+}} \ \max_{s\in[0,1]}F_\beta(\gamma(s)) -\min\{F_\beta(q^-),F_\beta(q^+)\}. \]
A solution is a value within \(\epsilon\) of this barrier; a path attaining the upper bound within \(\epsilon\) would make the answer more useful. This is a question about the continuous population’s macroscopic opinion masses and the computation of the bottleneck between free-energy wells.

I expect the fixed two-type member to be tractable: it is a fixed-dimensional barrier problem and admits additive approximation by discretizing the square and searching for a lowest-height connecting path. For variable support \(\tau\), I would leave the complexity open; this anchor gives no basis for claiming transferred discrete hardness or continuum-specific hardness. The \(K_{m,m}\) result is the low-noise instance to mirror: with the edge weights normalized by \(1/m\), the choice \(\beta=c/m\) in the paper corresponds to constant \(\beta=c\) here. The finite chain’s exponential lower bound is therefore evidence that a positive macroscopic barrier can survive continuization.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem: it keeps their two opinions, belief costs, network interaction, logit equilibrium, and the mixing bottleneck. It also makes the population continuous: \(q_A\) and \(q_B\) are fractions of mass, while each individual still chooses an integral opinion. The broader questions are whether the barrier determines the limit of \(N^{-1}\log t_{\mathrm{mix}}\), whether its computation is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), and how the barrier changes across the high-noise regime of Theorem 4.9.

The weak point is that Theorem 4.13 proves a finite-chain mixing lower bound, not an equivalence between its mixing exponent and this free-energy barrier. Establishing that equivalence is necessary before the barrier can be called a theorem-level mirror. This case covers Theorem 4.13’s two-cohort slow-mixing regime; it does not mirror the paper’s best-response results or its bounds for arbitrary network topologies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.13 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, and its two-type reduction is sound. On \(K_{m,m}\), agents in each partition are exchangeable: the logit chain can be lumped by the two counts of agents playing \(1\). After scaling edge weights by \(1/m\) and adjusting \(\beta\) accordingly, the stationary mass of a count pair has the form
\[ \pi_N(k_A,k_B)\propto {m\choose k_A}{m\choose k_B} \exp\!\left[-\beta N\phi(k_A/m,k_B/m)\right]. \]
Its large-\(N\) exponent is exactly the proposed free energy \(F_\beta\). So this is a real continuous-population model, not a mistaken appeal to fractional outcomes or noise.

The best case against is about what the mirror contributes to ChoCo. For this anchor, computing the continuous barrier is a fixed two-dimensional variational problem. It may help explain the finite chain’s bottleneck, but it does not open much of the complexity landscape the programme seeks: with two types, discretization gives an additive approximation; making the type count variable introduces a higher-dimensional optimization problem whose difficulty comes from the chosen interaction matrix, rather than from the paper’s \(K_{m,m}\) result. The paper proves a mixing-time bound, not a complexity classification for computing a barrier or a mixing exponent.

The natural stronger mirror—derive the asymptotic mixing exponent of the type-count chain—would be closer to the paper’s actual result. Even then, the main mathematical move is a mean-field free-energy analysis of a logit/Glauber process. There is substantial nearby work on mixing and metastability for mean-field spin systems and logit dynamics, including analyses of clique games and the Curie–Weiss model ([Auletta et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1212.1884), [Levin, Luczak, and Peres](https://arxiv.org/abs/0712.0790)). That is evidence that the proposed question may be a familiar analytic continuation; it is not a claim that this literature already answers the computational question or creates a novelty collision.

That leaves a narrow negative case: ChoCo is aimed at computational social choice questions, while this paper studies autonomous dynamics and convergence. The barrier proposal is a computational question one can ask, but its connection to the programme’s intended algorithmic landscape is weaker than its mathematical connection to Theorem 4.13.

I cannot make the universal negative case stick. The exchangeable \(K_{m,m}\) regime has meaningful multiplicity, and the type-count chain gives a crisp continuous mirror the authors could plausibly recognize. The negative case rests on the programme’s scope and expected computational yield, not on a fundamental failure of the mirror.
