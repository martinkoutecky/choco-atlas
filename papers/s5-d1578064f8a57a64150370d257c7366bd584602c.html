What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation corresponds to Theorem 6.2’s threshold-packing hardness, particularly its star case. It does not provide a distinct continuous version of that result; the paper’s balancedness results and approximation algorithms are outside this proposed formulation.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is \(\mathsf{MassThresholdPack}_1\), a continuous-population version of the paper’s star-district packing problem.

Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a graph of atomic regions. A type is a region together with the characteristics the model uses; \(\mu_v\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) is the fraction of the population living in region \(v\), with \(\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v=1\). Given a population threshold \(\beta\), choose a family \(\mathcal D\) of pairwise vertex-disjoint sets of regions to maximize
\[ \sum_{D\in\mathcal D}\sum_{v\in D}\mu_v. \]
Each \(D\) must have mass at least \(\beta\), and must be a star: there is a center \(z_D\in D\) adjacent to every other region in \(D\). The solution is the chosen family and its centers; any mass in an unchosen region is uncovered. As in the paper, a region type is assigned as a whole to at most one district.

A plausible regime is electoral redistricting over many census cells: millions of residents, but perhaps thousands of atomic regions, with many residents sharing each region type. The graph records adjacency between regions, while mass records each region’s share of the population. The support size \(\tau=|V|\) is much smaller than the number \(N\) of residents, though it grows across instances. This is a natural high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s stated model: the paper already treats regions as atoms and allows their weights to represent population. Normalizing those weights to rational shares and scaling the threshold to a population fraction preserves the question the authors posed.

My anchor is Theorem 6.2, proved in this paper by a reduction from Maximum Independent Set, using the reduction framework of [DGHY25]. It says that \(B\)-threshold districting with weak- or strong-radius-\(k\) districts is NP-hard on bounded-degree graphs and on planar graphs. On general graphs, approximation within a factor better than \(n^{1/2-\delta}\) is NP-hard for every \(\delta>0\); these hardness results hold even when districts are required to be stars of radius one.

The continuous version preserves that reduction directly. For the reduction’s graph \(G'\), make every vertex a region type of mass \(1/|V(G')|\), and set \(\beta=(\Delta+1)/|V(G')|\), where \(\Delta\) is the degree used in the construction. Then the threshold condition is exactly the paper’s requirement of at least \(\Delta+1\) unit-weight vertices in a district. The reduction’s valid stars correspond to independent-set choices, so both the optimum and its approximation ratios are preserved up to a common scaling of the objective. Equivalently, one can replicate every resident in each region \(M\) times: \(N=M|V(G')|\) grows while the support and the hard districting choices stay the same. Thus the expected classification is Class B: hardness transfers. The combinatorics live in the region graph and its support size \(\tau\), not in the number of residents per type.

This covers Theorem 6.2’s threshold-packing hardness, especially its star case; it does not attempt to mirror the paper’s balancedness results or all its approximation algorithms. The anchor also motivates a further question: can exact-threshold star packing on planar graphs have a PTAS, despite being NP-hard? That is a meaningful boundary question, since the paper obtains constant-factor guarantees for exact constraints and a PTAS when composition constraints are relaxed.

The soft spot is the whole-region assignment rule. Residents sharing a region type receive the same district-membership bundle, so this mirror does not let a district split one region’s mass among several districts. That may matter if a referee thinks fractional assignment within an atomic region is essential to the continuous variant. But the paper explicitly defines vertices as atomic regions and requires vertex-disjoint districts; preserving that indivisibility is what keeps this mirror recognizably the authors’ problem. If one instead permits fractional use of each region, that is a different districting model, and the hardness transfer would need to be reconsidered.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6.2 is a genuine computational anchor, so the negative case has to be narrow: the proposed mirror appears to be the paper’s weighted problem with its units changed, rather than a new continuous-population problem.

Let \(W=\sum_v w(v)\) be the total vertex weight. Set \(\mu_v=w(v)/W\) and \(\beta=B/W\). Then every district meets the normalized threshold exactly when it meets the paper’s threshold, and its covered mass is its paper objective divided by \(W\). Conversely, for rational \(\mu_v\) and \(\beta\), clearing denominators gives integer vertex weights and a threshold. The feasible packings, optimum, and approximation ratios correspond exactly. In particular, the proponent’s uniform-mass reduction is just the original reduction rescaled. The paper already accepts integer weights as population counts, so allowing rational shares adds no new computational question. This is not an objection that hardness transfers; it is that this version never changes the problem.

A stronger mirror would let a region’s population split between districts, with variables \(x_{v,D}\) for the mass of region \(v\) assigned to district \(D\). But if positive mass from one region can enter two districts, their supports share a vertex, contrary to the paper’s vertex-disjointness requirement. Forcing all mass at a vertex to go together restores the original discrete choice. Allowing shared supports instead requires a new account of how distinct connected districts occupy the same atomic region—likely adding finer geography or within-region boundaries that the paper’s graph does not specify. That may be a worthwhile districting problem, but its connection to Theorem 6.2 is much less direct, and the authors may not recognize it as their packing problem.

The weak point in this case is real: the paper’s region weights make a high-multiplicity regime plausible, and a reader could regard normalized rational weights over indivisible region types as a continuous society even though the decisions remain discrete. If ChoCo accepts that as a worthwhile mirror, Theorem 6.2 survives; the negative case cannot rule it out. My best argument is that the proposed version is only a reparameterization of the paper’s existing weighted model, while the version with genuinely divisible population changes the packing problem.
