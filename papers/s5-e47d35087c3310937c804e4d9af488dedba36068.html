The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a modest Class A mirror of the paper’s reserve-allocation problem. The paper contains no named NP-hardness, membership-in-\(\mathrm{P}\), or parameterized-complexity theorem, so I would not pretend that it supplies a hardness anchor. Its useful anchors are structural theorems whose finite-agent proofs lift naturally from counts to masses.

My lead mirror is a continuous version of the paper’s single-priority reserve problem. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete applicant types. A type \(t\) records whether the applicant is general-category or reserved-category, together with a priority class in a common order \(\succ\). A society is a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_t)_{t\in T}\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of applicants of type \(t\). Let \(\bar q_u\) and \(\bar q_r\) be per-capita unreserved and reserved capacities.

A solution is a mass allocation \(x=(x_t^u,x_t^r,x_t^\varnothing)_{t\in T}\), where each unit of mass receives either an unreserved slot, a reserved slot, or no slot. Thus \(x_t^u+x_t^r+x_t^\varnothing=\mu_t\), \(x_t^r=0\) for general-category types, and the two capacity constraints are \(\sum_t x_t^u\le \bar q_u\) and \(\sum_t x_t^r\le \bar q_r\). The continuous version of non-wastefulness requires that any unassigned reserved mass implies that both caps are full, while any unassigned general mass implies that the unreserved cap is full. Priority-respect means that an unassigned type cannot be displaced by lower-priority mass in the relevant allocation.

The objective is not an invented welfare function. It is the paper’s own comparison objective: let \(R(x)\) be the total reserved-category mass receiving any slot. Compute

\[ R_{\min}=\min_x R(x) \qquad\text{and}\qquad R_{\max}=\max_x R(x) \]

over all continuous allocations satisfying the statute axioms, and return optimal allocation witnesses. Equivalently, one may ask whether \(R_{\max}\ge \eta\) or \(R_{\min}\le \eta\) for a target fraction \(\eta\).

This is the continuous problem I would call Continuous Reserve Extremes\(_1\). It is not merely fractionalizing the outcome: every infinitesimal applicant still receives one integral slot or none; only the aggregate allocation is represented by masses.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper in Appendix A.1. For a finite visa problem with one common priority order \(\pi\), let \(\mu_{\mathrm{ef}}\) be the Exemptions-First outcome, \(\mu_{\mathrm{oa}}\) the Over-and-Above outcome, and \(\mu\) any matching complying with the statute. The theorem states, in full, that

\[ \mu_{\mathrm{ef}}(I_R)\subseteq \mu(I_R)\subseteq \mu_{\mathrm{oa}}(I_R) \]

and

\[ \mu_{\mathrm{oa}}(I_G)\subseteq \mu(I_G)\subseteq \mu_{\mathrm{ef}}(I_G). \]

Thus Exemptions-First is reserved-category-minimal and general-category-maximal, whereas Over-and-Above is reserved-category-maximal and general-category-minimal among all statute-compliant matchings.

In the continuous mirror, literal set inclusion becomes mass and prefix dominance, but the substantive statement is unchanged:

\[ R(x^{\mathrm{EF}})\le R(x)\le R(x^{\mathrm{OA}}) \]

and

\[ G(x^{\mathrm{OA}})\le G(x)\le G(x^{\mathrm{EF}}) \]

for every compliant mass allocation \(x\). The proof carries over by replacing the number of high-priority applicants in every prefix by the mass of high-priority types in that prefix. The two extremal witnesses are obtained by scanning the finitely many priority types and filling the relevant cap until its mass is exhausted.

I expect Continuous Reserve Extremes\(_1\) to be tractable. Given \(\tau=|T|\), it can be solved exactly by sorting the types and computing prefix masses, in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input encoding length. This is a genuine high-multiplicity question: a finite instance with \(n_t\) applicants of type \(t\) maps to \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and capacities scale as \(\bar q_u=q_u/n\) and \(\bar q_r=q_r/n\). The continuous result also gives immediate preprocessing and additive approximation information for the corresponding finite problem.

The regime is plausible in an H-1B setting if applicants are grouped by category and coarse priority class: filing cohort, lottery band, wage band, or another priority tier. A year may contain roughly \(190{,}000\) petitions and only a small or moderate number of such cohorts, so \(n\gg\tau\). The type is complete for the model: category and priority are the only applicant features used by the paper, and applicants within a type are interchangeable. If a lottery gives every applicant a unique real-valued rank, this mirror becomes weak; I return to that below.

A second, independently useful mirror follows the paper’s two-priority analysis. Let a type be \(t=(a,\rho_u,\rho_r)\), recording category \(a\), unreserved priority class \(\rho_u\), and reserved priority class \(\rho_r\). The society is again a distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many such types. The action is the same mass allocation, but priority-respect is imposed separately under the two priority orders.

Call this problem Continuous Reserve Extremes\(_2\): given \((T,\mu,\bar q_u,\bar q_r,\succ_u,\succ_r)\), compute the minimum and maximum reserved-category mass among all statute-compliant allocations, together with witnesses implementing the extrema. The natural candidate witnesses are the continuous Reserved-Initiated and Unreserved-Initiated rules: fill the reserved cap first using \(\succ_r\), then the unreserved cap using \(\succ_u\), or reverse those stages.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved here in Appendix A.3. The printed statement appears to contain a typographical error, defining \(\mu_{ur}\) as \(\phi_{ru}(E)\); Section 3.3 and the proof clearly use \(\phi_{ur}\). With that correction, the theorem says that for any pair of priority orders and any statute-compliant matching \(\mu\),

\[ |\mu_{ru}(I_R)|\le |\mu(I_R)|\le |\mu_{ur}(I_R)| \]

and

\[ \mu_{ur}(I_G)\subseteq \mu(I_G)\subseteq \mu_{ru}(I_G). \]

The continuous version predicts

\[ R(x^{\mathrm{RU}})\le R(x)\le R(x^{\mathrm{UR}}) \]

and the corresponding general-category mass inequalities. This is again tractable: scan the reserved-priority ordering for the first stage and the unreserved-priority ordering for the second, updating remaining type masses and capacities. The expected classification is therefore Class A, not hardness transfer or continuum-specific hardness.

These mirrors are plausible because they preserve every substantive ingredient of the paper: two applicant categories, two caps, priority orders, the three statutory axioms, sequential processing, and the paper’s exact objective of comparing reserved-category allocations. The only change is that repeated applicants are represented by masses and capacities are normalized per capita. Existing high-multiplicity work would support rather than undermine this interpretation.

The scope is deliberately limited. I would claim coverage of Theorems 1 and 3 and the underlying reserve-allocation comparisons. I would not claim that the continuous mirror covers the paper’s historical discussion of FY2005–FY2020, its legal interpretation, or its empirical estimates unless those are separately recast as distributions over priority types. The paper’s Section 3.5 calculations provide useful supporting evidence, but they are not named computational anchors.

The weakest point is that the paper’s original allocation rules are already easy to execute by scanning a finite priority order. Continuization therefore does not obviously unlock a dramatic new algorithmic toolbox, and if priority is individualized by a unique lottery number then the number of complete types may be essentially the number of applicants. The mirror is strongest in a regime with repeated filing or lottery cohorts, and it is best described as a clean high-multiplicity computational formulation rather than a breakthrough complexity result. That limitation weakens the ambition of the case, but not the claim that these are legitimate continuous versions of the paper’s own reserve-allocation problems.

The resulting further questions are natural: how do the extremal reserved shares vary with \(\mu\) and the cap rates; can one choose a processing order to hit a target reserved share; how robust are the extrema to uncertainty in the type distribution; and what happens with several overlapping reserve categories rather than the paper’s single reserved category?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is upstream of either theorem: this paper contains no named computational result. Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 are structural characterizations of matchings satisfying axioms. They do not state a complexity bound, an algorithmic theorem, an approximation guarantee, or a computational hardness result. The proponent manufactures a computational problem—minimizing or maximizing reserved-category mass—and then observes that the paper’s structural theorem solves it. That is a valid new exercise, but it is not a continuization of a computational contribution in this paper.

Even granting that construction, Theorem 1 does not generate a worthwhile continuous-computation problem. In the strongest high-multiplicity version, let \(R(z)\) denote the reserved-category mass among the first \(z\) units of population in the common priority order. The two extremes are obtained directly from prefix masses: Exemptions-First gives \(R_{\min}=\max\{\bar q_r,R(\bar q_u+\bar q_r)\}\), while Over-and-Above gives \(R_{\max}=R(\bar q_u)+\min\{\bar q_r,R_{\mathrm{tot}}-R(\bar q_u)\}\). A scan of the priority types computes these quantities.

This is not an objection that “continuous does not help”; that would indeed be impermissible. The more fundamental point is that the paper never posed a computational input-output problem here. Its theorem already identifies the extremal allocation rules as a universal order-theoretic fact. Replacing applicant counts by masses turns the same prefix argument into notation about a cumulative distribution. There is no missing algorithmic question for ChoCo to expose: no exponentially represented object, separation problem, optimization formulation, or complexity landscape.

The proposed H-1B type regime is also less natural than it first appears. In the paper, priority is a linear order on named applicants. If that order is part of an applicant’s complete type, then exact lottery rank or arrival position distinguishes essentially every applicant, so the number of complete types is close to the population size. To obtain few types, one must replace the strict priority order by priority bands or ties. That may be a reasonable new model, but it changes the paper’s primitive. If the bands are resolved by an individual lottery, the randomness has been reintroduced as a noise or priority model; the population itself has not supplied the computational structure.

Theorem 3 fares no better. With two independent priority orders, a complete type records category together with both priority positions. Under the actual lottery interpretation, the pairs are again essentially unique. Under a finite-band reinterpretation, the continuous rule is still just two successive quantile scans: fill one cap by one ordering, remove that mass, and fill the other cap by the second ordering. The typographical error in the printed statement is immaterial; correcting it does not create a computational anchor.

The most promising rescue would be to ask a genuinely new design problem: choose processing orders to hit a target reserved share, handle overlapping regional and occupational reserves, or optimize under uncertainty about the population distribution. Those could become worthwhile computational social-choice problems. But their combinatorics would come from newly added overlapping constraints, endogenous policy choice, or uncertainty—not from Theorems 1 or 3. They would be papers inspired by the H-1B setting, not continuous mirrors of this paper’s contribution.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s anchor discipline, though not metaphysically universal: a broader continuous reserve-design project could certainly be worthwhile. What cannot honestly be claimed is that this particular paper supplies a computational result whose population continuization opens a new complexity programme.
