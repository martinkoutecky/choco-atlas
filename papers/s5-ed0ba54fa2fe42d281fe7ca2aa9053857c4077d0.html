The closest structural seed is the theorem’s characterization of Pareto aggregation. With finitely many individuals and a finite outcome set, Pareto indifference holds exactly when the social utility set \(U_0\) consists of sums \(\sum_i \alpha_i u_i-\beta_i v_i+\gamma e\), for nonnegative \(\alpha_i,\beta_i\) and parameters in a closed set \(\Phi\) with the stated convex-section conditions. Under the additional closedness assumption, Pareto preference holds exactly when \(U_0\) consists of nonnegative weighted sums \(\sum_i\theta_i u_i+\gamma e\), with parameters in a closed set \(\Omega\). This is proved in the paper. It is a mathematical representation theorem, not a computational result.

One could imagine a research extension in which types are agents’ incomplete-preference specifications, \(\mu\) is their population mass, and social utility functions are formed by integrating type-level contributions. But that would introduce a new continuum-agent aggregation problem; the paper neither formulates nor computes it. Moreover, its own lotteries and utility sets are continuous objects while the society remains finite. Continuizing the population would be a plausible new angle, but it would not turn the theorem into a computational anchor.

So the positive case is limited: the paper offers an aggregation structure that might inspire a future continuum-population model. It does not supply a named computational result for this programme to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Its theorem characterizes which sets of social utility functions represent preferences satisfying Pareto indifference or Pareto preference. It gives no computational problem, input encoding, or complexity claim. There is therefore no anchor here for a continuous computational question.

The most charitable extension is to replace the finite population with a distribution over types of incomplete preferences, then ask which social preferences satisfy a continuum version of Pareto. That is a coherent model: repeated agents with the same preference specification can form a type, and their population mass can matter. But the resulting question is an axiomatic aggregation theorem about the structure of compatible social preferences—the sort of analytic question the programme distinguishes from computational complexity. The fact that the paper’s lotteries and utility sets are continuous does not change that: its society is still finite.

One could impose finite rational descriptions of the utility sets and invent a task such as recognizing whether a proposed social preference has the required representation. That would create a computational question, but the task, encoding, and objective would all be additions; the paper’s theorem does not single them out as its computational content. The honest negative case is strong for this paper as written, though not a proof that no independently motivated algorithmic project could ever be built from its ideas. Such a project would be new research, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of a result in this paper.
