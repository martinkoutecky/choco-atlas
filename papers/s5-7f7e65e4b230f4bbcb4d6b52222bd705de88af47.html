The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), per-capita budgets \(b_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t=1\), and normalized piecewise-linear valuations \(v_t\) on \([0,1]\) with common segments \(I_k=[a_{k-1},a_k]\), choose aggregate allocations \(y_{tk}\in L^\infty_+(I_k)\) satisfying \(\sum_t y_{tk}\leq 1\). Let \(q_{tk}=\int_{I_k}v_t(\theta)y_{tk}(\theta)\,d\theta\) and \(q_t=\sum_k q_{tk}\). Compute feasible segment utilities and allocations with \(q_t\geq q_t^*-\varepsilon\) for every type, where \(q^*\) maximizes \(\sum_t\mu_t b_t\log(q_t/\mu_t)\) over these feasible allocations; the per-capita allocation is \(x_{tk}=y_{tk}/\mu_t\).

The model it lives in

A continuum population with finite type support, represented by masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita valuations \(v_t\), and budgets \(b_t\). Aggregate allocations \(y_{tk}\) obey mass-weighted supply feasibility, and the weighted Nash objective is equivalent up to an additive constant to the paper's Eisenberg–Gale objective with representative-buyer budgets \(B_t=\mu_t b_t\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 8's approximate equilibrium allocation for piecewise-linear valuations with \(K\) segments, including allocation construction. It does not add individual-level fairness guarantees; the general-valuation method in §5 is not the anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • What bit-complexity bound follows for rational valuation data and rational masses and budgets, including the dependence on the smallest mass?
  • For a required per-capita utility error \(\eta\), can the algorithm's accuracy dependence be sharpened beyond setting \(\varepsilon=\eta\min_t\mu_t\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a piecewise-linear continuum-population Fisher equilibrium. Consider a large fair-division or recommender market with \(N\) agents but only \(\tau\ll N\) complete types: everyone of type \(t\) has the same valuation curve and per-capita budget. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Its members share a per-capita allocation \(x_t(\theta)\); aggregate supply is feasible when \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t(\theta)\leq1\). Thus \(\mu_t\) is the population share, and \(y_t=\mu_t x_t\) is the type’s aggregate allocation. This is population continuization: the item space remains the paper’s \([0,1]\), while agents are represented by mass.

Take type valuations \(v_t\) to be nonnegative, normalized piecewise-linear functions with \(K\) common segments, and per-capita budgets \(b_t>0\), normalized so \(\sum_t\mu_tb_t=1\). Each type demands a bundle maximizing \(\int_0^1v_t x_t\) subject to its per-capita budget; prices clear supply under the mass-weighted constraint. The equilibrium allocation maximizes the weighted Nash welfare \(\sum_t\mu_tb_t\log\int_0^1v_t x_t\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 8 in §4.4, proved in the paper using an ellipsoid result cited as Theorem 7. Theorem 8 says that, for \(n\) buyers with normalized piecewise-linear valuations on \([0,1]\), \(K\) segments, and \(\kappa=1/\min_i B_i\), for any \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\) one can compute feasible segment utilities \(u_{ik}\) such that each segment’s utility vector lies in its feasible set \(U(v,[a_{k-1},a_k])\) and each buyer’s total utility is at least its equilibrium utility minus \(\varepsilon\). The stated running time is \(O(n^4K(\log(nK)+\log(\kappa/\varepsilon)))\). The theorem also constructs an allocation in which each buyer receives a union of at most \(K\) intervals, with value at least that bound, in an additional \(O(nK)\) time. [Theorem 8 and its proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.03025)

The mirror question is: given rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), rational per-capita budgets \(b_t\), and normalized piecewise-linear \(v_t\) on \([0,1]\), compute a feasible aggregate allocation and its type utilities, approximately attaining the continuum population’s equilibrium utilities. More explicitly, for segment \(I_k=[a_{k-1},a_k]\), define
\[ U_k=\left\{q\in\mathbb R_+^\tau:\exists\,y_t\in L^\infty_+(I_k),\ \sum_t y_t\leq1,\quad q_t=\int_{I_k}v_t(\theta)y_t(\theta)\,d\theta\right\}. \]
The equilibrium utility target is given by maximizing
\[ \sum_t\mu_tb_t\log\!\left(\sum_k q_{tk}\right) \quad\text{subject to}\quad (q_{1k},\ldots,q_{\tau k})\in U_k\ \text{for every }k. \]
Here \(q_t/\mu_t\) is type \(t\)’s per-capita utility. The instance asks for feasible \(q_{tk}\) within \(\varepsilon\) of each type’s optimal aggregate utility and an allocation attaining them. A type’s aggregate budget is \(B_t=\mu_tb_t\), so this is exactly the paper’s \(\tau\)-buyer market with those budgets: the weighted objective differs from its Eisenberg–Gale objective only by a constant. Theorem 8 therefore predicts tractability in \(\tau\) and \(K\), with \(\kappa=1/\min_t(\mu_tb_t)\); masses and budgets contribute through their binary encoding length \(L\). If accuracy is instead required within \(\eta\) per capita for every type, use \(\varepsilon=\eta\min_t\mu_t\), so very small types worsen the accuracy dependence.

This is plausible as the authors’ problem: they explicitly treat very large buyer populations as a motivation, and grouping agents with identical valuations and budgets is the high-multiplicity regime. The result covers approximate equilibrium allocation for piecewise-linear valuations; it does not establish new fairness guarantees for a continuum of individual agents or cover arbitrary valuations. I expect this mirror to be tractable by representative-type aggregation, with running time depending on \(\tau\), not \(N\). Further questions are whether stochastic dual averaging extends this reduction beyond piecewise-linear valuations, and whether the aggregate allocation can be implemented for individual agents while retaining the desired fairness guarantees.

The weakest point is that the reduction is almost exactly a finite market with \(\tau\) representative buyers. That makes the mirror clean and credible, but its novelty is modest: it shows how Theorem 8 applies in a high-multiplicity regime, rather than revealing a new source of computational tractability. Also, Theorem 8’s pure allocation assigns item intervals to type aggregates; an individual-level implementation would need a separate account of how members of each type receive bundles consistent with their type-level average.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that it adds no distinct computational structure. For each type \(t\), let \(x_t\) be its per capita allocation and \(y_t=\mu_t x_t\) its aggregate allocation. Then \(\sum_t y_t\leq 1\), and the type’s aggregate budget is \(B_t=\mu_t b_t\). The weighted Nash objective differs from the paper’s Eisenberg–Gale objective only by the constant \(\sum_t \mu_t b_t\log\mu_t\). So the population model is exactly the paper’s market with \(\tau\) representative buyers. Theorem 8 applies with \(n=\tau\); for general valuations, the stochastic method in §5 likewise applies with \(\tau\) utility-price variables.

That exact reduction makes the mirror a weak candidate for a new computational contribution: it needs no new pricing method, optimization formulation, or population-specific algorithm. A genuinely continuous type distribution might avoid the reduction, but it is outside the stated finite-support, binary-rational input model. It would also need a representation of that distribution and suitable oracles before it posed a finite computational question.

This does not defeat the anchor under the programme’s criteria. The population interpretation is sensible, the per-capita allocation can be recovered by \(x_t=y_t/\mu_t\), and the algorithm’s dependence on \(\tau\) is meaningful in a high-multiplicity regime. Its answer being immediate from the paper is not, by itself, grounds to reject it. The honest negative case is therefore limited: the mirror looks like a straightforward reinterpretation of an existing result, with modest novelty. I cannot make a convincing case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
