The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population mirror here, but the paper has no anchor that meets the stated computational-complexity requirement. Its named results establish distortion guarantees, not that an underlying problem is in \(P\), hard, FPT, or similar.

The strongest near-miss is Theorem 36, proved in this paper: the triadic randomized Nash bargaining scheme \(nrand\) has distortion at most \(1.66\). Its proof uses a bound on expected pessimistic distortion and computes it through finitely many bilinear programs. That supports a computational performance claim, but Theorem 36 does not classify the complexity of computing distortion, and its proof does not establish a general runtime bound. Theorem 30 similarly proves a distortion upper bound of \(1.80\) for median schemes. Neither is a qualifying complexity anchor under the strict rule.

If a computation-backed distortion theorem is accepted as an anchor, the strongest mirror is the exact-distortion problem for \(nrand\). Let \(B_m\) be the budget simplex, and let an instance be a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu=\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\lambda_r\delta_{v^r}\) over preferred budgets \(v^r\in B_m\), with \(\sum_r\lambda_r=1\). A type is a voter’s complete preferred budget; its mass is the fraction of the population with that budget. In a large city where many residents submit the same or a small number of recurring budget proposals, \(\tau\) can be much smaller than the population size \(n\).

Draw \(a,b,c\) independently from \(\mu\), use \(c\) as the disagreement point, and run \(nrand\) for \(a,b\), producing outcome \(z\). The distributional distortion is

\[ D_{nrand}(\mu)= \frac{\mathbb{E}_{a,b,c,z}\!\left[\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\lambda_r\lVert v^r-z\rVert_1\right]} {\min_{p\in B_m}\sum_{r=1}^{\tau}\lambda_r\lVert v^r-p\rVert_1}. \]

The continuous problem is to determine the exact supremum of this quantity over such populations, as a function of \(m\), or over all \(m\). A solution would give the exact worst-case distortion and a matching population instance or limiting family. Theorem 36 supplies the upper bound \(1.66\); Theorem 25, also proved here, supplies the lower bound \(D_{nrand}>1.38\). Closing that gap is already identified by the paper as an open direction.

The mirror is recognizable: uniform voter sampling becomes sampling from \(\mu\), and sums of voter costs become population integrals. For rational finite-support \(\mu\), clearing denominators turns it exactly into a finite electorate covered by Theorem 36. That is also the main weakness: the proposed question largely restates the paper’s all-populations distortion analysis. Extending it to arbitrary, possibly non-atomic measures would need additional measure-theoretic care, but would not by itself create a new complexity result. So this is a credible population formulation of the paper’s distortion question, with \(nrand\) as its strongest result; it does not yield a compliant continuous-complexity case under your anchor criterion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor gate is the strongest case against a mirror here. Theorem 36 proves a distortion bound for \(nrand\); Theorem 25 gives a distortion lower bound; Theorem 30 does the same for median schemes. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The bilinear programs in the proof of Theorem 36 certify a bound using a fixed six-voter analysis; they do not give an algorithm for a society input or a running-time result in \(m,\tau,L\). So none of these results is a qualifying complexity anchor.

The strongest proposed mirror is a distributional version of the distortion question. It is well-posed, and its population interpretation is natural: residents with recurring preferred budgets form types, and their masses determine the sampling probabilities and aggregate cost. But for every rational finite-support \(\mu\), choose \(n\) to clear its denominators and repeat each budget \(v^r\) exactly \(n\lambda_r\) times. Sampling voters then gives exactly the same distribution over bargaining triples as sampling from \(\mu\), and the normalized social cost and optimum are exactly the same. Thus \(D_{nrand}(\mu)\) is the paper’s existing distortion objective for that profile. Taking its supremum over these populations is precisely the all-profiles supremum in Definition 6. Theorem 25’s lower-bound example translates the same way.

That makes “find the exact supremum over populations” a restatement of the paper’s stated open gap between \(1.38\) and \(1.66\), not a new computational-complexity question. The paper itself says closing that gap remains open. Asking for the per-population value \(D_{nrand}(\mu)\) would be a distinct task, but Theorem 36 does not establish its complexity; it gives only a uniform bound. Recasting the input as masses does not supply the missing algorithmic result.

I cannot make a strong case that high multiplicity is an implausible regime here: a large PB electorate with a small set of recurring budgets is sensible, and neither the costs nor the sampled triad vanish in the limit. The negative case rests on the narrower point that the proponent’s best anchor is either already the paper’s own profile-based distortion question or a new evaluation task that the cited theorem does not address. That defeats the proposed anchor under the stated criterion, but it does not prove that no worthwhile distributional study could be built from this paper.
