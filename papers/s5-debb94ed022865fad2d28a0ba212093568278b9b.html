What the mirror covers

Theorem 1’s finite-horizon equilibrium construction and Theorem 2’s horizon-limit value are the nearest computational results; Theorems 4 and 5 give comparative statics. No continuous-population problem is stated for them.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is only a possible future direction; it does not meet the required anchor standard. A plausible high multiplicity setting would be a large, standardized recruitment cohort in which applicants are grouped by the two skill measures the selectors use. A type could be a skill pair \((x,y)\), and \(\mu\) the fraction of applicants of each type. The selectors’ decision would still be whether to accept or reject each interviewee, with their payoffs based on their own skill measure and the consensus cost \(c\). That setting is imaginable, but the paper’s one-hire stopping problem makes the mirror weak: \(\mu\) alone loses arrival order, while adding interview position to each complete type can make the number of types grow with the cohort. And in a continuum, hiring one person selects zero mass.

There are no qualifying computational-complexity anchors in this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium using two acceptance thresholds. Theorem 2, also proved here, shows that the equilibrium values increase with the number of candidates and converge to a stated limit. Theorems 4 and 5 give comparative-static results for selector value and firm quality. These are substantive game-theoretic results, but none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The threshold characterization may help compute equilibrium values; it is not itself a named complexity result of the kind required.

That distinction is central to the proposed mirror. The paper already gives candidate skills a continuous distribution, but it keeps a finite sequence of candidates and studies its equilibrium analytically. Taking the candidate count to infinity is part of the paper’s own analysis; it does not supply a computational-complexity question about a society represented by type masses. So there is no defensible per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover here. The weakest point is also the decisive one: the plausible cohort story does not overcome the mismatch between a mass distribution and a sequential process that hires one individual.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that none of the proposed anchors is a computational-complexity result. Theorem 1 characterizes equilibrium strategies; Theorem 2 studies how the game value changes with the number of candidates and converges as \(n\) grows; Theorems 4 and 5 give comparative statics for selector value and firm quality. They are substantive results, but none classifies the complexity of a computational problem. A continuous version of any of them would ask a new question about a stochastic game, rather than mirror a complexity result in the paper.

The best candidate-cohort model still has a structural mismatch. Let applicants have types \((x,y)\), represented by masses, and arrive in random order. Those masses describe the law of the next interviewee. To preserve the game, the model also needs the sequential arrival process and the option to stop after any interview. With a fixed number of interviews, taking the cohort size large leaves the individual stopping game intact; the mass distribution does not become the object being manipulated. If the horizon grows with the cohort, arrival time and depletion become central. The resulting question is about a sequential process, and the paper already studies the \(n\to\infty\) horizon limit.

Theorem 1’s thresholds do not rescue an aggregate mirror: they govern whether to accept one particular candidate, and the consensus cost depends on which selector rejected that candidate. Theorem 2’s limit varies the number of future opportunities, not the population measure. Theorem 4 concerns selectors’ equilibrium values, while Theorem 5 concerns the quality of the single person hired. Averaging the outcome across many independent hiring rounds would give a population-level statistic, but the mass would then describe repeated instances or a distribution of outcomes. That would continuize repetitions or outcomes, leaving the hiring game itself unchanged.

A large panel of interchangeable assessors is another possible rescue. Type masses could represent assessors from different fields. But changing two strategic selectors into a large committee changes the game and its voting rule; the paper’s equilibrium results do not anchor that model. This paper’s continuous skill distribution does not count as continuous-population prior art either: it is a distribution from which individual candidates are drawn, while candidates still arrive one at a time.

The negative case is weakest against a generalized problem that takes a finite-support distribution over candidate types as input and asks for the complexity of computing equilibrium. That is a coherent computational question, and the authors might recognize it as related to their work. But the type masses there specify the stochastic input law; making the applicant population itself continuous adds no clear computational object to the paper’s results. The honest case against a worthwhile *continuous mirror of this paper* is therefore strong, though it cannot rule out a worthwhile new algorithmic study of generalized secretary games.
