The continuous mirror question

Given finite-support buyer and seller types with rational masses, links, buyer parameters, seller capacity functions, a current aggregate transaction vector \(w\), and residual per-agent demands \(d_a\), scale buyer demands by \(\mu_a\) and seller capacities by \(\nu_j\). For a buyer type \(a\), compute a coordinatewise maximal clinch vector of transaction masses \(\xi_{aj}\) such that fixing that type’s clinch leaves unchanged every other type’s set of feasible aggregate future quantities.

The model it lives in

A type is a complete buyer or seller profile, including preferences, budgets or reserve prices, and links or capacity function. Buyer type \(a\) has mass \(\mu_a\), seller type \(j\) has mass \(\nu_j\), and the state uses aggregate transactions and demands. A clinch is a transaction mass for a buyer type, distributed symmetrically as a per-agent increment; the selection rule is maximality subject to preserving other types’ feasible aggregate future quantities.

The objection that survived

The type-level no-harm condition preserves only other types’ aggregate possibilities, so a cohort clinch can change which members of the same type remain individually serviceable; the paper’s individual-buyer rule does not establish that cohort rule.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.6’s computation of a maximal clinching vector in Algorithm 2. It does not establish continuous versions of the full auction’s incentive compatibility, individual rationality, Pareto optimality, or iteration complexity.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the scaled type-level clinching polytope remain a polymatroid with a polynomial-time rank oracle, yielding a polynomial-time maximal-clinch computation?
  • Is symmetric distribution of each type’s clinched mass a canonical cohort rule that the authors would accept for this auction model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror is the continuous type clinching step, anchored in Theorem 3.6. That theorem says the clinching polytope \(P^i_{w,d}\) for buyer \(i\) is a polymatroid, its submodular function can be computed in polynomial time, and therefore a maximal clinching vector can be found in polynomial time by the greedy algorithm. The paper proves this in Section 5.1; it is not a result cited from elsewhere.

The natural regime is the paper’s display ad market: many advertisers and publishers, with repeated campaign and inventory profiles. A buyer type records the buyer’s valuation, bid, budget, and the publisher types it can reach. A seller type records its reserve price and its per-seller submodular capacity function. Agents of each type form a nonatomic mass; \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_j\) are the masses of buyer type \(a\) and seller type \(j\). Millions of participants could then be represented by a much smaller support of types. The same model applies when budgets or prices differ: those agents belong to different types. Goods are already divisible in the paper; the continuation here is of the population, with seller capacity scaled by seller mass.

Here is a prover-ready mirror, Continuous Type Clinching. Its input is finite-support buyer and seller type sets \(A,J\), a link set \(E\subseteq A\times J\), positive rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_j\), buyer parameters \((v_a,b_a,B_a)\), seller parameters \((\rho_j,f_j)\), and a current aggregate transaction vector \(w\) and per-buyer-type residual demands \(d_a\). The functions \(f_j\) are given by value oracles. For a proposed clinch vector \(\xi\) for buyer type \(a\), define
\[ Q_a(\xi)=\left\{u\in\mathbb R_+^{A\setminus\{a\}}: \begin{array}{l} \exists x\ge 0,\quad x_{aj}=\xi_{aj},\\ \sum_j x_{bj}=u_b\quad(b\ne a),\\ \sum_j x_{bj}\le \mu_b d_b\quad(b\in A),\\ w|_{E_j}+x|_{E_j}\in \nu_j P(f_j)\quad(j\in J) \end{array}\right\}. \]
Here \(\nu_jP(f_j)\) means the seller type’s aggregate capacity: its rank on a set of links is \(\nu_j f_j\) on that set. The clinching polytope is
\[ C_a=\{\xi\ge0:Q_a(\xi)=Q_a(0)\}. \]
Given the instance and state, the question is to output any coordinatewise maximal \(\xi\in C_a\). Its coordinates are transaction masses; each member of type \(a\) receives the corresponding per-agent increment \(\xi_{aj}/\mu_a\). This is the paper’s “not affecting other buyers” rule at type level. There is no welfare objective in this result: the selection criterion is maximal clinching that leaves every other type’s set of possible future quantities unchanged.

I expect this mirror step to be tractable—Class A—in time polynomial in the support sizes, link count, rational bit length, and the cost of the rank-oracle computations. Scaling the demand and capacity bounds by rational masses preserves the polymatroid structure used in the paper’s flow argument, so the theorem’s greedy-algorithm route appears to carry over. The authors should recognize this as their clinching computation: it retains their buyer and seller roles, budgets, price clocks, polymatroid constraints, and no-harm condition, while aggregating indistinguishable agents.

This case covers Theorem 3.6 and the corresponding clinching operation in Algorithm 2. It does not establish a polynomial-time continuous version of the entire auction, or transfer all its incentive and welfare properties. The paper itself notes that the number of clock iterations can depend on \(\sum_i\lceil v'_i/\varepsilon\rceil\), so a full-output algorithm needs a separate complexity analysis. The mirror’s weakest point is the scenario: exact matches on valuation, budget, and targeting are plausible for repeated campaign templates, but less convincing than homogeneity in some other domains. Splitting agents by those attributes is faithful to high multiplicity; it can, however, make the support large enough to erode the computational advantage.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.6 is a genuine computational result, so there is no “nothing to mirror” objection. The strongest problem with this anchor is that the proposed \(C_a\) changes what a clinch means. In the paper, buyer \(i\) may clinch only if doing so leaves the other *individual buyers’* feasible future quantities unchanged. In \(C_a\), a whole type is treated as one buyer, and only the feasible totals of the other types have to stay unchanged.

For example, take two identical buyers of type \(a\), one buyer of type \(b\), and a seller with capacity \(2\). Give each buyer residual demand \(1\). In the individual model, neither \(a\)-buyer can clinch a positive amount: doing so removes the feasible outcome in which the other \(a\)-buyer and the \(b\)-buyer each get \(1\). In the proposed type model, type \(a\) has total demand \(2\). It can clinch \(1\) while leaving type \(b\)’s entire feasible quantity set unchanged. Dividing that clinch equally among the two \(a\)-buyers does not restore the original no-harm condition. Thus the proposed computation is for a type coalition, not the paper’s clinching step.

A more faithful version would preserve the feasible allocations of the other members of the same type too. But then a “buyer type” is not a single finite-dimensional coordinate: it stands for a continuum of individual buyers and their possible allocations. In an atomless model, an individual has zero mass, so an individual-level clinch either has zero size or needs a new simultaneous, cohort-level rule. That rule could be a worthwhile mechanism-design question, but it is a redesign of the clinching operation, not a direct continuization of Theorem 3.6.

This is a real strike against the prover-ready mirror, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Repeated advertiser templates make type-level markets plausible, and the cohort-level version may be something the authors would recognize. The negative case is therefore weak on the universal claim: it identifies a substantive semantic fork, but cannot rule out a useful aggregate clinching problem on the other side of it.
