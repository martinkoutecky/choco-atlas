Why no mirror

The paper's named results report expectations and asymptotic behavior in random markets; none asserts an algorithm, computational bound, hardness result, or solver formulation. The proposed type-class DA computation changes the random market and is not a continuous version of any named result, so the computational-anchor gate fails.

What the mirror covers

No named result is covered: Propositions 1 and 3 give expected counts \(H_n\), Proposition 2 an asymptotic rank approximation, and Corollary 1 an expected-count approximation; the type-class DA extension does not recover these results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying computational anchors in this paper. Its named results are probabilistic claims, not complexity results. For example, Proposition 1, proved here, says that in the uniform random \(n\)-student, \(n\)-school market the expected number of students whom nobody envies is \(H_n\). Proposition 2 gives an asymptotic geometric approximation to students’ ranks under DA; Corollary 1 derives an expected number of students who envy nobody of approximately \(n/H_n\); and Proposition 3, also proved here, says RSD leaves \(H_n\) students unenvied in expectation. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classifies its complexity. I would not promote these into the required anchors.

The most plausible positive extension is a large school district with many students but a small number of shared preference and priority classes. Represent student classes by types \(T\), their population shares by rational masses \(\mu_t\), and school capacities by shares \(q_s\). Run student-proposing DA on the mass cohorts and ask for the resulting allocation \(x_{t,s}\), including the mass assigned to a top choice. That is a concrete population-continuous computational question about the paper’s subject. But it is an extension, not a mirror anchored by one of the paper’s named complexity results.

The fit is weakest precisely at the paper’s assumptions and central result. A complete student type must include priority information as well as preferences; with the paper’s individual strict priorities, that information distinguishes students, so the number of types grows with the market. Grouping students into shared priority classes changes the random-priority model. And Proposition 1’s coupon-collector event—one school receiving exactly one proposal—has no direct mass analogue.

So the honest case for a continuous mirror is that school-choice DA has plausible high-multiplicity applications and its welfare quantities can be restated as population shares. It does not yield a continuous computational question that this paper’s named results establish as their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

None of the paper’s named results is a computational result of the kind ChoCo mirrors. Proposition 1 and Proposition 3 give expected counts of \(H_n\); Proposition 2 approximates a rank distribution; Corollary 1 estimates an expected count of \(n/H_n\). They analyze random markets, but do not classify the complexity of an algorithmic problem. That leaves no qualifying result to anchor a continuous mirror.

The distinction matters for the proposed cohort model. Given preference and priority classes and capacity shares, one can ask for the mass allocation under deferred acceptance. That is a coherent continuous-population question, but it computes a deterministic aggregate outcome. The paper’s results instead concern expectations over independently sampled, individual strict preferences and priorities. Replacing those with shared priority classes changes the model, and the resulting allocation does not answer any of the paper’s propositions. Adding a probability law over the classes to recover expectations would define a new stochastic problem, not continuize a computational problem this paper studied.

The sharpest mismatch is Proposition 1. Its \(H_n\) count comes from schools receiving exactly one proposer in a finite coupon-collector process. In a normalized atomless population, one proposer has zero mass; “exactly one proposer” is not a positive-mass category. If types have atoms, “one proposer” depends on how the population was partitioned into types. Tracking the unnormalized singleton count would retain the finite-market effect the continuum removes. The other headline counts also vanish as population shares: \(H_n/n\to0\) and \((n/H_n)/n\to0\). Their limits cannot preserve the quantities the paper compares.

A continuous school-choice model with priority classes may still be useful research. But on this paper’s evidence, it would be a new computational study inspired by its topic, rather than a mirror of its results. The negative case is strongest for the paper’s specific contribution: there is no computational result to mirror, and the most natural mass version loses the finite-market quantities at the center of the paper. It is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile computational question could ever be built around continuous-population school choice; the paper alone cannot establish that universal claim.
