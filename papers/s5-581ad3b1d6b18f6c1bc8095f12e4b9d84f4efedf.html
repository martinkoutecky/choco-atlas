The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 characterizes the equitable solution axiomatically; Lemma 1 proves Lindahl equilibrium existence and efficiency; Theorem 2 proves that Lindahl equilibrium payoffs equal equitable outcomes; and Theorems 3 and 4 give matching and allocation correspondences. These are mathematical and economic results, not complexity classifications or computational formulations the paper analyzes. The optimization problems in the text define agents’ and the firm’s choices; they do not establish an algorithm or complexity result. So there is no anchor-linked continuous problem to state under your criteria.

The strongest plausible follow-up would be a large community choosing among finitely many projects, with residents grouped into finitely many complete utility types. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and utility vector \(u_t\) over the projects. A continuous Lindahl-equilibrium problem would ask for a common lottery \(q\) and type-specific prices \(p_t\): \(q\) must be each type’s minimum-cost utility-maximizing lottery under its unit budget, and it must maximize aggregate revenue \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t\cdot q\). This makes the population continuous while keeping the paper’s collective-choice model recognizable. Theorem 2 is the nearest analytic bridge: it proves that, for each finite utility profile, Lindahl-equilibrium payoffs coincide with the equitable solution of the associated bargaining problem.

That is a credible research direction, but it is not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. Theorem 2 does not give the required complexity anchor, and its finite-agent bargaining formulation does not by itself settle how the continuous population version should be computed.
