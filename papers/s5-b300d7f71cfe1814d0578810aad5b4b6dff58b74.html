The continuous mirror question

Given projects \(P\), lecturers \(L\), an offer map \(\lambda:P\to L\), and a finite support \(T\) of student types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), each type specifies a complete student preference order with ties and acceptable projects, and each lecturer specifies a preference tier for each type. Project and lecturer capacities \(\kappa_p,\delta_\ell\) are rational fractions of the population. An allocation has variables \(x_{tp}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_p x_{tp}\le\mu_t\), respecting capacities and acceptability; each individual still receives at most one project. For type \(t\) and project \(p\), let \(d_{tp}=\mu_t-\sum_{q:\,q\succeq_t p}x_{tq}\), the mass unassigned or assigned strictly worse than \(p\). A pair blocks if \(d_{tp}>0\) and one of the paper’s three cases holds: \(p\) and \(\lambda(p)\) both have spare capacity; \(p\) has spare capacity and its lecturer is full, with positive dissatisfied mass of type \(t\) already assigned to that lecturer or the lecturer strictly preferring \(t\) to its worst occupied tier; or \(p\) is full and its lecturer strictly prefers \(t\) to the worst occupied tier at \(p\). Stability means no such blocking mass. Is there an algorithm polynomial in \(|P|,|L|,|T|\), and input bit length that returns a stable allocation whose total assigned mass is at least two-thirds of the maximum stable mass?

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete student types, with rational type masses and per-capita project and lecturer capacities; decision variables allocate each type’s mass among acceptable projects, and the objective maximizes assigned mass subject to mass-based stability.

The objection that survived

The paper’s linear-time algorithm already handles large cohorts, so the mirror’s payoff depends on a support-sensitive algorithm or stable rounding; neither follows from Theorem 27.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 27’s two-thirds guarantee for maximum stable matching. It does not transfer the guarantee or running time automatically, and leaves the exact IP model, cloning results, and experimental comparisons outside the proposed question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a stable allocation with at least two-thirds of maximum stable mass be found in time polynomial in \(|P|,|L|,|T|\), and input bit length?
  • Does the discrete two-thirds guarantee extend to fractional stable allocations, or can the mass problem be solved exactly?
  • Can a mass allocation be rounded for a finite cohort while preserving stability and a useful size guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make a bounded case around Theorem 27, proved in this paper. It says that for every SPA-ST instance, the stable matching \(M\) returned by Algorithm 2 has size at least \(\frac{2}{3}|M_{\mathrm{opt}}|\), where \(M_{\mathrm{opt}}\) is a maximum-cardinality stable matching. This is the paper’s proved \(\frac{3}{2}\)-approximation result. The paper also reports NP-hardness for max SPA-ST, but presents it as prior work, not as a numbered hardness theorem proved here, so I would not use it as a second anchor.

The mirror I would put to a prover is Max SPA-ST\(_\infty\) (\(\frac{3}{2}\)-approximation). An instance has a finite set \(P\) of projects, lecturers \(L\), and an offer map \(\lambda:P\to L\). It has a finite support \(T\) of student types, each with rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). A type records a complete student preference order, including ties and acceptable projects, and each lecturer’s preference tier for that type. Project and lecturer capacities \(\kappa_p\) and \(\delta_\ell\) are rational fractions of the population.

A solution is a nonnegative rational allocation \(x_{tp}\): the mass of type \(t\) assigned to project \(p\). It must assign each type at most its mass, respect every project and lecturer capacity, and assign mass only to acceptable projects. Each individual still receives at most one project; \(x_{tp}\) is fractional because the type’s population can be divided among different assignments.

For precision, define
\[ w_{tp}(x)=\mu_t-\sum_{q:\,q\succeq_t p}x_{tq}, \]
where \(q\succeq_t p\) means that type \(t\) regards \(q\) as at least as good as \(p\). Thus \(w_{tp}(x)>0\) means some positive mass of type \(t\) is unassigned or assigned strictly worse than \(p\). Call \((t,p)\) a blocking pair if \(w_{tp}(x)>0\) and the corresponding continuous version of one of the paper’s three capacity cases holds: \(p\) and \(\lambda(p)\) both have spare capacity; or \(p\) has spare capacity while its lecturer is full and either already holds mass of type \(t\) or strictly prefers \(t\) to its worst occupied preference tier; or \(p\) is full and its lecturer strictly prefers \(t\) to the worst occupied tier at \(p\). Ties remain nonblocking, as in the paper. The allocation is stable if it has no such pair.

The problem asks whether, in time polynomial in the number of projects, lecturers, supported types \(\tau=|T|\), and input bit length, one can return a stable allocation with total assigned mass \(\sum_{t,p}x_{tp}\) at least \(\frac{2}{3}\) of the maximum stable mass. This directly mirrors the paper’s objective and stability condition; it changes the population to a distribution and scales capacities with it. It does not make project outcomes or preferences continuous.

A plausible regime is large-cohort project allocation: tens of thousands of students choosing from a shared catalog, with repeated preference lists and lecturer assessments based on standardized profile or score categories. The complete type must include every lecturer’s ranking information, so any difference on either side splits a type. If that creates nearly one type per student, the proposed regime offers little high-multiplicity benefit. But in a structured cohort, hundreds of preference-and-assessment profiles among tens of thousands of applicants is credible. The paper itself studies SPA-ST at large synthetic instance sizes, and it explicitly permits ties in lecturer preferences.

I expect the \(\frac{3}{2}\)-approximation version to be tractable, perhaps by adapting the paper’s proposal process to operate on type masses and handling capacity thresholds with fractional flow. That is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 27: its algorithm and proof work with individual students, and the paper’s exact IP model has binary variables. The discrete NP-hardness cited in the introduction does not by itself settle the mass version, since fractional allocation within a type can change the optimum. Natural follow-up questions are whether an exact polynomial-time algorithm exists, whether the approximation guarantee survives fractional capacities and ties, and how to round a mass allocation for a finite cohort while preserving stability.

The weakest point is that type-level splitting makes stability a substantive modeling choice. The blocking definition above treats positive mass as enough for a deviation, using the paper’s capacity and preference cases. The authors should recognize the result as their problem’s high-multiplicity version if they accept that interpretation; if they require every type to move as an indivisible block, the mirror changes the question. The case rests on making that interpretation explicit, and on showing that the moderate-support cohort regime is real.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 27 is a difficult anchor to defeat: it is a genuine computational result about maximizing the size of a stable matching. The objective does not track student identities, and the SPA-ST model already allows lecturer ties. There is no sound objection that the paper lacks a result to mirror, that matching has no meaningful population regime, or that fractional capacities change the question beyond recognition.

The strongest skepticism is about whether its complete types stay few in a convincing large-cohort instance. A type must include a student’s full project ranking and that student’s preference tier at every lecturer. The paper’s experiments randomize these preferences; under that kind of instance, many students would have distinct types, so \(\tau\) would approach the cohort size. Grouping students by broad preference templates and lecturer assessment bands could keep \(\tau\) moderate, but the grouping must coincide across all those dimensions. That is a restrictive condition. Still, it is not an implausible one: a project-allocation system using standard preference templates and coarse assessment bands fits the paper’s tied-preference model, and the paper itself motivates ties by lecturers being unable to distinguish groups.

There is also a real gap between the theorem and the proposed mass problem. The theorem returns a stable matching of indivisible students; the mass version can split one type across projects. Its \(2/3\) guarantee would be relative to the best *fractional stable allocation*, and would not by itself guarantee a discrete stable allocation for a finite cohort. The paper already gives a linear-time algorithm that returns a concrete stable matching, so the continuous version has no obvious operational advantage unless it also yields a useful rounding or high-multiplicity dictionary. But that is a demand on the mirror’s payoff, not a reason the computational question is ill-posed.

The best case against is therefore that moderate support requires unusually aligned student preferences and lecturer assessments, while the theorem’s existing algorithm already handles large instances and returns implementable assignments. That makes this a less compelling mirror than a problem whose discrete algorithm is actually blocked by population size. It does not establish the universal negative, though. A category-based large-cohort SPA-ST model gives a coherent continuous maximum-stable-mass problem, and there is no existing continuous-population complexity result in the paper or in the material provided that answers it. I can make the negative case only as a question of priority and likely payoff; the stronger claim that no worthwhile mirror survives is not supported.
