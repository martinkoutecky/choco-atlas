The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that this paper already has a natural small type space. In a large three-option election with a standardized utility menu, let a voter’s type be their ranking and their utility for the middle-ranked option: \(T=\{(ABC,3),(ABC,8),(BCA,3),(BCA,8),(CAB,3),(CAB,8)\}\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those six types; a plausible regime has many voters and at most six distinct types. This preserves the paper’s preferences, plurality rule, information treatments, and strategic-voting question. Its laboratory electorate of 12, however, is not itself a high-multiplicity regime.

A possible continuous research question is Atomless Plurality QRE. Given \(\mu\), an information regime matching the paper’s prior-only, aggregate-ranking, or full-information treatments, and a logit noise level \(\eta>0\), find a type-symmetric ballot strategy \(x_{t,a}\) for every type \(t\) and ballot \(a\in\{A,B,C\}\), together with the resulting plurality shares and winner probabilities. Each type’s strategy maximizes expected utility from the winner, with logit response \(x_{t,a}\propto\exp(U_{t,a}/\eta)\). The authors would recognize this as their plurality/QRE model expressed over type masses.

The central difficulty is that in an atomless society one voter’s ballot changes no vote share. Thus \(U_{t,A}=U_{t,B}=U_{t,C}\), and at positive noise the logit solution is \(x_{t,a}=1/3\) for every type. The literal continuum problem is therefore tractable in a trivial sense, but erases the pivotality that generates strategic voting in the paper. A further question is whether a nontrivial limit can be obtained from finite electorates with \(n_t/N\to\mu_t\), using an explicit scaling of noise with pivotality. That would need additional modeling choices; its complexity class is open.

There are no qualifying anchors in this paper. Its numbered items are behavioral predictions (1)–(8) and experimental findings; none is a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational-complexity result. The reference to “Theorem 3” is to McKelvey and Palfrey’s cited work, not a result established here. So the candidate QRE problem is a plausible extension, not a mirror anchored in a named result of this paper. There is no per-anchor tractability or hardness-transfer claim to make.

My weakest point is also the decisive one: continuization removes individual influence on the outcome, while the paper’s strategic behavior depends on precisely that influence. The type distribution and large-election scenario are credible; a continuous model that preserves the paper’s main strategic phenomenon is not yet in hand.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper to anchor a mirror to. Its numbered items are behavioral predictions, and its formal analysis studies QRE for a 12-voter game. The “Theorem 3” it cites belongs to McKelvey and Palfrey’s work. Computing an atomless QRE is therefore a proposed extension, not a mirror of a named computational result.

The proposed six-type population is credible: rankings paired with intermediate-option utility give a small support, and large elections are a natural setting. The obstacle is that plurality incentives come from a voter’s chance of being pivotal. In an atomless population, changing one ballot changes no vote share. Given any type-level ballot strategy, each type gets the same expected payoff from all three ballots, even when the aggregate shares tie and the tie-break is random. At any fixed positive logit noise, the resulting response is \(x_{t,a}=1/3\) for every type and ballot. The model loses the strategic-voting behavior the paper investigates, and its information treatments no longer affect an individual’s incentives.

A richer limit could keep finite electorates of size \(N\) and study their QRE as type proportions approach \(\mu\). But pivotality then depends on \(N\): with a unique leading share, pivotal events become exponentially rare; near a tie, their probability is typically on the order of \(N^{-1/2}\). Fixed logit noise therefore erases those incentives as \(N\) grows. A nontrivial limit needs an additional choice, such as scaling noise with pivotality or adding a common shock. Different choices can yield different limiting behavior. Keeping \(N\) and its relation to noise makes this a finite-size asymptotics problem, rather than a computational question determined by the continuous society \(\mu\) alone.

Coalition or positive-mass deviations could restore influence, but they change the individual-voting game. Winner-robustness or minimum-mass manipulation questions would also be possible; they would import new computational problems that the paper does not study. The strongest negative case is therefore about fit: the paper’s contribution is behavioral and experimental, while a population-only continuum removes the strategic mechanism that motivates it. I cannot rule out a worthwhile study of the singular large-\(N\) QRE limit, but it needs substantial new modeling choices and is weakly anchored to this paper’s results.
