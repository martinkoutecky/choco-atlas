I can’t make an anchor-compliant case for a continuous computational mirror: this paper has no named computational result asserting an algorithmic or complexity classification.

The closest results are Theorem 1 (proved here), which says that each \(x_i\)—the combination of firms’ \(i\)-th-best matches and workers’ \(i\)-th-worst matches across the stable matchings—is itself stable, and Corollary 1 (proved here), which concludes that a median stable matching exists. Theorem 2 (proved here) establishes a unique median stable utility imputation under the specified Lebesgue-measure condition. Theorems 3 and 4 (proved here) establish convergence as the transfer increment \(\Delta\) tends to zero: respectively, convergence of the discrete median imputation to the continuous one, and Hausdorff convergence of the discrete core to the continuous core. These are existence and convergence results, not computational results.

The paper’s “continuous case” means continuous side transfers for a finite set of firms and workers. It does not make the population continuous. Theorem 2 is the strongest conceptual support for lifting the median-core idea to firm and worker types with masses, but that would be a new computational question, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. I therefore wouldn’t name a per-anchor problem or assign it a tractability class.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper offers no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 prove existence of median stable matchings using the lattice structure of a finite market. Theorem 2 proves existence of a median utility imputation using Lebesgue measure on the core. Theorems 3 and 4 prove convergence as the transfer increment \(\Delta\) tends to zero. None gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or an approximation result.

That remains true for the best population-based reinterpretation of each result. Theorem 1 concerns each named agent’s median partner among a finite set of stable matchings. With masses of firm and worker types, one could ask for median partner types or type-level match flows, but those are different objects: agents of one type might be split across several counterpart types, whereas the theorem assigns each agent a match. Theorem 2 already allows continuous transfers, but its population is still finite. Extending it to masses over utility types would require a new market model and a computational question about its core medians. Theorems 3 and 4 take a limit in transfer granularity with the sets of firms and workers fixed; they do not take a population limit.

The strongest proposed mirror is still plausible: model a large labor market as distributions over complete firm and worker types, use a matching flow between those types, and ask how to compute median stable utilities. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and I would not reject it because the paper already allows continuous wages or because high-multiplicity matching is implausible. But it is a new computational project built around the paper’s subject, not a continuization of one of its computational results. The paper proves no such result.

So the negative case succeeds against these anchors, but it does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile computational follow-up exists. The type-based matching question is a real weakness in that broader claim; its value would have to be judged as a new research direction rather than as a mirror of this paper.
