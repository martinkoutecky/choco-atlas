Why no mirror

Theorem 5.2 has computational character: its polynomial-positivity formulation is used to certify selected cases. But every proposed mirror leaves each auction with \(n\) atomic bidders and treats \(q,F\) as the same prior; describing that prior as cohort shares adds no mass-based computation. Putting a growing cohort into one auction changes the incentive question, so none of the anchors yields a surviving continuous-population mirror.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirrors cover Theorems 5.2, 3.1, and 4.2, but all retain the paper's finite-auction Bayesian model; none continuizes the population participating in an auction.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a cohort version of the paper’s Bayesian VCG model. Imagine an exchange running many standardized two-item auctions. Its large pool of eligible bidders consists of cohorts: a type \(t=(g,\theta)\) specifies demand \(g\in\{1,2\}\) and per-item value \(\theta\), along with any other feature relevant to bidding or utility. The mass \(\mu_t\) is that cohort’s fraction of the pool. There may be many bidders overall but relatively few distinct types. Each local auction draws \(n\) bidders from the pool; the two items, VCG allocation and payments, and each bidder’s ability to submit false-name bids remain as in the paper.

For a type \(t\), the decision is whether to report truthfully or submit a finite list of false-name single-minded bids. Its objective is expected utility: its true value for the items won by its bids, minus the payments charged to those bids, averaged over the other \(n-1\) entrants drawn from \(\mu\). The continuous problem is whether truthful reporting is a Bayesian Nash equilibrium for this population, or what demand-one mass \(q\) suffices to guarantee that it is. This is a high-multiplicity interpretation of the authors’ model: \(\mu\) represents actual cohort proportions across a large pool, while local auctions retain their discrete outcomes.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5.2, proved in the paper. It says that, for \(n\) bidders and a beta value distribution \(F_{\alpha,\beta}\), truthful bidding is a BNE exactly when two polynomials are nonnegative throughout their valid domains: \(P^n_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y,q)\) for a type-\(1\) bidder and \(Q^n_{\alpha,\beta}(\theta,x,y,q)\) for a type-\(2\) bidder. Here \(q\) is the mass share of demand-one types, and \((1,x),(1,y)\) are the two singleton bids considered in an attack. The paper then uses exact real-algebraic methods, including Partial CAD, to certify particular granularity thresholds. Some plotted cases are fully verified; others establish only the threshold for the split attack.

The corresponding question, Beta-Cohort Truthfulness Verification, is: given \(n,q,\alpha,\beta\), does the population with type distribution \(qF_{\alpha,\beta}\) on demand-one types and \((1-q)F_{\alpha,\beta}\) on demand-two types have truthful bidding as a BNE? A solution is an exact yes/no answer, certified by proving both polynomial inequalities over their domains, or by giving a type and attack that violates one. This is a good mirror because the authors’ object of study is precisely the incentives of a representative bidder facing a distribution over types; the mass interpretation gives that distribution a literal population meaning. I expect this restricted family to be tractable by exact algebraic methods when the number of parameters is fixed. The paper does not establish polynomial running time as \(n,\alpha,\beta\) vary, so I would leave that complexity question open.

Two other theorems give useful, distinct resilience questions. Theorem 3.1, proved in the paper, states that with \(n=2\), the global granularity threshold is
\[ q^*_{n=2}=\max_{g'\in\{1,2\}}q^*_{n=2,g=g'}=\max\{1/2,2/3\}=2/3. \]
In full, for every per-item value distribution \(F\), once the fraction \(q\) of demand-one bidders exceeds \(2/3\), truthful bidding is a BNE for either true demand and every allowed attack; the threshold is tight because the demand-two case has threshold \(2/3\), while the demand-one case has threshold \(1/2\). Its mirror, Two-Entrant Cohort Granularity Threshold, takes the common value law \(F\) and varies the cohort mass \(q\), asking for the least threshold above which truth is an equilibrium for all bidder types and attacks. The answer is \(2/3\), independent of \(F\). I would expect this question to go in the tractable, structural direction: the paper already gives a sharp distribution-independent answer. A natural next question is whether any comparable threshold survives when the conditional value laws for the two demand types differ.

Theorem 4.2, also proved in the paper, gives the contrasting multi-entrant result: for every \(q<1\) and every \(n>2\), there is an attack and a value distribution \(F\) for which the attack benefits a bidder whose true type is \((1,1)\); consequently, the global threshold is \(q^*_n=1\). The construction uses the attack \((1,1),(1,\tfrac12)\) and a distribution concentrated on \(\{\tfrac12,\tfrac35,1\}\), with sufficiently small positive mass on the extremes. Its mirror, Multi-Entrant Resilience Witness, asks: for each \(n>2\) and \(q<1\), does there exist a cohort distribution with demand-one mass \(q\) and a positive-mass type that benefits from a false-name attack? The answer is yes for every such pair. I expect this universal witness question to be resolved by a structural construction, as it is in the paper. It is a meaningful negative boundary for the mirror, not a hardness result. A further question is to find the boundary for restricted, natural value laws—especially beta or uniform populations—where the paper reports substantially lower thresholds.

These anchors cover Bayesian equilibrium and false-name resilience, not every contribution in the paper. They do not supply an NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity classification. The computational anchor is the polynomial-positivity characterization and its exact verification for selected parameter values. That still gives a prover a concrete question: verify truthful BNE for the stated type population, or exhibit a violating type and attack.

The weakest point is that the paper already puts a prior distribution over bidder types. The proposed mirror therefore does not replace an otherwise finite model with a continuum inside each individual auction; it treats that prior as the actual type composition of a high-multiplicity bidder pool spanning many auctions. If a referee insists that the only relevant “society” is the finite set of bidders in one auction, this would look like a reinterpretation of the prior rather than a new population model. I think the cohort regime is plausible, and the discrete local mechanism and the false-name incentives remain intact, but that boundary should be stated plainly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed mirrors have one problem in common: the paper’s \(q\) and \(F\) are already a prior over the types of the \(n\) bidders in a finite auction. The paper draws those bidders independently from that prior and computes a bidder’s expected utility over the other \(n-1\) draws. Calling the prior an actual pool of cohorts across many auctions does not change the game or its computational input. Sampling from a large finite pool converges to the same prior model; the pool’s mass shares never enter the auction itself. That makes the proposed cohort reading a reinterpretation of the paper’s uncertainty model, not a continuization of the society being studied. Under ChoCo’s scope, that is the wrong object.

This defeats the Beta-Cohort Truthfulness Verification anchor most directly. Theorem 5.2 already characterizes truthful BNE by positivity of \(P^n_{\alpha,\beta}\) and \(Q^n_{\alpha,\beta}\), and the paper applies exact algebraic methods to verify selected cases. Treating the beta law as a cohort distribution leaves the polynomial conditions unchanged. A broader complexity study of BNE verification as \(n\) or the distribution’s description grows might be worthwhile, but it would be a complexity extension of the paper’s Bayesian prior model; the cohort interpretation adds no population-mass computation.

The Two-Entrant Cohort Granularity Threshold has the same issue. Its \(q\) is the probability that an individual entrant has demand one. Relabeling \(q\) as the fraction of demand-one bidders in a large pool preserves precisely the paper’s two-entrant distribution and its equilibrium question. Asking whether the same threshold holds when \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) differ is a natural extension of the Bayesian model, but it still varies conditional priors, not a continuous society.

The Multi-Entrant Resilience Witness is an even closer collision. Theorem 4.2 already constructs a finite-support probability law and asks whether some type and attack yield a profitable deviation. Making the law cohort proportions leaves the sampled auction and the witness question intact. If instead all cohort members enter one auction, the model changes: with two fixed items, a fixed bidder’s chance of winning generally vanishes as the number of exchangeable bidders grows, so the utility comparisons central to the theorem collapse in the limit. Scaling the number of items with the population could preserve a nontrivial market, but that creates a many-item large-market VCG problem with different allocation and payment incentives. It does not rescue this theorem’s mirror.

The most plausible alternative is a population of bidders in a large marketplace. But dividing them among many two-item auctions returns to the existing prior interpretation; putting them together forces either vanishing individual stakes or a different, scaled-supply model. I cannot rule out a worthwhile large-market study of that kind. The negative case is weaker there: it could be interesting research, but it would be a new auction model rather than a continuous mirror of the paper’s named results.
