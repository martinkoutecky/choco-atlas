The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a valid positive case under the anchor rule: this paper contains no named computational result about complexity or algorithms. Its numbered results concern whether a social choice function can be implemented, not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest candidate is Theorem 4, proved here. It says that for an ordinal implementation problem whose domain consists of unanimity states plus one exceptional state, if the target selects the common top outcome at every unanimity state and, at the exceptional state, is second-best for two agents with different top outcomes, then a finite-action stochastic mechanism UD∞-implements the target. That is a substantive possibility result, but it is not a computational anchor under the requested standard. Theorem 2’s finite-action UD impossibility and Theorem 3’s two-outcome UD∞ impossibility are likewise implementation results, not complexity results.

A continuous population of people with a small number of preference rankings is imaginable. But to mirror these theorems, one must define how the distribution of messages determines the lottery and whether deviations by individuals, positive-mass groups, or entire preference blocs count. Those choices change the implementation question: in a nonatomic model, a single person’s deviation may not affect the aggregate outcome, while making a whole bloc the deviator turns that bloc into a strategic player. I don’t have a continuous question here that I can honestly present as the paper’s own computational problem. Theorem 4 may inspire one, but the paper supplies no qualifying anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest objection is that the paper has no named result about computational complexity or algorithms. Theorem 2 classifies finite-agent UD-implementation, Theorem 3 classifies a two-outcome UD∞ case, and Theorem 4 gives a finite mechanism for a particular UD∞ possibility result. Theorem 5 concerns infinite-action implementation. None specifies a computational problem, input encoding, algorithm, or complexity bound. So there is no computational result here for ChoCo to continuize.

Theorem 4 is the strongest candidate for a mirror, and its best lift would represent agents’ rankings by a distribution over preference types, with a non-unanimous distribution corresponding to the exceptional state. But the construction’s force comes from named individuals making unilateral deviations and having their strategies iteratively deleted. In a nonatomic population with an aggregate-report mechanism, one person’s report does not change the distribution or lottery. Their payoffs are therefore identical across reports, so strict-dominance deletion has nothing to remove. The central device of the theorem has disappeared in the limit.

One could make positive-mass preference blocs into players so their deviations affect the aggregate. That may be a sensible model of coordinated groups, but it changes the strategic unit from an individual to a coalition and adds assumptions about coordination and bloc preferences. Or one could study the complexity of deciding whether a finite mechanism exists, then ask for a continuous analogue. That might be worthwhile work, but it would introduce a computational problem the paper does not study; it would not mirror Theorem 4. The same gap applies to the impossibility results in Theorems 2 and 3.

This is a strong case against a continuous mirror *of this paper’s results*, though not proof that no adjacent project could be valuable. A separate study of continuous-player implementation or mechanism-synthesis complexity could be interesting. The paper itself gives ChoCo no computational anchor for it.
