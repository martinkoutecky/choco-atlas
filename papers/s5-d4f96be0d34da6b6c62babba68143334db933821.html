The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result such as membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. Its results concern voting properties—such as inconsistency and the no-show paradox—established through examples or attributed to earlier work. Those are axiomatic findings, which the programme puts outside scope. The paper’s winner computations are not stated as named complexity results.

There is a plausible continuous version of the paper’s central rule. Call it Population Anti-Manipulation Borda Winner. An instance consists of candidates \(C\) and a finite support \(S\) of complete rankings, each with rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t=1\). For Borda score \(s(t,c)\), compute the population mean \(b_\mu(c)=\sum_t\mu_t s(t,c)\), then each type’s Manhattan distance \(d_\mu(t)=\sum_c|s(t,c)-b_\mu(c)|\). Choose retention weights \(a_t\in[0,1]\) that discard \(20\%\) of the population by distance: retain types below the cutoff, discard those above it, and fractionally retain types tied at the cutoff so the discarded mass is exactly \(20\%\). Return a candidate maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t s(t,c)\). The weights are the rule’s decision variables; there is no cost-minimization objective.

A plausible regime would be a large recurring association election over a small candidate slate, where many members submit the same ranking, so the number of voters \(N\) greatly exceeds the number of distinct rankings \(\tau\). The rankings are complete types because the rule uses no other voter attributes. This is a credible population-scale version of the paper’s outlier-discounting rule, though it is far from the paper’s actual eleven-person jury example. Given explicit support, the winner is computable in polynomial time by calculating the \(\tau\) distances and sorting them. Further questions include how this limit relates to the paper’s finite rounding and tie rules, and how the selected winner changes under small changes in the population masses.

That is the strongest positive angle I can offer, but it is not anchored in a qualifying result of the paper. It is a natural computational question about extending the rule the paper describes; its polynomial-time solution follows directly from the explicit-support input. It does not turn the paper’s property comparisons into a ChoCo complexity result. A claimed hardness or tractability mirror of those comparisons would overstate what the paper establishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Its findings concern voting properties: inconsistency, no-show vulnerability, homogeneity, monotonicity, and Condorcet paradoxes, mostly demonstrated with profiles and counterexamples. It gives no complexity theorem, and it does not define a manipulation, control, or robustness problem for which one could ask for a continuous complexity counterpart. That is a decisive gap for calling a new question a mirror of this paper.

The proposed association-election scenario is plausible: many members can share rankings, and a distribution over those rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity instance. The mass version’s exact \(20\%\) cutoff and fractional treatment of ties are also reasonable model choices. But its winner computation follows directly from the new definition: calculate each supported type’s distance from the mean, sort the types, apply the cutoff, and score the candidates. This may be a useful description of the extended rule; it does not mirror a computational result established in the paper.

The proposed finite-rounding comparison and “how the winner changes under small changes in the masses” do not supply the missing anchor. The former asks how to relate two rule definitions. The latter needs a specified perturbation model, such as which mass transfers are allowed and what it costs to make them. A carefully defined mass-robustness problem could be worthwhile, but it would be a new problem built around the rule. The paper’s property examples do not formulate or analyze it.

I would therefore vote against counting this paper as a ChoCo mirror. The case is weaker against the broader claim that no useful continuous research question could ever be built from the method: the association scenario is credible, and a new robustness problem might merit study. I can’t honestly rule that out. What the paper itself offers, though, is axiomatic analysis rather than a computational result to mirror.
