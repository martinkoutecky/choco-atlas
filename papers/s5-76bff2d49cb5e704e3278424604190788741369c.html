The continuous mirror question

Given finite support \(T\) of equal-size, independent path communities, where type \(t\) has one opinion boundary at \(x_t\), a fixed status gradient with right-step probability \(a_t\), and rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute the expected fraction of residents who eventually hold opinion \(1\). For each type, \(p(x_t,a_t)=\frac{a_t^{x_t}(1-a_t)^{L-x_t}-a_t^L}{(1-a_t)^L-a_t^L}\); the aggregate is \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p(x_t,a_t)\).

The model it lives in

A continuous population of equal-size, independent path communities, represented by a finite-support distribution over types recording each path’s initial boundary and status-induced transition probability. The output is the mass-weighted expected terminal share of opinion \(1\).

The objection that survived

The paper does not itself formulate a distribution over independent network copies, so the repeated-community regime is a plausible extension rather than its explicit setup.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror covers Proposition III’s one-boundary path case; it leaves the state-space results, star-network analysis, and numerical Erdős–Rényi results outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can exact aggregation extend to path-community types with multiple opinion boundaries or nonuniform status gaps?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is around Proposition III (proved in this paper): for the HOpS model on a line, with one boundary between the two opinions and a fixed status difference between neighboring nodes, the dynamics reduce to a bounded asymmetric random walk. Starting from position \(x_0\), with right-step probability \(a\) and left-step probability \(1-a\), the proposition gives the probability of reaching the right boundary:
\[ p(x_0,a)=\frac{a^{x_0}(1-a)^{L-x_0}-a^L}{(1-a)^L-a^L}. \]
In the HOpS setting, \(a=0.5\tanh(\sigma\Delta st)+0.5\). This is an exact answer for the chance that the system ends in the all-black consensus state, under the proposition’s stated path and initial-condition restrictions. The paper proves the result here.

A continuous mirror could study Continuous HOpS Consensus Share. A type \(t\) is a local community of a fixed size \(L\), arranged on a path, with a single opinion boundary at \(x_t\) and a fixed status gradient inducing rational right-step probability \(a_t\). The instance gives finitely many such types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. Interpret these as shares of a large collection of equal-sized, independent communities. The question is: what fraction of the society is expected eventually to hold opinion \(1\)? A solution is the exact value
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\,p(x_t,a_t). \]
For explicitly represented paths and rational transition probabilities, Proposition III supplies each term, and weighted summation gives the aggregate. This is a tractable mirror of the proposition’s exact consensus-probability calculation. Further questions include what happens with several opinion boundaries, unequal status gaps, or more general network topologies.

The regime is many repeated local groups—such as many same-sized communities with similar internal communication structures—with far more residents than distinct group types. Treating each community as the unit whose mass is distributed is the proposal’s main co-modification: it preserves the paper’s internal network dynamics while making a population of repeated systems continuous. That makes the case plausible as an extension of the authors’ HOpS question, though weaker than a mirror that continuizes the individual nodes directly.

There is an important qualification: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Proposition III is a named exact computation result, but it does not classify a problem’s complexity. So this is the best positive mirror I can make from the paper’s strongest computationally useful result; it is not an anchored claim that the paper establishes a complexity boundary. The mirror also covers only the restricted path result, not the paper’s broader discussion of network dynamics, consensus times, or random graphs.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition III is the only credible anchor, but it is an exact first-passage result for a restricted finite Markov chain, not a complexity result for a computational social-choice problem. The paper asks analytic questions about consensus, convergence and network structure. It defines no voting rule, winner, intervention, or optimization task whose complexity a continuous mirror could chart.

The proponent’s strongest repair—many independent path communities, with types recording \(x_t\) and \(a_t\)—is a coherent high-multiplicity scenario. But it makes whole network components the population units and asks for their aggregate terminal opinion share. That is a new ensemble question, not a population version of a social-choice computation in the paper. A direct limit over the path’s individual nodes would instead change the stochastic dynamics and ask for a continuum limit of the random walk; that may be worthwhile analysis, but it remains outside ChoCo’s computational target.

One could add costs for changing opinions or statuses, or ask how to force a desired consensus. Those would create computational control problems, but the paper supplies no such problem or result to mirror. So the negative case is strongest on programme fit: Proposition III supports a plausible continuous-dynamics extension, but not a worthwhile ChoCo mirror of this paper’s results.
