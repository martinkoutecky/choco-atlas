What the mirror covers

The proposed anchors cover Theorems 8.1, 2.6, and 9.1; they leave the paper’s characterizations and other tangle results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no anchor that meets your computational-result requirement. The paper proves existence and nonexistence of fair allocations; it does not classify a problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or otherwise.

The closest results are Theorem 8.1 (proved here): every graph in the lips topological class \(G(L)\) guarantees a contiguous EF1\(_{\text{outer}}\) allocation for exactly three agents with monotone valuations; and Theorem 2.6 (proved here): every non-stringable tangle has a finite upper bound on the number of agents for which connected envy-free allocations are guaranteed. Theorem 9.1 (proved here) further says that for a topological class containing a non-Hamiltonian multigraph, sufficiently large numbers of agents defeat EF\(_k^{\text{outer}}\) guarantees for every \(k\), eventually across the class. These are substantive allocation guarantees, but none is a computational-complexity result.

Section 8 does give constructive content: Theorem 8.2, stated as implicit in Bilò et al. [3], describes a discrete moving-knife algorithm for three agents. It does not establish a running-time bound or complexity classification. Calling it an “in P” result would overstate what the paper proves.

The strongest positive seed is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many residents divide connected parcels on a growing subdivision of a fixed network, with many residents sharing a small number of complete valuation types. But the paper’s fixed three-agent theorem does not establish a result for that regime, and its large-agent results warn that fairness guarantees can fail there. Turning this seed into a precise continuous computational question would require new modeling choices about per-capita parcel supply and how type-level mass assignments lift to integral connected bundles for individuals. That would be a follow-up problem, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result here. The paper’s use of continuous tangles concerns the object being divided, not a continuous population, so it does not itself supply the needed mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that none of the anchors is a computational result about a population. Theorem 8.1 guarantees an \(\mathrm{EF1}_{\text{outer}}\) allocation for three agents on certain finite graphs. Theorems 2.6 and 9.1 characterize when fairness guarantees fail, using the topology of tangles and graph classes. Section 8 does give a discrete moving-knife procedure; even granting it an efficient implementation, its input is a finite graph and three recipients. The graph may have many subdivision vertices, but those are objects being divided, not a high-multiplicity society.

A plausible repair is to treat many people with the same valuation as a constituency. Then Theorem 8.1 can be read as allocating one connected region to each of three constituencies. But constituency masses play no role: changing their sizes leaves the envy comparisons and the three-recipient problem unchanged. To make mass matter, each constituency would need per-capita allocations and fairness comparisons between individuals’ parcels. Aggregating a constituency into one mass and one parcel loses those individual constraints; keeping them requires a new distribution-over-parcels model. That may be a worthwhile extension, but it is not a continuous-population question supplied by this theorem.

The same gap remains for Theorems 2.6 and 9.1. One could ask how to compute a tangle’s agent bound or generalized gap threshold. That would be a plausible structural algorithm problem, but its input is the tangle and its output concerns a finite number of agents—not a distribution over agent types. Treating the agent count as continuous would also discard the finite-agent envy question these results address.

So the negative case is strong against these results as ChoCo anchors: their continuity is in the allocated space, and their contributions concern allocation existence, not computational complexity over a continuous society. It is weaker as a universal claim. A per-capita model for large constituencies sharing a growing network could be meaningful, but it would need its own fairness definition and computational question; this paper does not establish that such a model is a mirror its authors would recognize.
