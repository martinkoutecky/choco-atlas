The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot build the requested case around an eligible anchor. The paper has three numbered results, Propositions 1–3, and proves them here. They characterize weakly undominated Nash equilibria in a five-agent game; none asserts a computational result such as a problem being in P, NP-hard, or fixed-parameter tractable. In particular, Proposition 2 says that with conformity, truthful voting is an equilibrium for each threshold, alongside specified untruthful equilibria. That is an equilibrium characterization, not a complexity result.

There is a plausible continuous object behind the model: a large electorate with two complete voter types, \(A\) and \(B\), sharing the paper’s outcome preferences and conformity incentives. A society would be a distribution over those types, and the decision variable could be the mass of each type voting for each option; a threshold fraction would determine the outcome. One could ask for an equilibrium of that aggregate game. But moving from five potentially pivotal voters to a continuum of individually negligible voters changes the strategic problem. And equilibrium computation is not a result this paper establishes. Presenting it as a mirror of a named computational result would overstate the case.

So the paper offers a conceivable starting point for a continuous-population equilibrium-computation question, but no eligible anchor for the requested continuization case. The weakness is decisive: the paper’s named results concern equilibrium behavior in a fixed, finite game, not the computational complexity of a problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that Propositions 1–3 are not computational results: they characterize weakly undominated Nash equilibria in a game with five agents. The experiment estimates voting behavior. Neither supplies a computational problem whose complexity the paper studies.

The strongest proposed mirror is a large electorate with two voter types, a fractional quota, and the paper’s conformity payoff. In an atomless population, one voter’s ballot changes neither the outcome nor the mass supporting either option. If both ballot choices have positive mass, a voter can choose either and still match a positive mass of others, so conformity gives no reason to choose one over the other. If everyone chooses the same option, an individual deviation changes neither the outcome nor the aggregate masses and loses the conformity bonus. The finite game’s pivotality and its rule-dependent incentives therefore disappear. An equilibrium characterization might still be written down, but with two actions and an aggregate quota it reduces to simple conditions on vote shares, not a computational landscape.

Keeping pivotality requires preserving finite voters or positive-mass blocs. That retains strategic atoms rather than continuizing the population. Taking a continuum over repeated five-person committees instead gives a distribution of outcomes from independent finite games; it can summarize experimental frequencies, but adds no computational problem. Adding aggregate uncertainty or changing conformity to depend on the population share could produce a different continuous game. The paper gives no algorithmic target for that game, and with its two types and two options, aggregate equilibrium computation remains small unless substantial new structure is introduced.

The case against is strongest at the mechanism’s core: the paper’s findings rely on individual voters being pivotal and on matching at least one other voter. The proposed continuous population removes the first feature and makes the second automatic for positive-mass vote groups. A worthwhile computational question could be supplied from outside the paper, but it would need a new target as well as a new model.
