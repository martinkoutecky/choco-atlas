The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive angle is a plausible high multiplicity extension of the allocation model, but the paper gives no qualifying named computational anchor. Its only numbered result, Theorem 1, is proved here and characterizes \(f^{CEA,P}\): when \(|N|\ge3\), it is the unique rule satisfying FCA, CMA, and NATI; it is also the unique rule satisfying CD, FCA, and NATI. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a result placing a computational problem in a complexity class or giving a computational formulation. The paper computes examples using the rule, but that does not turn Theorem 1 into the kind of anchor requested.

A natural continuous allocation question would take types \(t\) to be complete claim vectors \(a_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^r\), with masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to one. Let \(e\) be the estate per claimant and \(q_k=\sum_t\mu_t a_{kt}\) the aggregate per-capita claim on issue \(k\). The task is to compute each type’s per-capita allocation \(z_{kt}\) under the paper’s mixed rule: choose \(\lambda\) such that \(\sum_k\min\{\lambda,q_k\}=e\), then set \(z_{kt}=(a_{kt}/q_k)\min\{\lambda,q_k\}\) when \(q_k>0\), and zero otherwise. A plausible regime is a large system of standardized public agencies or institutions, with many claimants but relatively few distinct claim profiles. The rule can be evaluated from the support and masses using rational arithmetic, so this extension looks tractable.

That is a sensible population-level allocation object, but it is not a computational finding of this paper, and computing the rule is not the paper’s central research question. The authors study finite-agent axioms and applications; their Caspian example has five countries and their university example has fifteen universities. My weakest point is therefore the key one: although the extension seems recognizable as the same allocation rule applied to claimant types, the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor it, and its examples do not establish a high multiplicity regime. I would not present this as a compliant anchored case for the programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper gives no computational result to mirror. Its sole numbered theorem characterizes \(f^{CEA,P}\) by axioms; the applications calculate allocations for two finite examples. Neither supplies a complexity question, algorithmic formulation, or bound. The theorem’s continuous analogue would itself be another axiomatic characterization, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The proposed type-profile extension is a sensible high-multiplicity allocation model. For types with claim vectors \(a_t\) and masses \(\mu_t\), compute each issue’s aggregate claim \(q_k=\sum_t\mu_t a_{kt}\), find \(\lambda\) from \(\sum_k\min\{\lambda,q_k\}=e\), then allocate each type its proportional share of each issue. Scaling the estate per claimant keeps the allocation meaningful as the population grows. Standardized agencies or institutions could plausibly share claim profiles, so the paper’s small examples are not a sound objection to the regime.

But computing that allocation is just evaluating the rule the paper defines: the main calculation uses the aggregate claims, and the resulting issue amounts are distributed by the stated proportions. A stronger-sounding mirror—such as asking how claim changes affect allocations—would add a new decision problem, objective, and constraints that the paper never studies. It might be worthwhile research, but it would not be a computational mirror of this paper’s results.

That is a substantial negative case, not proof that no useful continuous question could ever be attached to the model. A credible high-multiplicity setting exists; what is missing is a paper-level computational problem for the continuization programme to classify.
