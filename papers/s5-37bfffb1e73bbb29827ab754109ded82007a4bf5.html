There is still a plausible future mirror in the framework. Consider a large tax-policy setting with millions of households but a moderate number \(\tau\) of complete economic types, such as combinations of preferences, income, productivity, and information. Give each type rational mass \(\mu_t\); let the principal choose policy parameters \(\phi\), and let each type use a type-contingent Markov policy. A concrete research problem would be to compute a principal-optimal \(\phi\), subject to the paper’s policy-divergence constraint, together with a mean-field Nash equilibrium of the resulting population and its induced type-level mass flows. This follows the paper’s inner Stackelberg design problem and its stated scaling concern, while making the population the continuous object. But it is a proposed problem, not a result the authors establish. Its complexity and even its place in the trichotomy remain open; the seven-agent, locally interacting apple-picking example is not evidence that the high-multiplicity version has already been captured.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper to mirror. Its numbered statements define a framework; the complexity and scaling claims in Related Work belong to cited papers, and the paper presents its own computational questions as open. So the proponent has no result-level anchor. Their proposed mean-field problem is a possible new research direction, not a continuous restatement of something this paper proves.

The strongest version of that proposal would specify finitely many agent types, states and actions, rational model data, and anonymous interactions that depend on the population distribution. One could then ask for a principal-optimal policy subject to a mean-field equilibrium response. That is a coherent computational problem, and a large anonymous tax economy could make it plausible. But those assumptions do most of the modeling work: the paper permits general POMGs, arbitrary policy-implementation maps and objectives, while its concrete apple-picking example has local interactions and agents’ rewards depend on their neighbors. A faithful type description there would have to account for position, neighborhood and evolving state; the number of relevant types need not stay moderate. The finite anonymous model is therefore a substantial restriction of the framework, not a mirror compelled by its results.

The paper’s other natural route, voting on welfare objectives, is too underspecified to supply a stronger anchor. It leaves the voting rule open; its apple example simply maps average selfishness to a welfare weight. Making that a nontrivial high-multiplicity voting problem requires choosing a preference model, ballot space and rule that the paper does not provide. A literal continuous version of the example may reduce to computing an average, while a richer voting problem would be newly introduced.

That makes the negative case strong as an assessment of this paper: it offers no computational result to continuize, and its only promising computational mirror needs substantial new structure before it becomes a finite, encoded problem. It does not prove the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. An anonymous economy with a carefully specified mean-field model could still be worthwhile; the honest objection is that it would be a new formalization motivated by the paper, rather than a mirror of a result in it.
