What the mirror covers

The Strong Market Opportunity Theorem and Opportunity Convergence Theorem characterize regimes; the Existence Theorem proves existence under assumptions, and Proposition 1 gives a counterexample. None supplies a computational result for a continuous mirror to cover.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive clue is the paper’s own replication setup. In an \(r\)-fold replica, the population grows from \(n\) to \(rn\), while each person’s endowment and choice function are copied unchanged. Treating those replicated people as masses of complete types—where a type includes both the endowment and the choice function—would give fixed support as population grows. A continuous-population model of market-clearing regimes and the Strong Opportunity Criterion would therefore be recognizable to the authors as a natural extension of their setting.

The required computational anchors are absent, though. The Strong Market Opportunity Theorem and Opportunity Convergence Theorem are economic characterizations; the Existence Theorem establishes equilibrium under stated assumptions; Proposition 1 gives a counterexample. None asserts that an underlying problem is tractable, hard, or has another computational complexity classification, and the paper formulates no algorithmic problem. So there is no admissible anchor from which to give the required per-anchor computational question or predict its complexity.

My honest positive case is limited to this: the population mirror is plausible because replication is already central to the paper. A computational continuation could study that model, but it would be a new research question rather than a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a *mirror* is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. The Strong Market Opportunity Theorem and the Opportunity Convergence Theorem characterize regimes; the Existence Theorem proves existence under assumptions; Proposition 1 gives a counterexample. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. There is therefore no named result here with a corresponding continuous complexity question of the kind ChoCo is set up to study.

Replication is good evidence that a continuous-population version is sensible. It does not supply the missing computational problem. In the paper, replication copies each person’s endowment and choice function, and the convergence theorem asks what follows as the number of copies grows. But those choice functions are unrestricted mappings over opportunity sets, and the regimes themselves are arbitrary sets of acquisition vectors. The paper gives no finite encoding or evaluation procedure for either. Treating \((e_i,f_i)\) as a type preserves the model’s meaning, but does not make its types computable inputs. Without further structure, a running time in \(\tau\) and \(L\) has nothing to measure.

The best nearby computational question would be to find market-clearing prices or check the Strong Opportunity Criterion in a finitely represented, finite-type economy. To pose either one, however, we would have to choose a representation for choices and opportunity sets—say, a restricted family of rational polyhedra or an explicit demand oracle. That could support worthwhile research, but its computational content would come from those added restrictions. It would be a new computational economics model, not a computational mirror of a result in this paper. The paper’s behavioral point is precisely that choices may be context-dependent and need not come from coherent preferences, so replacing the unrestricted choice functions with a convenient representation also risks changing what the model was designed to capture.

That is the limit of the negative case. Replication defeats a claim that no natural continuous society exists for this setting. It does not establish that a computational continuation would be worthwhile, but the absence of an anchor cannot establish that no such continuation is worth pursuing. The case against a direct ChoCo mirror is strong; the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile computational question remains weak.
