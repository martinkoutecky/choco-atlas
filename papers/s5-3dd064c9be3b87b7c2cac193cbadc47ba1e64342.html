What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror targets only Theorem 3’s quadratic global-targeted incentive guarantee; it leaves Theorems 1–2, general-loss tuning, and the empirical evaluation untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor test rules out a complexity-theoretic anchor: no numbered result in this paper says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Theorems 1–3 concern strategyproofness. In particular, Theorem 1 is a failure of strategyproofness, not a hardness result. I can’t honestly present any of them as a qualifying complexity anchor.

The strongest nearby result for a positive mirror is Theorem 3, proved in this paper in Appendix E. It studies the objective
\[ \mathcal L(\rho\mid\theta^\spadesuit,S) =\|\rho-\theta^\spadesuit\|_1+\rho^\top S\rho, \qquad S\succ0, \]
where \(\theta^\spadesuit\) is a user’s reported model. It proves that Licchavi is \(\operatorname{Crooked}(S)\)-strategyproof: for every true model \(\theta^\dagger\) and every report \(\theta^\spadesuit\), the distance from the outcome under an honest report to \(\theta^\dagger\) is at most \(1+\operatorname{Crooked}(S)\) times the distance under the strategic report, with
\[ \operatorname{Crooked}(S) =\sup_{x\in\mathbb R^d} \inf_{\substack{y\in\mathbb R^d\\ \operatorname{sgn}(y)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)}} \frac{\|x\|_2\|Sy\|_2}{x^\top Sy}-1. \]
This is a mechanism-design result, not a complexity classification. It is nevertheless the best anchor for a continuous-population question because Section 4 already studies the large-user limit and introduces the expected reduced loss \(\bar R\).

A plausible regime is a large online platform learning global content-moderation parameters. Users fall into a relatively small number \(\tau\) of complete types: users of a type share a preferred model, query-answer process, local-loss family, and data-generation law. The type mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the background population in that cohort; the number of users is large compared with \(\tau\). Realized datasets vary, but the cohort’s limiting reduced loss is the same. A single strategic user remains distinguished, with the unit influence the paper assigns to an individual.

Here is a precise mirror of Theorem 3’s quadratic case. An instance consists of \(d\), a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a background-to-individual scale \(\lambda>0\), positive semidefinite rational matrices \(S_t\) whose aggregate \(S=\lambda\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tS_t\) is positive definite, a true model \(\theta^\dagger\), and a bound \(\alpha\ge0\). For report \(r\), the global model is
\[ \rho_{\mu,\lambda}(r) =\arg\min_{\rho\in\mathbb R^d} \left\{\|\rho-r\|_1+\rho^\top S\rho\right\}. \]
The question is whether
\[ \|\rho_{\mu,\lambda}(\theta^\dagger)-\theta^\dagger\|_2 \le (1+\alpha)\|\rho_{\mu,\lambda}(r)-\theta^\dagger\|_2 \quad\text{for every }r\in\mathbb R^d. \]
A solution is “yes,” or “no” together with a violating report and its resulting model. Theorem 3 says “yes” whenever \(\alpha\ge\operatorname{Crooked}(S)\). Its report \(r\) is the user’s strategic model report; the paper’s reduction from data attacks to model attacks supplies the connection to actual reported datasets under its stated assumptions.

For this quadratic, finite-support version, I expect the computational question to be tractable: each fixed-report outcome is a convex quadratic optimization problem, and the theorem gives a structural certificate for a uniform incentive bound. The exact tight bound and the complexity of evaluating \(\operatorname{Crooked}(S)\) remain open subquestions; the paper itself notes that it lacks a closed-form expression. I would not assign a complexity class to the broader version with general type-level reduced losses from this paper alone.

The mirror covers only global-targeted manipulation in the asymptotic quadratic regime. The authors should recognize it as their problem: it preserves the strategic user, the global model, the reporting action, the Euclidean-distance objective, and their reduced-loss analysis. The paper already moves toward an expected population loss, so the contribution here would be the complexity landscape for explicit type-distribution inputs, not the claim that taking a large-population limit is new.

My weakest point is that a literal atomless population gives an individual zero influence. Keeping a distinguished unit-force user is necessary to preserve the paper’s unilateral strategyproofness question, and a referee might view that tagged-user model as a hybrid rather than a fully continuous society. The finite-cohort scenario also covers a particular regime, not the paper’s entire empirical setting. If the mirror must make every agent atomless and remove the tagged user, the individual-incentive problem changes and this positive case fails.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the strongest anchor, but its result is an incentive guarantee for an abstract quadratic objective, not a computational result about a society. The proposed decision problem—whether the guarantee holds for every report—can be defined, but it is added by the mirror. The paper does not study the complexity of checking strategyproofness, and it gives no reason that this verification problem is a natural computational task for its authors. Its direct computational task is finding the loss minimizer, which is a convex optimization problem whose difficulty depends on how the losses are represented.

The type distribution in the proposed quadratic mirror also contributes no distinct computational structure. If type \(t\) has quadratic reduced loss with matrix \(S_t\), the aggregate objective depends on the population only through \(S=\lambda\sum_t\mu_tS_t\). Any two type distributions giving the same \(S\) produce the same incentive question. The support size \(\tau\) disappears; the proposed open problem is evaluating \(\operatorname{Crooked}(S)\), a matrix geometry problem. That could be worthwhile research, but its substance does not come from continuizing the population.

There is a more fundamental mismatch in the proposed scenario. With \(N-1\) background users and one strategic user, the finite objective has the form
\[ \|\rho-r\|_1+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}R_i(\rho). \]
After normalizing the background to unit mass, the strategic user’s term has weight \(1/(N-1)\). In the continuum limit, an individual has zero mass, so the outcome no longer depends on their report. Keeping the coefficient of \(\| \rho-r\|_1\) fixed while the background becomes a continuum preserves a finite individual influence that the population model itself has removed. A positive-mass strategic type can retain influence, but then the question concerns a group or coalition. Its incentives are not the unilateral strategyproofness result in Theorem 3. One could study first-order response to an infinitesimal report; that is a plausible sensitivity question, but it is a rescaled limit of finite-population influence, not the exact continuous-society outcome.

The broader version using type-specific reduced losses faces the same choice. If each individual deviation is unilateral, its effect vanishes with its mass. If an entire type mass changes its report, the problem becomes coalition manipulation. If the type data are summarized by expected losses, the paper already takes that analytic step in Section 4: it defines \(\bar R\) from a distribution of datasets and derives the quadratic asymptotic setting behind Theorem 3. Adding explicit rational type weights would make that representation useful for computation only if a new algorithmic task were supplied; it does not itself create one.

This is a case against assigning the mirror to ChoCo, not a proof that no related project could be worthwhile. The matrix-bound problem may deserve study on its own, and a finite-population sensitivity analysis could be valuable. The weakness in the case against is that the verification question is well-posed and could reveal a genuine complexity boundary. But its population role is either erased by aggregation or lost in the atomless limit, while the faithful unilateral incentive question is already handled analytically in the paper’s asymptotic section.
