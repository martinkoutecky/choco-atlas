The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of data categories \(K\), distances \(d\), a finite support \(T\) of buyer types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), type parameters \(b_t,w_{tq},r_{tq}\), and per-capita supplies \(s_j\), define \(C_t(S)=\sum_{q\in K}w_{tq}\mathbf{1}[\exists j\in S:\ d(q,j)\le r_{tq}]\) for each integral bundle \(S\subseteq K\). Choose masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq K}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le s_j\) for each category. What is the complexity of maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq K}x_{t,S}b_tC_t(S)\) in \(|K|\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the input bit length?

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.3's exact welfare-allocation question. It leaves aside the demand-oracle hardness, approximation algorithms, truthfulness and payment results, data burning, and experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does exact welfare optimization remain NP-hard for fixed \(\tau\) when mass can split across bundles and category supplies scale per capita?
  • Can the configuration LP be optimized efficiently for useful restrictions on distances, radii, or weights?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is on the buyer population. Imagine a large market of RAG deployments buying data from a seller: many local, enterprise, or industry-specific systems, but relatively few recurring procurement profiles. A type \(t\) records everything relevant about a buyer: its bid, its point weights \(w_{tq}\), and its coverage radii \(r_{tq}\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of buyers with that complete profile. A plausible regime has \(N\gg\tau\): perhaps thousands of deployments and tens of distinct profiles.

To make that regime meaningful while retaining exclusive allocation, the data supply must scale with the market. Each content category has a fixed per-capita supply \(s_j\) of distinct records; in a finite market of \(N\) buyers, there are \(Ns_j\) such records, and each individual record can still go to at most one buyer. These records can be similar in the seller’s distance model without being the same record. This scaling is a co-modification of the supply, not the point of continuization.

My anchor is Theorem 3.3, proved in this paper using reductions from dominating set and domatic number. It establishes that exact welfare maximization is NP-hard in either of two restricted cases: (i) there are two bidders, and each bidder’s radius is constant across data points, \(r_{ij}=r_i\), though the two bidders’ radii may differ; or (ii) there are three bidders, and all bidders and data points have the same radius, \(r_{ij}=r\). The paper also notes that the hardness reductions work with constant point weights. I rely on the theorem’s underlying welfare-allocation problem, not on its mechanism-design results.

I would call the continuous problem Continuous RAG Coverage Welfare. Its instance consists of a finite set \(K\) of data-point categories, their distance data \(d\), a finite support \(T\) of buyer types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita supplies \(s_j\), and rational buyer parameters \(b_t,w_{tq},r_{tq}\). Here \(b_t\) is the reported bid held fixed for the welfare calculation, as in the paper’s allocation problem. Each buyer still receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq K\), with coverage

\[ C_t(S)=\sum_{q\in K} w_{tq}\, \mathbf{1}\!\left[\exists j\in S:\ d(q,j)\le r_{tq}\right]. \]

The decision variable \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) is the mass of type-\(t\) buyers who receive bundle \(S\). A feasible allocation satisfies

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq K}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{for each }t, \qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le s_j \quad\text{for each }j. \]

The objective is to maximize

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq K}x_{t,S}\,b_t C_t(S). \]

The problem is to compute an optimal allocation, or decide whether its value is at least a given rational threshold. Its complexity should be studied in \(|K|\), support size \(\tau=|T|\), and rational input bit length \(L\). This is a genuine mass-allocation model: individuals get whole bundles and each record is exclusive; only the measure of buyers receiving each bundle is fractional. The two theorem restrictions have natural counterparts at \(\tau=2\) and \(\tau=3\).

I would initially expect Class B, hardness transfers, and would first try to establish hardness with \(\tau\) fixed at two or three and constant weights. The theorem’s reductions place their combinatorics in the neighborhood structure of the data points, not in a large number of bidders. That makes this a better candidate for surviving high multiplicity than a problem whose hardness depends on fine-grained differences among many individuals. A key further question is whether the configuration LP over bundle masses admits efficient pricing for any useful restrictions on the distance or radius structure.

The mirror covers Theorem 3.3’s exact welfare-allocation question. It does not claim a continuous version of the paper’s truthfulness, payment, or data-burning results.

The soft spot is that Theorem 3.3 does not prove hardness of this continuous problem. Splitting a type’s mass across different bundles may convexify away the discrete reduction, even when \(\tau\) is small. Also, the high-multiplicity scenario requires supply to grow with the buyer population; if the authors regard the database as a fixed collection of unique records, they may reject that regime. I still think the question is worth asking because it retains the paper’s coverage valuation and exclusive record allocation, while directly testing whether mass-based allocation removes the graph structure that made the finite problem hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.3 is a genuine computational anchor: it proves welfare maximization NP-hard with only two or three bidders, and its reductions use the data points’ neighborhood structure. So the case against cannot rest on the paper lacking a computational result or on its hardness transferring to the continuous version. Neither would show that asking the question is pointless.

The strongest objection is that the paper’s natural bidders are a small set of distinct organizations, not a population with many repeated members. A bidder’s full type includes its bid and its weight and radius for every data point. Although the bid comes from a finite set, those high-dimensional profiles may be different for nearly every platform. In that regime, the support size \(\tau\) grows with the number of buyers, so aggregating them gives little high-multiplicity benefit. The paper’s experiments, with two or three buyers, fit that interpretation.

The proposed large market of standardized RAG deployments is the best reply, but it needs a co-scaling of the seller’s catalogue. With a fixed database, each record can go to at most one buyer; as the buyer population grows, the share receiving any nonempty bundle tends to zero. Scaling supply with the population avoids that collapse, but changes the setting into a replicated market with growing per-capita supply. Whether those additional records preserve the paper’s intended exclusivity and coverage economics is a substantive modelling question, not an automatic consequence of having many buyers.

That objection does not defeat the proposed mirror, though. The programme explicitly permits supply to scale when population continuization requires it, and a shared seller serving many standardized deployments is plausible. Nor does the fact that the reductions’ combinatorics lie in the data points invalidate the question: the mirror still asks how mass allocation over buyer types interacts with that structure. Splitting each type’s mass across bundles might remove the finite hardness, and finding out would be a legitimate computational result.

So the negative case is weak at its hardest point. It casts doubt on whether this is a natural regime for the paper’s motivating market, but I cannot honestly rule out the standardized, expanding-market scenario or show that its continuous welfare problem is unworthy of study.
