What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns winner determination under the paper’s scoring rules; it does not cover the paper’s representation-theoretic decompositions, profile-realizability results, or analysis of voting systems.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is a population version of the paper’s points-based winner problem: voter types are complete rankings of the structured committees, mass is each ranking type’s share of the electorate, and the task is to find a score-maximizing committee. A large organization with recurring stakeholder groups could plausibly have many voters but relatively few distinct rankings.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor that mirror. Theorem 4.5, proved here, says that under its linear-independence conditions, infinitely many profiles in each ranking orbit produce any specified collection of zero-sum result vectors. Proposition 3.3 characterizes neutral rules using \(n+1\) parameters. Neither result establishes an algorithm, a complexity classification, or a hardness result for a computational problem. Theorem 4.5 also concerns a vector-space profile setting, not directly a distribution of nonnegative population masses.

So the winner-determination mirror is a plausible new question, but it cannot meet your required anchor standard. I would not present it as a case grounded in this paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s winner-determination idea is the only computational anchor, and it fails the stated anchor test: this paper proves no algorithmic or complexity result. Theorem 4.5 is a realizability statement about profiles and result vectors; Proposition 3.3 characterizes neutral rules algebraically. Neither studies the cost of computing a winner.

The strongest population version would give each complete ranking of committees a rational mass and ask which committee maximizes aggregate score. That is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario for a large organization with recurring stakeholder groups, so I cannot rule it out by claiming these voters must be unique. But it adds a computational task the paper never analyzes. A more direct population reading of Theorem 4.5 would ask which result vectors are attainable by nonnegative distributions. That replaces its signed-vector statement with a convex-range question; making it a complexity problem requires choosing a new input and decision or optimization task. It may be worth studying, but it is not a computational result from this paper being mirrored.

So the negative case is strong on the narrow point that this paper offers no named computational result to continuize. It is weak as a universal claim: plausible continuous populations exist, and an independent computational project could grow from the paper’s algebra. I would reject the paper as an anchor for a continuous-complexity mirror, while stopping short of claiming that no worthwhile related project can be formulated.
