The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly under the programme’s anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1, Theorems 2–3, Corollaries 1–2, Proposition 1, Theorem 4, and Theorem 5 are structural results about manipulability, strategy-proofness, dictatorship, and strategic equivalence. None states that a decision or optimization problem is in P, NP-hard, W[1]-hard, or similar. In particular, Theorem 2 is an impossibility theorem, not a complexity-hardness theorem, and Proposition 1 is a property of premise-based voting, not an algorithmic result. I would not mislabel any of them.

The strongest honest positive case is therefore a non-complexity mirror of Theorem 5, proved in this paper. Theorem 5 says that, on a conjunctive or disjunctive agenda, premise-based and conclusion-based voting are strategically equivalent on \(Y_{\mathrm{outcome}}=\{c,\neg c\}\) for outcome-oriented preferences: for every profile and every compatible preference profile, each rule has a weakly dominant-strategy profile, and the two resulting collective judgments agree on \(c\) and \(\neg c\).

A natural high-multiplicity regime is a large consultation or referendum on a fixed logical agenda. A type \(t\) consists of a complete consistent judgment set \(A_t\), together with an outcome-oriented preference relation. The society is \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of respondents of type \(t\), with \(n\gg |T|\). This is plausible whenever millions of participants repeatedly instantiate a finite collection of judgment and motivational patterns.

I would state the mirror as follows.

Outcome-Equivalence\(_\infty\). Given a conjunctive or disjunctive agenda, a finite type set \(T\), and a distribution \(\mu\), compute:

  • a mass-report distribution \(\nu^{\mathrm{PB}}\) induced by the paper’s canonical weakly dominant reports under premise-based voting;
  • the truthful mass-report distribution \(\nu^{\mathrm{CB}}=\mu\) under conclusion-based voting;
  • the resulting collective judgment on \(\{c,\neg c\}\).

For a conjunctive agenda, types with \(c\in A_t\) submit the canonical report accepting every premise and \(c\), while types with \(\neg c\in A_t\) submit the corresponding rejecting report. The continuous witness is

\[ \nu^{\mathrm{PB}}=r_{\#}\mu, \qquad \nu^{\mathrm{CB}}=\mu, \]

and both rules produce \(c\) exactly when

\[ \sum_{t:c\in A_t}\mu_t>\frac12. \]

Thus the continuous problem asks for the common outcome and the two dominant-strategy mass profiles. It is a genuine population mirror: individual counts are replaced by type masses, while the logical agenda, strategic preferences, and aggregation rules remain intact. For explicit \(T\), it is plainly tractable—essentially a finite collection of weighted sums—so I would expect a Class A answer.

The mirror covers only Theorem 5. It does not support a claim about the paper’s dictatorship impossibility. In a nonatomic population, one individual has zero influence, so individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous; preserving the force of Theorems 1–3 requires replacing individual deviations by positive-mass bloc deviations, which is a substantive co-modification. Nor does this paper establish a complexity landscape: the tractability claim above is a new observation about the mirror, not a result of Dietrich and List.

That is the weakest point and also the reason this is only a modest positive case. The paper supplies a clean, plausible continuous identity, but no named computational result for the ChoCo programme to anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper offers no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1, Theorems 2–3, Corollaries 1–2, Proposition 1, Theorem 4, and Theorem 5 are axiomatic or game-theoretic statements about aggregation rules. None defines a computational problem or proves a complexity, approximation, or algorithmic result. Theorem 2 is an impossibility theorem about dictatorship, not computational hardness. Thus the paper does not naturally enter ChoCo’s complexity programme.

The proposed Theorem 5 mirror does not repair that omission. Its “continuous problem” is only the pointwise lifting of a finite identity. For each judgment type \(A_t\), the canonical premise-based report is a deterministic report \(r(A_t)\) determined essentially by whether \(c\in A_t\). Replacing a finite profile by a distribution merely gives

\[ \nu^{\mathrm{PB}}=r_{\#}\mu \]

and the conclusion is determined by the single scalar

\[ p=\sum_{t:c\in A_t}\mu_t. \]

Both rules accept \(c\) exactly when \(p>\frac12\). Nothing is optimized, searched, approximated, or parameterized; there is no high-multiplicity relaxation whose computational structure needs investigation. The finite theorem already implies the formula for every empirical distribution, and passing from counts to masses adds only notation.

The consultation scenario is not implausible, and that is the strongest point for the proponent. Millions of respondents may indeed repeat a finite set of judgment and motivational patterns. But that establishes only that a continuous representation is possible, not that it is worthwhile for this programme. In the proposed representation, all structure except the mass supporting \(c\) disappears. The report distribution is an exogenous pushforward of \(\mu\), not an endogenous mass-transfer problem. The continuous society is therefore not doing analytical or computational work; it is merely encoding a weighted majority.

A stronger attempted mirror fares no better. If one retains the paper’s individual strategy-proofness notion, then in an atomless society one person’s report has measure zero and cannot change the collective judgment. Every report becomes weakly dominant, so the strategic content of Theorem 5 vanishes. The selected canonical reports \(r(A_t)\) are only convenient witnesses, not a substantive equilibrium characterization.

One can instead let positive-mass blocs deviate. That may produce a meaningful model, but it requires a new coalitional or bloc strategy-proofness concept, assumptions about coordination and bloc preferences, and a choice between treating types as weighted players or allowing arbitrary measurable coalitions. Treating each type as a player gives a finite weighted voting game; treating coalitions as deviators changes the strategic theorem. Either way, the result is new axiomatic work rather than a continuous mirror of Theorem 5, and still supplies no computational question.

The dictatorship results cannot rescue the paper either. In a nonatomic population, an individual dictator has zero mass, so the original impossibility loses its force. Replacing “individual dictator” by a positive-measure bloc would be a substantive new model, not a harmless limiting interpretation. Since the paper contains no algorithmic result in the first place, there is no compelling reason for ChoCo to undertake that reconstruction.

I would therefore reject this paper as a worthwhile ChoCo mirror. This is not because its high-multiplicity consultation story is incoherent, nor because an existing high-multiplicity literature would count against it. The negative case is narrower: the paper has no computational result to continuize, and its best continuous analogue is a tautological pushforward of a finite strategic identity. A continuous judgment-aggregation project could certainly be built around these ideas, but it would belong to axiomatic or game-theoretic social choice, not to the programme’s computational continuization agenda.
