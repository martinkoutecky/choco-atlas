The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only possible anchors are Theorems 2 and 5, and neither is a computational result. Theorem 2 characterizes rules satisfying axioms as those induced by a priority procedure; Theorem 5 proves an incompatibility among axioms. “Procedure” here means a normative order for assigning slots, not an algorithm whose running time or complexity the paper studies. A continuous version of either theorem would still be an axiomatic characterization or impossibility result, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The strongest proposed mirror would put a large triage population into a finite set of shared ethical profiles, assign each profile a mass, and ask how the characterization or impossibility changes. That is a plausible population regime, but it does not supply the missing computational problem. The paper’s rules choose exactly \(q\) named agents from a participant set, and its axioms track which individuals remain selected as that set or capacity changes. With repeated profiles, a mass allocation or lottery is a natural extension; it also changes the outcome and requires new versions of those axioms. Even granting that extension, proving a continuous analogue of Theorem 2 or 5 would remain axiomatic work.

One could instead ask how to compute the mass assigned under a specified procedure or aggregation. But then the procedure or aggregation is already chosen, and the paper gives no optimization objective for choosing among them. Computing the resulting allocation is a new task, not a mirror of either theorem. Making the design choice computationally difficult would require adding a problem the authors did not formulate.

So the negative case is strong for the question at hand: this paper offers no named computational result to mirror, and its nearest results do not become computational by moving to a continuous population. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous triage problem could ever be inspired by it; that broader claim is weaker. Such a problem could be valuable as new work, but the paper supplies no computational anchor for it.
