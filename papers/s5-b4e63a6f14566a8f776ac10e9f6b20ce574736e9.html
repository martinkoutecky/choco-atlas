The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is population Euclidean unfolding with held-out preference error. For \(A\) alternatives, let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution over a support of \(\tau\) complete rankings, with equal mass on each supported ranking to match the paper’s treatment of unique preferences. Choose shared alternative locations \(x_a\in\mathbb R^d\) and an ideal point \(w_t\in\mathbb R^d\) for each type \(t\), minimizing the population’s total Kendall error,
\[ \sum_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_t\,K(t,\rho_{x,w_t}). \]
For a held-out ranking \(\sigma\), measure its error by its Kendall distance to the nearest ranking induced by the chosen alternative locations. A natural regime is a large, balanced cohort of users with many repeated ranking types: \(\mu_t\) is each type’s share, and users of one type are indistinguishable for this problem. The held-out ranking is drawn uniformly from all \(A!\) rankings, as in the paper.

The closest result is Theorem 4, proved here. It states that for \(d<A-1\), a profile \(\Pi\) with \(I^*\) unique rankings, and a uniformly random ranking \(\pi\), the expected Kendall distance to its nearest ranking representable by an optimally fitting \(d\)-dimensional Euclidean model has the stated lower bound whenever \(I^*\ge r\):
\[ \mathbb E[m(\pi,\hat\pi)]\ge \sum_{k=0}^{K} \frac{(A!-n_{k,A})_{\hat r}}{(A!)_{\hat r}}\, \mathbf 1\!\left(\hat r<A!-n_{k,A}\right), \]
where \(K\le \binom A2\), \(n_{k,A}=\min\{(A-1)^k,A!\}\), \(r\) is the maximum number of distinct rankings simultaneously representable, and \(\hat r\) is the upper bound from Lemma 1. This is meaningful support for a continuous-population question about fitting preferences and expected approximation error.

But it is not an eligible anchor under your rule: Theorem 4 bounds error; it does not assert that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. Theorem 3 is a probability bound, Theorems 1 and 2 are representability results attributed to Bogomolnaia and Laslier, and none asserts computational complexity. Peters’s NP-hardness result appears only in related work, not as a numbered result of this paper.

The main weakness of the mirror is that the paper’s held-out ranking is sampled uniformly from all rankings, rather than from the society’s type distribution; equal mass on supported types is also a restrictive population regime. Peters’s cited hardness makes the computational direction worth investigating, but it cannot supply the required anchor. So there is a credible continuous follow-up question here, but the paper contains no named computational result that can anchor the requested case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but it does not establish a computational result. It gives a lower bound on expected Kendall error for a uniformly random held-out ranking. Theorem 3 bounds a probability, and Theorems 1 and 2 concern representability. The paper cites Peters’s NP-hardness result for recognizing Euclidean profiles, but does not prove it. So the paper supplies no named complexity result whose continuous counterpart this programme could classify.

A stronger mirror than the one proposed is easy to formulate: let \(\mu\) describe a large population of users by their ranking types, and find shared alternative locations and type-specific ideal points that minimize population-weighted Kendall error. Users with the same ranking are a sensible high-multiplicity type. But that formulation does not rescue Theorem 4 as an anchor. Keep its uniform held-out ranking, and the result says nothing about how population masses affect the fitted model; its bound concerns representable rankings and an impartial-culture test. Draw the held-out ranking from \(\mu\) instead, and the question becomes a new weighted unfolding problem with a different test distribution and objective. Theorem 4 does not bound that problem. The paper itself lists robustness to nonuniform preference distributions as future work.

That weighted problem could be worthwhile on its own, especially for recommender systems. The honest negative is narrower: it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper, and the proponent’s proposed mirror does not supply the missing anchor. I cannot rule out every useful project inspired by the paper; the case against a mirror is strongest when “mirror” means a computational continuation of the paper’s own results.
