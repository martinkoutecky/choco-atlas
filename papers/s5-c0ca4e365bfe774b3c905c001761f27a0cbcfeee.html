What the mirror covers

The proposed distributional sampling analogue reflects Random Rank's outcome distribution and its two-location fairness calculation; it does not preserve the paper's unilateral incentive notion or establish a computational result for Theorems 3, 5, or 6.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. The closest is Theorem 3, proved here: a randomized facility-location mechanism is universally anonymous, universally truthful, and satisfies Strong Proportionality in expectation if and only if it is Random Rank, which selects \(k\) uniformly from \(\{1,\ldots,n\}\) and places the facility at the \(k\)-th highest reported location. That is an axiomatic characterization of a mechanism, not a result that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. Proposition 1 is an incompatibility result; Theorems 5 and 6 are also mechanism-property results. None supplies the kind of anchor the requested case requires.

There is a natural population-continuous analogue worth mentioning, but it is not an anchor-backed ChoCo problem. In a large municipality, let \(\mu\) be the distribution of residents’ locations, with mass representing population share. The finite Random Rank mechanism’s output distribution is the empirical distribution of voter locations; its direct continuum analogue is to draw the facility location from \(\mu\). For a society with mass \(p\) at \(\alpha\) and \(1-p\) at \(\beta\), this gives each group the expected distance prescribed by Strong Proportionality. That makes the population interpretation plausible.

The gap is that turning this into the paper’s continuous analogue requires defining what truthfulness means when an individual report has zero effect on \(\mu\)—perhaps by studying deviations by positive mass. That would change the incentive notion, and the resulting question would still be an axiomatic characterization, not a computational complexity question. So there is no qualifying anchor here, and consequently no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover under the requested rubric. The strongest positive point is the clean population interpretation of Random Rank; the weakest, decisive point is that the paper does not study computational complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a characterization of axioms, not a computational result. The rest of the paper’s named results have the same limitation: Proposition 1 is an incompatibility result, and Theorems 5 and 6 characterize mechanisms by strategic and fairness properties. The paper defines no computational problem, encoded input, or complexity measure for a mirror to inherit. A computational question would have to be supplied from outside the paper.

The proponent’s strongest candidate is still a sensible population model: for a municipality with location distribution \(\mu\), choose the facility location from \(\mu\). With two locations, that reproduces the stated fairness calculation. But it does not yield a meaningful computational mirror of Theorem 3. In an atomless population, an individual’s report does not change \(\mu\), so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous. Letting a positive mass of residents report together would make the strategic actors blocs or coalitions, changing the paper’s unilateral incentive notion. Keeping reports influential by retaining finite \(n\) keeps the finite-agent model.

One could study how to compute or sample the \(\mu\)-distributed outcome, or characterize mechanisms over population distributions. Those are possible questions, but the former adds an implementation task and the latter remains axiomatic; neither is a computational problem this paper poses. So the case against a ChoCo mirror is strong on the programme’s stated criterion, though not a proof that no worthwhile algorithmic question could ever be attached to this setting.
