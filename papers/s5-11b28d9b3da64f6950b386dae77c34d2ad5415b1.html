The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive candidate is the one-issue homogeneous group-size problem behind Theorem 2 (proved in this paper). Under constant voting cost, if some group size \(K^\star\) gives representative competence at least \(1/2+\epsilon\), and \(\mu(K)\le 1-A/K^\alpha\) for every \(K\), then the optimal homogeneous group size satisfies \(K^\star_{\mathrm{hom}}(n)\le cK^\star\), where
\[ c=\frac{-4}{\ln(1-4\epsilon^2)} \left(\ln\frac{32}{9\epsilon^2A}+\alpha\ln\frac{4\alpha K^\star}{e} -\alpha\ln|\ln(1-4\epsilon^2)|\right). \]
This is a substantive result about the optimum, but it is not a named computational-complexity result: the paper does not classify an underlying problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise. I can’t present it as a qualifying anchor under your stated rule.

A plausible positive mirror would use a large electorate with a small number \(\tau\) of competence types. A type is a competence value \(q\in T\subseteq[0,1]\); its mass is \(\nu_q\), with rational \(\nu_q\ge0\) and \(\sum_q\nu_q=1\). The electorate has scale \(n\), so type \(q\) represents mass \(n\nu_q\). This fits, for example, a large population grouped into a few calibrated civic-knowledge categories, with \(n\gg\tau\).

For the paper’s MAX representative process, a concrete question is: given \(n\), \(\nu\), and benefit and cost functions, choose a homogeneous group size \(K\) maximizing expected social welfare. Each group draws \(K\) types according to \(\nu\), selects its most competent member, and that representative votes correctly with probability equal to their competence. With \(L=\lfloor n/K\rfloor\), the objective is the expected benefit from a strict majority voting correctly, minus \(\mathrm{Cost}(L)\); the last group can use the paper’s remainder convention. A solution is an optimal \(K\) and the resulting welfare. This preserves the paper’s selection rule, majority vote, and quality-versus-quantity objective while making the competence distribution the population description. I would expect tractability for restricted versions satisfying Theorem 2’s conditions and with fixed model constants, but that is an extrapolation, not a complexity result established by the paper.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s \(F\) is a distribution from which a finite electorate is sampled, while the proposed mirror treats type proportions as the society itself. That shift may change the stochastic structure the results rely on. So the mirror is plausible as a continuous-population re-formulation of the model, but the paper supplies no named computational anchor for the continuization programme’s complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 cannot carry the case for a ChoCo mirror: it bounds an optimum, but does not state the complexity of a computational problem. The paper gives no input encoding, algorithm, running time, or hardness result. Its other named theorems likewise characterize consistency and optimal group sizes. The proposed task—take a competence distribution as input and compute the welfare-maximizing group size—is a new computational problem, not a complexity result the paper supplies.

The strongest faithful version would take finitely many rational competence types with rational population shares, randomly form groups, select each group’s most competent member, and optimize group size for a finite electorate. But the paper’s own regime makes its continuous limit degenerate. Theorem 2 keeps optimal group size \(K=O(1)\) as \(n\) grows, so each group’s share of the population is \(K/n\to0\), while the number of representatives grows as \(\Theta(n)\). In that limit, the majority outcome concentrates: a group size with representative competence above \(1/2\) becomes consistent, and the finite-population accuracy tradeoff disappears. With constant voting cost, the limit no longer distinguishes among consistent group sizes. Keeping a finite number of positive-mass groups avoids that collapse, but changes the scaling regime behind the result.

One could retain finite \(n\) and ask for the best \(K\). That is coherent, but it leaves the paper’s asymptotic analysis and adds a scalar search problem. Or one could assign competence-type mass strategically among group compositions, which may create a richer optimization problem. But that replaces the paper’s i.i.d. sampling model and its group-size-only competence function \(\mu(K)\) with a new assortment problem. Either version might be worth studying on its own; neither makes Theorem 2 a computational anchor.

So the negative case is strong on fit: there is no named computational result to mirror, and the most faithful population limit erases the quality-versus-quantity objective that makes the theorem interesting. The universal claim is weaker. A finite-scale, finite-type design problem is plausible and could be worthwhile, but the case for it would need to come from that new problem’s computational structure, not from this paper’s results.
