The case FOR (proponent)

The paper’s strongest usable anchor is Theorem 1, proved here. It is a constructive existence result, not a complexity classification: the paper proves neither that a problem is in \(P\) nor that it is hard. The theorem says that if the couples’ responsive preferences satisfy extreme-altruism, then for every responsive completion by single-doctor and hospital preferences, the doctor-proposing deferred-acceptance algorithm (DPDA) produces a stable matching. If extreme-altruism fails at some hospital, the authors construct a responsive completion with no stable matching. The theorem’s boundary is therefore a *universal guarantee*: outside the condition, some completion fails, though a particular market may still have a stable matching.

Here is the continuous problem I would ask a prover to take on: Universal Stable Mass-Matching for Couples. Fix a finite set of hospitals \(H\), rational capacities \(q_h\) measured in doctor-mass units, and a finite support of couple types with masses and complete responsive preferences over ordered hospital pairs. A type specifies both members’ individual rankings and the couple’s joint ranking. Singleton doctor types have complete rankings over \(H\cup\{\lambda\}\). Normalize total doctor mass to one, so \(\sum_s \alpha_s+2\sum_c\beta_c=1\), where \(\alpha_s\) and \(\beta_c\) are singleton and couple masses. Hospitals have responsive preferences over feasible mass rosters, including the setwise replacement comparison used in the paper.

The instance fixes the couple-type profile and capacities; a *completion* supplies singleton types and hospital preferences. Ask whether every responsive completion has a stable mass matching. A matching is a collection of nonnegative masses \(x_{s,h}\) and \(z_{c,h,h'}\): singleton mass \(x_{s,h}\) goes to \(h\), while couple mass \(z_{c,h,h'}\) sends its two members to the ordered pair \((h,h')\). Each type’s assignments sum to its mass, and hospital \(h\)’s load is
\[ \sum_s x_{s,h}+\sum_c\sum_{h'}\bigl(z_{c,h,h'}+z_{c,h',h}\bigr)\le q_h. \]
A solution is either a stable such allocation for a supplied completion, or—when the universal answer is no—a responsive completion with no stable allocation. Stability retains the paper’s blocking rule: a positive submass must prefer its proposed destination, and each hospital involved must accept the corresponding mass under the same setwise replacement rule; when both partners go to one hospital, it must be willing to accept them together. The people are still assigned integral outcomes; only the distribution of people across those outcomes is fractional.

The continuous extreme-altruism condition is the natural limiting version of Definition 9. When total doctor mass is one, the paper’s finite condition \(\kappa_h\le |D|-2\) becomes \(q_h<1\): if a finite market has \(n\) doctors and \(\kappa_h=q_h n\), then every fixed \(q_h<1\) eventually satisfies \(\kappa_h\le n-2\). For each couple type \(c=\{f,m\}\), hospital \(h\), and alternatives \(h',h''\), require, when \(q_h<1\), that
\[ h'\succ_f h,\quad h'\succeq_f h'',\quad h''\succeq_m\lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (h',h'')\succ_c(h,h), \]
and symmetrically that
\[ h'\succ_m h,\quad h'\succeq_m h'',\quad h''\succeq_f\lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (h'',h')\succ_c(h,h). \]
The continuous mirror asks whether this condition again exactly characterizes a guarantee over all responsive completions, and, on the guaranteed side, asks for a mass-DPDA construction.

A plausible regime is a large centralized placement market—say, a national training or public-service program—with many candidates and some paired applicants, a fixed menu of large programs, and rank lists shaped by a relatively small set of standard location and contract choices. Types must encode the *complete* preferences used by the model, including the couples’ joint preferences; merely similar rank lists do not count. In such a regime the number of applicants \(N\) could greatly exceed the support size \(\tau\). I think the authors would recognize the yes-side as their stable-matching problem in a high-multiplicity regime: it preserves couples, responsive preferences, hospital capacity, and the paper’s blocking notion.

I would expect the extreme-altruism region to be tractable (Class A): with finite type support, a mass version of DPDA can split proposals and acceptances by rational mass, and the number of types and hospital-preference lists—not the number of people—should govern the work. The paper does not establish that running-time claim. Nor would I assume its “only if” direction survives unchanged. The authors’ nonexistence construction uses a few individual doctors and integer capacities; fractional assignment may dissolve that obstruction or require a different condition. That is the weakest point in this case, and the main follow-up question: does the continuous universal guarantee still have extreme-altruism as its exact boundary, or does mass splitting enlarge the stable region? My mirror covers Theorem 1’s universal existence characterization and constructive positive side; it does not claim to mirror Theorem 2 or to find a complexity result for the paper’s unrestricted market.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a constructive existence result, but it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Its positive side applies the familiar doctor-proposing deferred-acceptance algorithm; its negative side constructs a preference profile with no stable matching. A continuous version could be a worthwhile matching-theory question, but the case for it as a ChoCo complexity mirror is already weaker than for a paper about bribery, control, or robustness.

The proposed type definition also leaves out information the paper’s model uses. It groups couples by their members’ rankings and their joint ranking, while each hospital has a strict ranking of named doctors and an acceptability set. A hospital’s ranking can distinguish doctors who otherwise have identical preferences. Include each doctor’s hospital-specific status in the complete type, and the support can grow with the population. Omit it, and the proposed aggregation cannot determine which mass a hospital accepts. This matters especially because Theorem 1 quantifies over arbitrary responsive hospital completions.

There is a related problem in the phrase “responsive preferences over mass rosters.” The paper defines hospital preferences over finite sets of named doctors. It does not specify a preference over fractional rosters or how a hospital accepts part of a type’s mass. A finite responsive order does not select a unique extension to those rosters. An additive utility or type-priority rule could provide one, but it would restrict the hospital preferences that Theorem 1 quantifies over, including the setwise comparisons central to the paper. Keeping all responsive extensions instead leaves the continuum with no stated finite representation, so its computational complexity is unclear.

The strongest version of the proponent’s case is therefore a model with repeated couple types and explicit, type-based hospital priorities or utility scores, plus a mass version of deferred acceptance. That could be sensible for a large placement market. It would also be a new, more anonymous preference domain. Its universal stability guarantee would ask whether extreme-altruism remains the right condition under those choices; it would not preserve Theorem 1’s guarantee over arbitrary strict, individual-level hospital preferences. The algorithmic question would depend on the added representation.

I would oppose this as a strong ChoCo mirror: the paper’s main result is a domain characterization, and the proposed continuous version has neither a settled type representation nor a settled computational input model. The case is not airtight. An author-recognizable model with type-based hospital preferences could yield a worthwhile continuous matching result, though its connection to this theorem would be narrower than the proponent claims.
