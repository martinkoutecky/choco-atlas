The continuous mirror question

Given \(d\) item kinds, rational per-capita supplies \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^d\) with \(\sum_j q_j\geq 1\), and a normalized, nonnegative, \(\sigma\)-subadditive valuation \(v:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^d\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\) whose one-item marginals have absolute value at most \(\Lambda\), find a finite-support mass allocation \(x_z\geq 0\) over integral bundles \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^d\) such that \(\sum_z x_z=1\), \(\sum_z x_z z_j=q_j\) for every kind \(j\), \(x_0=0\), and \(\max_{x_z>0}v(z)-\min_{x_z>0}v(z)\leq 5\Lambda+1\).

The model it lives in

A unit-mass cohort with one valuation type, integral bundle vectors \(z\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q\). The decision variables are masses \(x_z\); find a feasible supply allocation whose used bundles’ values have spread at most \(5\Lambda+1\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 24’s approximate-equitability result for identical valuations. It leaves the cake-division results, envy-freeness results, heterogeneous valuations, and the other named results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can such a mass allocation be found in time polynomial in \(d\), the bit length of \(q\), and \(\Lambda\), given value-oracle access to \(v\)?
  • Can bundle-pattern pricing be done efficiently for a candidate utility band, or does the compressed problem have a meaningful hardness boundary?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: the paper’s result for identical valuations gives a credible high-multiplicity anchor. Theorem 24, proved here, says that for \(n\) agents dividing \(m\) indivisible items, all with the same normalized \(\sigma\)-subadditive valuation \(v\) and marginal values bounded by \(\Lambda\), a polynomial-time algorithm returns nonempty bundles whose values differ pairwise by at most \(5\Lambda+1\). Under the paper’s standing assumptions, the valuation is also globally nonnegative. This is an algorithmic result; the paper supplies no comparable named hardness result to anchor.

Here is a continuous population problem that keeps its allocation and fairness question intact. There is a unit mass of agents, all of one type, sharing a valuation \(v:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\) over bundles of \(d\) item kinds. Assume \(v(0)=0\), \(v(a+b)\leq v(a)+v(b)\), and each one-item marginal is at most \(\Lambda\) in absolute value. The supply of kind \(j\) is \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\) units per unit of population, with \(\sum_j q_j\geq 1\). An allocation is a finite-support list of masses \(x_z\geq 0\), where \(z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}\) is an integral bundle and \(x_z\) is the population mass receiving it. The masses must satisfy
\[ \sum_z x_z=1,\qquad \sum_z x_z z_j=q_j\ \text{for each }j,\qquad x_0=0. \]
Ask for such an allocation whose used bundles have utility spread
\[ \max_{x_z>0}v(z)-\min_{x_z>0}v(z)\leq 5\Lambda+1. \]
The solution is the mass list \(x_z\); each agent still receives an integral bundle. The fairness criterion is the paper’s approximate equitability condition, expressed over the bundles used by the population.

A plausible regime is a large standardized course or training cohort: members share the same complete preferences over course kinds, while the supply of seats in each kind scales with cohort size. With \(N\) agents, take \(Nq_j\) copies of item kind \(j\) whenever those counts are integral. Then \(N\gg d\), but there is only one agent type. This is a property of that regime, not a claim that fair division generally has high multiplicity. The paper’s identical-valuation theorem makes this scenario especially recognizable to its authors. The replicated supplies are a co-modification needed to keep per-capita resources meaningful as the population grows.

I would tentatively expect the mass problem to be tractable (Class A). Its supply constraints are linear, and continuous mass lets the solution describe a small number of integral bundle patterns with rational weights. The central algorithmic question is how to find those patterns efficiently: a configuration formulation has a column for each bundle, so a useful next step is to understand the pricing problem for bundles within a candidate utility band. Further questions are whether that pricing can be done in time polynomial in \(d\) and the input bit length, and whether the model extends to several valuation types without changing the fairness meaning.

The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 24 runs in \(n,m,\Lambda\), whereas this mirror asks for a compressed algorithm in the bit length of the rational supply vector \(q\). Expanding denominators into a finite population may take time polynomial in \(N\), which can be exponential in that bit length. And the mirror uses just one agent type. So the theorem supports the model and its fairness target; it does not already solve the compressed continuous problem. The case rests on that being a worthwhile computational question in the authors’ own identical-valuation setting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious case against this anchor is that the proposed problem changes the input model substantially. Theorem 24 gives an algorithm polynomial in the explicit number of agents and items, \(n\) and \(m\). The proposed mirror instead asks for an algorithm polynomial in the bit length of the per-capita supplies \(q\). To get that bound, it also treats copies of each item kind as interchangeable and allows their counts to be encoded in binary. Neither is covered by Theorem 24’s runtime guarantee.

For rational \(q\), though, this gap is exactly the high-multiplicity question. Clear denominators, create \(N\) agents and \(Nq_j\) copies of each item kind, and Theorem 24 supplies a finite allocation with the stated utility spread. That gives the continuous model a known existence guarantee and an algorithm polynomial in the expanded instance. It does not give an algorithm polynomial in the compressed input length. Calling the mirror already solved would mistake a finite expansion for the compressed computational problem ChoCo is meant to study.

There is also a real narrowing of scope: the society has just one valuation type, so the input type distribution is degenerate, and the main algorithmic work is finding a mixture of integral bundle patterns that meets the supply vector. Extending this to heterogeneous types would require a fairness notion and results not supplied by this paper. But Theorem 24 itself singles out identical valuations, and a large standardized cohort with per-capita supplies is a plausible regime. A one-type mirror is still a population mirror.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The strongest objection is that the proposed contribution depends on a new compressed resource representation, and its value hinges on finding tractable pricing or establishing a meaningful hardness boundary for that representation. That makes the research question less developed than the proponent’s wording suggests; it does not make it unworthy. Theorem 24 is a named algorithmic result, and the proposed model preserves its integral-bundle fairness criterion while putting the multiplicities in the input encoding. Under the programme’s stated standard, this is a credible mirror.
