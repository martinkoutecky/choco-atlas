The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) item categories, type masses \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\), per-capita supplies \(q_g=1/2\), additive type valuations \(v^A,v^B\) with common total \(T\), and \(\alpha\le T/3\), construct or rule out a deterministic rule \(\mathcal M_\infty\) mapping every reported pair to a partition \((S_A,S_B)\) such that each type receives its integral bundle, the outcome is PMMS and Pareto optimal, ordinally equivalent reports preserve true utility, and every permitted perturbation \(v^{i'}\) satisfies \(1/(2+12\alpha/T)\le v^i(\mathcal M_\infty(v^{i'},v^{-i})_i)/v^i(\mathcal M_\infty(v^i,v^{-i})_i)\le 2+12\alpha/T\).

The model it lives in

A two-type high-multiplicity allocation model with \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\) and \(q_g=1/2\), retaining integral bundles for each individual. The decision variable is the type-homogeneous partition \((S_A,S_B)\), selected by lexicographically maximizing bundle ranks, subject to PMMS, Pareto optimality, and the stated perturbation-ratio guarantee.

The objection that survived

The paper studies one named agent's perturbation, whereas the mirror treats a positive-mass type's report as collective and restricts allocations to be type-homogeneous; allowing split mass would require new stability and fairness definitions.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4's approximate-stability guarantee and Theorem 3's PMMS and Pareto-optimality guarantee on the two-type homogeneous slice, and it also gives a clone-lift interpretation of Theorem 1. It leaves Theorem 2, the modified-​\(\mathcal M\) framework, and the broader multi-agent results without a genuinely multi-type mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can stability under a positive-mass type perturbation be formalized as the faithful high-multiplicity analogue of the paper's individual-report stability?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s fair-division problem, with indivisibility retained at the individual level.

Let \(G=\{g_1,\ldots,g_m\}\) be item categories and let \(t\in T\) denote valuation types. A society is a mass distribution \(\mu\) over types, with each type \(t\) having an additive valuation \(v^t\). Items are replicated with per-capita supplies \(q_g\). If \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq G\), feasibility is

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq G}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}=q_g. \]

Thus the aggregate allocation is fractional in mass, but every individual still receives an indivisible bundle. This is exactly the population continuization contemplated by ChoCo, rather than continuity of the outcome space.

The most faithful regime for this paper has two large homogeneous populations \(A\) and \(B\), each of mass \(1/2\), with \(N/2\) clones of each type in an \(N\)-agent approximation and \(N/2\) copies of every good. A type-homogeneous allocation gives every \(A\)-agent the same bundle \(S_A\) and every \(B\)-agent the complementary bundle \(S_B=G\setminus S_A\). Hence \(q_g=1/2\). This is a sensible model for, for example, a large benefits or housing programme serving two standardized household cohorts whose members have identical valuations for a catalogue of indivisible package components. The regime is genuinely high multiplicity: \(N\) tends to infinity while \(\tau=2\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that for two agents with additive valuations and total value \(T\), the rank-leximin algorithm is \((2+12\alpha/T)\)-approximately-stable for every \(\alpha\le T/3\). In full, this means that the algorithm is stable on valuation functions that are ordinally equivalent over all bundles, and that for every \(\alpha\)-neighbouring report \(v_i'\), the true utility ratio lies between

\[ \frac{1}{2+12\alpha/T} \quad\text{and}\quad 2+\frac{12\alpha}{T}. \]

The theorem is proved here. The companion Theorem 3, also proved here, says that rank-leximin returns a PMMS and Pareto-optimal allocation for two agents.

I would formulate the corresponding continuous problem as follows.

Continuous Rank-Leximin Stability. The input consists of \(m\), a common total value \(T\), two type masses \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\), per-capita supplies \(q_g=1/2\), two additive valuation vectors \(v^A,v^B\), and a perturbation radius \(\alpha\). A solution is a deterministic rule \(\mathcal M_\infty\) that returns a partition \((S_A,S_B)\) of \(G\), interpreted as giving every member of type \(A\) the bundle \(S_A\) and every member of type \(B\) the bundle \(S_B\).

The rule must return a PMMS and Pareto-optimal allocation on every report profile. For every type report \(v^{A'}\) satisfying the paper’s \(\alpha\)-neighbour conditions—same total, same singleton order, and \(L_1\)-distance at most \(\alpha\)—the true utility must satisfy

\[ \frac{1}{K} \le \frac{v^A\!\left(\mathcal M_\infty(v^{A'},v^B)_A\right)} {v^A\!\left(\mathcal M_\infty(v^A,v^B)_A\right)} \le K, \qquad K=2+\frac{12\alpha}{T}, \]

and the utility must be unchanged when \(v^{A'}\) is fully ordinally equivalent to \(v^A\). The same must hold for type \(B\).

This is not merely the original problem renamed. Its interpretation is a continuum population with mass-level supply constraints, and it has a natural enlargement in which types may be split across several integral bundles through the variables \(x_{t,S}\). The two-type homogeneous slice is the exact high-multiplicity core on which the paper’s theorem can be tested.

I expect the two-type version to have a constructive answer: applying rank-leximin to the two representative types gives precisely the paper’s PMMS, PO, and approximate-stability guarantees, now interpreted as guarantees for arbitrarily many clones. But I would not claim that this yields a polynomial-time continuous algorithm. The rank objective ranges over \(2^m\) bundles, and the paper explicitly remarks that finding PMMS allocations is NP-hard even for two identical agents, by a reduction from Partition. That remark is not a numbered theorem and therefore is not my anchor, but it strongly suggests that the relevant combinatorics live in the number of goods, not in population multiplicity. I therefore expect hardness to transfer in the variable-\(m\) regime, while fixed \(m\) or restricted type structure may admit a configuration-LP treatment.

A second, complementary anchor is Theorem 1, also proved here. It states that if an algorithm is stable and always returns an EF1 allocation, then for every \(\beta\in[1,(T-3)/2]\) it cannot always return a \(\beta\)-Pareto-optimal allocation. Thus exact stability, EF1, and even approximate Pareto optimality are jointly impossible.

The continuous question is:

Continuous Exact-Stability Feasibility. On the same two-type clone society, does there exist a deterministic rule that, for every pair of additive type valuations, returns a type-homogeneous partition that is EF1 and \(\beta\)-PO, while remaining stable under every permitted cardinal perturbation? Here \(\beta\)-PO means that no alternative partition weakly improves both type utilities while improving one by a factor of at least \(\beta\). The solution is either such a rule or a proof of nonexistence.

Theorem 1 transfers directly to this continuous problem. Take \(N/2\) clones of each of the paper’s two agents and \(N/2\) copies of each good. Every type-homogeneous continuous allocation corresponds to the original two-agent allocation, and every type’s per-agent utility is unchanged by cloning. The paper’s four-good construction therefore rules out the continuous rule for \(\beta\in[1,(T-3)/2]\). This is a particularly useful mirror because it shows that continuization does not automatically dissolve every obstruction: the conflict is structural and survives arbitrary population replication.

The two anchors point in different but compatible directions. Theorem 4 supplies a meaningful positive continuous design target with an explicit stability guarantee; Theorem 1 supplies its boundary, showing that exact stability cannot be retained alongside EF1 and approximate efficiency. The continuous programme would then ask whether relaxing exact stability, enlarging from two types to \(\tau\) types, or allowing type masses to split across bundles changes the boundary.

The weakest point is that the cleanest mirror is deliberately a two-type clone regime. A sceptic can say that this is only a replicated two-agent problem, not a genuinely new continuum allocation problem. That criticism has force. The defence is that high multiplicity is precisely the intended regime: many agents, few complete types, and per-capita replicated supplies. Moreover, the paper itself supplies no numbered NP-hardness or polynomial-time theorem; its strongest anchors are algorithmic and impossibility results. So the positive case should be modest: this paper supports a credible continuous-population mirror and a sharp stability programme, but it does not by itself establish a new polynomial-time continuum algorithm.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper offers no numbered computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 is an impossibility theorem about deterministic allocation rules; Theorem 2 is a characterization; Theorems 3 and 4 are guarantees for an explicitly exponential rank-leximin procedure. The only complexity statement—NP-hardness of finding PMMS allocations—is an informal remark, not a proved complexity theorem. Thus the proposed mirrors do not yet ask for a continuous complexity classification, algorithm, approximation scheme, or separation oracle. They turn existential allocation statements into population language.

Theorem 4 is especially weak as a continuous anchor. In the proposed two-type homogeneous model, \(\mu_A=\mu_B=1/2\) and \(q_g=1/2\) are merely normalization. Every member of type \(A\) receives the same bundle and every member of type \(B\) receives its complement. The rule is therefore exactly a deterministic map from two valuation vectors to one partition of \(G\): the original rank-leximin problem. The quantities \(T\), \(\alpha\), the bundle ranks, and the utility ratio are unchanged. This is a legitimate high-multiplicity regime, but it supplies no population-level computational object, no mass-transfer decision, and no new input parameter on which an algorithm could operate.

The natural attempt to make the population matter is to allow

\[ x_{t,S} \]

to split a type across several integral bundles. That exposes a more fundamental mismatch. The paper’s stability notion is about the utility of one named agent when only that agent changes its report. A positive-mass type has no single bundle or single utility once it is split. One must choose between a pointwise guarantee for every member and an average guarantee such as

\[ \frac{1}{\mu_t}\sum_S x_{t,S}v^t(S). \]

The former is a new, much stronger allocation problem; the latter treats the distribution over integral bundles as a lottery and changes the meanings of PMMS, EF1, PO, and stability. Neither is established by Theorem 4.

The identity issue cannot be repaired by simply calling a type a representative agent. If one individual in a continuum misreports, that individual has measure zero and the aggregate allocation is unaffected, making stability vacuous at the population level. If an entire positive-mass type changes its report, then the perturbation is collective, not the one-agent perturbation in the theorem. A small positive submass can be introduced, but that creates a new robustness problem with a mass parameter and requires new definitions and results. It is no longer a continuous mirror of Theorem 4.

Theorem 1 has the same dichotomy. If every clone of a type must receive the same bundle, its four-good counterexample transfers exactly—but only because the continuous feasible set has been forced to be the original two-agent feasible set. This is a clone-lift of the impossibility, not a population-level computational result. If clones may receive different bundles, the proof no longer transfers: its key step uses the fact that every EF1 allocation gives the two original agents particular bundles, so stability fixes one agent’s utility. With a mass of clones receiving different bundles, there is no distinguished \(S_2\) whose value must remain fixed. An average-\(\beta\)-PO notion would likewise be a different domination problem over aggregate allocations.

So each anchor fails in the only way that matters here. Theorem 4 either remains a two-agent finite theorem with passive cloning, or becomes a new fractional/aggregate stability problem. Theorem 1 either remains a replicated finite impossibility, or loses the proof when genuine mass splitting is allowed. Neither supplies the computational population question ChoCo is seeking.

The honest weakness of this negative case is that a high-multiplicity fair-division model is certainly sensible. The objection cannot be that cloning is illegitimate, and existing high-multiplicity work would support rather than undermine it. The narrower conclusion is that this particular paper is a poor source for a worthwhile continuous-computational mirror: a genuinely nontrivial mirror would have to invent the population-level allocation and stability notions rather than continuize one of the paper’s computational results.
