What the mirror covers

The proposed extension applies Theorem 1 to types in the population support, using Proposition 1 for the single-peaked domain. It leaves the paper's other domain results, examples, and local-implementation results untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a narrow high-multiplicity reading of the paper’s incentive-compatibility result. Imagine a large population of firms choosing delivery dates. A firm’s type is its full valuation vector over dates, with a single-peaked ranking; firms in the same cohort have the same vector, not merely the same ranking. Represent the population by a finite-support distribution \(\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\delta_{t_i}\), where each \(\mu_i\) is a rational share and the number of firms is much larger than \(\tau\). A deterministic mechanism \(f:T\to A\) assigns a date, and \(p:T\to\mathbb R\) sets the transfer. This fits the paper’s scheduling example, and its authors explicitly say their results extend to multiple agents.

The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for \(T=\operatorname{cl}(V(D))\), where \(D\) is top connected, and a deterministic allocation rule satisfying strict type range, three conditions are equivalent: local incentive compatibility plus payment-only incentive compatibility; adjacent incentive compatibility plus payment-only incentive compatibility; and full incentive compatibility. In particular, the first two conditions suffice for every type to prefer truthful reporting to every report. Proposition 1, also proved here using Sato’s cited result that the single-peaked domain is connected without restoration, establishes that the single-peaked domain is top connected.

A population version I would call Mass Local-to-Global IC asks: given such a distribution \(\mu\) and a mechanism defined over the full type space \(T\), does local incentive compatibility together with payment-only incentive compatibility guarantee that every positive-mass cohort is globally truthful against all reports in \(T\)? A “yes” solution means no supported type has a profitable misreport; a “no” solution would give a supported type and a profitable report. Under Theorem 1’s hypotheses, the answer is yes—in fact, the result holds for every type in \(T\), not just the population support. This is recognizable as the paper’s problem applied to a plausible many-firm regime.

But this is not a qualifying computational anchor under the requested standard. Theorem 1 proves an equivalence among incentive constraints; it does not place a decision or optimization problem in P, prove hardness, or give an algorithm with an input encoding and running time. The paper contains no named computational-complexity result of that kind. Nor does \(\mu\) affect the theorem: the shares add an aggregate population description, while incentive compatibility remains a pointwise question about each firm’s report. Since the mechanism is defined over a continuum of cardinal types, the paper also supplies no finite representation of the mechanism from which to compute a complexity classification.

That is the case’s weakest point, and it limits the verdict: the scheduling population is plausible, and the theorem transfers cleanly to it, but this is a population interpretation of an economic theorem rather than a computational continuous mirror in ChoCo’s sense. The next genuine computational question would need to specify a finite representation for \(f\) and \(p\), then ask for the bit complexity of verifying the relevant incentive conditions or finding a violating report. The paper does not answer that question, so I cannot honestly assign its mirror to the tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hard classes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s named anchor, Theorem 1, is a characterization of incentive compatibility, not a computational result. Proposition 1 supplies a domain property needed by that characterization. Neither gives a complexity classification, an algorithm with an input representation and running time, or a hardness result. Their “Mass Local-to-Global IC” question adds a distribution \(\mu\), but Theorem 1 already guarantees the conclusion for every type in the domain. The shares and support size play no role; the proposed question is the same theorem with population notation attached.

The strongest repair is a large population of firms whose full cardinal valuation vectors are the types, with a mechanism assigning delivery dates and transfers. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s scheduling example supports it. But the paper’s result still concerns whether an individual firm can profit by misreporting. Reweighting the firms’ types into \(\mu\) does not change those incentive constraints. If the mechanism instead depends on aggregate reports, a single firm has zero influence in an atomless limit; avoiding that collapse requires a new model and new questions. Either way, the population distribution adds no computational problem that the paper studies.

One could propose a new task: encode a mechanism finitely and decide whether it is incentive compatible, or calculate the mass of types with profitable deviations. That would require choices the paper leaves open—how mechanisms are represented, which reports must be checked, and whether the objective concerns all types or only those in the population. With Theorem 1’s hypotheses promised, the local-to-global answer is already fixed. Without them, any complexity may come from the chosen encoding or new objective, rather than from continuizing the population. That looks like a research extension, not a computational mirror of this paper.

So the negative case is strong against the anchors actually offered: they do not identify a computational result for ChoCo to continuize, and adding high multiplicity does not make their theorem computational. The universal claim is harder to establish. A well-designed computational project in population mechanism design might be worthwhile, but it would need a new target beyond this paper’s result.
