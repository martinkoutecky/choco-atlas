What the mirror covers

The cohort formulation lifts Theorem 4.1 across independent dyad types; it leaves Theorems 3.1, 3.2, and 3.6 and the simulations outside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a population of repeated crowdsourcing dyads. Imagine a large platform pairing workers on many statistically similar binary labeling tasks. A dyad’s type is its signal distribution \(P_\theta\) and the two workers’ learning rules \((f_\theta,g_\theta)\); its mass is the fraction of matched pairs with that complete behavioral type. There may be many pairs but only a small support of types.

This gives a concrete candidate, Continuous CA Cohort Convergence: given a finite support of types and rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), run a separate sequential CA process for each dyad type. Ask for the expected mass of dyads whose two strategies fail to converge jointly to truth-telling or jointly to signal-flipping. A solution reports that mass. If every supported type meets the paper’s assumptions, the answer is zero: the result applies to each type, so the mass of failures is zero. The authors could plausibly recognize this as a deployment-scale version of their result.

The paper’s strongest nearby result is Theorem 4.1, proved here. It says that under Assumptions 2.1 and 2.2—i.i.d. binary signals with full support and positive correlation—the sequential CA mechanism achieves truthful convergence when the agents use reward-based algorithms whose update functions satisfy exchangeability, order preservation, and full exploitation (Assumptions 3.3–3.5). As the paper defines it, convergence allows either both agents to tell the truth or both to flip, almost surely. Theorem 3.1, also proved here, says that for any rank-\(k\) sequential information-elicitation mechanism, there are no-regret algorithms for which truthful convergence fails.

But neither theorem is an admissible anchor under the requested rule: neither asserts a computational-complexity result such as tractability, hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. The cohort question therefore cannot supply the required anchor-based case. It also merely replicates independent dyads; the theorem does not use population mass to solve a computational problem. The number of repeated tasks and the agents’ mixed strategies are tempting continuous dimensions, but those continuize time or strategy probabilities rather than the population.

That is the proposed mirror’s weakest point, and it limits the positive case sharply. There are no named computational-complexity results in this paper to anchor a ChoCo mirror. The dyad-cohort formulation is a plausible population-scale deployment question, but it is not the paper’s complexity question, and its answer under Theorem 4.1’s assumptions is an immediate lift of the per-dyad theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper proves no computational complexity result to continuize. Theorem 4.1 proves almost-sure convergence in a stochastic learning process; Theorem 3.1 gives a mechanism-level impossibility result for no-regret learners. Neither studies the running time or complexity of solving a computational problem. A continuous population cannot supply the missing computational question.

The dyad-cohort proposal is a sensible deployment summary: assign each type a mass and aggregate which dyads converge. But if dyads run independently, this just weights the paper’s per-dyad analysis. If agents interact across the pooled population, the result is a new heterogeneous learning model; the paper’s theorem does not analyze it. Either way, the proposal asks about aggregate dynamics, not a computational task whose complexity the programme could chart.

A more ambitious population model could ask for the share of agents that converge under common interactions, or the complexity of deciding whether that share exceeds a threshold. Those may be worthwhile questions, but they require a new model and a new computational problem. They are not continuous mirrors of the paper’s results. The negative case is strongest on that boundary: the paper’s subject could inspire later complexity work, but this paper offers no computational result for ChoCo to continuize.
