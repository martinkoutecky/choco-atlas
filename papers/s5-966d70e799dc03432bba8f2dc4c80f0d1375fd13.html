The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Its two numbered results are proved here, but they are game-theoretic claims: Proposition 1 says the optimal proto-coalition is always the entire legislature \(G=M\), given the preceding bargaining result and \(B>1\). Proposition 2 says that, in an equilibrium with expected policy \(X(\sigma)\), voter \(i\) votes for the leftmost party if \(\theta_i\le X(\sigma)-1/n\), and for the rightmost party if \(\theta_i\ge X(\sigma)+1/n\). The latter leaves a band of width \(2/n\) in which it gives no such conclusion. Neither proposition asserts that a computational problem is tractable or hard, so neither is a qualifying anchor.

The strongest positive lead I can offer is a new question motivated by Proposition 2: Large-Electorate Limit Strategic Voting. Give the paper’s party positions, legislator and bargaining primitives, and a finite set of voter bliss points \(\Theta\) with rational population masses \(\mu_\theta\). For each electorate size \(N\), form the paper’s game with \(N\) voters whose private types are drawn independently from \(\mu\). For every symmetric Bayesian equilibrium, record the type-by-party vote masses \(a^N_{\theta k}=\mu_\theta\sigma^N_k(\theta)\) and the expected implemented policy \(x_N\). The problem is to return the set of all limits \((a,x)\) obtainable from sequences of these equilibria as \(N\to\infty\). A solution is an exact description of that limit set. The scenario is a large electorate with many voters in each of a relatively small number of ideal-point groups; the mass of a type is its share of the electorate.

This is a plausible extension of the authors’ strategic voting question: it keeps their preferences, parties, proportional vote shares, government formation, and policy objective, while making the electorate’s type masses the object of study. Proposition 2 gives it a particularly apt foothold because its cutoff explicitly depends on \(1/n\). But this is not a computational mirror anchored by a result in the required sense. I would leave its complexity class open; the paper does not establish whether limit-equilibrium computation is tractable, inherits hardness, or is hard for continuum-specific reasons. Natural follow-up questions include how much limiting vote mass can remain on non-extreme parties at a type exactly at the limiting policy, and whether the limit equilibria agree with equilibria of a direct nonatomic voting game.

The weakest point is also central: in a direct nonatomic game, one voter’s ballot has zero effect on policy, so the strategic incentives Proposition 2 studies disappear. Defining the mirror through limits of finite-electorate equilibria preserves those incentives, but makes the question a limit problem rather than a self-contained continuum game. I would therefore make this only a narrow, explicitly speculative positive case about strategic voting—not claim that the paper supplies a qualifying computational anchor or that it has been continuized successfully.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes the government chosen in a bargaining game; Proposition 2 characterizes voters’ incentives. Neither states the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm. So the case for a ChoCo mirror has to supply a new computational question, rather than continue one of the paper’s computational results.

The strongest candidate is the proponent’s limit-equilibrium question, and the electorate is a sensible place to consider high multiplicity. But the continuum changes the strategic mechanism that Proposition 2 studies. With a nonatomic electorate, one voter’s ballot cannot change the vote shares, government, or policy. Every ballot is then a best response on policy grounds, so the direct continuum game loses the individual strategic incentives at the heart of the proposition. Taking limits of finite-electorate equilibria can preserve information about those incentives, but it does so by carrying the finite-\(N\) pivotal effect through a sequence of games before it vanishes. That is a potentially interesting asymptotic equilibrium-selection problem; it is not a computational mirror of a result in this paper, and it sits closer to analytic game theory than to ChoCo’s computational questions about interventions.

A more ambitious version could ask for an exact algorithm to describe all limiting equilibrium profiles for rational masses of finitely many voter types. That would make the output representation and computational task explicit, but it would still be a new problem built around finite-game limits. Recasting voters’ choices as transfers of mass would instead introduce coordinated deviations by groups, changing the strategic actors in the model. Either route might support a separate research project; neither supplies the missing computational anchor.

This is the strongest negative case I can make, rather than a proof that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The paper’s electorate admits a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and equilibrium computation could itself be worth studying. What is weak is the claim that such work would be a continuous computational mirror of this paper in the ChoCo sense.
