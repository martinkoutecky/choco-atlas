What the mirror covers

The candidate model attempts to recast Theorem 6’s general-domain impossibility; it supplies no continuous question for the greedy-domain characterization or the other results.

The case FOR (proponent)

Under the stated anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results says that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The nearest negative anchor is Theorem 6, proved here. It says that on the general valuation domain no deterministic direct mechanism satisfies all five criteria: incentive compatibility at generic profiles, individual rationality, symmetry at generic profiles, non-wastefulness, and non-bossiness for losing bidders. That is a mechanism-existence impossibility, not a computational-hardness result.

The nearest positive counterpart is Theorem 5, also proved here: on the greedy domain, augmented ISPA is incentive compatible, individually rational, symmetric, non-wasteful, and non-bossy. ISPA orders bidders by budget, gives each the unique maximally relevant bundle among the remaining goods, and charges a winner the highest budget of a bidder who would receive an overlapping bundle in the winner’s absence. These results make the paper’s rules concrete, but they do not establish a complexity classification.

The strongest continuous-population question I can construct from Theorem 6 is this: Per-Capita Budget-Auction Mechanism Feasibility. Fix a finite set of goods \(G\), a finite explicit type set \(T\) of complete bidder descriptions \(t=(u_t,b_t)\), a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), and per-capita supply \(r_g\) for each good. Decide whether there is a type-anonymous direct mechanism that assigns mass \(x_{t,S}\) of each type to integral bundles \(S\subseteq G\), with per-capita payments, so that total use of each good is at most \(r_g\), truthful agents are individually rational and prefer truthful reports, and the appropriate mass versions of non-wastefulness and non-bossiness hold. Each person still receives an integral bundle; \(x_{t,S}\) records what fraction of a type receives each bundle. A plausible regime is many regional telecom operators buying spectrum packages, with repeated valuation-and-budget templates and license supply scaled to the population.

This is a real population-continuum question, and Theorem 6 motivates asking whether its impossibility survives. For an explicitly listed finite type and bundle table, I would expect the allocation and incentive constraints to have a tractable finite optimization formulation. But that expectation concerns this newly specified model; Theorem 6 does not establish it. In particular, an infinitesimal agent cannot change \(\mu\), so individual incentives no longer work as in the paper, and non-bossiness may become vacuous. The fractional type assignments also change the mechanism’s outcome model.

That is the weak point, and it is substantial: the proposed mirror changes the strategic force of the result, and the paper’s main contributions are mechanism characterization and axiomatic impossibility rather than computational results. I would present it as a narrow, testable continuous-population extension—not as a ChoCo complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor starts with a mismatch in the kind of result. Theorem 6 is an impossibility theorem about whether a direct mechanism can satisfy five axioms; it gives no computational problem, input encoding, or complexity classification. Theorem 5 characterizes ISPA on the greedy domain, but it also gives no complexity result. A continuous feasibility problem can be built from either theorem, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The proposed mass-allocation model also changes where incentives come from. At a fixed population distribution \(\mu\), one agent in an atomless society has zero mass: changing their report does not change \(\mu\). If the mechanism is specified only by type-level masses \(x_{t,S}\), that deviation cannot change the allocation of other agents. In Theorem 6’s counterexample, bidder 2’s report matters precisely because a finite bidder can alter the outcome facing bidder 1. Requiring a positive mass of agents to deviate instead would impose a group-incentive condition, which the paper does not study. Thus the proposed model does not carry over the strategic question that Theorem 6 answers.

The strongest repair is to use a growing finite population with supplies scaled per capita. But then exact individual incentives at each population size return to the paper’s finite-agent question; taking an atomless limit removes the effect of a single report on aggregate allocations. Preserving strategic influence through coalition deviations or an approximation parameter would define a new problem. A mass-priority version of ISPA faces the same issue: it would have to specify how a type’s mass is split across indivisible bundles and how externality payments work at the resulting thresholds. That could be interesting mechanism design, but it is not a continuization of Theorem 5’s characterization or a complexity result from the paper.

So the case against is strongest as a claim about fit with ChoCo: the proposed question supplies both the computational task and the population-level resource model that the paper itself lacks. It is not strong enough to establish that no worthwhile extension exists in any scenario. A market with many repeated bidder types and per-capita supply could motivate a useful new study; it would need its own strategic model and computational question.
