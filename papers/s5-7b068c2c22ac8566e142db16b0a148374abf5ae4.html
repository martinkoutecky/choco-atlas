The continuous mirror question

Given alternatives \(A=U\cup K\), fixed positive reference weights \(r_k\) for \(k\in K\), and a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of assessor types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), suppose each type reports positive rational ratios \(c^t_{ij}\) on the same comparison set \(E\). If every connected component of the graph on \(U\) has a reported comparison to \(K\), compute the log-priority vector \(z_U\), with \(z_k=\log r_k\), minimizing \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t\sum_{(i,j)\in E:\{i,j\}\cap U\ne\varnothing}(\log c^t_{ij}-(z_i-z_j))^2\), and return the priorities \(w_i=e^{z_i}\) or their ranking.

The model it lives in

Types are complete pairwise-judgment patterns on a shared comparison schedule, with mass \(\mu_t\). Pool judgments by mass-weighted geometric means, keep reference weights fixed, and compute the unknown log priorities by solving the resulting linear least-squares system.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror is a large panel of people making quantitative pairwise comparisons under a shared rubric. For example, residents on a planning platform could compare a fixed shortlist of proposals, including benchmark proposals with established reference weights. The alternatives remain finite; the continuous object is the population of assessors. A type is one complete pattern of pairwise judgments, including which comparisons were made and their ratios. Its mass is the fraction of the panel with that pattern. In a plausible regime, millions of participants produce only a much smaller number \(\tau\) of distinct patterns.

The cleanest anchor is Theorem 23, proved in this paper. It says that when the non-reference alternatives split into connected components under their observed comparisons, with no comparisons between components, the geometric incomplete HRE system has a unique real solution if every component contains an alternative directly compared with at least one reference alternative. Exponentiating that solution gives a unique positive priority vector. This is an existence and uniqueness result, not a named complexity classification.

Here is a continuous population problem built directly around that result: Mass-Aggregated giHRE. An instance has alternatives \(A=U\cup K\), fixed positive reference weights for \(K\), and a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of assessor types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. Assume every type follows the same comparison schedule \(E\), but may report different positive rational comparison ratios \(c^t_{ij}\). For each observed pair, pool the reports by their mass-weighted geometric mean, \(\bar c_{ij}=\prod_{t\in\mathcal T}(c^t_{ij})^{\mu_t}\). Require each connected component among the unknown alternatives \(U\) to have a direct comparison to a reference alternative. The task is to compute the giHRE priority vector for \(\bar C\), keeping the reference weights fixed; a solution is the resulting positive weight for each alternative, or equivalently their ranking.

Theorem 23 gives the intended guarantee for this mirror: under the stated graph condition, the pooled giHRE system has a unique solution. I expect Class A. In log weights, the system is linear and has \(q=|A|\) variables; the rational masses affect its right-hand side through weighted sums of log judgments. A solver can return the log weights as a linear-system solution and the priorities as exponentials of those values. The natural parameters are \(q,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). If a finite panel has \(n_t\) assessors of type \(t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) recovers its population proportions; arbitrary rational masses give the high-multiplicity version of that panel problem.

This mirror covers geometric incomplete HRE priority recovery, not the paper’s arithmetic variant or its other claims. The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time complexity, or parameterized-complexity result to anchor a hardness-dissolution question. The authors’ examples do, however, describe groups of experts or guests supplying comparisons, and the method’s purpose is to turn those comparisons and fixed reference values into a ranking. That makes a population of assessors a plausible extension of their task.

The weak point is that the paper itself models a single comparison matrix, not a distribution over assessors. The shared comparison schedule and geometric pooling are part of the proposed mirror, and the authors might regard this as a group-decision extension rather than their original problem. The case survives if they accept that extension: the masses represent people who make the judgments, and changing those masses can change the collective priorities.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 23 is the proponent’s only anchor, and it is an existence-and-uniqueness theorem, not a computational result. A stronger mirror than the one proposed is easy to formulate: let each assessor type \(t\) supply judgments on the same comparison graph, and define the group’s giHRE vector as the minimizer of the population’s mean squared logarithmic error, with the reference weights fixed. By the paper’s Proposition 25, this reduces to giHRE on the mass-weighted geometric mean of the judgments. It is a coherent group-decision extension, but the continuous population adds no new structure to the computation. The mass-weighted logs can be summed edge by edge, after which the paper’s existing \(q\)-variable linear system is solved. The support size \(\tau\) only affects the cost of forming those sums; it does not change the system or create a new algorithmic question. That makes Theorem 23 a guarantee for the familiar calculation after aggregation, not a computational result about societies.

The deeper concern is whether assessors are the population in the sense this programme studies. The paper takes a comparison matrix as input and estimates priorities for alternatives. Its examples mention experts and guests, but they do not define a population of judgment-makers whose distribution is the object of the problem. Treating their reports as votes and pooling them requires adding a group aggregation rule. Geometric pooling is attractive because it matches giHRE’s error function, but that choice comes from the proposed extension; the paper does not ask how a population should aggregate judgments. If assessors use different comparison schedules, the extension also needs a convention for missing reports—whether to omit them, impute them, or otherwise adjust their influence. The paper’s missing entries instead represent comparisons that were not elicited in the input matrix. Those are different modeling choices.

A large panel could certainly make a continuous assessor model plausible, and the authors might accept it as a useful group-decision application. But the continuous object would then be the distribution of data sources for a new aggregation problem, while the paper’s own problem remains priority estimation from one incomplete matrix with fixed reference values. The proponent’s case is therefore not empty: it identifies a reasonable extension with an immediate solution. Its weak point is that Theorem 23 does not anchor a new computational question about a continuous society, and the paper does not supply the missing population-level aggregation model. I would resist calling this a worthwhile continuous mirror of the paper, while recognizing that this is a judgment about scope, not a proof that no such extension could interest the authors.
