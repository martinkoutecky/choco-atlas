The continuous mirror question

Given rational role shares \(\mu\), a nonnegative rational piecewise-linear worth function \(V\) on \([0,\mu]\) with \(V(0)=0\) that vanishes near the origin, and a rational payoff-rate vector \(p\), determine whether there is an unbounded sequence \(q_j\) with \(q_j\mu_t\) integral for every role \(t\), and grand-coalition kernel allocations \(x^{(q_j)}\) of the corresponding replicated games, such that \(q_j x_i^{(q_j)}\to p_t\) for every player \(i\) of role \(t\).

The model it lives in

A society has finitely many role types with mass distribution \(\mu\); a coalition with role-mass vector \(z\) has worth \(V(z)\). In the \(q\)-replica game each player is atomic with mass \(1/q\), and the limiting object records per-role payoff rates \(p_t=\lim qx_i\).

The objection that survived

The limiting payoff rates may depend on one-player excess differences that vanish at ties or kinks, and the proposal does not establish a finite continuous characterization capturing those effects.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers limiting grand-coalition kernel membership for replicated role populations; it leaves the paper's existence proof, dimension bounds, and general coalition-structure results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the set of limiting payoff rates always admit an effective finite description?
  • When do the replicated kernels converge, rather than merely have cluster points?
  • What bounds relate finite-replica kernels to the limit, especially at ties and kinks?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result about \(P\), NP-hardness, or another complexity class. My strongest honest anchor is the formulation exception in your instructions: Theorem 4.5, proved here, gives a representation that the authors use to compute kernels.

In full, Theorem 4.5 says that the kernel is the union over all profile-generating sequences \(\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_{n-1}\) of the intersection of their payoff regions. At each stage, a sequence chooses families of coalitions for the current player classes. Each region requires that players separated out by a chosen family receive zero, and that every coalition in that family attain maximum excess among the coalitions crossing the relevant class. The paper makes this representation concrete in its kernel computations in §10. This is a computational formulation, not an efficiency theorem; its union can be enormous.

My lead mirror is Replication-Limit Kernel Membership. It models a large cooperative workforce with a fixed number \(\tau\) of roles, such as occupations with identical skills, costs, and bargaining attributes within each role. Let \(T\) be the roles, and let \(\mu_t>0\) be each role’s share of the workforce, with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). The regime is \(q\) workers with \(q\mu_t\) workers of role \(t\): \(q\) grows while \(\tau\) stays moderate.

The instance consists of rational \(\mu\), a nonnegative rational piecewise-linear worth function \(V\) on \([0,\mu]\), and a rational candidate payoff-rate vector \(p\). Assume \(V(0)=0\) and \(V\) is zero near the origin, so sufficiently small singleton coalitions have the paper’s required value zero. For each sufficiently large \(q\) with \(q\mu_t\) integral, form the finite game \(G_q\) with \(q\mu_t\) workers of each role. A coalition \(S\) has mass vector \(z_t(S)=|S\cap N_t|/q\) and worth \(V(z(S))\). Compute the paper’s grand-coalition kernel of \(G_q\). If worker \(i\) of role \(t\) receives payoff \(x_i\), record the per-unit-mass rate \(q x_i\). Identical workers receive equal kernel payoffs: Theorem 9.3’s desirability result gives this for symmetric players.

The question is whether \(p\) is a limit of those per-unit-mass payoff vectors along some sequence \(q_j\to\infty\). A yes-solution supplies such a sequence of kernel allocations; a no-solution rules out every such sequence. This is a feasibility question, like the kernel computation itself, with no separate optimization objective. The individual payoff scales as \(p_t/q\), while role \(t\)’s total payoff scales as \(\mu_t p_t\).

I think authors could recognize this as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime: every finite approximating game is exactly a cooperative game with their kernel, and the limit asks which per-capita kernel allocations persist as the population grows. The continuous object is the society’s role-mass distribution and its coalition worth function. It does not rely on fractional outcomes or on continuizing time.

For fixed \(\tau\) and an explicit piecewise-linear subdivision for \(V\), I would initially expect Class A: the excess calculations reduce to fixed-dimensional piecewise-linear optimization, with the asymptotic comparisons concentrated near faces of the mass polytope. That is a conjecture, not a result of this paper. With variable \(\tau\), the number of regions and their interactions could make the problem hard. Questions for a prover include whether the limit set always has a finite polyhedral description, when the finite kernels converge rather than merely have cluster points, and what error bounds relate finite \(q\) to the limit.

The weakest point is that a direct atomless kernel can lose the paper’s central pairwise comparison: a single worker has zero mass, so including or excluding that worker may not change any coalition’s worth. My mirror forms the kernel in each finite replication before taking the limit, preserving those one-worker comparisons in the construction, though some may disappear in the limit. If they all wash out in important regimes, this mirror may be too weak to interest the authors. It remains a specific, testable high-multiplicity version of their kernel computation, and it covers that problem alone—not the paper’s existence proof or dimension bounds.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s strongest computational anchor is Theorem 4.5, but it is a representation theorem: it expresses the finite-player kernel as a union of polyhedra indexed by profiles. It gives no algorithm or complexity bound for computing that union. The paper does apply the representation to finite-game computations in §10, but “compute the kernel” is not a named computational result whose complexity the continuous mirror would classify.

The proposed replication-limit question is a substantial new problem: given \((\mu,V,p)\), decide whether \(p\) is a limit point of kernels of replicated finite games. It may be worth studying, but the paper neither asks whether those kernels converge nor identifies that limit as a computational problem. It is an asymptotic extension of the authors’ object, rather than a continuization of a computational theorem they establish.

There is also a real modeling singularity. In an atomless society, adding or excluding one particular player does not change a coalition’s type-mass vector. The kernel’s comparison between a player who outweighs another and the reverse depends on precisely those individual membership distinctions. At the level of coalition masses, the two maximum-excess calculations can collapse to the same optimization, leaving the defining pairwise test without its intended content.

The replica construction preserves that test by retaining \(1/q\)-sized individual changes at every finite \(q\). A more direct repair would use directional marginal worth or a derivative-based kernel. Either could be a coherent new model, but its conditions would have to recover the finite kernel’s one-player comparisons from first-order behavior, including at ties and kinks. That is the strongest objection to the better mirror: the paper’s central structure lives at individual granularity, while a continuous population records mass. The replica-limit proposal keeps the granularity; the atomless proposal risks losing it.

This makes the negative case narrower than the requested universal claim. A large workforce with repeated roles and coalition worth determined by role composition is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and Maschler and Peleg might well recognize the study of its limiting kernel as their problem. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror there. The defensible case against is that the paper offers no named complexity result to mirror, and that its kernel’s defining comparisons do not pass directly to a population distribution without additional limiting structure.
