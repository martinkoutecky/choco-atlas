The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Propositions 1–3 characterize equilibria of a finite voting game; none asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, fixed parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally classified.

The nearest result is Proposition 3, proved in the paper: the game always has two forms of fully symmetric equilibrium—unanimous support for a party, and interior threshold equilibria in which voters support A when their policy value \(\varepsilon\) exceeds a common threshold. That threshold is pinned down by the voters’ outcome and prize pivotality. This is a game theoretic existence and characterization result, not a computational result, so it cannot honestly serve as an anchor under the requested standard.

The strongest plausible population regime is still easy to identify: three stable, observable electoral blocs, each containing many residents who share a bloc and a policy-preference type \((g,\varepsilon)\). The society would be a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with the number of people much larger than the number of recurring types. Each type chooses probabilities of voting for A, voting for B, or abstaining. The paper’s group-level prizes and vote-count rule carry over naturally as aggregate vote shares.

An exploratory problem would be Atomless Contingent-Prize Equilibrium: given \(\mu\), the parties’ policy and prize values, the voting cost, and the paper’s prize-allocation rule, find a symmetric Nash strategy for each type and return an exact verification of the equilibrium conditions. Its solution is a strategy profile, not an optimized objective. The expected result is tractability, but for a damaging reason: in a nonatomic society, one voter’s deviation changes neither the election result nor their group’s prize share. With a positive voting cost, abstention is optimal; without one, voters are indifferent. The problem therefore loses the pivotal incentives that Proposition 3 describes.

That is the weakest point, and it is central. The bloc scenario makes high multiplicity credible, and a continuum of policy types looks like a natural extension of the paper’s distribution \(F\). But the continuous limit removes individual prize pivotality—the paper’s main mechanism. Keeping that mechanism requires retaining finite vote granularity, or changing the solution concept to let blocs coordinate their mass. Either move would weaken the claim that this is the continuous form of the authors’ problem.

So the positive case is limited: the paper offers a compelling high-multiplicity *setting* for a possible mirror, but no named computational result to anchor one, and the most direct atomless mirror makes its central equilibrium incentives disappear.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 3 is not a computational result: it establishes equilibrium existence and forms, but gives no complexity classification of a problem. More importantly, the proposed equilibrium-computation mirror loses the mechanism that makes the proposition interesting.

In an atomless society, one voter’s choice changes neither the election result nor their group’s relative support, and so cannot change the group’s chance of winning a prize. With a positive voting cost, abstention is optimal; without one, voters are indifferent among actions. Computing an equilibrium then returns a trivial or uninformative answer, rather than the threshold equilibria driven by outcome and prize pivotality in Proposition 3.

A stronger mirror could keep finite electorates with many repeated types and ask how their equilibrium thresholds behave as population size grows. But the relevant incentives then depend on the probability that a single vote changes the result or prize allocation. Those are finite-vote fluctuations, not properties of the mass distribution alone. Preserving them requires retaining finite counts or adding a stochastic correction; taking the atomless limit removes them. At the unanimity equilibria, the problem is especially stark: a one-vote deviation can break a prize tie in every finite game, while a single voter has zero mass in the limit.

One could instead let whole blocs coordinate changes in their voting mass, but that changes the strategic actors and solution concept. It is a different computational game, not a continuous-population version of the paper’s individual-voter equilibrium. The negative case is strongest for the equilibrium results: this paper offers a plausible high-multiplicity setting, but its prize-pivotal mechanism appears to require the very individual granularity continuization removes.
