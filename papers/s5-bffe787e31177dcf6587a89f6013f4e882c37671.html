The continuous mirror question

Given a unit-mass continuum of identical firms, a symmetric worth function \(v(a,\mathcal P)\), and a belief distribution \(h_a\) over mass partitions containing a coalition of mass \(a\), determine whether there is a measurable allocation \(x\) of grand-coalition worth \(W\) such that every measurable coalition \(A\) satisfies \(\int_A x(i)\,di\geq b(|A|)\), where \(b(a)=\mathbb E_{\mathcal P\sim h_a}[v(a,\mathcal P)]\).

The model it lives in

One complete type of identical firms carries total mass one. A coalition is a measurable set of mass \(a\), its belief is a distribution over mass partitions of the outsiders, and the decision variable is an integrable allocation \(x\) with total worth \(W\); the objective is to find an allocation immune to every coalition’s expected-worth objection.

The objection that survived

Symmetry reduces the proposed continuum core test to checking \(b(a)\leq aW\) for each coalition mass \(a\), so the mirror may amount to an envelope characterization rather than a rich computational problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the core nonemptiness conclusions of Propositions 1 and 2 as mass-population questions; it does not directly transfer their finite-player induction or belief restrictions across integer population sizes.

Open questions for a prover

  • What mass-based belief restrictions correspond to the admissibility conditions in Propositions 1 and 2?
  • What finite representation of \(v\) and \(h_a\) makes checking the continuum core condition computationally meaningful?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper to anchor a ChoCo mirror. The strongest nearby results are Propositions 1 and 2, both proved here, but they establish core nonemptiness, not that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. Proposition 1 says that in a symmetric finite-player game with positive externalities, if \(V^{\gamma_{n+1}}(S)\le \frac{n}{n+1}V^{\gamma_n}(S)\), every coalition has admissible beliefs, and singleton coalitions’ \(h_{3,S}(1)\) is sufficiently low, then the core is nonempty. Proposition 2 gives the same conclusion when the inequality is reversed, provided beliefs are \(\mathcal R\)-admissible instead. Both are existence results proved by induction.

The best positive seed is a continuum-population core problem for a large industry of ex ante identical firms. Give the industry total mass one. A coalition is a measurable set \(A\) of firms, its size is its mass \(|A|\), and a partition describes the masses of the coalitions formed by it and the outsiders. The instance specifies normalized worths \(v(|A|,\mathcal P)\) and, for each coalition, a belief distribution \(h_A\) over partitions containing it. The question is whether there is an integrable allocation \(x\) satisfying
\[ \int_0^1 x(i)\,di=v(1,\{\,[0,1]\,\}) \]
and, for every positive-mass coalition \(A\),
\[ \int_A x(i)\,di\ \geq\ \mathbb E_{\mathcal P\sim h_A}[v(|A|,\mathcal P)]. \]
A solution is such an allocation: no coalition blocks it under its beliefs. This uses one complete type—identical payoff-relevant firms—with a continuum of mass. The paper’s symmetry and its concern with games having many players make this a plausible research extension; Propositions 1 and 2 suggest that, under suitable scaled belief conditions, the equal per-capita allocation might be in the core.

That is the positive case’s limit. The propositions do not establish a continuous-population theorem, much less the complexity of deciding or finding such an allocation. Their beliefs are probabilities over partitions of a finite player set; those probabilities do not make the society itself continuous. A finite representation of the continuum instance and corresponding algorithmic question would also need to be supplied. So this is a credible analytic extension to ask the authors about, but it is not an anchored computational mirror of the results in this paper. The main soft spot is therefore also decisive for the requested scope: the paper offers a natural large-population motivation, but no named computational result for the continuous programme to investigate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper gives ChoCo no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 prove that specified restrictions on beliefs imply a nonempty core. They do not classify a computational problem: there is no input encoding, algorithm, or complexity claim. So the proposed continuum core problem is a plausible new analytic extension, but it is not a computational mirror of either proposition.

The proposed industry of identical firms is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; that point supports the model rather than undermining it. Grant the strongest version of the extension: use coalition mass, allow beliefs over partitions of outsider mass, and scale worths so they remain meaningful in a continuum. If the induced game stays symmetric, let \(b(s)\) be the expected worth of any coalition of mass \(s\), and let the grand coalition’s worth be \(W\). Core existence then reduces to checking \(b(s)\leq sW\) for every \(s\in(0,1)\). Necessity follows by averaging the core inequalities over coalitions of mass \(s\); sufficiency follows from the equal allocation \(x(i)=W\). The continuum core question is an envelope condition over coalition sizes. The paper neither asks nor solves the computational problem of evaluating that envelope.

One could make the extension computationally harder by encoding \(b\), or by allowing coalition beliefs and worths to vary across types or identities. But then the research must supply that encoding and the resulting algorithmic question. Type or identity variation also adds the structure that drives the computation; it does not come from continuizing the paper’s symmetric-player results. The finite induction and its belief restrictions may inspire a new continuum theorem, but the paper gives no computational result for that theorem to extend.

The negative case is strongest on fit with ChoCo’s computational remit. It does not rule out a worthwhile analytic continuum paper on this model; it says that such a paper would need to create its own computational target, rather than inherit one from this paper.
