The case FOR (proponent)

A credible population regime is a large election with millions of voters but relatively few distinct ballot types. Let each type \(t\) be a complete description of a ballot—including any truncation or ties—and let \(\mu_t\) be its share of the electorate. From the finite support of this distribution, form the pairwise scores \(v_{xy}=\sum_t \mu_t\,p_t(x\succ y)\), using the paper’s conventions for expressed preferences. The mass is the share of voters with each ballot type; the output is the CLC-Zermelo vector of fractions allocated to the options. This is close to the authors’ stated purpose of determining mixing fractions from relative preference scores.

A precise follow-on question would be: given \(m\), rational masses for \(\tau\) ballot types, and a rational tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\), compute a vector within \(\varepsilon\) of the CLC-Zermelo fractions for the resulting Llull matrix. A solution is such an approximating vector. Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper, establishes continuity of those fractions with respect to the original Llull matrix, which motivates this question. I would tentatively expect tractability: the population can be aggregated into pairwise scores, and the Zermelo step has a convex likelihood formulation on the top dominant component. The paper establishes no runtime or approximation guarantee for that pipeline, however, so this is a proposed complexity question, not a result of the paper.

There are no eligible computational anchors here: the paper has no numbered result asserting that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or in another complexity class. Theorem 2.1 characterizes the Zermelo solution and its smoothness; Theorem 4.4 proves continuity; the remaining named results establish properties of the rating method. None analyzes computational complexity. The proposed question would mirror the computation underlying those results, but it cannot serve as the required per-anchor mirror.

The weakest point is therefore decisive for this assignment: the population mirror is plausible and specific, but the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor the requested complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its theorems establish properties of the CLC-Zermelo rating—existence, continuity, decomposition, and majority consistency—not the complexity of computing it. The iterative scheme in §2 gives a way to seek Zermelo strengths, but no runtime or approximation guarantee. The proponent acknowledges this, so their proposed computation cannot serve as a surviving result-specific anchor.

The strongest version of their proposal is still clear: given rational masses on ballot types, compute the CLC-Zermelo fractions to a specified precision. But the paper’s input is already the normalized Llull matrix \(v_{xy}=V_{xy}/V\), and its output is already a vector of fractions. A ballot distribution feeds into the rule only through those \(O(m^2)\) pairwise scores. Aggregating type masses therefore produces the paper’s existing input; the support size \(\tau\) adds a preprocessing step, not a new computational object the paper analyzes. Theorem 4.4 says the output varies continuously with the scores. It does not give a complexity bound, or the uniform precision control needed to infer one.

That leaves a plausible standalone question about the complexity of evaluating this rating rule. It may be worth studying. But it is a new algorithmic analysis of a method the paper defines on normalized scores, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper. The negative case is strong on that assignment criterion; the broader claim that no such algorithmic question could ever be worthwhile is weaker.
