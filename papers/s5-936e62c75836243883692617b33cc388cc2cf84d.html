The continuous mirror question

Given \(C\), \(q_I\), a finite support of types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), responsive course rankings \(\succ_t\), rational bids \(b_{tc}\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_c\), with each type's mass distributed uniformly over a shared atomless tie-breaking rank \(r\in[0,1]\), compute a measurable assignment \(S(t,r)\subseteq C\) of integral schedules, with \(|S(t,r)|\le q_I\) and mass assigned to each course \(c\) at most \(\kappa_c\), that is stable under bid-and-rank priorities and weakly Pareto-dominates every stable assignment; determine whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\).

The model it lives in

Types carry course rankings and bid vectors with masses \(\mu_t\); each unit receives an integral schedule, and a shared atomless rank \(r\) resolves positive-mass bid ties. The decision variable is the measurable assignment \(S(t,r)\), summarized by \(x_{t,S}=\mu_t\lambda\{r:S(t,r)=S\}\), subject to per-capita course capacities \(\kappa_c\). The objective is a student-optimal stable assignment.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a narrow but credible positive case. This paper gives no named complexity-class result such as NP-hardness or membership in P. Its best named algorithmic anchor is Proposition 3, proved here: when students have responsive preferences over schedules, the paper’s Gale–Shapley Pareto-Dominant Market Mechanism produces a matching \(\mu_{\mathrm{GS}}\) that Pareto-dominates every other matching that is a market outcome for the same bids and preferences. This is a guarantee about the matching computed by the mechanism, not a runtime bound.

Lead mirror: Continuous student-optimal stable course allocation. Picture a large university elective market with thousands of students and a relatively small set of recurring preference-and-bid templates—for example, students in the same degree track using the same course ranking and bid plan. A student’s type records their complete course ranking, bid vector, and maximum course load. Let \(\mu_t\) be the rational fraction of students of type \(t\), and let \(\kappa_c\) be course \(c\)’s capacity per student in the population. The intended regime is \(N\) in the thousands and a much smaller base support \(\tau\), perhaps dozens or hundreds of templates; those are illustrative scale assumptions, not measured facts about a particular school.

An instance consists of a finite course set \(C\), a common course-load limit \(q_I\), the type distribution \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\), each type’s strict responsive course ranking \(\succ_t\), rational bids \(b_{tc}\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_c\). The decision is an assignment \(S(t,r)\subseteq C\) for each unit of population, with \(|S(t,r)|\le q_I\). Here \(r\in[0,1]\) is an atomless lottery rank used to break equal-bid ties, as in the paper’s tie-breaking rule. Each person receives an integral schedule; the mass assigned to schedules is fractional. In particular, \(x_{t,S}=\mu_t\cdot\lambda\{r:S(t,r)=S\}\), with
\[ \sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:c\in S}x_{t,S}\le \kappa_c. \]

Call an assignment stable if, for almost every \((t,r)\), there is no course \(c\) that the student would add to an incomplete schedule or use to replace a less-preferred course, while \(c\) has spare capacity or a seat held by someone with lower priority under the course’s bid-and-lottery order. The requested solution is a stable assignment that gives each student a schedule at least as preferred as their schedule in every other stable assignment. The output can be represented by the masses \(x_{t,S}\), with rank subdivisions where needed. Its objective is student-optimality in the Pareto sense, not a scalar score.

I expect this mass version to be Class A: the paper’s own route is student-proposing deferred acceptance, and responsive preferences give the structure needed to aggregate proposals and rejections by type. The open algorithmic question is whether that aggregation has a compact representation and runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), rather than in the number of students \(N\). A successful proof would turn Proposition 3’s finite matching guarantee into a high-multiplicity algorithm.

The mirror covers Proposition 3’s responsive-preference course-allocation result. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s strategic analysis of bids or its results for non-substitutable preferences. Further questions include whether the mass assignment can be supported by a natural per-capita price vector, and whether the aggregate deferred-acceptance computation has polynomially many breakpoints.

The weakest point is the tie issue. Repeated students of one type also repeat their bids, creating positive-mass bid ties, while the paper assumes no positive ties between students. The lottery coordinate makes the mass matching well-defined, but it is an added continuum detail, and the price-support result may need a tie-aware equilibrium notion. This remains recognizably the paper’s course-matching problem because preferences, bids, capacity constraints, and its deferred-acceptance logic are preserved. The case is therefore a plausible algorithmic mirror, though it does not supply the discrete hardness anchor that would make a “does hardness dissolve?” question available.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Proposition 3 is not a complexity result. It says the deferred acceptance outcome Pareto-dominates other market outcomes; the paper gives the algorithm producing that outcome but does not study its computational complexity. The proposed mirror therefore changes the question: it asks whether deferred acceptance can be compressed for a succinct, high-multiplicity input. That could be worthwhile, but it is a new algorithmic question prompted by the paper, not a continuous version of a complexity result the paper establishes.

There is a real modeling wrinkle in the proposed version too. Repeated types have identical bids, so the paper’s lottery tie-breaker matters on positive mass. To preserve its common priority order across courses, the model needs the lottery rank \(r\). If the output records only masses \(x_{t,S}\), it may lose which portions of a tied type had priority; if it retains \(r\), it has continuous within-type variation rather than just \(\tau\) types. A cutoff-based representation might handle that cleanly, so this is a challenge for the proposed algorithm, not a reason the mirror is ill-posed.

Most other objections do not hold. Large cohorts with repeated preference-and-bid templates and per-capita course capacities are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Fractional masses can still represent people receiving integral schedules, and the paper’s result is conditional on bids, so fixing bids in the type description does not by itself discard its anchor. The proponent’s case survives those tests.

So the negative case is weak. Its best point is the gap between a welfare theorem with an explicit deferred acceptance mechanism and a new complexity question about compressing that mechanism. That may not be enough to reject the mirror: a continuous student-optimal stable-allocation problem is coherent, and its algorithmic answer could be useful. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
