What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 and the other numbered results characterize disclosure equilibria, effort incentives, and deliberation protocols; none states a computational result. Appendix B.1 makes outcomes continuous while keeping the team finite, not the population.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population-level version of this model, but the paper supplies no named computational-complexity result to anchor it. Its Theorem 1, proved in Appendix A.2, says that a full-disclosure equilibrium always exists; a partial-disclosure equilibrium exists exactly when at least one team member cannot unilaterally choose disclosure; and, if no member can choose disclosure unilaterally, every partial-disclosure equilibrium is interior, whereas if some member can, none is interior. That is an equilibrium characterization, not a result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The other numbered results are also economic or equilibrium statements. Appendix B.1’s continuous outcomes change the outcome space while keeping the team finite; they do not continuize the population.

The strongest plausible setting for a *new* mirror is a large cross-functional project consortium with many contributors but relatively few recurring roles. A type would have to include everything relevant to a contributor’s preferences and incentives: role, reputational value of outcomes, disclosure incentives, and effort cost. Mass \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of contributors of type \(t\). Given type-specific outcome signals and a disclosure quorum \(q\), type members use disclosure strategies; the team discloses when the mass favoring disclosure reaches \(q\). A natural computational question would be: given rational type masses, finite-support outcome laws conditional on effort, costs, and \(q\), find Bayes-consistent threshold-disclosure equilibria and determine whether one supports full effort. The solution would be the equilibrium strategies, no-disclosure beliefs, and effort choices—not an invented social objective. This preserves the paper’s central mechanism of reputationally motivated disclosure through a team protocol, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as their model in a large-team regime.

But that question is not a mirror of a qualifying anchor in this paper, and I cannot responsibly assign it to one of the programme’s complexity classes on the paper’s evidence.

The main weakness is strategic: in a nonatomic population, one contributor’s vote has zero effect on the quorum, while the paper’s pivotality and coalitional-deviation reasoning drives its equilibrium results. Making positive-mass types act as coordinated voting blocs could recover pivotality, but that changes the strategic model. So the honest positive case is that the paper offers a promising setting for a future continuous-population computational study—not an existing computational result whose hardness or tractability can be continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that Theorem 1 is not a computational result. It characterizes equilibria for a finite team: full disclosure always exists, while partial disclosure depends on whether members can disclose unilaterally. The paper gives no computational problem, input encoding, algorithm, or complexity classification to continuize. Appendix B.1 makes each member’s outcome continuous while keeping the team finite; it does not supply a population mirror.

A large consortium with recurring roles is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Types could include each contributor’s role, reputational incentives, and effort cost. I would not reject that scenario on the grounds that team members must remain individually named. The harder problem is that the paper’s mechanism relies on pivotal members and coalitions changing the disclosure decision. With a nonatomic population and a quorum based on vote share, one person’s vote changes the share by zero. Their disclosure choice no longer affects the decision or the observer’s beliefs. In a finite sequence of larger teams, individual pivotality can likewise vanish in the limit.

One could preserve strategic influence by treating each type as a coordinated bloc, or by adding a coalition or communication mechanism. But then the model must explain how members with different realized outcomes coordinate their votes and how the bloc’s decision is made. That is a new strategic structure, not a population version of the paper’s deliberation protocol. The same issue reaches the effort results: a type-level share of effort could have a nonzero effect on outcomes, but the paper’s individual effort incentives need a new scaling and equilibrium formulation as team size grows.

So the proposed task—computing Bayes-consistent mass-threshold equilibria and identifying when they support full effort—could become a worthwhile research question. The paper does not provide a computational result that makes it a mirror of this paper, though, and a direct continuum version risks eliminating the pivotality that drives its main results. I would argue against counting this as a ChoCo mirror, while conceding that the stronger claim that no useful extension exists in any scenario is not established.
