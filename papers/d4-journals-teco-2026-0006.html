The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(I\) and \(J\), rational masses \(\mu_v\ge 0\) for \(v\in I\cup J\) summing to \(1\), a compatibility graph \(E\subseteq I\times J\) partitioned into \(E_1\) and \(E_2\), and complete weak preferences over compatible types and being unmatched (with every compatible type preferred to being unmatched), decide whether a stable mass assignment exists and construct one if it does. A feasible assignment has variables \(x_e\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{e\in E(v)}x_e\le\mu_v\); let \(r_v=\mu_v-\sum_{f\in E(v)}x_f\), \(W_v(e)=r_v+\sum_{f\in E(v):\,e\succeq_v f}x_f\), and \(S_v(e)=\sum_{f\in E(v):\,e\succ_v f}x_f\). For \(e=\{u,v\}\in E_1\), it is blocking exactly when \(W_u(e)>0\), \(W_v(e)>0\), and \(W_u(e)>x_e\) or \(W_v(e)>x_e\); for \(e\in E_2\), it is blocking exactly when both weakly willing masses are positive and \(S_u(e)>0\) or \(S_v(e)>0\). Ask for complexity in the number of positive-mass types, \(|E|\), and the binary input length \(L\).

The model it lives in

An atomless two-sided market with finitely many types, type masses \(\mu_v\), and type-level preferences. Decision variables \(x_e\) give the mass matched along each compatible edge; each individual still receives at most one partner. Stability uses the corrected positive-mass blocking tests stated in the mirror question.

The objection that survived

Theorem 1 relies on integral matching and Hall-deficiency structure; the corrected positive-mass blocking tests introduce equality conditions, and expanding rational masses into clones may require a number of agents exponential in their binary encoding length.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for a finite bipartite graph whose edges are partitioned into \(E_1\) and \(E_2\), with each vertex having a complete, transitive preference order over its neighbors and being unmatched, there is a polynomial-time algorithm that either finds a non-uniformly stable matching or reports that none exists. Here stability means that no edge in \(E_1\) weakly blocks the matching and no edge in \(E_2\) strongly blocks it. The paper proves this result; it is not merely a cited theorem. The paper offers no comparable hardness result, so this algorithmic anchor is the right one to use.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large centralized one-to-one placement market: many candidates and one-seat positions, with both sides drawn from a modest set of recurring preference and eligibility profiles. A type records the side, acceptable counterpart types, the ranking of those types, and the applicable blocking category. That is a complete description for this problem: people sharing a type are indistinguishable in every respect the matching and stability conditions use. The number of people can be very large while the number \(\tau\) of positive-mass types is much smaller. This is a property of that market regime, not of matching markets in general.

I would ask the authors to study Continuum Non-uniform Stable Matching. An instance consists of finite type sets \(I\) and \(J\) on the two sides; nonnegative rational masses \(a_i\) and \(b_j\), summing to one across both sides; a compatibility graph \(E\subseteq I\times J\); a partition \(E=E_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}E_2\); and complete, transitive weak preferences of each type over its compatible counterpart types and being unmatched. As in the paper, every compatible edge is preferred to being unmatched.

A solution is a nonnegative flow \(x_e\) on compatible type pairs, with total incident flow at each type at most its mass. The flow is an aggregate assignment: every person still receives at most one partner, while a type’s mass can be split among counterpart types. Because the populations are atomless, such a flow can be realized as a measurable matching of individuals.

For a type \(v\), let \(r_v\) be its unmatched mass. For an incident edge \(e\), define
\[ W_v(e;x)=r_v+\sum_{\substack{f\in E(v)\\e\succeq_v f}}x_f, \qquad S_v(e;x)=r_v+\sum_{\substack{f\in E(v)\\e\succ_v f}}x_f. \]
These are the masses at \(v\) who weakly, or strictly, prefer \(e\) to their current assignment. An edge \(e=\{u,v\}\in E_1\) blocks if \(W_u(e;x)>0\) and \(W_v(e;x)>0\). An edge in \(E_2\) blocks if both weakly willing masses are positive and at least one of \(S_u(e;x)\) or \(S_v(e;x)\) is positive. Atomlessness matters here: positive willing masses contain individuals who are not already matched to each other. The problem is to decide whether a stable flow exists and, if so, output one. The complexity question should be stated in \(\tau\), \(|E|\), and the binary input length \(L\), rather than in the total population represented by the masses.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). Theorem 1’s algorithm is built around matching and Hall-deficiency structure, including maximum matchings and minimal minimizers of submodular functions. That gives a concrete reason to investigate a capacity or flow version with rational masses: the corresponding operations may be implementable directly on the type graph, without expanding each mass into individual agents. This is a research prediction, not a consequence already proved by Theorem 1. A central question is whether the algorithm extends with running time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|E|\), and \(L\), or whether the positive-mass blocking conditions introduce new difficulty.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s existence-and-construction result, not the paper’s full polyhedral and lattice results. Theorem 2, for example, characterizes the convex hull of discrete non-uniformly stable matchings; that convex hull is not automatically the set of stable mass flows above. Further questions are whether there is a precise discrete-to-continuous dictionary at rational mass vectors, whether the weighted algorithm is polynomial in the bit lengths of the masses, and which extensions to capacities or many-to-one matching preserve tractability.

My weakest point is the treatment of ties among people of the same type. In a continuum, positive pools of mutually willing people can block even when the corresponding finite market would have only a tiny number of such people—or when the relevant pair depends on individual identities that the type representation omits. The proposed question is therefore a credible aggregate high-multiplicity analogue, but its relationship to finite instances needs to be made formal. That is a real limitation, not a reason to dismiss the mirror: the paper already studies ties and blocking, and the proposed problem preserves those conditions while making population mass the object of computation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational anchor: it proves a polynomial-time existence-and-construction result, and a large placement market with recurring preference profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. I don’t see an honest way to argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The strongest case against is narrower: the proponent’s proposed mass formulation is not yet correct, and the corrected problem may depart sharply from the structure that makes this paper’s result work.

For an edge \(e=\{u,v\}\in E_1\), their blocking test counts all mass weakly willing to take \(e\), including mass already matched along \(e\). In the simplest instance—one compatible edge, equal masses, and all mass matched on that edge—the test says \(e\) blocks, although every willing person is already paired with the only possible partner. Atomlessness does not fix this: the willing pools can be exactly the two sets paired to each other. A correct pairwise blocking test must account for that matched mass. If \(W_u,W_v\) are the weakly willing masses and \(x_e\) is the mass matched along \(e\), then, under the proposed type assumptions, the edge blocks when both \(W_u,W_v\) are positive and they are not both exactly \(x_e\). The strong-blocking test avoids this particular problem when one side has positive strictly willing mass, since those people cannot already be paired along \(e\).

That repair leaves a coherent mirror, though. With complete types and an atomless population, the flow records the mass paired along each type edge, so the corrected blocker test can be stated from the aggregate data. And collapsing a market to recurring preferences over partner types is allowed by the programme’s definition of a type; it is not inherently a loss of identity information. A large placement market can plausibly have such repeated profiles.

The more substantial objection is that the corrected stability condition is driven by exact zeroes and equalities of masses, rather than by the linear blocking inequalities in the paper’s polyhedral characterization. Theorem 1’s Hall-deficiency and matching arguments therefore do not immediately yield an algorithm polynomial in the number of types and the bit length of the masses. Scaling rational masses to a finite clone market gives a dictionary, but the required number of clones can be large relative to the binary input. The mirror is a genuine new computational question; the paper does not already answer it.

That is not enough to reject the anchor. The discrete results for many-to-many markets and the paper’s polyhedral work make the mirror look related to existing matching machinery, but related work is not a collision, and the population-compression question remains. My negative case can reject the proposed formulation as written and question how much of the paper’s proof survives. It cannot defeat the better, corrected mirror or establish that it is not worth asking.
