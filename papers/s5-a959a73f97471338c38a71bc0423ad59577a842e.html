The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) goods, population scale \(N\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and value oracles for W-MRS valuations \(v_t\), choose \(x_t\in[0,1]^m\) subject to \(\sum_t N\mu_t x_{tj}\le 1\). With each type-\(t\) agent independently receiving good \(j\) with probability \(1-e^{-x_{tj}}\), can one compute an \(x\) in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log N,1/\epsilon\) such that its expected welfare is at least \(1-\epsilon\) times the best welfare in this product-distribution range and at least \(1-1/e-\epsilon\) times the optimum over all feasible masses assigned to integral bundles?

The model it lives in

A society has type \(t\) mass \(N\mu_t\), with identical W-MRS valuation \(v_t\) within each type. Decisions assign mass to integral bundles, with total mass receiving each good at most one; the paper’s product range is represented by \(x_t\), with expected welfare \(\sum_t N\mu_t\,\mathbb{E}[v_t(\hat p_t)]\) and per-capita supply \(1/N\).

The objection that survived

With one unit of each good and population scale \(N\), inclusion probabilities shrink as \(O(1/N)\), so mass receiving multiple goods vanishes and the welfare benchmark reduces to per-good singleton choices; the proponent does not directly rebut this degeneracy.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.5’s allocation rule and welfare guarantee. It leaves Theorem 1.6’s approximate-truthfulness transformation and Theorem 4.1’s lottery-query hardness outside the surviving mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the type-compressed product-range objective be optimized to the stated guarantees using time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log N,1/\epsilon\)?
  • How does the finite-\(N\) welfare gap from the per-good singleton limit depend on \(N\), the type masses, and the valuations?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the continuous mirror of Theorem 1.5. The paper proves it here: for every \(\epsilon=1/\mathrm{poly}(m,n)\), a polynomial-time value-oracle algorithm gives a \((1-\epsilon)\)-MIDR allocation rule and a \((1-1/e-\epsilon)\)-approximation to social welfare for combinatorial auctions with weighted-matroid-rank-sum bidders.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large marketplace assigning bundles of resources to many customers. Customers with the same complete valuation function form a type; there are \(N\) customers but only \(\tau\ll N\) supported types, with type \(t\) having fraction \(\mu_t\). Think, for example, of many accounts with the same coverage or resource-substitution needs. Each customer receives an integral bundle, while the mass assigned to each bundle can be fractional. With one unit of each good available, the per-capita supply is \(1/N\). That supply scaling is part of the mirror: keeping a finite population while making the population continuous requires a type-level allocation of each scarce good.

Call the resulting problem Continuous W-MRS Product-Range Welfare. Its input is \(m\) goods, population scale \(N\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and a value oracle for each supported type’s W-MRS valuation \(v_t\). For a type \(t\), choose \(x_t\in[0,1]^m\), subject to
\[ \sum_t N\mu_t x_{tj}\le 1\qquad\text{for each good }j. \]
Set \(p_{tj}=1-e^{-x_{tj}}\), and let \(\hat p_t\) be the bundle formed by independently including each good \(j\) with probability \(p_{tj}\). The objective is
\[ W(x)=\sum_t N\mu_t\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[v_t(\hat p_t)\right]. \]
The solution is a feasible choice of \(x\), equivalently the induced type-level masses assigned to integral bundles. The constraints ensure that total mass receiving any good is at most one.

The continuous problem asks for \(x\) that is within a \(1-\epsilon\) factor of the optimum over this product-distribution range and achieves at least \(1-1/e-\epsilon\) times the full continuous welfare optimum. Define that benchmark by type-to-bundle masses \(\lambda_{t,S}\ge0\), with \(\sum_S\lambda_{t,S}=N\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}\lambda_{t,S}\le1\), and welfare \(\sum_{t,S}\lambda_{t,S}v_t(S)\). This is a specific welfare-allocation question, not a continuous-outcome or truthfulness question.

I expect this mirror to be tractable at the paper’s approximation guarantee. The paper’s concave objective and local-search method group naturally by type: identical bidders contribute the same objective term, and the constraints aggregate their \(x\)-coordinates. A prover should investigate a runtime polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,\log N,1/\epsilon\), where \(L\) includes the rational input and value representation. The authors should recognize this as their allocation problem over the same product-distribution range, with identical bidders compressed into masses. It does not claim that exact discrete welfare maximization becomes easy.

A second, narrower anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved here. It shows that, for a class of succinctly represented matroids, computing the expected rank of a uniformly random subset is \(\#\mathrm P\)-hard; with only an independence oracle, computing that expectation requires exponentially many queries.

Its continuous-population mirror is Continuous Lottery-Value Aggregation. Given a supported distribution \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) over matroid-rank valuation types \(M_t\), a common rational vector \(x\in[0,1]^m\), and independent random subsets \(R_t\) formed by including each good with probability \(x_j\), compute exactly
\[ Q(\mu,x)=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbb{E}[r_{M_t}(R_t)]. \]
The output is the exact rational per-capita expected value of the lottery across the society. At \(\tau=1\) and \(x_j=1/2\), this is precisely Theorem 4.1’s query. The theorem therefore gives hardness for this continuous problem even with one type: the hard combinatorics live in the goods and the matroid, not in population multiplicity. I expect this to remain hard, while approximation by sampling may still be efficient. It is a useful boundary result, though a less central mirror than the welfare problem: the paper studies the individual lottery-value query directly, and the continuous society here aggregates that query by type.

The main further questions are whether the type-compressed welfare algorithm achieves the stated bounds with only \(\tau\) type-level variables per good; how its sample and precision costs depend on \(N\); and which valuation classes beyond W-MRS retain the needed concavity. For the aggregation problem, the natural follow-up is whether exact \(\#\mathrm P\)-hardness persists under approximation guarantees relevant to the welfare algorithm.

The weakest point is that a continuum of customers sharing valuations changes how scarce indivisible goods are represented: the assignment gives each infinitesimal recipient an integral bundle, but bundle masses can be fractional. That is the co-modification needed for a population limit, and the programme explicitly allows such type-level averages. Still, the market interpretation is less literal than a finite auction allocating each item to one named bidder. The continuous welfare problem remains recognizable because it preserves the paper’s W-MRS bidders, scarcity constraints, objective, and product-distribution allocation range; the exact lottery-query mirror is a narrower supporting case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest negative case is against Theorem 4.1. At \(\tau=1\) and \(x_j=1/2\), the proposed continuous aggregation problem is exactly the paper’s computation of \(\mathbb{E}[r_M(R)]\). With several types, it is just a weighted sum of independent copies of that same query. The masses add no population-level interaction, and the hard instance already fits in a one-type society. Calling the query’s answer a per-capita population value does not create a new computational question.

Theorem 1.5 is the harder anchor, and the fixed-supply mirror has a specific degeneracy. Keep the paper’s one copy of each good while the number of bidders grows, with each supported type having a fixed positive fraction \(\mu_t\). Its proposed constraints imply \(x_{tj}\le 1/(N\mu_t)\), so the inclusion probability \(1-e^{-x_{tj}}\) is \(O(1/N)\). For any pair of goods, the total mass of type-\(t\) bidders receiving both is therefore \(O(1/N)\). In the limit, the product-distribution range puts mass only on empty bundles and singletons.

That also collapses the full W-MRS welfare benchmark. Since these valuations are monotone and submodular, any allocation’s welfare is at most \(\sum_j\max_t v_t(\{j\})\). Once each positive-mass type contains enough people, that bound is attained by giving each good to a separate member of a type with maximum singleton value. The limiting problem is a per-good choice; the paper’s optimization over meaningful multi-good product lotteries has disappeared. This is a population-limit degeneracy, not merely the possibility that the answer to a live computational question is easy.

A nondegenerate version can keep supply proportional to population, so that multi-good bundles retain positive mass. That is a sensible nearby question, but it changes the unit-supply auction into a multi-unit or per-capita-supply market. The programme allows such co-modifications when they belong to the authors’ problem, so I cannot rule out that they would accept this version. It is the main weakness in the negative case: the fixed-supply limit degenerates, but a supply-scaled allocation mirror may still be worthwhile.

The paper’s truthfulness results do not strengthen the case for a population mirror: in an atomless population, an individual has zero effect on aggregate allocation, so bidder-level incentive claims lose their force unless the model adds a non-negligible strategic unit.

So Theorem 4.1’s anchor looks like a direct restatement, and the natural fixed-supply limit of Theorem 1.5 loses its central allocation structure. But I cannot honestly say that every recognized supply-scaled version fails. The universal negative verdict is therefore vulnerable on Theorem 1.5.
