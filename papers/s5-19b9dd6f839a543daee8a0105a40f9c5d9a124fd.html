What the mirror covers

The proposed mass restatement covers the axiomatic democratic trilemma; it covers no computational algorithms or complexity results, which the paper does not provide.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind needed for an anchored case. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity classification. Its unnumbered “democratic trilemma” is an axiomatic impossibility result, and the appendix’s unnumbered generality result characterizes when that trilemma applies: an agenda must have a minimally inconsistent subset of at least three propositions (with the stated caveat about electorate size). Neither result concerns algorithmic complexity.

The closest honest population mirror would take complete, consistent judgment sets on a fixed agenda as voter types. A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction holding judgment set \(t\). The decision variable is a collective judgment set. One could ask whether there is a rule that, for every tie-free \(\mu\), returns a complete, consistent collective judgment set and accepts a proposition only when a majority of the mass accepts it. The paper’s trilemma predicts that such a rule does not exist for agendas with the requisite minimally inconsistent subset; the three equal masses in its examples already witness the conflict.

That is a plausible continuous-population restatement of the paper’s central axiomatic question. But it has no objective to optimize, and the paper gives no computational result to anchor a complexity question about it. Its agenda is the *logical space of decision procedures*, not the computational landscape for finding or evaluating them. Under ChoCo’s stated scope and the required anchor rule, I cannot honestly turn this into a positive computational mirror case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s central result is the democratic trilemma: a global impossibility claim about procedures that must satisfy three axioms across a domain of profiles. It gives no complexity classification for finding a procedure, computing an outcome, or modifying a society. The appendix characterizes when the axiomatic conflict arises by the structure of the agenda. That is the strongest case against an anchored ChoCo mirror: the paper’s contribution is a map of competing requirements, not a computational problem.

The proposed society of complete judgment sets is coherent. For a large referendum or survey, one could let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of people with judgment type \(t\). But asking whether a rule exists for every such \(\mu\) simply recasts the paper’s axiomatic question in mass notation. The trilemma’s witness already consists of three groups with equal shares. Continuization makes those shares natural to state; it does not turn the result into an algorithmic task. Asking for an algorithm to evaluate or construct a rule would require an additional, specified problem.

A stronger candidate would be to take a distribution over judgment sets and compute a consistent collective judgment minimizing total distance from the population. The paper discusses distance-based procedures and names Kemeny as an example. That is a plausible computational social choice problem, but it selects one of the paper’s responses to the trilemma and adds a distance objective. Its complexity would concern that chosen rule, not the trilemma or the paper’s map of democracy’s logical space. I would not present it as a computational mirror of the paper’s result.

The case against a mirror of the paper’s contribution is strong; the claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is weaker. Large populations with repeated judgment types are plausible, and a continuous weighted version of a distance-based rule could be worth studying in its own right. That possibility does not rescue the proposed axiomatic anchor, but it prevents me from defending the universal negative without qualification.
