The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of temporal report types \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a temporal delegation network whose approved targets are types, choose a time-stamped outgoing delegation for each delegating type. Require the selected edges to form a tree toward a sink spanning all non-abstaining types, with every path respecting its type’s trust horizons; decide whether the mass-weighted first-edge satisfaction \(\sum_{t \in T_D}\mu_t w(e_t)\) can reach a given rational threshold \(q\).

The model it lives in

Represent each cohort as a type node with mass \(\mu_t\) and a complete temporal report, including its casting, abstaining, or delegating status, approved target types, Borda scores, and trust horizons. Select one time-stamped edge per delegating type, add sink edges for casting types, require a time-conscious tree spanning the non-abstaining types, and maximize \(\sum_{t \in T_D}\mu_t w(e_t)\).

The objection that survived

A type-level tree treats a cohort as one routing node, so the mirror does not preserve individual-level confluence in arbitrary networks; its plausibility depends on the proposed bloc-organized regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Theorem 1’s time-conscious, confluent satisfaction maximization and Theorem 4’s time-conscious path optimization have type-level counterparts. The mirrors leave aside Theorems 2, 3, and 5, along with the paper’s axiomatic and empirical directions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does NP-hardness persist when the support size \(\tau\) is bounded or parameterized?
  • How does allowing a type’s mass to split among delegation choices affect the confluent problem?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is a narrow but real mirror: treat large, behaviorally homogeneous groups as weighted voters in a temporal delegation network. Keep the deliberation rounds discrete. The population, represented by rational masses over a small set of complete temporal report types, is what becomes continuous.

The most compelling scenario is a large governance platform organized into chapters, departments, or caucuses. Members of each group share the same delegation policy, ranked approvals of other groups’ representatives, scores, and trust horizons; the platform asks one representative to route the group’s ballots. There may be millions of members but only a few dozen distinct report types. This is most plausible where delegation is actually organized through group representatives. A type must include the full report used by the problem, including its approved targets and their identities as representative types.

Lead anchor — Theorem 1. The paper proves that RESOLVE-DELEGATION with both time-consciousness and confluence is NP-hard, even for profiles of retrospective trust and under the Borda-scoring function. Its proof reduces from the NP-hard Minimum Temporal Spanning Tree problem. The paper’s discussion also says the hardness persists when voters change their minds at most once and have at most two preference groups per round.

A continuous counterpart is Mass-Confluent Temporal Delegation. An instance has supported types \(T\), a binary-rational mass \(\mu_t>0\) for each type with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and a finite temporal delegation network on those types plus a sink \(\bot\). Each type records whether its members cast, abstain, or delegate; its approved targets and Borda scores over time; and its trust horizons. The decision is to select a time-stamped outgoing delegation for each delegating type, with casting types connected to \(\bot\), so the selected edges form a directed tree toward \(\bot\) spanning all non-abstaining types. Every path must satisfy the type’s time-consciousness constraints. The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{t\in T_D}\mu_t w(e_t)\), where \(e_t\) is the selected first edge for type \(t\). A decision version asks whether this value is at least a rational threshold \(q\). Each type’s members follow its selected edge; their number is represented by mass.

For this formulation, hardness transfers: make every voter in a discrete instance its own type, give each mass \(1/n\), and divide the original utility threshold by \(n\). The tree and time-consciousness constraints are preserved, so the continuous decision instance has the same answer. This is Class B hardness: the reduction’s combinatorics lie in the temporal delegation network and the number of report types, not in the integer number of voters. It does not show hardness when the support size \(\tau\) is small. That leaves useful questions: is the problem fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), and does hardness persist in a few-type, very-large-cohort regime?

Second anchor — Theorem 4. The paper proves that Procedure 2 solves RESOLVE-DELEGATION in a time-conscious manner in polynomial time for retrospective-trust profiles. It also proves polynomial solvability for the variant that allows walks when all voters share a common, fixed trust horizon.

Its continuous counterpart is Mass Time-Conscious Path Delegation. Use the same typed temporal input and rational masses, but drop confluence. Each unit of delegating mass must receive a time-conscious path to a casting type, and the objective is the integral of the first-edge score, \(\sum_{t\in T_D}\mu_t w(e_t)\) when all members of each type take the same best path. Equivalently, one may permit mass to split among paths and maximize the mass-weighted average first-edge score. With no capacity constraints or confluence coupling, splitting cannot improve on giving each type’s mass a best feasible path. In the walk variant, paths are replaced by the paper’s permitted walks.

This mirror should be tractable: run the paper’s path or walk procedure once per supported type and weight its utility by \(\mu_t\). Its running time is polynomial in the encoded temporal network, the support size \(\tau\), and the rational bit lengths. This preserves the paper’s central path-feasibility question while making the population size irrelevant once its distinct reports are listed.

Together, these questions cover the paper’s core utility-maximizing delegation problem under two different axiom sets: time-consciousness plus confluence in Theorem 1, and time-consciousness without confluence in Theorem 4. They do not continuize time, and they do not cover the paper’s entire framework or axiomatic agenda. The paper itself is not a novelty collision: it studies individual voter vertices, whereas these mirrors ask about computation from a rational distribution over temporal report types. Existing high-multiplicity work supports the plausibility of the regime rather than preempting the question.

The soft spot is the lead mirror’s treatment of confluence. A tree over types says that all members of a type take the same delegation edge. That fits group-level delegation well, but it is an added restriction if otherwise-identical individuals may choose different representatives. A more faithful mass-splittable version would need to say precisely how individual-level trees and cycles are represented using only type masses. Theorem 1’s discrete reduction does not settle that version; allowing such splits could change the complexity. The no-confluence mirror is less exposed to this issue, because the paper’s path objective separates across voters.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s basic units are vertices in a temporal network, not interchangeable ballots. A voter’s approved representatives are named people, and whether a route exists depends on which individual has which outgoing edges at which times. Two voters with the same rankings and trust horizons can still play different computational roles because other voters point to different ones. A type that preserves those roles must include the relevant network position and temporal history; in an asymmetric instance, that can make nearly every voter a separate type. Then the support size is essentially the original population, so the proposed compression has not happened.

Theorem 4 is especially exposed. Without confluence, each delegator can choose a best feasible route independently. In a type-level model, multiplying a type’s score by its positive mass does not change which route that type chooses. But compressing to types is only valid if the routes are also determined by type-level relationships. Replacing individual targets with target groups supplies that information only by changing the network model. If the individual network is retained, the paper’s path procedure still runs over its individual vertices; attaching masses does not make the path problem a high-multiplicity one.

Theorem 1 faces a deeper version of the same problem. Confluence requires a tree over individual voters, with each person forwarding all ballots they receive through one representative. A tree over types instead makes a whole class act as one node. A mass-splittable formulation would need to specify how delegation flows between individual members, how it avoids cycles, and whether every member’s path reaches a casting voter. Fractions over report types alone do not encode those facts. A graph-limit formulation might preserve more network structure, but then the tree and reachability conditions themselves need a carefully chosen limit; ordinary dense graph limits, for example, do not preserve an \(O(n)\)-edge tree in a useful way.

The proposed chapter or caucus scenario is the strongest reply. If each bloc really does share one policy, delegates to other blocs, and routes its ballots collectively, then a mass-weighted temporal tree is coherent. But that is a model of bloc-level delegation: its nodes make collective choices, whereas the paper’s voters individually retain the right to change or revoke a delegation. It is not clear that the paper’s authors would treat the bloc problem as a mirror of their individual-voter results rather than as a new delegation model.

That leaves the negative case strongest against Theorem 4 and plausible, but not decisive, against Theorem 1. I cannot honestly rule out a useful mirror for a genuinely bloc-organized electorate: there, masses and temporal delegation can coexist in a recognizable model. The universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists is therefore weak at precisely that point.
