Section 3 reports computationally generated counts of non-isomorphic domains through \(n=9\), but it does not analyze the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 4 characterizes when Borda satisfies Arrow’s IIA; Theorem 5 gives the maximum size of a hierarchically cyclic domain. Both are structural results, not complexity or algorithmic results about a society. That leaves no result here for the programme’s computational mirror to take as its anchor.

The best population version of Theorem 4 would give each ranking type a mass \(\mu_t\) and ask whether Borda satisfies Arrow’s IIA on the society’s domain. But the domain is just \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\): the masses do not enter the condition. This is a coherent restatement for a high-multiplicity society, but it supplies no population-based computational question. Theorem 5 has the same limitation. Replacing “domain size” with support size preserves its combinatorial partition problem; maximizing mass over societies supported on an allowed domain is trivial if the society itself is free to choose.

A stronger alternative would start from an observed society and ask for the least mass that must change rankings to make its support satisfy Arrow’s IIA, with specified costs for changing types. That could be a worthwhile continuous robustness or repair problem. But the paper neither formulates nor solves it, and the answer depends on adding an intervention model and costs. It would be a new computational problem suggested by the paper’s subject, not a mirror of Theorem 4 or 5.
