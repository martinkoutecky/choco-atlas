The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive follow-up I can see is to compute equilibria in a finite-type version of paired serial dictatorship. A type would specify a complete utility function \(v_t\) over course bundles and \(w_t\) over dorms. The instance would give rational masses \(\mu_t\), course and dorm capacities, and the finite signal set \(S\). The decision variables would be masses \(x_{t,s}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\). The question would be to find an equilibrium: every signal used by positive mass of type \(t\) must maximize that type’s expected utility, given the masses using all signals and the mechanism’s tie-breaking. A solution would give \(x\) and the resulting allocation distribution.

A plausible regime is a large university where many students share complete preference profiles, perhaps with a moderate number of relative course-versus-dorm preference types. That is a genuine high-multiplicity setting only when students sharing a type also share all the utility information the model uses. The paper’s simulation, with idiosyncratic course and dorm values, does not itself establish that regime.

This is a plausible computational follow-up to the authors’ model: they prove existence of a pure-strategy equilibrium in Proposition 8, and their simulations use a learning algorithm to approximate equilibrium. The closest welfare anchors are Theorem 1, proved in the paper, which says that any equilibrium inducing a deterministic allocation is Pareto efficient and envy-free; and Theorem 2, also proved there, which says that with common values and one course and one dorm, paired serial dictatorship is ex-ante optimal among mechanisms with the same signal space. These are meaningful analytic results, but they do not assert a computational complexity classification or give an encoding the paper computes with.

So I cannot honestly supply the required named computational anchor or assign the proposed equilibrium-finding problem to a class in the programme’s trichotomy. The paper already models a continuum population, with students on \([0,1]\) and Lebesgue measure. The proposed problem would add a computational question to that model; it would not mirror a discrete computational result whose hardness might change under continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that there is no population continuization left to perform. The paper’s model already places students on \([0,1]\) with Lebesgue measure and studies paired serial dictatorship in that nonatomic economy. A large university might make that a sensible model; it cannot supply the missing discrete computational result that a continuous mirror would continue. Changing the application or choosing a finite set of complete preference types does not change that direction of travel.

Proposition 8 is the closest anchor, but it proves existence of a pure-strategy equilibrium by an existence theorem. It gives no encoded equilibrium-finding problem, algorithm, or complexity bound. The proposed finite-type problem—find masses \(x_{t,s}\) satisfying best-response conditions—is a coherent computational follow-up. The stronger version, with rational masses, capacities, and complete utility tables, is still a computational analysis of an already nonatomic game. To make it a mirror, one would need a discrete computational result for the corresponding finite-player game and a formal bridge between that game and this continuum one. The paper supplies neither.

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 do not provide separate computational anchors. Theorem 1 relates deterministic equilibrium allocations to Pareto efficiency and envy-freeness; Theorem 2 establishes ex-ante optimality under common values and single-unit demand. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. One could ask for an algorithm that finds deterministic equilibria or computes the optimal assignment for finite type distributions. But those are newly posed algorithmic questions about this paper’s continuous model, not mirrors of its theorems. Theorem 2’s optimal-transport proof gives the best opening for such work, though the paper itself notes that the structural argument fails for combinatorial course bundles.

The simulations do not fill the gap: EXP3 is used to approximate behavior in a finite simulation, but the paper makes no named complexity result about that algorithm or the mechanism. A finite-type formulation may be a worthwhile research direction. The case against calling it a ChoCo mirror is stronger than any claim that the type regime is implausible: the paper starts on the continuous side, and none of the cited results is a discrete computational claim whose continuous counterpart could be classified.

That makes the negative case strong on the programme’s stated gate, but weaker as a universal claim that no computational work on this model could ever be worthwhile. The finite-type equilibrium problem is credible; its weakness as a ChoCo candidate is that it is new computational analysis of a continuum model, not population continuization.
