The continuous mirror question

Given \(k\) behaviors, their rational costs and utilities, an intent weight, a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), a rational block contact matrix \(\omega_{tq}\), and type-specific resources and adoption thresholds, choose seed masses \(z_{t,i}\geq 0\) subject to \(\sum_i z_{t,i}\leq\mu_t\) and fixed quotas \(\sum_t z_{t,i}=\beta_i\), with each seeded unit assigned one behavior and topped up when needed. Under the deterministic block-mass version of the paper’s sticky, threshold-based, utility-maximizing adoption process, maximize the final mass holding at least one behavior; determine whether an additive-\(\epsilon\) optimum can be computed in time polynomial in \(k\), \(|T|\), \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\).

The model it lives in

A dense, cohort-structured population represented by finitely many types with masses \(\mu_t\). Each type has a resource level and threshold vector, and contacts follow the type-level mixing matrix \(\omega_{tq}\). Seed-mass decisions \(z_{t,i}\) assign campaign behaviors, and the objective is the final mass that adopts at least one behavior under the sticky mass dynamics.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the sticky total-participation seed-selection result under fixed behavior quotas; it leaves resource utilization, total adoption, non-sticky dynamics, arbitrary individual networks, and the empirical heuristic comparisons aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what conditions on the contact matrix and adoption rule does the final-participation objective have a continuous diminishing-returns property?
  • How does optimal seed-mass allocation compare with finite-population seed selection as the population grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest continuous mirror is the paper’s sticky total-participation seed-selection problem, restricted to a large population with a block-structured social network. This keeps the authors’ central ingredients: costly behaviors, limited resources, threshold responses to neighbors, utility-maximizing adoption, and a campaign choosing early adopters. The population becomes continuous; the behavior model does not.

The paper’s cleanest numbered computational anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved here. It says that, for an arbitrary instance of the Sticky Multiple Behavior Diffusion model, the expected total-participation function \(\sigma(\cdot)\)—the expected number of people who adopt at least one behavior—is submodular in the seed set. The paper then uses that property to obtain a greedy \((1-1/e-\epsilon)\)-approximation for total-participation maximization in the sticky model. The theorem’s direct claim is submodularity; the approximation is its algorithmic consequence, not a separate numbered theorem.

There is no numbered hardness theorem to anchor on. Section 4.1 says that P1 and P2 are NP-complete by reducing the single-behavior linear-threshold problem, and the abstract and conclusion repeat the hardness claim. Those are relevant claims, but they are not numbered Theorem/Lemma/Corollary/Proposition results, so I would not present them as anchors under your rule. Theorem 4.1 is enough for one focused mirror.

A plausible regime is a large public-health or sustainability campaign run through a regional social platform. Millions of participants might fall into a much smaller number of cohorts: for example, by workplace or municipality, resource band, and adoption-threshold profile. People in a cohort face the same behavior costs and utilities, have the same available resources and thresholds, and receive social influence from the same mix of cohorts. A few hundred such types among millions of people is a credible high-multiplicity regime. The block assumption is substantive: it suits a platform with strong cohort-based interaction, not every arbitrary friendship graph.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover. Call it Sticky Block-Population Participation Maximization. An instance has \(k\) behaviors with rational costs \(c_i\), utilities \(u_i\), and intent weight \(w\); a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); and a rational contact matrix \(\omega_{tq}\), where \(\omega_{tq}\) is the share of a type-\(t\) person’s social influence coming from type \(q\), with each row summing to \(1\). A type \(t\) records its contact block, resource level \(r_t\), and threshold vector \((\theta_{t,1},\ldots,\theta_{t,k})\). Thus everyone within a type is indistinguishable for the model.

The campaign has a fixed seed mass \(\beta_i\) for each behavior, with total seed mass \(\beta=\sum_i\beta_i\). Its decision is a rational allocation \(z_{t,i}\geq 0\): mass of type \(t\) selected as seeds for behavior \(i\). It must satisfy \(\sum_i z_{t,i}\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t z_{t,i}=\beta_i\). As in the paper’s topped-up, single-behavior-per-seed variant, selected seed mass adopts its assigned behavior even if it needs additional resources to do so. The rest of the population starts inactive.

At each epoch, type \(t\)’s signal for behavior \(i\) is
\[ \ell_i(t)=\sum_q\omega_{tq}\sum_{A:\,i\in A}x_{q,A}, \]
where \(x_{q,A}\) is the mass of type \(q\) currently holding behavior set \(A\). Non-seeds follow the paper’s sticky adoption rule: consider behaviors whose signal reaches the type’s threshold and whose cost the type can afford, then choose a utility-maximizing feasible subset under its resource limit, retaining adopted behaviors. The process runs to its fixed point. The objective is to choose \(z\) maximizing the final mass holding at least one behavior, \(\sum_t\sum_{A\ne\varnothing}x_{t,A}\). A solution is a feasible seed-mass allocation achieving the optimum, or an allocation within additive \(\epsilon\) of it; the algorithmic question is whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(k,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\), where \(\tau=|T|\) and \(L\) is the input bit length.

I expect the first result to be a tractable approximation under suitable block-population assumptions. Theorem 4.1 gives a real reason to investigate that direction: its diminishing-returns structure is what makes greedy seed selection effective in the discrete sticky model. The continuous question is whether that structure survives when seed choices are masses and contacts are represented by a block kernel. The theorem does not prove that extension; establishing it, or finding the right conditions for it, is part of the proposed problem. Follow-on questions include whether the objective is submodular or has a continuous diminishing-returns analogue in \(z\), how its optimum relates to finite-population seed selection as \(N\) grows, and whether the result changes for total adoption or resource utilization.

The weak point is that a block contact matrix replaces the paper’s arbitrary individual network, and the theorem covers a stochastic finite-graph model rather than this deterministic type-mass dynamic. If the relevant interaction profiles are so varied that the number of types grows with the population, the high-multiplicity case loses force. Still, in the stated cohort regime, the mirror retains the paper’s actual seed-selection question and its participation objective; it does not obtain tractability by dropping costly behaviors or resource constraints.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 4.1: expected total participation is submodular in the seed set, which supports a greedy approximation for the sticky model. That is a real computational result. I would not dismiss it because the paper has no numbered hardness theorem.

The theorem’s structure comes from random live edges on a particular finite graph. For each realization, participation is counted through reachability from the chosen seeds. The value of a seed therefore depends on its exact position and paths through that graph. In the proposed mirror, two people with the same block, resource, and threshold may still have different neighbors and different influence. Replacing their neighborhoods with a block contact matrix replaces random local signals with their averages. At a threshold, those are not interchangeable: in general, \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{L\geq\theta\}]\neq\mathbf{1}\{\mathbb{E}[L]\geq\theta\}\). In sparse networks, local differences need not vanish as the population grows. Keep the realized graph and exact neighborhoods become part of a person’s type, so the number of types can grow with the population.

The strongest version of the mirror would use exchangeable blocks, distributions of resources and thresholds within each block, and seed budgets that scale with population size. That is a plausible population model. But in the sparse case, a block mean does not preserve the neighborhood fluctuations that drive adoption; preserving them requires richer local-neighborhood information. In a dense, highly symmetric case, the block model may be exact, but then it is a deterministic mean-field diffusion model. Its seed-mass optimization is a new model whose connection to Theorem 4.1 needs to be established; the theorem’s live-edge argument does not supply it. Either way, the proposed mirror needs substantive assumptions beyond “many people of the same type.”

That is the strongest negative case I can make, and it does not establish that no scenario is worthwhile. Repeated, densely connected cohorts with shared resource and threshold profiles are a credible high-multiplicity regime. Choosing seed masses for those cohorts retains the campaign’s participation objective, and the paper offers no existing continuous computational treatment that settles it. The honest objection is that the proponent has not shown that their block model preserves the theorem’s operative structure. It is not an objection to every continuous mirror; a well-defined symmetric-block version could still be worth studying.
