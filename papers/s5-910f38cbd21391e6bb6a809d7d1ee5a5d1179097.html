The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying named computational result under your anchor rule. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Equations (1)–(3), the simulation findings, and the Zeeburg pseudocode do not qualify. So there are zero valid anchors, and no per-anchor mirror I can honestly submit.

The strongest candidate mirror is the paper’s minimum-average-rank assignment problem in §2. This is a candidate based on the problem the paper defines, not a named result. Take \(m\) schools and a finite support \(T\) of complete school rankings. The input is a rational mass \(\mu_t\) for each ranking \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a rational per-capita capacity \(q_j\) for each school, with \(\sum_j q_j\ge1\). Let \(r_t(j)\) be the position of school \(j\) in ranking \(t\). Choose assignment masses \(x_{tj}\ge0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \sum_t x_{tj}\le q_j \quad\text{for every }j, \]
to minimize \(\sum_{t,j}r_t(j)x_{tj}\). The solution is the assignment mass \(x\), and the objective is the continuum version of the paper’s average-rank qualifier \(Q\).

A plausible regime is a large school district assigning thousands of pupils across roughly ten schools, where many pupils share the same complete ranking because their choices reflect common neighbourhoods or school advice. The type is the ranking the assignment problem uses; mass is the fraction of pupils with that ranking. Here \(\tau=|T|\) could be far smaller than the pupil count. School capacities become per-capita capacities, a natural co-modification of the allocation model. The authors should recognize this as their problem: §2 explicitly defines the optimal solution by minimizing average rank subject to school capacities. This mirror covers that objective, not the paper’s claims about lottery sensitivity, strategy-proofness, or the Zeeburg procedure.

I expect this candidate to be tractable: it is a transportation linear program, equivalently a min-cost-flow problem, with a polynomial-size formulation in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). When the masses and capacities come from integer pupil counts, the corresponding flow formulation also has an integral optimum. The paper’s statement that no algorithm can find the optimum in a reasonable time is not a numbered complexity result; for the stated assignment constraints, standard min-cost flow already gives an exact algorithm. A useful follow-up would be to use this optimum as a benchmark for the paper’s simulated mechanisms and ask how closely their assignments approach it as support size changes.

The weak point is decisive for the requested evidence standard: this candidate is anchored only in the paper’s defined objective, not in a named computational result. It also does not reveal a hardness result dissolving under continuization—the underlying discrete optimization is already polynomial-time solvable. The paper’s lottery and strategic-behaviour findings are not good substitutes: continuizing lottery noise or strategy frequencies would leave the population finite and miss the programme’s scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. It reports simulation comparisons and proposes algorithms, but gives no theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. Its claim that finding an optimal assignment is impractical is not such a result. So under the requested anchor standard, the proponent has no qualifying anchor.

The strongest candidate is still the minimum-average-rank assignment problem in §2. Its mass version is exactly a transportation or min-cost-flow problem: each ranking type supplies its mass, each school has a capacity, and assigning type \(t\) to school \(j\) costs \(r_t(j)\). The standard flow formulation already handles rational masses and capacities, and its integral structure recovers discrete assignments when the inputs come from voter counts. That leaves little continuous complexity landscape to chart; the paper defines this objective as a benchmark but does not study an algorithm for it.

This is a meaningful argument against that candidate, but it does not establish the universal negative. A large school district with many pupils sharing a modest set of rankings is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the average-rank objective depends only on type and assignment, not individual identity. I can’t honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The strongest case against is that the paper supplies no complexity result to anchor, while its best replacement is already the standard flow problem.
