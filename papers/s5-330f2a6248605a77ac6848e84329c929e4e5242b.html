The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of buyer types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), \(m\) item classes with rational supply \(\sigma_j\) per unit of population, and consistent utility functions \(u_{tj}(p_j)\), decide whether a competitive equilibrium exists and, if so, compute a minimum equilibrium price vector and supporting assignment. Each individual remains unit-demand and receives at most one indivisible item; assignment variables \(y_{tj}\) may be fractional because they represent the mass of type \(t\) assigned to class \(j\). Require \(\sum_j y_{tj}+z_t=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t y_{tj}\leq\sigma_j\), and nonnegative assigned and unassigned masses; assigned mass must receive a utility-maximizing item, unassigned mass must have no positive-utility item available, and unsold supply must have price zero. Interpret a reported \(p_j^+\) by equilibria with the same assignment at prices arbitrarily close above \(p_j\); measure complexity in \(m\), \(|T|\), and input bit length \(L\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s main algorithmic result, Theorem 4.1. It is proved in this paper. It says that for assignment markets with *consistent* utility functions—continuous and strictly decreasing up to each buyer’s budget, satisfying the paper’s consistency condition, with the functions and inverses computable in polynomial time—if a competitive equilibrium exists, then a minimum one exists; moreover, deciding whether an equilibrium exists and computing a minimum one takes strongly polynomial time. The theorem includes quasi-linear buyers with budget constraints and the paper’s ROI-based utility model.

I would mirror it with High-Multiplicity Budgeted Assignment Equilibrium. An instance has \(m\) classes of interchangeable indivisible items and a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) buyer types. Each type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a utility function \(u_{tj}(p_j)\) for each item class \(j\), including its budget. Each class \(j\) has rational supply \(\sigma_j\) per unit of buyer mass. The utility functions come from the paper’s consistent class, represented under the same computability assumptions; rational quasi-linear utilities and budgets give a concrete subcase. Types are complete descriptions for this market: buyers sharing a type have the same utilities and budgets for every item class.

The decision variables are prices \(p_j\), assigned buyer mass \(y_{tj}\), and unassigned mass \(z_t\). A solution must satisfy
\[ \sum_j y_{tj}+z_t=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t y_{tj}\leq \sigma_j,\qquad y_{tj},z_t\geq 0. \]
Mass of type \(t\) can be assigned to class \(j\) only when \(j\) gives that type maximum utility at the stated prices, counting the outside option of utility \(0\); unassigned mass must also have no positive-utility item available. Any item supply left unsold must have price \(0\). The question is: does such an equilibrium exist, and if so, what is a minimum equilibrium price vector and a supporting assignment of mass? “Minimum” means componentwise minimum across equilibria, with the paper’s \(p_j^+\) convention when an infimum price need not itself support an equilibrium. As in the paper’s definition, a \(p_j^+\) output must be supportable by equilibria with the same allocation at prices arbitrarily close above \(p_j\).

The expected running time should be measured in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) includes the binary encodings of the masses, supplies, and utility descriptions—not in the number of individual buyers or item copies. This is a natural high-multiplicity regime for the advertising markets motivating the paper: many advertisers share utility and budget profiles, while each ad-slot class has supply proportional to market size. A finite realization still assigns each buyer at most one indivisible item; only the aggregate assignment \(y\) is fractional. Scaling item supply with the population is necessary to preserve a meaningful market-clearing question. It is a co-modification of the market model, not a change of goal from population continuity to divisible outcomes.

I expect this problem to be tractable, in Class A, probably in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). At a fixed price vector, assigning demanded mass to item classes is a capacitated matching or flow problem on only the supported types and item classes. The promising route is to adapt the paper’s price-increment method, replacing cardinal matching conditions with capacity-weighted flow conditions. The finite set of type–item demand edges and their budget and value boundaries gives a plausible way to control price changes. This is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence already proved by Theorem 4.1.

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1’s existence decision and minimum-equilibrium computation. It does not try to continuize the paper’s mechanism result or make its strategic claims about buyers. The paper’s own conclusion calls multi-unit supply and demand a natural, technically challenging extension; that makes a market with scaled supply recognizable to its authors as their market-clearing problem. Existing finite-market equilibrium algorithms are supporting context, not a collision: the question here is whether equilibrium computation can be done from a compressed distribution over types, with complexity governed by \(\tau\).

The weakest point is that the source theorem is already an efficient algorithmic result, rather than a hardness result whose obstacle might disappear in the continuum. Also, the weighted-flow version of the paper’s critical-set argument needs proof: budget discontinuities and the \(p_j^+\) issue could complicate minimum prices even if flow feasibility is easy. That leaves a real computational question, but a narrower prize than showing that continuization removes discrete hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem 4.1: it is a proved computational result, so there is no decisive “nothing to mirror” objection.

The strongest negative case concerns the paper’s literal market. It has \(m\) individual goods, not a growing stock of repeated goods. If the buyer population grows while those \(m\) goods stay fixed, their supply per capita vanishes. In a normalized continuum, at most a vanishing fraction of buyers receive anything; the equilibrium allocation no longer describes a population-scale market. The motivating advertising setting also does not guarantee a small support: a buyer’s type must include their complete utility and budget profile across goods, and distinct advertiser values can make \(\tau\) nearly the population size. Coarse grouping is especially risky here because budgets create discontinuities.

The best repair is the proposed one: repeated item classes with supply scaled to the population. But that is a capacitated assignment market, a multi-unit extension the authors themselves identify as a technically challenging direction. For rational masses and supplies, clearing denominators turns it into a finite market with repeated buyers and item copies; the aggregate flow corresponds to an assignment of those copies. This does not make the mirror pointless: the finite expansion can be enormous, and an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) could be valuable. The paper’s algorithm does not establish such a bound.

So the negative case is narrow. The literal fixed-supply model degenerates, and realistic utility profiles may not compress well. But the scaled-supply version is plausible, recognized by the authors as their market-clearing problem, and remains a genuine high-multiplicity algorithmic question. I cannot honestly defeat that stronger mirror or claim that no scenario makes it worth asking.
