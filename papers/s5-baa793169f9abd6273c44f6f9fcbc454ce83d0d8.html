What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a qualifying continuous mirror; the proposed mass-market extension touches the capacity and stability questions in Propositions 2–3.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive-side point is the setting: a large placement market can have many applicants and positions but relatively few complete preference-and-capacity types. A continuous model could put mass on those types, run an interview stage followed by matching, and measure matched mass, blocking-pair mass, and welfare changes when the interview cap changes. That is recognizable as the paper’s problem. The obstacle is that strict rankings over named counterpart agents, which drive the paper’s results, can make nearly every agent a different type; replacing them with rankings over cohorts or scaling interview capacities with mass would require a substantial model extension.

I cannot identify a numbered computational result to anchor that mirror. The closest results are proved in this paper, but they are analytical characterizations:

  • Theorem 1: when doctors’ interview capacities weakly increase, any doctor who strictly prefers her new match was unmatched before; her new hospital forms a blocking pair with her under the original match; and every hospital in her original interview set was preferred to being unmatched.
  • Proposition 2: with common preferences and homogeneous interview capacities \((l,k)\), the resulting match is stable exactly when \(l=k\) or both capacities are at least \(\min\{|D|,|H|\}\).
  • Proposition 3: with common preferences and hospital capacity \(l\) fixed, moving the doctor capacity farther from \(l\) on either side yields more blocking pairs and a weakly lower match rate.

These results do not classify the complexity of a problem or give a paper-specific algorithmic result. The paper uses deferred acceptance, but does not present a numbered result about its computational complexity. The simulations do not supply such an anchor either.

An adjacent research question would be to choose a common doctor interview cap, given distributions over resident and program preference types, to maximize final matched mass and then minimize blocking-pair mass under a precisely defined mass version of the two DA stages. But that model needs additional choices about how type-level mass preserves individual interview limits and strict rankings. It is a plausible extension, not a continuous problem established by the paper’s named computational results.

So the positive case is meaningful as a possible population-level extension, especially for a large standardized placement market. Its weakest point is also decisive under this brief: I cannot honestly provide the required continuous problem *per computational anchor*, because the paper has no qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is program fit: Theorem 1, Propositions 2 and 3 are not computational results. They make analytical claims about the outcome of a two-stage matching process. The paper does not classify the complexity of a problem, give an algorithmic result beyond using deferred acceptance, or analyze a pricing or separation problem. That leaves no named computational claim for a continuous mirror to carry forward.

The best mirror of Theorem 1 would ask whether a positive mass of doctors can gain when interview capacity increases, and how much mass is harmed. But the paper’s mechanism tracks finite interview lists and individual proposals and rejections. In its simulations, pair-specific fit shocks help determine each agent’s ranking. A complete type therefore includes rankings over named counterpart agents; with those shocks, nearly every agent may have a distinct type, so the support grows with market size. Grouping agents by broad preference categories loses the individual rankings and interview lists that drive the theorem. Modeling preferences over counterpart *types* instead is possible, but it changes the deferred-acceptance process and requires a new mass-level model.

The best mirror of Proposition 2 would replace the common-preference market with ordered masses and interview-cap shares, then characterize when the resulting mass matching is stable. That is a coherent analytical question, but the proposition itself is a characterization, not a computational claim. And its capacities \(l\) and \(k\) count interviews per person. Keeping those counts fixed preserves the practical meaning of a cap, but an aggregate type-flow model does not record which finite set of counterpart agents interviewed whom. Scaling the caps to fractions of the whole market makes interview flows aggregable, but changes the policy being studied.

For Proposition 3, one could ask whether moving a doctor-cap share away from a hospital-cap share increases the mass of blocking pairs and reduces matched mass. That, too, is a sensible comparative-statics question for a newly specified mass model. It is not a complexity question supplied by the paper. Treating blocking pairs as mass does not resolve the central modeling choice: the paper’s blocking and matching outcomes are generated from individual interview lists, while the aggregate model would need its own rule for forming and using interview flows.

A large residency market is the strongest setting for this work, and high multiplicity there is plausible. The limitation is not that the setting lacks many participants; it is that preserving the paper’s finite, identity-specific interviews does not give a fixed-support population model. A continuum market with preference cohorts and interview capacities measured in population shares might still be worthwhile. But its primitives and matching process would need to be built anew, so it would be an adjacent research project rather than a mirror anchored in these results.

That is the honest limit of the negative case: I cannot rule out a useful continuum matching model inspired by this paper.
