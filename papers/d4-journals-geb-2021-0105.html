What the mirror covers

No named computational result is mirrored; the equilibrium propositions, experimental treatment comparisons, and fundraiser-return calculations remain outside a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting behind the paper: a large municipality repeatedly crowdfunding local public projects, with residents grouped by valuation and any other attributes that affect their contribution decisions. A type’s mass would be its share of residents; the decision would be a type-contingent contribution path over the campaign, and the outcome of interest could be provision probability or the fundraiser’s net return. The funding target would need to scale with population.

But I cannot make this a compliant anchor-based case. The paper contains no named result asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic classification. Its closest results are Proposition 1, which says that the assurance contract without bonuses has both efficient and inefficient equilibria, and that all efficient equilibria have the same probability of provision; and Proposition 2, which says that some assurance contract with proportional and/or fixed refund bonuses has no inefficient equilibria. These are equilibrium claims, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another computational class. Proposition 1’s equal-probability claim is presented as new here; Proposition 2 is proved here. Neither supplies a computational anchor.

The proposed setting also needs care: the paper’s theory has finitely many strategic agents, and its experiment uses groups of ten. Its continuous-time campaign and continuous valuation distribution do not make the population continuous. In a nonatomic population, an individual may cease to affect whether the threshold is met, changing the pivotal incentives central to the paper’s analysis.

So the positive case is that large-community crowdfunding offers a sensible population regime and a promising direction for a new mirror. I cannot honestly turn that into a precise prover-ready computational problem anchored in this paper’s results. Doing so would require adding a computational question and a continuum equilibrium model that the paper does not analyze.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 characterize equilibria: one compares the provision probabilities of efficient equilibria without bonuses; the other establishes that a bonus scheme can eliminate inefficient equilibria. The hypotheses, experimental treatment comparisons, and net-return calculations likewise concern behavior and outcomes. None defines an algorithmic problem or establishes a complexity result. A continuous equilibrium analysis could be interesting economics, but it would not fill the programme’s computational gap.

The municipality example is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Its best direct mirror would put residents into types by valuation and other payoff-relevant traits, then ask whether the two propositions persist for the resulting masses. The population limit creates a more basic problem for those propositions: a single resident has measure zero and cannot change whether the funding threshold is met. Without bonuses, below the threshold a pledge is refunded and cannot affect provision, so contributors are indifferent; once the threshold is met, an individual contribution costs money without affecting provision. The pivotal incentives behind a nontrivial assurance-contract equilibrium disappear. In the standard nonatomic model, failed profiles can remain arbitrary, while the proposition comparing efficient equilibria has no efficient equilibria to compare. The finite group’s contribution coordination has degenerated.

Refund bonuses do not restore the paper’s strategic mechanism in a stable way. If provision fails regardless of an individual’s pledge, an eligible pledge earns that individual a bonus; if provision succeeds regardless, contributing is costly. The paper’s result that bonuses remove zero-provision equilibria depends on finite agents being able to affect the outcome. In a nonatomic society, the individual’s expected payoff from a pledge \(x\), when their pledge cannot change the provision probability \(q\), is \(qv+x((1-q)r-q)\), where \(v\) is their valuation and \(r\) is the bonus rate. The individual’s incentive is then to choose an endpoint or be indifferent, rather than to make a pivotal contribution. Early aggregate contributions might still be studied as behavioral signals, but the paper’s treatment of named contributors and conditional cooperation would need a new model to give those signals strategic force.

The strongest repaired computational proposal would be to optimize a bonus schedule for success or fundraiser returns, given a finite rational distribution of resident types. That could be a worthwhile new mechanism-design question. It is not a computational question established by this paper: the paper compares a small set of bonus designs using experimental behavior, and a continuum version would first need a specified behavioral model and equilibrium-selection rule. Its hard work would be constructing that model, rather than solving a computational problem the paper already identifies.

So the negative case is strong on fit: neither named proposition supplies a computational anchor, and the direct population limit strips away the pivotality central to both. It is weaker as a claim of universal impossibility. A suitably enriched large-population crowdfunding model could support new computational questions, but it would be a substantial extension rather than a continuous mirror of these results.
