The continuous mirror question

Given a finite country set \(C\), refugee types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita country quotas \(q_c\), and strict preferences over countries for each type and over types for each country, with every pair acceptable, construct a stable mass assignment \(x_{tc}\ge 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{t,c}x_{tc}\). Require \(\sum_c x_{tc}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tc}\le q_c\); stability means no type has positive mass assigned to a less-preferred country or left unassigned while a country has spare capacity or assigns positive mass to a lower-ranked type.

The model it lives in

A large relocation or resettlement cohort is represented by \(\tau\) complete refugee profiles with masses \(\mu_t\); each country has a capacity \(q_c\) scaled to the cohort and a preference ranking over those profiles. The decision variables \(x_{tc}\) assign profile mass to countries, and the objective is to maximize assigned mass subject to stability. Individuals still receive one destination; the fractional variables describe aggregate shares.

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational result. Its only numbered result is Proposition 1, proved here: in College Admissions instances where every refugee–country pair is acceptable, a stable maximum matching exists. The proof uses the country-proposing deferred acceptance algorithm, but Proposition 1 asserts existence, not a complexity classification. The paper discusses algorithms and cites computationally relevant matching results, but it does not give a numbered result saying that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classifying its complexity.

The closest positive seed is a continuous version of Proposition 1. Call it Maximum-Mass Stable Asylum Assignment. An instance has a finite set \(C\) of countries; a support \(T\) of refugee types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); rational per-capita country capacities \(q_c\); a strict ranking of countries for each type; and a priority ordering of types for each country. Every pair is acceptable. A type must include all information relevant to matching: refugees in it share preferences and every attribute that determines a country’s ranking. A solution is a rational mass assignment \(x_{tc}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_c x_{tc}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tc}\le q_c\). It must be stable: there is no type–country pair for which positive mass of that type prefers the country to its current assignment, while the country has spare capacity or assigns positive mass to a lower-priority type. Among stable assignments, maximize \(\sum_{t,c}x_{tc}\).

The regime would be a large relocation or resettlement cohort in which many applicants share the same full preference and priority-relevant profile, with \(N\gg\tau\), where \(N\) is the number of refugees and \(\tau=|T|\) is the number of distinct types. Countries’ capacities scale with cohort size. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, though whether real preference profiles leave \(\tau\) small is an empirical question. The mass assignment represents fractional shares of a cohort; individuals still receive one destination.

I would expect this restricted mirror to be tractable by a weighted deferred-acceptance procedure. Proposition 1 supplies the matching anchor: its content predicts that, with universal acceptability, stability need not sacrifice maximum assigned mass. But the tractability expectation is an extension, not a result proved by the paper. The mirror covers only that all-acceptable existence claim; it does not capture the paper’s broader normative arguments about rejecting country preferences or choosing between fairness and efficiency. Further questions include whether stable maximum-mass assignments persist with unacceptable pairs, and how to translate between the fractional solution and finite assignments.

The soft spot is substantial: this is a matching-theory paper focused on normative analysis, and its closest anchor is an existence proposition rather than a computational result. Also, the finite deferred-acceptance problem is already algorithmic; the continuous formulation may simply aggregate it, rather than expose a new complexity question. So this is a plausible continuous population model, but it is not a positive case that satisfies the required named-computational-anchor test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no numbered computational result to continuize. Its only numbered result, Proposition 1, proves that a stable maximum matching exists when every refugee–country pair is acceptable. The proof uses deferred acceptance, but it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s contribution is normative: it argues about which criteria an asylum mechanism should prioritize and how it should be designed.

The proposed mass-assignment model is coherent in a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large cohort could contain many refugees with the same complete preference and priority-relevant profile. I would not reject it on the ground that refugees are individuals. Its stability definition needs a small repair, since mass of one type can be split among destinations, but stability can be defined over the shares assigned to each destination.

That repair does not make it a mirror of Proposition 1, though. The proposition is an existence statement about finite College Admissions instances; “Maximum-Mass Stable Asylum Assignment” adds a continuous population and a computational task the paper never states. One could also pose a stronger problem about maximizing assigned mass subject to stability when some pairs are unacceptable. That might be worthwhile new matching research, but it would be an adjacent project suggested by the paper, not a computational result of the paper translated to a continuous society. The article’s central question—whether fairness should take priority over efficiency—is a policy judgment that such an algorithm would not settle.

So I would reject the proponent’s anchor under the required test. But I cannot honestly establish the broader claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile continuous work: repeated refugee types and cohort-scaled capacities are plausible. The negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchored continuization candidate, but weak as a universal claim that no useful continuous asylum-matching problem exists.
