The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. It establishes no result about an underlying problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. So I can’t honestly state a continuous computational problem per anchor; the paper’s own instruction says to report that plainly when there are none.

The closest result is Theorem 5, proved here: for an order \(R\) on \(\ell_\infty^r\) satisfying super weak Pareto and weak translation invariance, there exists a nonzero, continuous, positive linear functional \(\phi\) such that \(\phi(x)>\phi(y)\) implies \(x\succ y\). This is a representation theorem, not an algorithmic result. Theorem 3, also proved here, decomposes such functionals into a weighted summable component and a component whose restriction to \(c^r\) is proportional to the weighted limit functional. Theorem 1 is a finite-dimensional result cited from d’Aspremont and Gevers (2002); it is also axiomatic, not computational.

The most favorable connection is that intergenerational welfare has infinitely many people indexed by time, and the paper’s results concern social rankings over their utility streams. But its mathematical move is to make the horizon infinite, not to represent society as a distribution over types. A proposed type-and-mass model would need a separate aggregation problem that this paper neither formulates nor solves. That is the weak point the positive case cannot overcome: the paper offers relevant social-choice mathematics and a possible setting for future work, but no anchor-backed continuous computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the anchor stage: this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its theorems and corollaries concern axioms, representation, and the structure of social welfare orders. They give no finite input, computational task, algorithm, or complexity bound. Theorem 1 is a cited finite dimensional characterization; Theorem 3 is a decomposition of a functional-analytic dual space; Theorem 5 is the paper’s main axiomatic existence result. None supplies a computational problem for ChoCo to continuize.

The best attempt to build one from Theorem 5 would be: given a finitely described intergenerational model, find a positive continuous linear functional witnessing the theorem’s implication, or compute a maximal feasible utility stream. That could be a worthwhile project if the model were specified. But the theorem takes an order on all of \(\ell_\infty^r\) as its premise; it gives no finite representation of that order or of a witness. Its Hahn–Banach proof is nonconstructive, and the witness need not be uniquely determined. Restrict the model to a finite horizon and the paper’s distinctive infinite-horizon issue disappears, leaving a finite dimensional characterization. Preserve the infinite horizon and one must choose an encoding for streams, feasible sets, and tail behavior. The resulting complexity question depends on those additions, rather than mirroring Theorem 5.

Theorem 3 has the same gap. A computational question about decomposing or evaluating a functional needs a finite description of that functional. The short-term component can be represented by summable weights; the linear-limit component depends on asymptotic behavior of infinite streams, with no general finite encoding provided here. Restrict to finite-support weights and that component vanishes. Choose a special computable class of limits and the question becomes specific to that class. Theorem 1 cannot fill the gap: it is both axiomatic and cited from prior work.

A generous population interpretation would put many people in each generation and aggregate each cohort. But the date index is essential to the paper’s distinction between short-term and long-term interests. Treating date as part of a type preserves that structure at the cost of infinitely many date types; collapsing dates loses the tail behavior the paper studies. A continuous time model would continuize time, while a continuum within each cohort would introduce a population model without adding a computational result from this paper. Either could motivate new research, but neither is a mirror of the results here.

So I would judge the case against a ChoCo mirror as strong: there is no paper-defined computational question to carry across, and the most plausible reconstructions require substantial new input models. I would not claim that no computational project on intergenerational welfare could ever be worthwhile. The narrower conclusion is that such a project would be a new computational agenda, not a continuous mirror anchored in this paper.
