The continuous mirror question

Given buyer types \(B\) and supplier types \(S\), with rational masses \(\mu\), per-agent demands \(d_b\), willingness-to-pay values \(v_b\), capacities \(q_s\), asks \(a_s\), and eligible routes with costs \(w_{bs}\), compute an allocation \(x_{bs}\ge 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{b\in B,s\in S}(v_b-a_s-w_{bs})x_{bs}\), subject to \(\sum_s x_{bs}\le \mu_b d_b\) and \(\sum_b x_{bs}\le \mu_s q_s\), and return supporting equilibrium prices or dual values. The question is whether this mass allocation and its supporting prices can be computed for resource-pooling supply auctions over a society represented by distributions of firm types.

The model it lives in

An atomless procurement market with buyer and supplier types that specify complete bid, quantity, location, route, and pooling profiles. Each type carries mass; decision variables \(x_{bs}\) allocate goods between types, with mass-scaled demand and capacity bounds. A linear allocation objective and dual supporting prices describe the proposed equilibrium.

The objection that survived

The proposed surplus objective and dual prices replace SAMSRP’s bidder-ranked hedging prices and auctioneer objective; the proponent flags the pricing change but does not explain why this objective shift remains the paper’s equilibrium problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s SAMSRP allocation and Walrasian-equilibrium claim. It leaves the incentive-compatibility result and the DAMDRP and DAMRP algorithms and equilibrium claims aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what conditions does the type-mass allocation approximate outcomes of large finite SAMSRP instances?
  • Can weighted bid cutoffs in the mass model retain any of SAMSRP’s incentive-compatibility guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is the type-continuum supply auction with resource pooling, anchored on Theorem 3. The theorem, proved in this paper, says: “The auction model (SAMSRP) with algorithm (SAMSRP) has Walrasian equilibrium.” In full, its equilibrium consists of hedging prices and an allocation from suppliers to manufacturers such that each winning manufacturer receives its allocated amount at an acceptable hedging price, the auctioneer’s utility is maximized by the allocation, and the supply committed to the auction is sold to the winning manufacturers. The algorithm computes the allocation using the linear program in (10).

A plausible regime is a large procurement network for standardized components: many small manufacturers and suppliers participate, but their bids, per-firm demand or capacity, location, logistics costs, and pooling status fall into relatively few recurring profiles. A type records that complete profile; its mass is the fraction of the participating population with it. Thus \(N\) firms can be large while the numbers of buyer and supplier types, \(\tau_B\) and \(\tau_S\), remain moderate. This is a scenario claim, not a claim that all supply-chain auctions have high multiplicity.

I would hand a prover the following problem, TC-SAMSRP-WE. The input is a finite set \(B\) of buyer types and \(S\) of supplier types, with rational masses \(\mu\) over \(B\cup S\), summing to one. Each buyer type \(b\) has a per-agent demand \(d_b\) and per-unit willingness to pay \(v_b\); each supplier type \(s\) has per-agent capacity \(q_s\) and per-unit ask \(a_s\). The input also gives the eligible buyer–supplier routes and their per-unit logistics costs \(w_{bs}\), including costs induced by resource-pooling networks. The question is to compute a mass allocation \(x_{bs}\ge 0\) and supporting equilibrium prices or dual values, with

\[ \sum_{s\in S}x_{bs}\le \mu_b d_b,\qquad \sum_{b\in B}x_{bs}\le \mu_s q_s, \]

that maximize total net allocation value,

\[ \sum_{b\in B}\sum_{s\in S}(v_b-a_s-w_{bs})x_{bs}. \]

The output must include a certificate of optimality and supporting prices satisfying the corresponding complementary-slackness conditions. Aggregate flow may be fractional: it represents allocating goods to a fraction of a type’s mass, with each firm receiving an integral bundle where the goods are indivisible.

This is a close mirror of the paper’s supply-auction allocation and equilibrium claim: it retains buyer and supplier populations, resource pooling, logistics costs, an allocation objective, and a Walrasian-equilibrium output. Its expected direction is Class A: the type-level allocation is a rational linear program with \(O(\tau_B\tau_S)\) flow variables and polynomially many constraints, so standard exact LP methods should give polynomial time in the support size and input bit length. That is a proposed complexity result for the mirror; the paper itself does not prove it.

The main qualification is that an atomless population does not preserve the paper’s literal individual second-highest-bid pricing rule. The mirror uses aggregate competitive prices from the LP’s dual, the natural price notion for an equilibrium among negligible participants. Whether those prices can also implement the paper’s incentive-compatibility claim—especially against deviations by positive-mass groups—is a further question, not something Theorem 3 establishes. I would keep the mirror scoped to Theorem 3’s allocation-and-equilibrium result.

This paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise; it lists complexity analysis as future work. Theorem 3 is my anchor because it is a named result about an allocation algorithm that the paper actually computes with, not because the paper settles its complexity. Further questions include whether the continuum allocation approximates large finite SAMSRP instances, how small support can be exploited in practice, and when equilibrium prices retain any incentive-compatibility guarantee.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against this anchor is that Theorem 3 is not a complexity result. It says the finite SAMSRP algorithm’s allocation satisfies the paper’s definition of Walrasian equilibrium; its proof points back to the algorithm’s LP and the conditions in that definition. The paper leaves complexity analysis for future work. So the theorem does not itself identify a computational problem whose complexity a continuous mirror could chart.

The proposed TC-SAMSRP-WE also changes what the algorithm computes. It maximizes total surplus and returns dual prices. The paper’s SAMSRP algorithm uses bid-ranked hedging prices, pooled supply commitments, and an auctioneer objective. A closer continuum version would have to specify what happens to those prices and incentives. Under the natural atomless version, individual bidders have no price-setting influence; hedging prices become market-clearing cutoffs. That can be a sensible model, but it is a different market mechanism from the one Theorem 3 verifies.

Even granting that version, its computational core is a capacitated transportation problem. For identical buyer and supplier types, the aggregate flow is \(x_{bs}=\sum_{i\in b,j\in s} Q_{ij}\), with capacities \(\mu_b d_b\) and \(\mu_s q_s\). Grouping identical rows and columns in the paper’s finite LP gives that same type-level problem. Its dual supplies the proposed price certificate. Computing market equilibria through LP formulations and complementary slackness is established algorithmic machinery; for example, [Garg and Kapoor formulate market equilibrium as a parameterized LP](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/moor.1060.0216). Thus this mirror appears to offer an immediate aggregation of an LP the paper already solves, rather than a new complexity question.

That is a case for low priority, not a proof that every mirror fails. A repeated-profile procurement setting is plausible, and the proposed mass allocation is mathematically coherent. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous version. The negative case is strongest if ChoCo requires a paper’s anchor to expose a distinct computational gap; it is weak if a straightforward high-multiplicity reformulation of an existing algorithm counts as a worthwhile mirror.
