What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive angle is that the population model fits naturally. For a large registry where many people share the same \(k\)-attribute bundle, let each bundle \(t\in T\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^k\) be a type and let rational \(\mu_t\) be its share of the population, with \(\tau\ll n\). A Pigou–Dalton bundle transfer then moves mass between richer and poorer bundles while preserving their order. The paper’s sums over individuals can be replaced by expectations under \(\mu\), making this a genuine continuous-population version; attributes remain individual bundles.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor that mirror. Its closest results, Propositions 1 and 3, are proved here: Proposition 1 characterizes relative aggregative inequality measures satisfying PDB, and Proposition 3 characterizes separable social evaluation functions satisfying PDB and the resulting indices. Neither asserts an algorithmic or complexity result. Their continuous-population counterparts would be axiomatic characterizations, while ChoCo’s scope is computational questions. Turning them into an index-evaluation or recognition problem would add a new computational question rather than mirror one the paper studies.

So the population-continuum model is plausible, but I cannot give a qualifying anchor or a prover-ready continuous problem *per anchor* without stretching the paper’s contribution. That is the case’s weakest point—and, under the stated anchor rule, decisive.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed registry scenario is sensible: if many people share the same attribute bundle, representing their population shares by masses is a legitimate high-multiplicity model. A PDB transfer can also be defined between masses of richer and poorer types. I cannot make a convincing case that the population itself has no continuous interpretation.

That interpretation does not give this paper a computational result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 3 characterize families of inequality measures and social evaluation functions; Proposition 2 relates axioms. None poses an algorithmic problem or studies its complexity. Replacing averages over \(n\) people with expectations under a distribution would give continuous versions of the paper’s characterizations, but those remain axiomatic results.

One could propose a stronger computational follow-up: given costs and a target inequality level, find the least-cost PDB-compatible mass transfer that reaches it. That is coherent, but it adds the costs, target, and optimization problem the paper never studies. It would be a new project, not a mirror of a result here.

So the negative case is strongest at the boundary of the programme: this is not a computational paper to continuize. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile computational question could ever grow from it. The high-multiplicity setting is plausible; what is missing is a computational question in the paper itself to anchor the mirror.
