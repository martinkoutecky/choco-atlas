What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror reproduces Theorem 2's minimum over cultures and its cyclic attaining distribution; it does not cover Theorem 1's impartial-culture asymptotic or evaluate the probability for an arbitrary fixed culture.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror is an exact optimization over population composition, anchored only by Theorem 2. Picture a large association with many members but relatively few distinct preference rankings. Each ranking is a type, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the association with type \(t\). A randomly selected committee of \(2k-1\) members decides by pairwise majority. The continuous problem is: over all mass distributions \(\mu\) on the complete rankings of \(n\) alternatives, find the distribution that minimizes the probability the committee has a Condorcet winner, and return both that minimum and an attaining distribution. Sampling a fixed-size committee without replacement from a finite association converges to the paper’s independent sampling model as the association grows.

Theorem 2, proved in this paper, gives the exact answer: for every \(n\ge1\), \(k\ge1\), and distribution \(\pi\) over rankings, the probability that \(2k-1\) independently drawn voters have a Condorcet winner is at least
\[ n^{-(2k-2)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\binom{2k-1}{\ell}(n-1)^\ell. \]
Equality is attained by placing mass \(1/n\) on each of the \(n\) cyclic rankings \((A_i,A_{i+1},\ldots,A_n,A_1,\ldots,A_{i-1})\), and zero elsewhere. Read as a population-composition problem, this is an exact optimization result with an explicit sparse optimizer and value. I expect this unrestricted version to be tractable; the theorem provides the solution, though it does not state a formal runtime bound.

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s worst-case probability over cultures. It does not cover Theorem 1’s impartial-culture asymptotic, or establish how to compute the probability for an arbitrary fixed society. Further questions include uniqueness of the minimizing composition, restrictions on which types may occur, and the finite-population correction when the committee is sampled without replacement.

The weak point is that the source population is an interpretation of \(\pi\), which the paper already uses as a probability distribution over voter types. The large-population committee scenario makes mass mean actual population share, but the mathematics of the sampling question is unchanged. The paper also does not present a complexity classification. So this is a plausible, narrow population mirror of an exact optimization result, rather than a strong new complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, and it is also where the mirror collapses into the paper’s existing model. Its \(\pi\) is already an arbitrary distribution over complete voter types, and its objective is already the probability of a Condorcet winner for independent draws from that distribution. Calling \(\pi\) the type masses of a large association leaves both the feasible distributions and the objective unchanged. The theorem already minimizes over that entire simplex and gives the minimum and an attaining distribution. This is a collision with the paper’s model, rather than an answer supplied by outside literature to a new mirror.

The association scenario makes the interpretation plausible: a fixed-size random committee sampled without replacement from a large population approaches the paper’s independent-draw model. But the committee remains the electorate being analyzed. If the whole population votes and its type masses are the continuous society, pairwise majority is determined by those masses; the paper’s probability over \(2k-1\) independent rankings is no longer the quantity being studied. Keeping a random committee preserves the paper’s probability question and its existing distributional model.

The paper offers no separate complexity result to mirror. Theorem 1 varies the number of alternatives while holding the voter count fixed; Theorem 2 solves an exact extremal probability problem over cultures. Asking for the probability under an arbitrary input distribution could be a computational question, but it computes the paper’s existing random-profile probability, with population mass serving as the sampling law. Constraining the allowed cultures or keeping exact finite-population sampling without replacement could produce new problems; those additions supply the new substance, and the latter retains finite-population effects that disappear in the continuous limit.

So the negative case is strongest for this reason: the proposed population reading is sensible, yet it does not leave a new continuous question for ChoCo to chart. The universal claim remains hard to establish—one could build worthwhile problems around this setting—but those would need a new constraint or objective, rather than a continuous mirror of the result raised here.
