The continuous mirror question

Given a finite bipartite graph of worker and firm types with rational type masses \(\mu_v\), rational contract capacities \(b_e\), and finite descriptions of continuous substitutable, size-monotone choice functions, can one construct in time polynomial in the support size, description size, and bit length a standard stable-allocation instance with at most \(2|E|\) vertices? Require aggregate output capacities to equal \(b_e\), output quota totals to equal the common stable contract mass at each type, and every stable mass allocation \(x\) to map to a stable output allocation whose contract masses sum to \(x_e\), with the map preserving lattice order and respecting an order-saturating decomposition.

The model it lives in

A finite bipartite graph whose vertices are worker and firm types carrying rational masses, with rational capacities on type-pair contracts and finite piecewise-linear descriptions of type-level choice functions. A contract-mass vector \(x\) gives type \(v\) per-capita offers \(x_v/\mu_v\); it is stable when acceptable and no feasible positive increase on a contract is attractive to both endpoint types. The task is to construct a smaller standard stable-allocation instance with linear preferences and a map from stable mass outcomes that preserves their order.

The objection that survived

With ordinary ranking and quota preferences, cohorts already fit the standard stable-allocation model; the mirror becomes nontrivial only with richer choice functions, making its link to population-driven complexity gains less direct.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6.1’s aggregated compression and its order-preserving map. It leaves Theorem 3.1’s stable-matching embedding and the rotation and poset results aside. The known Alkan–Gale real-valued model provides stable outcomes and lattice structure, but not this compression.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the aggregated splitting representation be constructed in time polynomial in the type support, choice-function description size, and input bit length for continuous piecewise-linear choice functions?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named \( \mathrm{P} \), \( \mathrm{NP} \)-hardness, or parameterized-complexity result in this paper. Its strongest computationally relevant anchor is instead a constructive representation theorem. That falls short of the programme’s usual hardness anchor, so the case below is for a new algorithmic mirror of the paper’s central construction.

The anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper’s Section 6, with some final construction details left to the reader. It says that an integer stable generalized allocation instance \((G,b,C)\) can be transformed into an aggregated stable allocation instance. For each original vertex \(v\), the output quotas over its preimages sum to \(\max\{\theta(v),1\}\), where \(\theta(v)\) is the common total allocation at \(v\) across stable outcomes; for each original edge \(e\), the output capacities over its preimages sum to \(b(e)\). Each stable generalized allocation \(x\) maps to a stable allocation \(\kappa_x\) in the new instance, with its values summing to \(x(e)\) over each edge’s preimages and respecting the stated capacity-saturation order. The map preserves lattice order, and the output has at most \(2|E|\) vertices when the original graph has no isolated vertices. The construction’s accompanying size estimate is \(O(N^2|E|)\) edges, where \(N\) is the length of a full route and \(N\le b_{\max}|E|\). The theorem does not claim that every stable allocation in the output comes from an original one, or that the order is reflected.

A plausible continuous mirror is Mass Stable-Lattice Compression. Its instance is a finite bipartite graph of worker and firm *types*, with rational masses \(\mu_v>0\) on the types, normalized to one on each side; rational capacities \(b_e\) on type-pair contracts; and rational piecewise-linear per-capita choice functions \(C_v\), given by finite descriptions of total size \(K\). Each choice function acts on per-capita contract offers and satisfies the continuous versions of the paper’s substitutability and size-monotonicity conditions. An outcome is a contract-mass vector \(x\), with \(0\le x_e\le b_e\); type \(v\) sees per-capita offers \(x_v/\mu_v\). It is acceptable when those offers are chosen by \(C_v\), and stable when no under-capacity contract can be increased by a positive amount that both endpoint types would choose. The computational task is to construct a standard stable allocation instance with linear preferences, rational capacities and quotas, and a map \(h\) back to the input types and contracts. A solution must have at most \(2|E|\) output vertices, preserve edge capacities in aggregate, and give every stable mass outcome a stable output allocation via an order-saturating decomposition of its contract masses. The map must preserve the stable-set order. Quota totals should be the normalized mass analogue of \(\theta(v)\): the common total contract mass at type \(v\); when that mass is zero, a zero-quota node suffices. The question is whether such a representation can always be constructed in time polynomial in the support size \(\tau=|V|\), \(K\), and input bit length \(L\), independent of the number of people represented by the masses.

The regime is a large two-sided labor market with millions of workers and positions but relatively few complete types: each type fixes preferences, contract choices, and quotas, so members are interchangeable for the model’s purposes. Mass is the fraction of the market of each type, and \(x_e\) is the mass entering a contract between two types. The target is the whole stable-solution structure, not a welfare objective. This keeps the paper’s central question—compressing a stable lattice into a simpler stability model—while making population mass, rather than individually named agents, the object.

I would expect the piecewise-linear version to be Class A, as a conjecture: finitely many choice-function regions may support a finite continuous analogue of the rotation construction, with LP or polyhedral methods replacing unit-by-unit moves. The mirror does not weaken stability to obtain that hope; it asks for a stable representation of every original stable outcome and preserves their order. It also raises whether the map can be an order isomorphism, and whether discrete high-multiplicity instances converge to this representation with controlled rounding error.

The soft spot is substantial: in Theorem 6.1, \(\theta(v)\) counts contracts held by one original agent, and the splitting proof uses integer rotations, a full route, and the one-peak behavior of edge values. A type cohort’s mass is not literally a high-multiplicity version of that individual, and the discrete edge bound depends pseudo-polynomially on \(b_{\max}\). A continuous construction polynomial in \((\tau,K,L)\) would therefore need new ideas, not a direct limit argument. The paper’s named results support the representation question; they do not establish its computational answer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that Theorem 6.1 is primarily a representation theorem, not a result about the computational complexity of a market problem. Its output size depends on the length \(N\) of a full route, with \(N\le b_{\max}|E|\); the construction is tied to integer rotations and, in the paper’s surrounding results, pseudo-polynomial bounds and unit-cost choice-function oracles. So the theorem does not establish an efficient algorithm in the binary input size that a continuous version could straightforwardly improve.

The best population-based reformulation is still coherent: model large cohorts of interchangeable workers and firms as types, use rational masses and type-level choice functions, and ask whether their stable mass outcomes admit the same kind of representation by a smaller stable-allocation market. The objection is that this no longer mirrors the theorem’s concrete computational setting. In a symmetric market with ordinary quota and ranking preferences, the cohort model is already a standard stable-allocation model, so the proposed compression is immediate. To make it nontrivial, one needs general piecewise-linear choice functions; then the question is whether one continuous market formalism can represent another, rather than whether continuizing the population unlocks computation.

The paper itself places real-valued capacities and continuous choice functions in the Alkan–Gale framework, which already supplies stable outcomes and a distributive lattice. That is not a collision: it does not answer the proposed compression question. But it means the continuous mirror’s remaining contribution is narrow—the additional representation by a simpler model—while the usual population-complexity payoff is not apparent. The integer proof’s route and rotation machinery does not show that this compression is efficiently computable from a finite description of continuous choice functions.

This is not a decisive obstruction. A finite-description representation problem could be worthwhile, and the symmetric cohort scenario is sensible. I cannot honestly rule out every useful mirror of Theorem 6.1; the negative case is weak precisely because the stronger reformulation survives. My case is that the paper gives little reason to expect this particular mirror to advance the programme’s computational agenda, not that the question is ill-posed or already answered.
