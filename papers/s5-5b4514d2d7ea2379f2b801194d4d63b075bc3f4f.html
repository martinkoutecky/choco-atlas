The continuous mirror question

Given \(a_0\prec a_1\), a rich domain \(\mathcal D\) of classical preferences, and finite support \(\{(R_t,\mu_t)\}_{t=1}^{\tau}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), where \(r_t=V^{R_t}(a_1,(a_0,0))\), choose a deterministic DSIC and individually rational mechanism on \(\mathcal D\) that maximizes revenue per capita for a continuum of customers with type masses \(\mu_t\). Equivalently, find \(q^\star\in\arg\max_{q\in\{0,r_1,\ldots,r_\tau\}}q\sum_{t:r_t\ge q}\mu_t\) and the posted-price rule that serves type \(t\) when \(r_t\ge q^\star\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of customers with complete preference types in \(\mathcal D\), represented by masses \(\mu_t\); each customer receives either \(a_0\) or \(a_1\), and supply scales with population. Choose a common price \(q\) to maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t q\,\mathbf{1}\{r_t\ge q\}\); there is no shared-capacity constraint.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers finite-support revenue maximization with two alternatives and per-person service supply; it leaves the general multi-alternative optimization problem and the paper’s other results without a complexity classification.

Open questions for a prover

  • For \(m>2\), what is the exact complexity of finite-support revenue maximization over the paper’s canonical monotone mechanisms on a fixed rich preference domain?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: the paper’s optimal-contract problem has a plausible high-multiplicity version in a large population of recurring customers. It is not an auction for one scarce object. Think of a service with one slot per customer, so supply scales with the population and each person still receives either the service or the outside option.

My sole anchor is Corollary 2, proved in this paper. It says that for \(\delta>0\), if the preference domain \(\mathcal D\) satisfies \(\mathrm{TP}^{\delta}_{\succ}\)-richness, every optimal mechanism is a solution to the program that maximizes \(\int_{\mathcal D} p_f(R)\,d\mu(R)\) over allocation rules \(f:\mathcal D\to A\), subject to the canonical mechanism \((f,p_f)\) being monotone. Here \(p_f(R)=0\) when \(f(R)=a_0\); when \(f(R)=a\ne a_0\), \(p_f(R)=\inf_{R':f(R')=a}V^{R'}(a,(a_0,0))\). The paper uses this formulation in Section 7 to work out the two-alternative case. This is a formulation anchor under the prompt’s exception, not a numbered P/NP/FPT result: the paper gives no complexity classification.

A concrete mirror is Per-Capita Non-Quasilinear Service Pricing. Fix two alternatives \(a_0\prec a_1\), where \(a_0\) is the outside option. The admissible report domain is a rich domain such as the paper’s positive-income-effect preferences. An instance gives \(\tau\) complete preference types \(R_t\) from that domain, with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one. Each type has reservation value \(r_t=V^{R_t}(a_1,(a_0,0))\), supplied as a rational. The population consists of many customers, often millions, grouped by identical complete preferences; \(\mu_t\) is their actual population share. The task is to return a deterministic DSIC and individually rational mechanism maximizing revenue per capita, together with its revenue.

In this two-alternative setting, the paper’s Section 7 shows that an optimal mechanism is a cutoff: offer \(a_1\) at price \(q\), and give \(a_0\) at price zero otherwise. The finite-support population problem is therefore to choose \(q^*\in\arg\max_{q\in\{0,r_1,\ldots,r_\tau\}} q\sum_{t:r_t\ge q}\mu_t\). The solution is the maximizing price and its induced cutoff mechanism; ties at the price may be assigned the service. Sorting the reservation values and checking the candidate prices takes \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) comparisons and polynomially many exact rational operations in the input bit length. This is a tractable, narrow Class A mirror. That runtime is a consequence for this finite-support specialization, not a bound claimed by the authors.

The scenario is a large service provider with repeated, independent customer opportunities and one service slot per person. Customers of one type share the same preferences over service and payment, including income effects; the number of customers is large while the number of distinct types is moderate. Every customer’s outcome remains integral. Only the aggregate mass served can be fractional. The authors should recognize this as their revenue-maximizing deterministic contract problem applied across a type-mass population.

The weakest point is also the main limit: the paper already uses \(\mu\) as a probability measure over a single agent’s possible types and optimizes expected revenue. Interpreting that measure as realized population shares changes the population semantics, but leaves the two-alternative optimization formula essentially the same. So this is a credible population mirror, but it does not reveal a new complexity landscape or show discrete hardness dissolving. The next substantive question is whether the multi-alternative canonical-mechanism optimization admits exact algorithms parameterized by \(m,\tau,L\), and how that changes when a shared capacity constraint couples the types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Corollary 2 is a structural reduction, not a computational result. Its feasible objects are allocation rules \(f:\mathcal D\to A\) on a rich, uncountable preference domain. The theorem gives no finite representation of \(\mathcal D\), of a preference, or of such a rule. Giving the instance as \(\tau\) types and their masses does not fix that: incentive constraints still range over all of \(\mathcal D\), and the canonical payment uses an infimum over all preferences assigned the same alternative. Restrict the reports to the \(\tau\) listed types and you have changed the problem, losing the rich-domain result that anchors the proposal.

The binary service-pricing mirror avoids that obstacle, but it runs into a narrower collision. Its input measure and objective are exactly the paper’s expected-revenue setup. Section 7 already derives the cutoff and revenue \(q(1-G(q))\); with finite support, checking the candidate reservation values is just the finite-support form of that formula. Calling the probabilities population shares gives a plausible story, but independent customers with supply scaling one-for-one introduce no new aggregate decision or constraint: total revenue is the per-customer objective multiplied by population size. So this is a natural application of the paper’s continuous type distribution, not a new continuous-population computational question.

The best attempt to make the mirror more substantive is to add a shared capacity constraint that couples service across types. That could yield a worthwhile continuous optimization problem. But Corollary 2 does not establish it: the paper’s single-agent revenue characterization does not solve the resulting coupled allocation problem. It would need a new model and new results, rather than a continuization of the anchor. Likewise, a multi-alternative version might be interesting, but finite support alone still does not provide an algorithmic input for optimizing over mechanisms on the full rich domain.

This is a strong case against the proposed anchor, but it does not establish the universal negative the prompt asks for. A well-specified capacity-coupled extension could survive; the honest limitation is that its value would come from new structure beyond this paper’s stated optimization result.
