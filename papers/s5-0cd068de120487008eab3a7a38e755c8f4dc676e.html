The case FOR (proponent)

There are no eligible anchors in the supplied paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its computational findings are simulation results, especially the Section 4 comparison at the League C/D boundary and the sensitivity analyses in Section 6. The strongest anchor-like finding is that, in the authors’ model, moving Lithuania from rank 39 to rank 40 raises its simulated qualification probability from about \(1.26\%\) to \(9.01\%\), roughly \(7.14\)-fold. That is a substantive result, but it is not a named result of the kind this task requires. I can’t honestly attach a theorem number to it.

The best positive case I can make is therefore an unanchored extension of that finding: a *High-Multiplicity Tournament Fairness Audit*. An instance would give a finite set \(T\) of complete team types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a prior-performance rank \(\rho_t\) for each type, and rational match-win probabilities \(p_{tu}\). A type would include every feature the format uses: strength, league and seeding class, eligibility, and relevant location or home-advantage data. The format family would specify group assignment, match schedules, team selection, and play-off path formation. For a large club-qualifying system, the number of entrants and qualifying places would scale together, so the format remains meaningful as the population grows.

Let \(q_\pi(t;\mu)\) be the limiting probability that a representative team of type \(t\) qualifies under policy \(\pi\), including the randomness of draws and match results. Given a finite set \(\Pi\) of allowed policies, the problem is to choose a policy minimizing
\[ \max_{\substack{t,u\in T\\ \rho_t>\rho_u}} \bigl(q_\pi(u;\mu)-q_\pi(t;\mu)\bigr)_+ . \]
A solution is an optimal policy and a type pair witnessing its largest fairness violation, or a certificate that no pair violates the ordering. The continuous object here is the entrant population \(\mu\); matches remain random and each actual team either qualifies or does not.

A plausible regime is a large system with thousands of entrants but a modest catalogue of repeated team profiles: for example, teams in a multi-tier club qualification system that share strength, seeding, and eligibility characteristics. Then \(N\), the number of teams, can greatly exceed \(\tau=|T|\). The qualification quotas and group structure would need to scale with \(N\), which is a legitimate co-modification of the model. The authors could plausibly recognize the question: it preserves their central criterion—qualification chances should respect prior ranking—and their comparison of play-off path policies. It does not claim their one-off tournament of 55 distinct national teams is itself a high-multiplicity population.

For this exact audit and scalable-format version, I would expect hardness to persist when the format size is part of the input: match and bracket combinatorics remain, even when types are represented by masses. That is a reasoned prediction, not a result of this paper; the paper supplies no hardness proof to transfer. Natural follow-up questions are whether additive approximation is efficient, whether algorithms can be parameterized by \(\tau\) or bracket structure, and how to choose a format that remains fair across a range of population distributions.

This mirror covers the paper’s qualification-probability comparisons and its proposed path-formation changes. Its weakest point is substantial: the paper studies a single, small tournament with highly differentiated teams, and its main findings are statistical rather than formal complexity results. The positive case depends on moving to a large, repeated-type tournament regime and turning the authors’ fairness analysis into an explicit computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no named result: they agree the paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about computational complexity. The League C/D comparison in Section 4—including the roughly sevenfold increase for Lithuania at the rank boundary—is a simulation finding for one fixed 55-team event. Sections 5 and 6 compare policy variants and strength assumptions through further simulations. There is no computational problem in the paper with a stated complexity result to mirror.

The proposed audit supplies a new problem, but it does not repair that gap. The paper evaluates a few path-formation policies; it does not define an input family of tournament policies or ask for one that minimizes the largest fairness violation. If \(\Pi\) is just the three policies studied, choosing among them is a small comparison of estimated probabilities. If \(\Pi\) ranges over general formats, the computational problem depends on how schedules, draws, quotas, and restrictions are represented. Any difficulty could come from that new design space, without the continuous population doing substantive computational work.

A large club-qualification system is the strongest way to make the population idea plausible. But it changes the setting substantially. To preserve the paper’s probabilities, a team’s type must capture features that affect its treatment: strength, ranking, seeding, eligibility, and relevant constraints. In the UEFA instance, those features often give each national team a distinct role; retaining them leaves little multiplicity. Coarsening types can make \(\tau\) small, but then teams grouped together may face different seeding or path treatment, so a single \(q_\pi(t;\mu)\) no longer describes them uniformly.

Scaling a club system could resolve that by specifying how groups, quotas, and play-offs grow with the entrant population. That is a legitimate co-modification, but the mass distribution alone does not determine the tournament or its limiting qualification probabilities. The chosen scaling and matching rules would define a new stochastic tournament model, rather than a continuous version of the paper’s fixed-format result.

There is a real weakness in this negative case: the authors explicitly hope to inspire similar fairness studies, and they might well recognize a large club-qualification audit as an extension of their work. Repeated team profiles in such a system are plausible. So I cannot rule out every worthwhile project in the neighborhood. The strongest case against is narrower: the proponent’s proposal may be a worthwhile new tournament-design question, but the paper itself provides no named computational result to mirror, and its plausible high-multiplicity version requires enough new structure that it is difficult to call it this paper’s continuous mirror.
