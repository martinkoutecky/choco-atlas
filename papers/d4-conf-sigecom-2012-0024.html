The continuous mirror question

Given total population mass \(N\), data categories \(j\in[h]\), known finite-support conditional cost laws \(F_j\), privacy parameter \(\epsilon\), a fixed privacy-unit mass \(\delta\), and the paper’s common Bayesian belief \(G\), design a randomized contract and estimator that work for every hidden category-mass vector \(\theta\) with \(\sum_j\theta_j=N\). Require BIC and EIIR for each privacy unit, \(\epsilon\)-differential privacy when one unit changes data category and its cost is redrawn from the corresponding \(F_j\), perfect-data-privacy compensation under \(G\), and \(\Pr[|\hat\theta_1-\theta_1|\ge K]\le 1/3\); minimize expected total payment for the prescribed accuracy \(K\).

A hidden-mass society over complete types \((j,v)\), with conditional cost law \(F_j\), privacy-unit mass \(\delta\), type-dependent acceptance contracts and payments, and an estimator based on accepted category-\(1\) mass plus privacy noise.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a mass version of selling privacy for statistical estimation. A credible regime is a very large data cooperative estimating how many vehicle owners have damaged cars. There are many users, but only a few data categories and standardized privacy-cost tiers. A complete type is \((j,v)\): the user’s data category \(j\) and privacy cost \(v\). Many users share each type, so the number of distinct types stays small as the population grows. This is the paper’s setting, with the number of users replaced by mass.

The paper has no named result classifying a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its best anchors are constructive mechanism-design results, rather than complexity classifications.

Lead anchor: Theorem 3.9, proved in this paper. It states that Mechanism 1 is BIC, EIIR, \(O(\epsilon^{-1})\)-accurate, perfectly data private, and \(\epsilon\)-differentially private. The mechanism offers type-dependent thresholds, uses the data of users who accept, adds Laplace noise to its estimate and payments, and estimates the count of users in a target data category.

A precise mirror is Continuous Privacy-Selling Estimation. The instance has total population mass \(N\), a fixed set of data categories \(j\in[h]\), a publicly known finite-support cost distribution \(F_j\) for each category, a privacy parameter \(\epsilon\), and an unknown mass \(\theta_j\) in each category. The target is \(\theta_1\). A solution is a contract and randomized mechanism that specifies acceptance and payment rules for each complete type \((j,v)\), and returns an estimate \(\hat\theta_1\). Require BIC and EIIR for each unit of mass, \(\epsilon\)-differential privacy when one unit of mass changes data category and its cost is redrawn from the corresponding \(F_j\), and the paper’s perfect-data-privacy compensation condition. For a chosen accuracy bound \(K\), require
\[ \Pr[|\hat\theta_1-\theta_1|\ge K]\le \tfrac13. \]
The mechanism’s operational objective is to estimate \(\theta_1\) while paying users enough to participate; its expected total payment is the cost to report and, in a payment-minimization version, to optimize.

The paper’s threshold construction gives this mirror a concrete route. Choose a constant acceptance rate \(c\), and choose thresholds \(\alpha_j\) (randomizing at a cost atom when needed) so that every data category accepts at rate \(c\). In a continuum population, the accepted mass from category 1 is then exactly \(c\theta_1\). Estimating with
\[ \hat\theta_1=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,N]}\left(\frac{M+\operatorname{Lap}(1/\epsilon)}{c}\right) \]
gives \(O(\epsilon^{-1})\) count accuracy for constant \(c\); the finite-sample acceptance variance in the paper’s bound disappears in the mass limit. The threshold also makes acceptance uninformative about data category in aggregate, while accepted mass is compensated. With finite cost support, this is a finite-type contract-design problem with a direct quantile-based construction. I expect this mirror to be tractable, in Class A.

Second anchor: Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper. For the paper’s nonnegative-cost procurement comparison, it states that Mechanism 1’s expected payment is a factor of at most \(2\) above the benchmark. In the single-category \(w\)-unit comparison, the benchmark is the minimum expected payment for the corresponding truthful, ex-post individually rational, envy-free procurement mechanisms; the paper uses the lower bound \(w\mathbb E[v_{w+1}]\). The proof here relies on the paper’s Lemma 4.2 and on Lemma 4.1, which is cited from Ghosh and Roth [8].

Its mirror is Continuous Mass Procurement for Privacy Data. Given a mass \(N\) of users with cost law \(F\), and a desired procurement mass \(w=qN\), choose an allocation fraction \(x(v)\in[0,1]\) for each cost type and a payment rule. Require \(\int x(v)\,dF(v)=q\) and truthful, individually rational behavior; minimize total expected payment and compare it with the continuous envy-free benchmark for procuring the same mass. The solution is an allocation and payment schedule, not an encoded formulation. With finite cost tiers, the input and allocation variables are compact; with a non-atomic cost law, the natural candidate is a posted price at the \(q\)-quantile. The finite theorem gives a constant-factor reference point, while the mass version may admit an exact quantile characterization. This also looks Class A.

Both mirrors cover the paper’s central problem of estimating a sensitive population statistic while compensating users whose data are used or learned. They do not treat its Bayesian analysis as a differential-privacy problem, nor confuse cost distributions with a continuous society: the proposed change is in the population representation itself. The authors should recognize the connection because the target statistic, data-cost correlation, user incentives, and compensation objective all remain intact.

My weakest point is the privacy unit. In a truly atomless society, changing one individual changes zero mass, so individual-level differential privacy would become vacuous. The mirror therefore needs an explicit privacy unit—mass \(1\), or \(1/N\) in a normalized society—and mechanisms that still return payments and guarantees for each such unit. That is a co-modification of the model, but it preserves the paper’s individual privacy and incentive questions. If one instead takes an atomless limit with no privacy unit, this would no longer be a strong mirror of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a mismatch between the paper’s object and ChoCo’s usual input model. Neither Theorem 3.9 nor Theorem 4.3 classifies a computational problem by complexity or gives a running-time bound. Theorem 3.9 proves incentive, privacy, and accuracy guarantees for an explicit mechanism; Theorem 4.3 bounds its expected payment against a procurement benchmark. A direct continuous restatement would therefore not yield the kind of complexity result ChoCo is built to chart.

That mismatch is sharpest for Theorem 3.9. In ChoCo’s model, the society’s type masses are given. If the types are the complete types \((j,v)\), those masses reveal the target mass \(\theta_1=\sum_v\mu_{(1,v)}\); estimating it is no longer the paper’s problem. The proponent avoids that by making \(\theta\) unknown and supplying only the conditional cost laws \(F_j\). That is a coherent Bayesian mechanism-design question, but its input is not a known society distribution. To keep the paper’s unknown database, the mirror must add a hidden-composition or prior model. This makes the mirror harder to place within ChoCo’s stated framework.

Theorem 4.3 has a related limitation. Its anchor is a single-category procurement comparison, which leaves out the correlated data, statistic estimation, and perfect-data-privacy features that make the paper distinctive. A richer continuous procurement problem could restore those features, but it would again require a hidden population composition and individual-level incentive and privacy constraints. The resulting problem may be worthwhile; it would be a new mechanism-design optimization problem, rather than a direct continuous version of the theorem’s payment comparison.

These objections do not establish that no scenario works. Finite data categories and finite privacy-cost tiers give a credible high-multiplicity regime: complete types are finite, and the paper’s discrete-cost extension already covers that setting. A privacy unit can also preserve the individual-level privacy question. The proponent’s first anchor can thus be made coherent if ChoCo admits mechanism-design synthesis with an unknown aggregate composition.

So the negative case is conditional and, against the requested universal claim, weak. If ChoCo requires the society distribution itself to be the known instance, the estimation mirror breaks because its target is already encoded in that input. If the programme includes Bayesian privacy-selling with hidden aggregate composition, I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror of Theorem 3.9.
