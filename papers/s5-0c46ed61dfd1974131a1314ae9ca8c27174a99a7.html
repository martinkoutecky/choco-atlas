The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified: the paper has a very natural continuous-population mirror, but it has no qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s rules.

The best candidate is the paper’s Kemeny result. A type is a complete ranking \(\sigma\in S_m\). In a large consultation or expert panel, many agents may submit one of a relatively small number \(\tau\) of recurring ranking templates, with type mass \(\mu_\sigma\). The continuous society is the rational distribution \(\mu\), where \(n_\sigma/n=\mu_\sigma\). The number of agents is enormous compared with the number of distinct ranking types.

Continuous Kemeny Consensus. Given alternatives \(A\), a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, and Kendall distance \(d_K\), output a consensus ranking

\[ \pi\in\arg\min_{\rho\in S_m}\sum_{\sigma\in S_m}\mu_\sigma d_K(\sigma,\rho). \]

Equivalently, using the paper’s election matrix \(X^\mu\), output any \(\pi\) maximizing \(\langle X^\mu,X^\pi\rangle\). The decision variable remains an integral ranking; only the population has been continuized.

The strongest would-be anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: “Kemeny’s rule is the unique preference function that is neutral, consistent, and Condorcet.” In full, among preference functions returning consensus rankings, Kemeny’s rule is the sole rule that treats alternatives symmetrically, satisfies consistency under aggregation of electorates, and respects the Condorcet principle. The paper also explicitly explains how to compute \(K(\Phi)\) by maximizing the profile matrix’s inner product with a permutation matrix.

This is a convincing mirror in subject-matter terms. The authors’ finite electorate already consists of repeated preference types, and their consistency proof is essentially linearity in the profile matrix. Passing from counts \(n_\sigma\) to masses \(\mu_\sigma\) therefore preserves the rule exactly. A finite profile embeds by setting \(\mu_\sigma=n_\sigma/n\), so the continuous problem is the high-multiplicity form of their consensus problem, not an arbitrary simplification.

Its expected complexity classification would be Class B: discrete Kemeny winner determination embeds directly through normalized multiplicities, so hardness should transfer rather than disappear. The paper’s permutation-polytope discussion also suggests the right computational continuation: study separation, approximation, and robustness for the mass-weighted Kemeny objective.

However, Theorem 3 is axiomatic, not a complexity theorem. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting NP-hardness, polynomial-time solvability, parameterized tractability, or a formal computational formulation. Theorem 2 and Corollary (10) are also structural impossibility results, not computational results; Theorem 1 is cited from elsewhere. Thus Continuous Kemeny Consensus is a legitimate and plausible ChoCo question, but it is not a formally compliant mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

Natural follow-ups would be continuous Kemeny approximation, minimum mass needed to make a target alternative top-ranked, and winner robustness under \(L_1\) perturbations of \(\mu\). The weakest point is decisive: the proposed problem mainly exposes the paper’s existing profile linearity, while the paper itself does not supply the computational anchor ChoCo requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong because this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 3 is an axiomatic characterization of Kemeny’s rule; Theorem 2 and Corollary (10) are impossibility and structural results. Section 5 mentions an LP over the permutation polytope, but proves no complexity bound, algorithm, approximation theorem, or computational hardness result. Thus there is no named computational result here for ChoCo to mirror.

The proponent’s Kemeny construction is mathematically legitimate:

\[ \pi\in\arg\min_{\rho\in S_m}\sum_{\sigma\in S_m}\mu_\sigma d_K(\sigma,\rho). \]

But for every rational \(\mu\), writing \(\mu_\sigma=n_\sigma/n\) produces exactly the finite profile already covered by the paper. Kemeny depends only on the aggregate matrix

\[ X^\mu=\sum_{\sigma}\mu_\sigma X^\sigma, \]

and multiplying all multiplicities by \(n\) changes nothing. The paper itself explicitly passes to rational election matrices and uses their convex structure. Consequently, “continuous Kemeny consensus” does not expose a new computational object: it is the same weighted Kemeny optimization written in normalized coordinates. Extending from rational to real masses is a closure operation, not a new computational population regime.

This is not the objection that the answer happens to be unchanged. The programme expressly allows that. The problem is that the population has already disappeared from the problem before continuization: anonymity and the election matrix reduce the electorate to weighted pairwise margins. There is no population-sensitive algorithmic phenomenon for the mirror to investigate.

The stronger proposed follow-ups do not repair this. Approximation and winner determination are simply weighted Kemeny problems, with rational weights rescalable to finite multiplicities. \(L_1\)-robustness over the profile simplex could be an interesting new sensitivity study, but it is not a computational consequence of Theorem 3; the same question can be posed for a weighted finite profile. Likewise, “minimum mass needed to make \(c^\star\) Kemeny-winning” requires adding conversion operations, prices, budgets, and tie conventions absent from the paper. That could launch a new Kemeny-manipulation project, but it would be imported from ChoCo rather than mirrored from a computational result of this paper.

A more elaborate type space does not help. If the extra attributes affect the problem, the model is no longer the paper’s Kemeny rule; if they do not, they are irrelevant. The plausible large-panel story establishes that high multiplicity is sensible, but not that continuization creates a worthwhile computational question.

I would therefore reject this paper under the programme’s stated gate. The broad claim that no researcher could ever formulate a useful continuous Kemeny manipulation or robustness problem would be too strong; the proponent is right that such questions can be invented. But they are new ChoCo problems attached to an axiomatic paper, not continuous mirrors of one of its named computational results.
