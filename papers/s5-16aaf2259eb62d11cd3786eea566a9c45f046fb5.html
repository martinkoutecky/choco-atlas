The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

No computational anchor is raised in the attached case, and the paper offers none. Proposition 3.1 gives a witness to a logical contradiction; Proposition 3.2 gives a witness to an unfair path assignment; Proposition 4.1 proves that a fair assignment can be reached by swaps. These are claims about consistency, fairness, and existence. The paper does not analyze the complexity of computing a solution. Its observation that UEFA supplies no algorithm is a gap in the regulation, not a complexity result.

The strongest nearby computational question would be: given teams’ league, group-winner status, rank, and qualification status, construct a valid, fair partition into paths. But the paper’s tournament has 55 distinct national teams and 16 places in four paths. Preserving the details relevant to its rules—especially rank and who hosts a semifinal—leaves essentially one type per team. Grouping teams into broader types loses information those rules use. Repeating the tournament across seasons instead creates a distribution over instances, which continuizes uncertainty rather than the population.

One could scale the format to many teams and many paths, then ask for a type-level or fractional assignment. That could become an interesting optimization problem. It would change the paper’s fixed tournament and its team-by-team fairness claim: with mass assignments, one would need a new way to express whether a particular group winner faces a harder path. That is a separate research question, not a continuous mirror of any proposition here. The case against a mirror is strongest at this boundary; the broader claim that no related continuous tournament problem could ever be worthwhile would be harder to defend.
