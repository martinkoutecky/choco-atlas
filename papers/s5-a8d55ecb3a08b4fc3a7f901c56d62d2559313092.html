The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of secondary weight types \(T\), masses \(r_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}r_t=1\), per-member weights \(v_t>0\), and parameters \(\lambda,q\), compute the pair of minimum coalition masses: \(\min \sum_{t\in T}x_t\) subject to \(0\le x_t\le r_t\) and \(\lambda+(1-\lambda)\frac{\sum_{t\in T}v_tx_t}{\sum_{t\in T}v_tr_t}\ge q\); and \(\inf \sum_{t\in T}x_t\) subject to \(0\le x_t\le r_t\) and \((1-\lambda)\frac{\sum_{t\in T}v_tx_t}{\sum_{t\in T}v_tr_t}>1-q\).

The model it lives in

A designated primary authority retains voting share \(\lambda\), while a continuum of secondary units is distributed across finitely many weight types \(t\in T\), with mass \(r_t\) and per-member weight \(v_t\). A coalition selects mass \(x_t\) of each type; its total selected mass is the objective, and the paper's winning and blocking thresholds define feasibility.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's minimum winning and secondary-only blocking coalition sizes, extending its uniform result to mass across weight types; it leaves the paper's other structural results and GST-specific desiderata aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. For a uniform game with \(n\) participating secondary players, it states that the game is \((p,\beta)\)-efficient, where
\[ \rho=\left\lfloor\frac{n(1-q)}{1-\lambda}\right\rfloor,\qquad p=n-\rho,\qquad \beta=1+\rho, \]
so \(p+\beta=n+1\). Here \(p\) is the minimum number of secondary players needed alongside the primary player to win, and \(\beta\) is the minimum size of a secondary-only blocking coalition. The paper writes the second parameter as \(q\), reusing the symbol for the winning threshold.

A plausible mirror would model a large federation or association with one primary authority and many secondary units sharing a small set \(T\) of voting-weight types. Let \(r_t\) be the present mass of type \(t\), with a priori weight \(v_t\); a coalition chooses mass \(x_t\in[0,r_t]\). Its first question is to minimize \(\sum_t x_t\) subject to
\[ \lambda+(1-\lambda)\frac{\sum_t v_t x_t}{\sum_t v_t r_t}\ge q, \]
the least secondary mass needed for the primary to pass a resolution. Its second is to find the infimum of \(\sum_t x_t\) subject to
\[ (1-\lambda)\frac{\sum_t v_t x_t}{\sum_t v_t r_t}>1-q, \]
the least secondary mass that can block one. In a uniform population, the two thresholds are \((q-\lambda)/(1-\lambda)\) and \((1-q)/(1-\lambda)\), which sum to \(1\); Theorem 3’s finite counts converge to that relationship. The many-agent regime would be many interchangeable local units, with the number of units much larger than the number of weight types. These are linear optimization questions once the participating masses are fixed, and they lead naturally to questions about heterogeneous types and participation.

That is a credible *candidate* continuous analysis, but I would not count Theorem 3 as a qualifying computational anchor under your stated rule. It gives an exact structural formula for finite uniform games; the paper does not name or analyze a complexity problem, establish an algorithmic classification, or use an optimization formulation computationally. The other numbered results are likewise structural. The main weakness is that the proposed continuum shifts the paper’s 31 state governments into a replicated federation and adds the computational framing. So while this may be a sensible follow-on question, the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor the required positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 3 is not a computational result. It gives an exact structural formula for the minimum coalition sizes in a uniform finite game; the paper poses no search, optimization, or decision problem and makes no complexity claim. The other theorems likewise characterize voting rules and coalitions. So the paper supplies no named computational problem whose continuous version would fill the programme’s stated gap.

The proponent’s setting is plausible: a large association could have one central authority and many interchangeable member bodies grouped by voting weight. Theorem 3’s uniform case is especially amenable to that model. With \(n\) secondary members, its limits are
\[ \frac{p}{n}\to\frac{q-\lambda}{1-\lambda}, \qquad \frac{\beta}{n}\to\frac{1-q}{1-\lambda}. \]
Those are sensible continuous coalition shares. But they are a limit of the theorem’s structural formula, not a computational question the paper asks.

The proposed minimum-mass problems make that distinction visible. They add a divisible coalition and ask for its minimum mass at a given weight threshold. In the uniform case the shares follow directly from the threshold; with finitely many weight types, the optimization is a fractional knapsack problem. That could be a useful follow-on, but it is a newly posed optimization problem, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. Optimizing the schema itself would require adding an objective the paper does not specify.

A stronger repair would study the high-multiplicity discrete problem first—many member bodies per weight type—and then use the continuous model as its relaxation. That could be worthwhile on its own. But it changes the paper’s structural study into an algorithmic one; the paper provides no complexity result for that new task. Existing high-multiplicity work would support the regime’s plausibility, but would not supply the missing anchor.

I would not argue that the proposed population is inherently meaningless: the association scenario can work, and the coalition identity depends only on weights and mass. The negative case is therefore strongest under the programme’s stated criterion, not as a universal claim that no useful continuous study can grow from this paper. If a structural theorem with a newly formulated optimization problem counts as an anchor, this case weakens substantially.
