What the mirror covers

The proposed model covers HodgeRank inference on a fixed comparison graph; it leaves the paper's network and noise experiments, retrieval threshold, and scaling findings without a continuous counterpart.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying named computational-result anchor: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity classification such as tractability or NP-hardness. Equations (3)–(6) formulate HodgeRank, and Figures 2–4 report numerical findings, but neither is such an anchor. For example, Fig. 3 reports that mean ranking error stays at zero up to a nontrivial noise threshold and then grows; that is an empirical observation, not a named complexity result. I can’t honestly present it as the required anchor.

The strongest positive mirror I see is Continuous HodgeRank from a Voter Distribution. Fix alternatives \(C\), a connected graph \(G\) of compared pairs, and rational exposure weights \(q_{ij}>0\). Let \(T\) be the support of voters’ complete rankings of \(C\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to one. For each compared pair, let \(p_{ij}(\mu)\) be the mass preferring \(i\) to \(j\), and assume \(0<p_{ij}(\mu)<1\). Set \(f_{ij}(\mu)=\ln((1-p_{ij})/p_{ij})\). The task is to return, for a requested rational \(\varepsilon>0\), an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal score vector and its induced ordering, where the scores minimize

\[ \sum_{\{i,j\}\in E} q_{ij}\bigl(f_{ij}(\mu)-(w_j-w_i)\bigr)^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{i\in C} w_i=0. \]

This mirrors the underlying least-squares inference in Eq. (3), not its encoding. It makes the *voter population* continuous: a type is a complete ballot, and its mass is the fraction of society casting it. A plausible regime is a large election or review panel with many people but a moderate number \(\tau\) of distinct ballots. That is a real high-multiplicity setting, though it applies only when repeated types are common and any factor affecting comparisons—such as reliability or access to particular pairs—is included in the type.

For this proposed problem, I expect Class A: after aggregating the ballot masses, the score computation is a weighted least-squares problem on a connected graph, equivalently a Laplacian system. Approximate scores should be computable in polynomial time in the graph and input size, subject to the requested precision and numerical conditioning. That expectation is my analysis of the mirror; the paper does not prove it as a named result. Further questions include how this model relates to finite-voter inference as a high-multiplicity relaxation, and how to test ranking robustness when the ballot masses or comparison signals vary.

This mirror covers the inference step and offers a population-based counterpart to the paper’s ranking-retrieval metric. It does not directly mirror the paper’s random network generation, its Gaussian edge-noise experiments, or their reported scaling laws. The weakest point is that the paper’s vertices are the entities being ranked, while this mirror’s continuum is the voters supplying comparisons. The paper mentions voting preferences over movies, which makes the connection plausible, but changing the source of the comparison data means Fig. 3’s noise threshold does not transfer as a result. And because the paper supplies no qualifying complexity anchor, this is a plausible continuous problem inspired by its method—not a positive case anchored in one of the required named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. It reports how HodgeRank’s rating and ranking errors vary with network topology and quenched edge noise; Fig. 3’s retrieval threshold is an empirical finding, not a complexity result. Equations (3)–(6) specify the inference method, but do not classify the complexity of a computational problem.

The strongest repair is to keep the items and comparison graph fixed, and make the *raters* a continuous population: each type gives a complete ranking, and its mass determines pairwise preference rates that feed the HodgeRank least-squares objective. That is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario. It also changes the paper’s data model. The paper starts from comparison sequences on item pairs and studies independently perturbed edge signals across generated networks; it has no electorate or shared ballot population. A distribution over rankings also couples the pairwise rates, so it cannot represent all the edge signals in the paper’s experiments. Types encoding every rater’s pairwise judgments could restore that flexibility, but would introduce a crowdsourced-comparison model absent from the paper.

That alternative might be a worthwhile project in its own right, but it asks about feeding population-generated data into HodgeRank. It does not continuize a computational result from this paper, or capture the paper’s main finding about retrieval under noise and topology. Sensitivity to changes in voter masses could be posed as a new question, but it would be more distant still.

The negative case is strongest on that scope and anchoring point, not on the claim that a continuous electorate model is nonsensical. The paper’s mention of voting preferences over movies leaves a plausible voter-based reinterpretation, so I cannot rule out a worthwhile adjacent project. What the paper does not supply is a computational-result anchor showing that this reinterpretation is a continuous mirror of its contribution.
