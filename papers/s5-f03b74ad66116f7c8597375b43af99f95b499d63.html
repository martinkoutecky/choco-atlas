The continuous mirror question

Given nonnegative rational item values \(v_j\) and rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\in[0,1]\) for a population of mass \(1\) with one common additive valuation, does there exist a distribution \(x_B\geq 0\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\) such that \(\sum_B x_B=1\), \(\sum_{B\ni j}x_B=b_j\) for every item type \(j\), and the continuum Atkinson index is zero? Equivalently, when \(\bar v=\sum_j b_jv_j>0\), does such a distribution exist with \(v(B)=\bar v\) for every bundle with \(x_B>0\); when \(\bar v=0\), all allocated utility is zero.

The model it lives in

A mass-one population has one agent type with common additive valuation \(v\). Each person receives an integral bundle \(B\), while \(x_B\) is the mass receiving it; bundle masses must sum to \(1\) and realize the fixed per-capita supplies \(b_j\). The objective is to attain continuum Atkinson index zero, or more generally to minimize that index over feasible mass assignments.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the perfect-index feasibility question in Proposition 3. It leaves the distributed deal convergence and deal complexity results, and the bounds on deal sequence length, outside the stated question.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of CPIO for binary-encoded \(v\) and \(b\), as a function of the number of item types and input bit length?
  • Which valuation or supply restrictions make the equal-utility bundle pricing problem tractable, and how does the fractional optimum compare with finite allocations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror is Continuous Perfect Index Optimisation (CPIO), for Proposition 3, the paper’s strongest anchor. Proposition 3, proved in this paper, says that deciding whether a scenario has an allocation with Atkinson index \(I(A)=0\) is strongly NP-hard even for additive scenarios with symmetric utility functions. The proof reduces from 3-PARTITION: given \(3q\) items with values \(T/4<s(x)<T/2\) and total value \(qT\), create \(q\) identical agents with common additive valuation \(s\). An allocation has \(I(A)=0\) exactly when every agent has utility \(T\); the value bounds make each such bundle contain exactly three items. Thus a perfectly equal allocation exists exactly when the items have a 3-partition.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover. The population has mass \(1\), consists of one type, and shares a nonnegative additive utility vector \(v=(v_j)_{j\in G}\) over a finite catalogue \(G\) of indivisible good types. Good type \(j\) has per-capita supply \(b_j\in[0,1]\), given as a rational. A solution is a mass assignment \(x_B\geq 0\) to integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\), with \(\sum_B x_B=1\) and \(\sum_{B\ni j}x_B=b_j\) for every \(j\). Each person receives a whole bundle; \(x_B\) is the fraction of the population receiving \(B\).

The question is whether such an assignment can make the distribution of individual utilities perfectly equal. Write \(v(B)=\sum_{j\in B}v_j\) and \(\bar v=\sum_j b_jv_j\). For \(\bar v>0\), define the continuum Atkinson index by \(I_\infty(x)=1-\exp(\sum_{B:v(B)>0}x_B\ln v(B))/\bar v\), taking the geometric mean to be zero if positive mass receives zero utility. CPIO asks whether there is a feasible \(x\) with \(I_\infty(x)=0\), and returns such an \(x\) if one exists. Equivalently, all bundles receiving positive mass must have utility \(\bar v\). This is a mass assignment across individuals, not a lottery giving any individual a fractional bundle.

The regime is plausible for large allocations of standardized resources to people with the same valuation schedule. The number of people can be very large while the support of agent types is \(\tau=1\); per-capita supplies scale with population. This makes the question a direct high-multiplicity version of the paper’s allocation problem. For a finite allocation, bundle counts divided by the number of agents give \(x_B\) and \(b_j\). Conversely, rational feasible masses can be scaled to finite counts, though the required population size may be large. The object being continuized is the population; the indivisibility of each person’s bundle remains.

This mirror also shows why Proposition 3’s hardness does not automatically transfer. In its 3-PARTITION construction, set \(b_j=1/q\) for each item. Then \(\bar v=T\), and the only bundles eligible for a zero-index assignment are triples summing to \(T\). CPIO asks for a fractional exact cover by those triples: after setting \(y_B=qx_B\), each item must be covered to total weight \(1\), with \(y_B\geq0\). The discrete reduction requires an integral cover. The continuous version of this particular hardness witness is a polynomial-size LP: there are at most \(\binom{3q}{3}\) candidate triples. So this slice is Class A, and the paper’s reduction alone no longer proves hardness. Existing high-multiplicity optimization work is support for treating this regime as meaningful, not a novelty collision.

For unrestricted binary-encoded additive values, CPIO still has exponentially many possible bundles. Its equal-utility bundles are subsets summing to \(\bar v\), so the relevant pricing step is subset-sum-like. My tentative forecast for the full CPIO problem is continuum-specific hardness (Class C), but that is a prediction, not a result: the paper’s discrete hardness is not itself a proof of it. A central follow-up is to characterize when this pricing problem is tractable, including under bounded or structured valuations. The same model also invites questions about the integrality gap between mass assignments and finite allocations, and about approximation when perfect equality is impossible.

The mirror covers Proposition 3’s central allocation question. It does not claim a continuous counterpart for the paper’s distributed-deal results. Its weakest point is that the full continuous problem may retain substantial combinatorial difficulty in bundle pricing. That is precisely why the question remains faithful to the paper: it preserves the Atkinson objective, indivisible bundles, and resource constraints while asking what changes when agent counts become masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Proposition 3, and the strongest negative case is that the proposed CPIO gets its computational content from the catalogue of goods, while its population continuum relaxes the counts of bundle assignments. With a fixed finite stock of goods, an atomless population makes each good reach zero mass, so the Atkinson index stops describing the distribution of resources in a useful way. The proponent repairs this by scaling supplies per capita. That repair is coherent, but it changes the setting into a repeated economy of standardized goods.

In that repaired setting, CPIO asks whether the supply vector lies in the convex hull of incidence vectors of bundles \(B\) with \(v(B)=\bar v\). The paper’s 3-PARTITION witness then becomes a fractional exact-cover LP over the eligible triples, so its discrete hardness does not establish hardness for the mirror. This is the best evidence against the anchor’s *particular* formulation: it may leave the main reduction’s difficulty behind.

It does not defeat the mirror. Scaling supplies is explicitly within the programme’s scope, repeated standardized goods and identical valuations are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the change from integral bundle counts to masses is exactly the relaxation being studied. Nor does the easy 3-PARTITION slice make the broader convex-hull feasibility question uninteresting. A stronger mirror could minimize the continuous Atkinson index rather than ask only for perfect equality; with fixed supplies and common additive valuations, that amounts to maximizing \(\sum_B x_B\ln v(B)\) over feasible bundle masses. That remains a clear computational question.

So the honest negative case is weak: fixed-stock continuization degenerates, and the paper’s hardness reduction loses force after the fractional relaxation. But the better model addresses the first problem, and the second is a possible result of the mirror, not a reason it is unworthy. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor universally.
