The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its closest computationally adjacent material is the no-communication equilibrium analysis in Section 3, with predictions reported in Table II. Those are calculations for the experimental treatments, not a named complexity result. The main findings are empirical comparisons of outcomes and communication patterns. So there are no qualifying anchors, and no honest way to give one continuous problem per anchor.

The strongest positive lead I can see is an extension of the paper’s no-communication voting model to very large deliberative panels. A type could be a preference class—neutral, weak red partisan, weak blue partisan, or strong red partisan—together with a private signal. The instance would give the state prior, signal accuracy, payoff table, voting threshold, and rational masses \(\mu\) over these types. The question would be to determine the limiting symmetric equilibrium voting behavior and the induced probabilities of choosing red or blue in each state. A large panel with a small number of preference and signal classes is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and it preserves the paper’s private information, preference heterogeneity, and threshold voting.

But that is an unanchored extension, not a mirror of a named result in this paper. Its central weakness is that an atomless voter cannot affect the aggregate vote, so the strategic pivotal-voter mechanism analyzed by the authors needs a carefully specified finite-population limit. And this candidate leaves out free-form deliberation, which is central to the paper’s contribution; preserving it would require modeling messages, histories, and possibly leadership as part of a type’s description. The paper’s experiments themselves use groups of nine, so they do not establish that this large-panel regime is one the authors would recognize as their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the central obstacle: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Section 3 derives equilibrium predictions for a nine-person jury under a few fixed treatments; the rest reports experimental comparisons and coded communication patterns. Those are not complexity results or algorithmic problems. So there is no qualifying anchor here.

The proposed large-panel extension also runs into a deeper problem. In an atomless society, one voter’s ballot changes the total vote mass by zero. The pivotal-voter calculation that drives Section 3’s strategic behavior therefore disappears: a voter’s action cannot change the collective decision, so it cannot affect their payoff. The aggregate masses of preference and signal types still determine which threshold is crossed, but they no longer determine the strategic voting behavior the paper studies.

The best rescue is to retain a finite panel of size \(n\), represent it by rational type proportions, and ask how equilibrium behavior varies as \(n\) grows. That could be a sensible high-multiplicity problem for large elections. But exact pivotality then depends on finite-\(n\) counts and the discrete event that a particular vote swings the threshold. If \(n\) is removed, pivotality vanishes; if it stays, the object is a sequence of finite games, not the paper’s direct continuous-population mirror. A limiting refinement could select behavior, but choosing and analyzing that refinement would add a new model.

The communication findings offer no stronger anchor. The paper’s free-form chats, message histories, and occasional leaders are observed in groups of nine, but are not specified as a computational problem. One could formalize a restricted communication protocol over type masses and optimize its performance; that might be worthwhile work, but it would create a new problem rather than continuize a result in this paper. With the paper’s fixed two alternatives and small set of preference and signal classes, computing outcome frequencies under a fixed protocol would also be a small calculation, not an evident complexity landscape.

A large electorate with heterogeneous preferences and private signals is a plausible setting, so I cannot honestly rule out every useful continuous model related to the paper. That is the weak point in the negative case. But the plausible version either loses the pivotal strategic mechanism in the atomless limit or restores it by keeping a finite population, and neither gives a direct computational mirror of this experimental study.
