The continuous mirror question

Given a finite item-class set \(M\), graph-valuation types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational per-capita supply \(\lambda\) for each class, take compatible populations of \(N\) buyers and \(\lambda N\) copies of each class. Order the copies uniformly at random and assign each to a uniformly selected eligible buyer with maximum current marginal value, where a buyer may receive at most one copy of each class. Compute the limiting expected type-bundle masses \(x_{t,S}\) and normalized welfare as \(N\to\infty\), and determine the sharp worst-case ratio to the mass-allocation optimum \(\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\), including whether welfare is always at least \(4/7\,\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\); give the running time in \(m,\tau,L\).

The model it lives in

A society has type masses \(\mu_t\) over buyers whose values are vertex-cover functions on item classes \(M\). Each class has supply \(\lambda\) per buyer, and each unit of mass receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\). Feasible allocations satisfy \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le\lambda\); welfare is \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\). The algorithm is the large-population limit of random-order greedy over class copies.

The objection that survived

The limiting process has up to \(\tau 2^m\) type-bundle states, and the proposal gives no algorithm polynomial in \(m\) or concrete continuous-optimization advantage.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the random-copy process converge to a well-defined limiting mass allocation, and how can it be computed as a function of \(m,\tau,L\)?
  • What is its sharp worst-case approximation ratio to \(\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\), and does the \(4/7\) guarantee persist?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3.6, proved in this paper. For \(n\) bidders with vertex-cover valuations over a common item set, the algorithm draws a uniformly random item order and gives each item to a bidder with maximum current marginal value. The theorem says its expected welfare is at least \(\frac{4}{7}\mathrm{OPT}\), equivalently an approximation ratio of at most \(\frac{7}{4}\). This is an algorithmic approximation result, not a complexity classification. The paper’s APX-hardness statement is only a citation to earlier work, so I would not use it as an anchor.

Here is a continuous high-multiplicity version I find plausible. Consider a large procurement market with a finite catalog \(M\) of \(m\) item classes. A buyer’s type is a graph \(G_t=(M,E_t)\); their value for receiving classes \(S\subseteq M\) is
\[ v_t(S)=|\{e\in E_t:e\cap S\ne\varnothing\}|. \]
The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over a finite set \(T\) of these types, with support size \(\tau\). The market has \(\lambda\) units of each class per buyer, for some rational \(0<\lambda<1\): in a discrete approximation with \(N\) buyers, there are about \(\lambda N\) interchangeable copies of each class. A buyer needs at most one copy of each class, so their bundle remains an integral subset of \(M\).

The continuous allocation variable is \(x_{t,S}\), the fraction of the whole population that has type \(t\) and receives bundle \(S\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le\lambda\quad(j\in M). \]
The per-capita welfare is
\[ W(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}v_t(S), \]
and \(\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\) is its maximum over feasible allocations. This keeps bundles integral for every buyer while allowing fractional type masses, as a continuous population requires.

My lead question is Mass Random Greedy for Vertex-Cover Allocation: given \((M,T,\{G_t\},\mu,\lambda)\), determine the limiting allocation and welfare of random-order greedy as \(N\) grows, and establish its guarantee against \(\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\). In the finite market, greedily assign each arriving copy to a buyer with maximum marginal value; track the fractions of each type holding each bundle, and take the expected normalized welfare in the large-\(N\) limit. A solution is that limiting mass allocation and a guarantee for its welfare. The direct target suggested by Theorem 3.6 is \(W_{\mathrm{greedy}}\ge\frac{4}{7}\mathrm{OPT}_\lambda\); determining the sharp factor as a function of \(\lambda\) would be a further question. I expect a tractable mass-flow algorithm at least for fixed \(m\), with a constant-factor guarantee plausible. I would not claim the cited APX-hardness transfers to this regime without an embedding.

The scenario is many small buyers—such as firms purchasing from a shared catalog—with relatively few graph-valuation types and supply growing with the buyer population. It preserves the paper’s welfare objective, graph-based values, and marginal-value greedy decision. Scaling supply per capita is the co-modification that makes a mass allocation meaningful.

The soft spot is that the paper has one copy of each distinct item, while this mirror has many interchangeable copies of each item class. The resulting valuation over physical copies is not literally a vertex-cover function on that expanded item set, so Theorem 3.6 does not automatically prove the proposed guarantee. That is a real extension to establish. The mirror still asks the paper’s authors’ recognizable question—how well random-order marginal greedy allocates goods to graph-valued bidders—and keeps the population, rather than the outcome space, as the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.6 is the paper’s strongest anchor: it proves that random-order greedy achieves welfare at least \(\frac{4}{7}\mathrm{OPT}\) for vertex-cover valuations. The sharpest objection is that the theorem’s randomness and combinatorics live in the items, while the only available population axis is the bidders.

Keep the paper’s \(m\) distinct items fixed and let the number of bidders grow. At most \(m\) bidders receive anything, so total welfare stays bounded while welfare per bidder tends to zero. More fundamentally, once each positive-mass type has at least \(m\) bidders, its exact mass no longer constrains either an allocation or the greedy process. The limit remembers which types are present, but forgets their proportions. That leaves no meaningful mass-allocation version of Theorem 3.6 in the paper’s original market.

The proposed mirror restores meaningful masses by giving each item class \(\lambda N\) copies. This is a coherent procurement-market model, and the programme allows supply to scale when continuization requires it. Still, the scaling changes the object behind the theorem. The paper orders each distinct graph vertex once; its proof uses the vertex’s random rank among its neighbors. The scaled market orders many copies of each class, and greedy allocation evolves across masses of type-bundle states. The proposed result is now about that fluid allocation process. In particular, the suggested fixed-\(m\) tractability has state space of size \(\tau 2^m\); it does not establish an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or identify a pricing problem or other source of continuous-optimization leverage.

That is the strongest negative case: in the fixed-supply model, bidder continuization loses the masses; in the supply-scaled model, the mass limit concerns a substantially different allocation process whose computational payoff is still unspecified. But the second model is plausible, preserves welfare maximization and marginal-value greedy, and its limiting computation could itself yield a worthwhile result. I cannot honestly rule out every valuable mirror on these grounds. The universal “no worthwhile mirror” claim remains weak against this anchor.
