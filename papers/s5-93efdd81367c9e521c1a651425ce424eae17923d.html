The continuous mirror question

Given binary candidate positions \(c_i\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}\), a finite support \(T\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\ell}\) of voter types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and the paper’s tie-breaking order, does there exist a shared salience vector \(w'\in[0,1]^{\ell}\) with \(\sum_k w'_k=1\) such that \(c_1\)'s vote mass is at least every other candidate’s? Each type votes for its nearest candidate under weighted \(L_1\) distance, with ties broken by the given order.

The model it lives in

A finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over binary issue-position types, fixed candidate positions, deterministic distance-based voting with the paper’s tie-breaking rule, and one shared decision vector \(w'\). The question is whether some feasible \(w'\) makes \(c_1\)'s total vote mass maximal.

The objection that survived

Because rational masses have a common denominator, the mirror is exactly interconvertible with the finite count problem and may offer little new algorithmic leverage; this limits its payoff, but not the validity of the question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 1 and 2 for binary issue positions and unrestricted attacker strength. It leaves the paper’s other deterministic results, constrained-budget variants, and stochastic voting results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of this problem in the support size \(\tau\), issue count \(\ell\), and mass encoding length \(L\)?
  • Does the problem admit an algorithm fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is an election whose population is a distribution over issue-position types. The paper already has a continuous *action*—the attacker chooses real-valued issue weights—but its voters are finite individuals. The mirror makes the *society* continuous: it replaces the finite vote count with the mass of each type that votes for a candidate.

A type is a complete binary issue-position vector \(v_t\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}\). Given candidate positions \(c_i\), a shared salience vector \(w'\), and the paper’s deterministic distance and tie-breaking rule, everyone of type \(t\) votes the same way. The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\), with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\); \(\tau\) is the number of supported types. The attacker still chooses one common \(w'\in\Delta_\ell=\{w'\in[0,1]^\ell:\sum_k w'_k=1\}\). For the unrestricted-strength cases in the paper, this is equivalent to allowing any feasible \(w'\). The instance is encoded by the candidate vectors, the supported type vectors and their binary-rational masses, and the tie-breaking order. Complexity is measured in \(m,\ell,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

This is a plausible regime for a large electorate voting on a set of binary policy issues: many residents may share the same recorded issue profile, while a campaign changes the common public agenda or issue salience. A finite electorate with counts \(n_t\) gives \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), so its vote counts become masses exactly after normalization. Conversely, any rational \(\mu\) has a common denominator and corresponds to a finite high-multiplicity electorate. Cloning every voter \(q\) times leaves \(\tau\) and the normalized optimization problem unchanged while making the population arbitrarily large relative to its support. The reduction in Theorem 1 also uses large blocks of identical voters, which can be aggregated into types.

My lead is Continuous Max-Support Salience Control. Given the instance above, choose \(w'\in\Delta_\ell\) to maximize
\[ S_\mu(w')=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[c_1\text{ receives the vote of type }t]. \]
A solution is an exact maximizing \(w'\) and the resulting maximum mass. For two candidates and binary issue positions, this is the direct mass version of the paper’s Max Support objective; fixing \(p=1\) preserves its vote comparisons.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper by reduction from TCMS, whose hardness is cited from Bais et al. The theorem states that, with two candidates, \(n\) voters, and \(\ell\) binary issues, maximizing the number of votes for \(c_1\) is NP-hard even when the attacker has unrestricted normed budget \(B=\infty\), or the full interval constraint \(I=[0,1]^\ell\). The continuous problem contains every such finite instance by setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), so an exact solver for it would recover the discrete optimum by multiplying the maximum mass by \(n\). I expect Class B: hardness transfers. The reduction’s issue-selection choices survive normalization; making repeated voters into mass does not remove them.

A second, distinct question is Continuous Plurality-Winner Salience Control. Use the same instance and define each candidate’s vote mass by
\[ V_i^\mu(w')=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[t\text{ votes for }c_i]. \]
Ask whether there exists \(w'\in\Delta_\ell\) such that \(V_1^\mu(w')\ge V_i^\mu(w')\) for every candidate \(c_i\). A solution is YES or NO, with a feasible \(w'\) as a witness for YES.

Its anchor is Theorem 2, also established in this paper by extending the preceding hardness construction. It states that, for two or more candidates, \(n\) voters, and \(\ell\) binary issues, deciding whether \(c_1\) can win the plurality is NP-complete under either the unrestricted budget \(B=\infty\) or the full interval \(I=[0,1]^\ell\). The same normalized-count correspondence carries the decision answer over exactly. I again expect Class B. This is a separate underlying task from maximizing support: it asks whether the target can win, not how much support it can obtain.

Both questions preserve what the authors compute: an attacker changes shared issue salience, voter preferences are induced by weighted distance, and the objective is either target support or a plurality win. The continuum enters through population mass, not through the already-real-valued weight vector. I would not claim these anchors cover the paper’s stochastic model, its constrained-budget variants, or all its tractable special cases.

The strongest caveat is that the hard results do not show hardness at fixed \(\tau\). The discrete-to-mass translation preserves hard instances, and cloning shows that the population can be much larger than the support, but the number of distinct types may still grow with the issue dimension. The continuous questions therefore leave useful follow-ups: whether either problem is tractable or fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), and how the paper’s tractable cases extend when the input is given as rational type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed mirror changes the electorate’s encoding without changing the control problem’s structure. For a type \(t\), deterministic voting is decided by linear comparisons such as \(\langle w',a_t\rangle\ge 0\). The attacker chooses \(w'\); the type masses are fixed coefficients. The objective is therefore a weighted sum of indicators, constant within cells of the hyperplane arrangement. The population is continuous only in the sense that ballots carry rational weights. There is no optimization over population mass, as there is in a mass-transfer problem, and no new continuous structure for column generation or convex optimization to exploit.

That point narrows the case against Theorems 1 and 2, but does not defeat them. Aggregating identical voters gives a rational type distribution, and multiplying the resulting masses by a common denominator recovers a finite electorate with exactly the same maximizing weights and winner. So both anchors are well-posed high-multiplicity questions, and the paper itself does not already answer them for continuous populations. The reductions’ issue choices may remain the source of hardness, but under the programme’s rules that is not a reason to dismiss a mirror.

A more ambitious mirror could put a distribution over real-valued issue positions and ask for the mass of voters who prefer each candidate under \(w'\). That would make the population more than a weighted list of binary profiles. But its computational meaning would depend on how the distribution is represented: arbitrary distributions do not give a finite input, while a restricted density family defines a further modelling choice. That is a real concern about scope and payoff, not a decisive objection; a carefully encoded distribution could produce a worthwhile computational-geometry problem.

So the negative case is weak. It can argue that the binary-type mirrors may offer mainly a weighted-input reformulation, with little new continuous optimization. It cannot honestly rule out their value, and it does not defeat either anchor. The plurality mirror survives for the same reason as Max Support: normalization preserves the finite decision answer exactly.
