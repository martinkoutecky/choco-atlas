The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(G\) of game types with masses \(\mu_g\), action sets \(A_g\), horizon \(T\), and \(N\) dyads distributed across types, but unknown reward laws \(P_{g,a}\), characterize the minimax population-average individual-rational regret. Each round, allocate fractions \(x_{g,a,t}\) of type \(g\) to joint actions \(a\), with \(\sum_{a\in A_g}x_{g,a,t}=\mu_g\); pooled feedback consists of the corresponding finite-\(N\) dyad observations. Determine the regret rate in \(N,T,\mu\), and the action-set sizes, and give a matching algorithm and lower bound.

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over complete repeated-game types \(g\), with fractional mass allocations \(x_{g,a,t}\) to joint actions and finite-\(N\) pooled stochastic feedback; minimize worst-case population-average EBS regret over \(T\) rounds.

The objection that survived

In a genuinely nonatomic population, pooled rewards from any positive mass may reveal action means exactly, so the \(N\)-dependent sampling question may describe a finite-sample interpolation rather than the strict continuum limit.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the individual-rational-regret learning bounds in Theorems 4.3 and 4.5. It leaves the EBS structural propositions, the known-reward computation, and the safety-regret result outside the proposed question.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the minimax regret scale with \(N\), especially for unequal type masses and small cohorts?
  • Does the strict nonatomic feedback limit make regret vanish, and how does that limit relate to the finite-\(N\) bounds?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror is a large service running the paper’s repeated bargaining protocol across many independent dyads: for example, repeated negotiations or coordination sessions on a platform. The dyads are matched within cohorts whose members share the same complete game description. Let \(g\) identify that description: both roles’ action sets, their joint-action reward laws, and the observation and coordination rules. The population has finite support \(G\), with mass \(\mu_g\) in cohort \(g\), where \(\sum_g\mu_g=1\) and \(\tau=|G|\). A finite realization has \(N\mu_g\) dyads of type \(g\), with \(N\gg\tau\). Each dyad still contains the paper’s two players; the continuization is across the many copies of each game, not across actions or rewards.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover. The reward laws \(P_{g,a}\) for joint actions \(a\in A_g=A_g^1\times A_g^2\) are unknown, as in the paper, and rewards lie in \([0,1]^2\). In round \(t\), a coordinator assigns mass \(x_{g,a,t}\) of each cohort to joint action \(a\), with \(x_{g,a,t}\ge0\) and \(\sum_{a\in A_g}x_{g,a,t}=\mu_g\). A public signal can implement the correlated policies the paper requires. Feedback consists of the observed action and rewards from the dyads, which can be pooled within each type. Let \(V^i_{\mathrm{Eg}}(g)\) be role \(i\)’s EBS value in game \(g\), and \(\bar r^i_{g,t}\) its cohort’s per-capita reward in round \(t\). The objective is population-average individual-rational regret:
\[ \mathcal R_{N,T}(\Lambda;P,\mu) = \mathbb E\!\left[ \max_{i\in\{1,2\}} \sum_{g\in G}\mu_g \left( T V^i_{\mathrm{Eg}}(g)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar r^i_{g,t} \right) \right]. \]
The lead problem is Mass-Parallel EBS Minimax Regret: given \(G,\mu,N,T\), the action sets, and the feedback protocol—but not the reward laws—characterize \(\inf_\Lambda\sup_P\mathcal R_{N,T}(\Lambda;P,\mu)\), by giving an adaptive mass-allocation algorithm and a matching lower bound. The continuous model uses fractional mass allocations; finite-\(N\) realizations give a high-multiplicity sequence in which each type’s mass corresponds to many indistinguishable dyads.

My strongest anchor is Theorem 4.5, proved in this paper. It says that for any algorithm \(\Lambda\), any action-set sizes \(|A_1|\ge2\), \(|A_2|\ge2\), and horizon \(T\ge |A_1||A_2|\), there is a general-sum game with \(|A|=|A_1||A_2|\) joint actions for which \(\Lambda\)’s expected individual-rational regret after \(T\) rounds is \(\Omega(T^{2/3}|A|^{1/3})\), up to constants. This is a lower bound on learning regret, not a conventional NP-hardness result. In the mirror, the question is whether the lower bound still holds when a positive mass of each type supplies parallel samples each round, and how the minimax regret depends on \(N,\tau,\mu\), and the action-set sizes. I expect the serial-sampling lower bound to weaken as \(N\) grows: the theorem’s hard instance needs repeated exploration, while a cohort can explore with a small fraction of its mass and pool the resulting observations. The finite-action learning problem still looks algorithmically tractable; the main uncertainty is the statistical rate, not an expected transfer of discrete hardness. Questions for the prover include whether an \(N\)-dependent bound beats the \(T^{2/3}\) floor, and how unequal cohort masses affect it.

A complementary anchor is Theorem 4.3, also proved here. In self-play, UCRG achieves, for each player and with probability at least \(1-\delta\), individual-rational regret at most
\(5\sqrt[3]{|A|\ln(BT/\delta)}\,T^{2/3} +2|A|\log_2(8T/|A|) +\sqrt{T\ln(BT/\delta)}(\sqrt{1/2}+\sqrt{12|A|}) +\sqrt T\),
for \(T\ge|A|\), with \(B_t=16|A|\ln t+2|A|+1\), \(\delta_k=\delta/B_{t_k}\), and \(\epsilon_k=(2|A|\ln(1/\delta_k)/(1.99t_k))^{1/3}\). Its finite-support mirror is Typewise Mass UCRG: on the same population instance, give an adaptive mass-allocation rule and a high-probability bound on the displayed population regret, with its dependence on \(\tau,\mu,N\), and the action sets explicit. A typewise use of UCRG is the natural baseline; pooled cohort feedback may improve the rate. I expect this algorithmic problem to be tractable for explicitly listed action sets, with computation polynomial in the support and the action descriptions. Theorem 4.3 supplies an algorithmic starting point; it does not by itself prove the population-level bound.

The main soft spot is the one the opposing case is likely to press: the paper studies a single two-player game, and neither theorem has a population-size parameter. My scenario is an exchangeable, high-volume deployment of their game-learning problem, so the authors could plausibly recognize the mirror as asking about their task at population scale. But that recognition is essential. If they require one dyad only, then using fractions of dyads changes the setting, and treating the continuous policy lottery itself as the “population” would be the wrong mirror. The paper has no named P-versus-NP-style result; its strongest anchors are its proved learning-regret upper and lower bounds.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s hard part is learning an unknown reward law from sequential interaction inside one dyad. Its population-level analogue needs many copies of that dyad. That is a coherent deployment scenario, but it changes the source of information: the added population supplies parallel samples.

For Theorem 4.5, the proposed question asks how \(N\) parallel copies change the \(\Omega(T^{2/3}|A|^{1/3})\) regret lower bound. With no information sharing, each dyad faces the original learning problem, so its per-dyad regret retains the paper’s bound. With pooled observations, the learner gets more samples per round; the resulting question is a finite-action, multiple-sample bandit problem whose central parameter is the sample count \(N\). It could be useful, but its substance comes from the sampling and information-sharing protocol. The continuous mass allocation adds no pricing problem or other continuous-optimization structure.

Taking the copies to a genuine continuum creates a sharper problem. Under the usual interpretation where aggregate feedback from any positive mass is observed, an arbitrarily small mass can sample each action, and each action’s aggregate reward reveals its mean exactly. The exploration cost can then approach zero. Keeping finite \(N\) avoids that collapse, but leaves a family of large finite experiments with fractional allocation as a scheduling relaxation. This makes the continuum limit a poor mirror of the lower bound’s serial exploration trade-off.

Theorem 4.3 has the same issue. If each dyad runs UCRG on its own, the typewise population guarantee follows by applying the paper’s bound to each type and averaging by \(\mu_g\). If copies pool data, a type’s effective sample count grows with its cohort size, and a UCRG-style confidence analysis is the natural starting point. Again, this may yield a useful rate in \(N,\tau,\mu\), but the type masses simply weight otherwise separate learning problems unless one adds cross-type constraints absent from the paper.

A stronger computational mirror might use the paper’s exact EBS computation for known deterministic rewards. On an explicitly given distribution of game types, compute the EBS separately for each type and aggregate the values using \(\mu_g\). The work remains typewise; the mass distribution introduces no hard optimization. Creating coupling across types, succinct action descriptions, or shared resources could make a harder problem, though that difficulty would come from those added assumptions.

The negative case is weakest at the authors’ recognition gate. A platform running many copies of the same repeated bargaining protocol is plausible, and the distribution of complete game types could be a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Theorem 4.5’s \(N\)-dependent version might be worth studying. I would argue that it is a parallel-learning extension whose main question is statistical, rather than a strong continuization result for computational social choice. I cannot honestly rule out the platform mirror as worthwhile in some version; I can only make the case that the population continuum itself contributes little, and can erase the very exploration trade-off the paper proves.
