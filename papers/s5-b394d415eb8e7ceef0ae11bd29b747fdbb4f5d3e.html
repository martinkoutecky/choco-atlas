What the mirror covers

The proposed cohort interpretation covers only Theorem 3.1. The Jordan-curve reductions in Theorems 3.2 and 4.1 establish computational hardness but do not supply a population-level result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a mass-cohort version of connected envy-free cake division. It fits a narrow but plausible setting: three large constituencies, each made up of many members with the same valuation, jointly receive one connected share of a common resource. The constituencies compare their assigned shares; they are the recipients in the division.

Formally, the society has three role types \(t_1,t_2,t_3\), each of mass \(\mu_{t_i}=1/3\). Role is part of a type because it determines which share the constituency receives. All three types have the same nonnegative, \(1\)-Lipschitz valuation \(v\) on intervals of \([0,1]\). Given \(v\) and binary \(\epsilon>0\), find cuts \(0=c_0\le c_1\le c_2\le c_3=1\), assigning \(A_i=[c_{i-1},c_i]\) to type \(t_i\), such that

\[ v(A_i)\ge v(A_j)-\epsilon \qquad\text{for every }i,j\in\{1,2,3\}. \]

Call this problem Mass-Cohort Connected Envy-Free Division. A solution is the two cuts satisfying those inequalities; it is a feasibility search problem, not an optimization problem. The regime is many constituency members but only \(\tau=3\) role types, with each constituency sharing one package.

My anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It states that finding a connected \(\epsilon\)-envy-free division for three agents with identical non-monotone valuations is \(\UEOPL\)-hard in the standard Turing machine model when \(\epsilon\) is given in binary; in the query model, the problem requires \(\poly(1/\epsilon)\) queries. In the proposed cohort problem, setting each constituency’s common valuation to \(v\) gives exactly the same cut conditions. Thus the finite three-recipient instance embeds directly, and the expected direction is hardness transfers. The reduction through Jordan Curve, including Theorem 3.2 and Theorem 4.1, explains the hardness proof; I would not treat those as separate population-mirror anchors.

The claim is deliberately limited to this collective-recipient interpretation. A plausible application is a division negotiated among large, internally homogeneous organizations or constituencies, each of which receives a shared contiguous right or facility. The masses then describe the share of the represented population in each constituency, while the small support reflects a small number of collective claimants. Under that interpretation, the authors could recognize the same connected envy-free division question, with high multiplicity in who each claimant represents.

The main weakness is that the mass does little computational work: the anchor has three recipients, and their equal masses do not alter the envy constraints. More seriously, if the paper’s agents must each receive their own bundle, this mirror changes the problem. Replacing each claimant with many individual recipients creates many cuts, and Theorem 3.1 says nothing about that growing-agent problem. So this is a credible but borderline mirror for collective cake division, not evidence that the paper’s individual-agent problem has a robust high-multiplicity version. The next questions would be whether the cohort interpretation is acceptable to the authors, whether unequal rational masses yield a natural per-capita fairness condition, and whether hardness survives when members receive separate pieces.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.1 is a genuine computational anchor, so I would not argue that the paper lacks a result worth mirroring. The problem is that the theorem offers no population variable to continuize. Its hard instance has exactly three recipients, all with the same valuation \(v\), and asks for two cuts. The Jordan-curve reduction encodes its curves in that one valuation function; there is no profile of voters or distribution of agent types in the input.

The proposed constituency model preserves the theorem by giving each of its three recipients a collective label. That can be a coherent cake-division problem for organizations sharing packages. But changing a constituency’s mass leaves the cuts and envy constraints unchanged. The labels are allocation roles, not behavioral differences in the theorem’s agents. If role is part of the type, the model still has three collective decision-makers; if types are determined by valuation, the three identical agents collapse into one type. Either way, the mass distribution does no computational work. This is not a complaint that the continuous answer might be unchanged; it is that the proposed mirror keeps the finite-recipient problem intact and adds population language around it.

The stronger person-level version exposes the difficulty. Give each member their own connected piece, and the theorem no longer applies: with \(n\) members there are \(n-1\) cuts, while Theorem 3.1 encodes its instance using just two. In a literal continuum of recipients sharing one finite cake, at most countably many disjoint pieces can have positive length. Almost every person must receive a zero-length piece; with continuous valuations, that makes individual envy constraints degenerate. One can instead give each type a collective interval, but that returns to the constituency model, where mass does not affect the envy conditions. Scaling the cake with population to preserve positive per-person shares would define a different resource model and a new computational problem.

A distribution over finitely many valuation types, with one collective interval per type, is the best generalization I can see. It is sensible as a group-fairness question, but the theorem’s three identical agents do not supply a population-level result for it: one must choose types, entitlements, and allocation semantics anew. So my case against is strongest on the individual-allocation mirror, and weaker against collective cake division. The latter is plausible research, but it is hard to call it a continuization of this theorem in which the society itself becomes the continuous object.
