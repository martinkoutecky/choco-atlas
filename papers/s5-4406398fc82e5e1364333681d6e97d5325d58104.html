The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is possible only as an extension of the paper’s investment model. Under the programme’s anchor rule, this paper has no admissible anchor: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Proposition, or Corollary asserting a computational result. Hypotheses 1–4 are empirical hypotheses, and the paper’s tests and regressions are not complexity results. Its theoretical equilibrium formulas are attributed to Kıbrıs and Kıbrıs (2013), with the formal proofs there. I would not present any of these as a qualifying anchor.

The most credible continuous question is nevertheless clear. Call it Continuous Bankruptcy-Investment Equilibrium. Imagine a large project-finance pool with many creditors on standardized terms, drawn from a small number \(\tau\) of risk and endowment classes. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), CARA risk-aversion parameter \(a_t\), and endowment \(w_t\); the \(\mu_t\) are binary rationals summing to one. Let \(x_t\in[0,w_t]\) be each type’s per-creditor investment, and let \(S=\sum_t\mu_t x_t\) be average investment. The common contract parameters are success probability \(\pi\), return \(r\), and surviving fraction \(\beta\), and the bankruptcy rule is PRO, EA, or EL.

On success, a type-\(t\) creditor has wealth \(w_t+rx_t\). On bankruptcy, wealth is \(w_t-(1-\beta)x_t\) under PRO, \(w_t-x_t+\beta S\) under EA, and \(w_t-(1-\beta)S\) under EL. The last two expressions reflect per-capita awards or losses as the population grows. Each creditor maximizes expected CARA utility, taking the aggregate \(S\) as fixed when considering a unilateral deviation. The problem is: given these inputs and a rational \(\varepsilon>0\), compute a type-level investment vector and consistent aggregate \(S\) with regret at most \(\varepsilon\) for every type. A solution is the vector and aggregate satisfying those conditions.

I would expect this problem to be tractable for each fixed rule: the types interact through the single aggregate \(S\), so the central computational task is to find an aggregate-consistency point from the type-level best responses. That expectation needs a proof, especially for the equal-awards rule. The most informative follow-up is to compare equilibrium \(S\) across rules and test whether the differences are independent of \(\pi\) when both equilibria are interior. Those questions mirror the mechanisms behind the paper’s Hypotheses 1 and 2: replacing PRO with EL is predicted to increase total investment, while replacing PRO with EA is predicted to decrease it, with both differences independent of bankruptcy probability under the stated interior condition. Hypotheses 3 and 4 add predictions that investment falls as bankruptcy becomes more likely and as a creditor’s risk aversion rises. The authors report experimental support for all four; those findings motivate the mirror, but they are not its computational anchors.

This scenario has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large creditor pool with many participants in a small number of standardized risk and contract classes, so \(n\) is large relative to \(\tau\). The paper’s Section 3 model already treats creditor investment and aggregate investment under these three rules, so its authors could recognize the equilibrium question as an extension of their model. The proposed problem covers that investment model and its cross-rule comparisons, not the experiment’s estimates of human behavior, Holt–Laury risk elicitation, or repeated pair matching.

The weak point is substantial: the experiment itself pairs only two students at a time. In the continuum, an individual creditor takes the estate aggregate as fixed, which removes the effect that one creditor’s investment has on other creditors’ shares. That change may alter the strategic feedback the paper studies. It makes this a plausible mirror of the underlying investment model, but not a continuous restatement of the paper’s experimental result—and, because the paper lacks a qualifying named computational result, not a case that satisfies the requested anchor standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this manuscript. Hypotheses 1–4 are empirical hypotheses, and the paper’s regressions and tests are not complexity results. Its equilibrium formulas are attributed to Kıbrıs and Kıbrıs (2013); they do not make this paper a computational study. That is the strongest objection under the programme’s anchor rule.

The proposed creditor-pool scenario is plausible: many creditors on standardized terms can naturally fall into a small number of risk and endowment types. But its atomless version changes the equal-losses game at the point that matters. For a type-\(t\) creditor taking aggregate investment \(S\) as fixed, bankruptcy wealth under equal losses is \(w_t-(1-\beta)S\), independent of that creditor’s own investment \(x_t\). With \(r,\pi>0\), expected utility therefore rises with \(x_t\): under the proposed cap, the best response is full investment; without a cap, there is no finite maximizing choice. The finite-player incentive to limit investment comes from how one creditor’s action changes their own loss by a term proportional to \(1/n\). That effect vanishes as the population becomes atomless. Keeping it requires a positive-sized strategic player or finite \(n\), which gives up the continuous-population limit; treating each whole type as a strategic bloc instead changes the game. So the limit removes the interior equal-losses behavior behind the paper’s main comparison.

A narrower PRO-versus-EA mirror avoids that collapse, but still offers little computational substance. With finitely many rationally weighted risk types, the paper’s CARA formulas aggregate through type-level sums such as \(\sum_t\mu_t/a_t\); in the interior case they give the equilibrium directly. Endowment caps may add a scalar consistency calculation, but the paper supplies no combinatorial or pricing problem for the continuous-optimization toolbox to resolve. A richer project about learning, rule design, or heterogeneous contracts could be worthwhile, but it would add a new research question rather than mirror a named computational result here.

The negative case is not airtight: a large pool of standardized creditors is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and a computational study of its equilibrium could stand on its own. The case against is that this manuscript supplies no qualifying computational anchor, while its strongest proposed mirror loses the equal-losses incentive in the continuum and its narrower alternatives appear explicitly solvable.
