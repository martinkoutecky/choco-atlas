Why no mirror

Evaluating the rule on rational masses is a well-posed, direct high-multiplicity version, and large electorates with repeated approval sets are a plausible regime. But the two anchors raised prove proportionality guarantees, not computational results; the paper’s procedure itself is not a numbered computational result. The mirror therefore fails the required computational-result gate.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a natural population mirror here, but the paper has no named computational result to anchor it to. The numbered results are proportionality and monotonicity statements, not claims that a problem is in P, hard, or fixed parameter tractable. The unnumbered discussion in Section 7 says that choosing a suitable quota may be difficult to compute, but gives no complexity classification.

The closest results are Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper: for any candidate set \(J\) and \(\ell\le\min(n,m_J)\), if more than \(\ell q\) votes approve *exactly* \(J\), then \(J\) receives at least \(\ell\) seats. Theorem 3.2, also proved here, says that if more than \(\ell q\) votes approve every candidate in \(J\), then at least \(\ell\) seats go to \(J^*\), the union of the approval sets containing \(J\). Both are substantive results, but neither is a computational-complexity anchor under your criteria.

The strongest plausible mirror is Continuous Eneström–Phragmén Evaluation. An instance has candidates \(C\), a seat count \(n\), candidate capacities, and a finite list of nonempty approval sets \(A\subseteq C\), each with rational population mass \(\mu_A\ge0\) and \(\sum_A\mu_A=1\). Fix a tie order. Starting with residual masses \(x_A=\mu_A\), repeatedly choose an eligible candidate \(i\) maximizing \(W_i=\sum_{A\ni i}x_A\), allocate a seat to \(i\), and update the masses containing \(i\): if \(W_i\ge 1/(n+1)\), multiply them by \(1-\frac{1}{(n+1)W_i}\); otherwise set them to zero. The solution is the resulting ordered sequence of \(n\) winners. This is the paper’s procedure on a distribution over approval types, with the quota expressed as a fraction of society.

A credible regime would be a large electorate voting on party lists or candidates, where many people share the same approval set and the number \(\tau\) of distinct sets is much smaller than the electorate size. The authors should recognize this as their problem: their vote “types” are already approval sets, and their procedure already updates fractional vote weights. The mirror covers the allocation procedure and gives continuous readings of Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 by replacing vote counts with masses. It does not treat the paper’s large-seat asymptotics as population continuization.

I would expect evaluation to be tractable, since the rule makes \(n\) rounds of updates over \(\tau\) types. But the paper does not analyze binary-rational bit growth, so that expectation is not a complexity result. My weakest point is also the central limitation: the paper supplies no named hardness or algorithmic classification to motivate a complexity mirror, and the candidate problem may amount to analyzing a direct simulation. So the population model is convincing; the required anchored computational case is absent.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The first and strongest objection is that the paper establishes no computational-complexity result to mirror. Theorem 3.1 proves a proportionality guarantee for voters approving exactly \(J\); Theorem 3.2 proves one for voters approving a set containing \(J\). Their natural mass versions are still statements about what the rule guarantees, not computational questions. Rephrasing votes as masses does not turn either theorem into an algorithmic classification. The paper’s numbered results on monotonicity have the same problem. Section 7 does mention difficulty in choosing a quota, but it does not define or classify a computational problem.

The proposed Continuous Eneström–Phragmén Evaluation is the strongest candidate, but it is essentially the procedure the paper already gives, run on its existing vote types. If the masses are rational, multiply them by a common denominator to obtain integer vote counts. Every support comparison and every update factor is unchanged by that scaling, so the sequence of elected candidates is unchanged too. The procedure already creates fractional residual vote weights; the continuous formulation changes the interpretation of the input, but supplies no new computational task in the paper’s results. One could investigate exact bit growth or tie-sensitive evaluation, but that would be a new complexity analysis of a direct simulation, not a complexity question this paper formulates. It may be worth studying on its own; it is a weak anchor for this programme.

Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 3.2 do have faithful readings over rational masses, and the paper’s vote types make large electorates with repeated approval sets a sensible regime. Those facts support the modelling premise; they do not supply the missing computational anchor. I would not use high multiplicity itself as an objection. Nor would I object that the theorem statements remain true after rescaling: the issue is that their continuous readings are still proportionality claims, outside the programme’s computational focus.

A better proposal might ask how much approval mass must shift to change the elected set, or how hard it is to find a quota with the desired properties in Section 7. Such questions fit the programme’s broader interests, but they add intervention or optimization problems the paper neither studies nor anchors with a result. They could be worthwhile new work; they do not rescue a continuous computational mirror of this paper’s named results. So the negative case is strongest under the programme’s stated standard of mirroring computational results, and weaker if any new computational problem built around the rule is allowed to count.
