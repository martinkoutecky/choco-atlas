Why no mirror

The voter population has a natural high-multiplicity formulation, and the price ratio carries over when voter counts become masses. But Proposition 1 and the other named results give quantitative bounds and comparisons, not algorithms, computational hardness, or formulations used to compute. The proposed exact-evaluation task is a separate, routine algorithmic problem rather than a computational result of this paper.

What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation covers the price-of-diversity metric and its committee selections; it does not cover the individual price of diversity or the paper's bounds as computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a sound population mirror, but this paper has no eligible computational anchor. Its numbered Propositions 1–5 and Corollaries 1–3 give bounds and comparisons for the price of diversity; none asserts that a problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. So I can’t present one as a result meeting your anchor rule.

The closest substantive result is Proposition 1, proved in this paper: for any weakly separable committee scoring rule \(F\), committee size \(k\), diversity quota \(q\), and preference profile \(\succ\), the price of diversity satisfies
\[ \operatorname{POD}(F,\succ,q)\le \frac{1}{\min_{j\in[l]}\alpha_j}, \qquad \alpha_j=q_j/k. \]
The manuscript further states in Equation (6) that this bound is attained over profiles for fixed \(q\). That is a useful mathematical anchor for a mirror, but it is not a computational-complexity result.

A concrete continuous problem is Continuous Price-of-Diversity Evaluation. An instance consists of \(m\) candidates partitioned into categories \(A_1,\ldots,A_l\), a committee size \(k\), feasible positive quotas \(q_1,\ldots,q_l\), a rational weakly separable scoring vector \(w\), and a society given by \(\tau\) complete rankings \(t\), each with positive rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to one. For each candidate, compute
\[ S_\mu(a)=\sum_t\mu_t w_{r(t,a)}. \]
Return the exact ratio
\[ \frac{\max_{|W|=k}\sum_{a\in W}S_\mu(a)} {\max_{\substack{|W|=k\\|W\cap A_j|\ge q_j\ \forall j}} \sum_{a\in W}S_\mu(a)} \]
and committees attaining both maxima. The population types are voter rankings; the candidate categories remain the paper’s diversity classes. The winning committee remains an ordinary \(k\)-element set.

This is plausible in a large election or selection process with many voters but relatively few distinct rankings: a ballot type is a complete description of everything this paper uses about a voter, and its mass is the share of voters casting it. The mirror changes the paper’s voter sum into a mass-weighted sum while keeping its candidates, scoring rule, quotas, and committee objective. For an empirical distribution \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), every candidate score is the paper’s score divided by \(n\), so the price ratio is unchanged. Conversely, any rational \(\mu\) corresponds to a finite profile after taking a common denominator.

The evaluation problem is in polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length: calculate the candidate scores, take the top \(k\), and find the best quota-respecting committee by selecting the required number within each category and then filling the remaining places with the best available scores. Proposition 1’s bound carries over directly; without a support-size restriction, its stated tight worst-case value is \(k/\min_j q_j\). This is a precise, author-recognizable mirror, but the easy evaluation and the bound make it a weak computational-programme result. A more open follow-up would restrict the adversarial society to support size at most \(\tau\) and ask for the exact worst-case price and an extremizing distribution.

That is the soft spot: the paper measures the cost of a diversity constraint; it does not study the complexity of computing it. The continuous version is credible as a high-multiplicity formulation, but the paper supplies no named hardness or algorithmic result whose dissolution or transfer could anchor a ChoCo complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the nearest thing to an anchor, but it is not a computational result. It bounds the price of diversity; none of the paper’s propositions or corollaries classifies an algorithmic problem. The proposed Continuous Price-of-Diversity Evaluation therefore cannot mirror a complexity result in the sense this programme is targeting.

The population model itself is convincing. In a large election, voters with the same ranking are a natural type, and their masses form a sensible continuous society. The candidate categories and the \(k\)-person committee can remain exactly as in the paper. There is no identity objection: the paper’s price depends on voter rankings only through the candidates’ aggregate scores.

But for a given society, that mirror is immediate to compute. Calculate \(S_\mu(a)=\sum_t\mu_t w_{r(t,a)}\) for each candidate. The unconstrained committee is the top \(k\); for the constrained committee, take the required \(q_j\) highest-scoring candidates from each category, then fill the remaining seats with the highest-scoring candidates left. This takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. It is the paper’s existing score aggregation and selection procedure with weighted sums in place of voter counts.

The stronger version—maximize the price over continuous societies—also has little computational yield. The paper already gives closed-form worst-case bounds, including rule-specific ones, and its proofs are score-based. Rational masses can be scaled to integer voter counts without changing the price, since every score is multiplied by the same factor. Thus the continuous version of the unrestricted maximum inherits the paper’s result directly. That is a legitimate continuous formulation, but it adds no complexity question for ChoCo to chart.

The proposed support-bounded follow-up is the best possible rescue, and it is also where this negative case is least secure. A bound of \(\tau\ge m\) on the number of ranking types cannot restrict the scores: every score vector lies in the convex hull of permutations of \(w\), an affine space of dimension at most \(m-1\), so Carathéodory’s theorem gives an equivalent society supported on at most \(m\) rankings. For \(\tau<m\), maximizing over societies with at most \(\tau\) types would be a distinct sparse-mixture problem. It may be worth studying, but the paper neither poses that restriction nor proves a result about it. Its difficulty would come from adding a support constraint to the extremal problem, not from a computational question already present in the manuscript.

So the best case against is not that continuous populations are implausible here; they are quite natural. It is that every named result concerns a quantitative bound, not computational complexity, while the proposed evaluation is routine and the unrestricted worst case is already settled. A sparse-support variant could be a new project, but it does not rescue a ChoCo mirror of this paper’s results.
