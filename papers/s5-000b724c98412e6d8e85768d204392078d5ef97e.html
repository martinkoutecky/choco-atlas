What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the unconstrained bipartite \(b\)-matching core question in Theorem 10 and Corollary 6; it leaves the assignment-game, concurrent-game, constrained \(b\)-matching, and nucleolus results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for the unconstrained bipartite \(b\)-matching result, though there is an important qualification: the paper has no numbered theorem that explicitly classifies a problem as being in P or NP-hard. Its best computational anchor is Theorem 10, proved here, whose constructive LP result yields a polynomial-time algorithm. The paper’s cited hardness result for computing the nucleolus concerns a different problem, so I would not use it as an anchor for this mirror.

Theorem 10 says that in an unconstrained bipartite \(b\)-matching game, every optimal solution \((u,v)\) to the displayed dual LP gives a core imputation by assigning \(\alpha_i=b_i u_i\) to each \(i\in U\) and \(\beta_j=b_jv_j\) to each \(j\in V\). That allocation divides the game’s full matching value among the players, and every coalition receives at least the value it could generate on its own. Corollary 6, also proved here, concludes that the core is always nonempty. Solving the dual LP and applying the theorem’s map therefore computes a core imputation in polynomial time.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large two-sided contracting market: many professionals and many firms, with a relatively small set of types. A professional’s type specifies their capacity and surplus with every firm type; a firm’s type specifies its capacity and surplus with every professional type. Agents of the same type are indistinguishable for this problem. A type pair can form many distinct contracts, which makes the paper’s unconstrained repeated-edge model natural.

Here is the mirror I would ask a prover to study: Continuous Population Core for Bipartite \(b\)-Matching. The input is finite type sets \(U,V\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to \(1\), positive integer per-agent capacities \(b_i,b_j\), and nonnegative rational match values \(w_{ij}\) for allowed type pairs. For any coalition masses \(a_i\in[0,\mu_i]\) and \(d_j\in[0,\nu_j]\), define its worth \(W(a,d)\) as the maximum of \(\sum_{ij}w_{ij}x_{ij}\), over \(x_{ij}\geq0\) satisfying \(\sum_jx_{ij}\leq b_i a_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\leq b_jd_j\). The question is to output nonnegative per-agent profit rates \(p_i,q_j\) such that \(\sum_i\mu_i p_i+\sum_j\nu_jq_j=W(\mu,\nu)\), and every coalition satisfies \(\sum_i a_ip_i+\sum_jd_jq_j\geq W(a,d)\). A solution is the vector of type-level profit rates.

I expect this problem to be tractable. Its grand-coalition dual minimizes \(\sum_i\mu_i b_i u_i+\sum_j\nu_j b_jv_j\), subject to \(u_i+v_j\geq w_{ij}\) and nonnegativity. An optimal dual solution gives profit rates \(p_i=b_i u_i\), \(q_j=b_jv_j\); dual feasibility protects every subpopulation, and strong duality gives grand-coalition efficiency. This is a compact LP with size polynomial in the number of supported types and the input bit lengths, independent of the number of people. In fact, the atomless coalition model appears to give an “if and only if” characterization: sufficiently small coalitions containing only types \(i,j\) force \(p_i/b_i+q_j/b_j\geq w_{ij}\) for each allowed pair. Together with efficiency, these are exactly dual feasibility and optimality.

This is a sensible mirror of Theorem 10’s core-allocation question, not a claim about the whole paper. It covers the unconstrained bipartite \(b\)-matching result and its nonemptiness corollary. It does not cover the assignment-game characterizations, the nonbipartite concurrent games, the constrained version’s pair-specific edge limits, or nucleolus computation. A useful next question is how the continuous core relates to the full core of finite markets with many identical agents: the paper’s finite game can have core imputations beyond those generated by its dual, while the mirror’s divisible coalitions appear to force the dual characterization.

The soft spot is that this mirror lets coalitions split type mass continuously, whereas a finite market only permits whole agents. That changes the deviation set and may shrink the core. I think the change is defensible in a large-market regime: it preserves the paper’s capacities, pairwise surpluses, and coalition-stability question while making fractional population mass the honest unit of deviation. But the paper does not itself establish that its authors would accept this atomless model as their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the sole anchor, Theorem 10 (with Corollary 6), is that it is a core characterization, not a theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. The paper does not study the running time of computing a core imputation; its construction is simply to solve the displayed dual LP and map the solution to profits. That computation is already polynomial in the finite graph size. So the proposed mirror supplies a new population model for an algorithm the paper already makes immediate, rather than continuizing a complexity result the paper establishes.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. In a large two-sided market with a small number of complete types—capacity and surplus against every other type included—mass is meaningful, and the type-level LP is well-posed. Its stability constraints force \(p_i/b_i+q_j/b_j\geq w_{ij}\) for every allowed type pair: take a small coalition of types \(i,j\) with equal total capacity on both sides. Those pairwise inequalities are exactly dual feasibility; efficiency gives dual optimality. Thus the continuous core has a clean characterization and a polynomial-time computation in the number of supported types. The difference between named people and mass does not break this model.

There is also established nonatomic assignment work relating continuum assignment, linear programming, and the core. That makes the setting familiar, but it is not a novelty collision under the programme’s stated test: it does not settle the computational question for a finite-support input. [Gretsky, Ostroy, and Zame, “The Nonatomic Assignment Model”](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/joecth/v2y1992i1p103-27.html)

So the negative case is weak at its decisive point. The paper offers no explicit complexity theorem to mirror, but Theorem 10 still supports a sensible, computable continuous question. I cannot honestly defeat that upgraded anchor or argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
