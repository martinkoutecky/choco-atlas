What the mirror covers

The proposed extension covers the existence results in Theorems 1 and 2; it leaves the noncooperative foundations in Theorems 3 and 4 and the merger-paradox application aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result: it proves no hardness, running-time, or algorithmic-complexity claim. Its action spaces may be continuous, but its population is a finite set of players. So under ChoCo’s strict scope, there is no eligible computational anchor. The strongest positive case is an analytically natural population-continuum extension of the paper’s existence theorems. That is a plausible mirror of its model, but it does not yet make the paper a computational result for the programme.

The scenario I find most convincing is a large industry network with a few interdependent R&D or standards consortia. Many firms participate, sometimes in overlapping consortia, and each consortium chooses a shared investment or standard. Firms fall into a relatively small number \(\tau\) of complete types: the same consortium memberships, technology, payoff function, and disagreement payoffs. For example, there could be tens of thousands of firms but dozens of types. Type mass is the fraction of firms of that type. This preserves the paper’s central structure: each decision group makes a collective bargain, its members’ payoffs depend on the choices of other groups, and groups can overlap.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper (Appendix C). It says that if each group’s feasible alternative set \(S_C\) is compact and convex in a Euclidean space, every player’s utility \(u_i\) is continuous, and each player’s utility is concave in a group’s own alternative whenever that player belongs to the group, then a Nash solution *with transfers* exists. Here is a precise type-continuum version. Let \(K\) be a finite set of bargaining groups, \(T\) a finite support of types, and \(\mu_t>0\) each type’s population share. Type \(t\) specifies its membership \(M(t)\subseteq K\) and its complete utility function \(u_t\). For each \(k\in K\), let \(S_k\) be compact and convex, with disagreement alternative \(\bar s_k\). Assume the utilities are continuous and concave in \(s_k\) for every type in group \(k\). Write \(T_k=\{t:k\in M(t)\}\), \(\mu_k=\sum_{t\in T_k}\mu_t\), and
\[ g_{t,k}(s)=u_t(s_k,s_{-k})-u_t(\bar s_k,s_{-k}). \]
A transfer-continuum Nash solution is a profile \(s^*\) and per-capita transfers \(r^*_{t,k}\) such that, for every group \(k\), \((s_k^*,(r^*_{t,k})_{t\in T_k})\) maximizes
\[ \prod_{t\in T_k}\bigl(g_{t,k}(s)+r_{t,k}\bigr)^{\mu_t/\mu_k} \]
over \(s_k\in S_k\) and transfers satisfying \(g_{t,k}(s)+r_{t,k}\ge 0\) for each \(t\in T_k\), and \(\sum_{t\in T_k}\mu_t r_{t,k}=0\), holding \(s_{-k}=s^*_{-k}\). The question for a prover is: under those assumptions, does every such instance admit a transfer-continuum Nash solution? The weighted product is the continuum analogue of the paper’s Nash product; mass records how many indistinguishable participants of each type bargain in that group.

A useful companion anchor is Theorem 1, also proved here (Appendix A). Under the same compactness, convexity, continuity, and own-action concavity assumptions, it says that a Nash solution without transfers exists. Its mirror uses the same type and group model, but each group \(k\) chooses \(s_k\) to maximize
\[ \prod_{t\in T_k}g_{t,k}(s)^{\mu_t/\mu_k} \]
subject to \(g_{t,k}(s)\ge 0\) for every participating type; a solution is a profile where every group’s choice is simultaneously optimal given the others’ choices. The prover’s question is: does every such instance admit a profile satisfying those groupwise Nash-product conditions? Theorem 2 is my lead because transfers make the type-level mass interpretation especially explicit; Theorem 1 gives the cleaner no-transfer case.

Both are sensible high-multiplicity questions: if \(n_t\) discrete players share type \(t\), then \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and the normalized exponents within a group are exactly the type counts divided by group size. Thus the distribution records composition without preserving individual names. I would expect existence to extend to finite type support, essentially by the arguments behind the paper’s theorems. But that expectation is not an algorithmic classification: neither theorem gives a way to compute a solution. The next computational question would require an explicit finite encoding of utilities and ask for the complexity of finding an exact or approximate solution, parameterized by quantities such as \(|K|\), \(\tau\), and input bit length.

My case covers the paper’s existence results in Section 3, not its noncooperative foundations in Theorems 3 and 4. Those results depend on individual proposers, responders, and repeated rounds; taking their population continuum would require specifying how those roles work when an individual has zero mass. The soft spot is therefore also decisive for ChoCo: the continuum population is plausible and closely preserves the bargaining model, but the paper supplies no computational anchor, and its continuous-action spaces are not themselves population continuization. I would call these promising analytic mirrors and new computational questions, not evidence that this paper already contains a ChoCo-style complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither anchor is a computational result. Theorems 1 and 2 establish existence under continuity, compactness, and concavity assumptions; they give no finite input representation, running time, or algorithm. A continuous population model does not turn an existence question into the kind of complexity question ChoCo studies.

For the proposed mirror of Theorem 2, the weighted transfer product largely repackages the finite theorem. With rational type masses, each type can be represented by that many identical players in a finite population. The normalized exponents reproduce the replicated players’ Nash product. More tellingly, the paper’s Lemma 1 reduces each group’s transfer bargaining to maximizing its members’ total payoff; in the type model, that is maximizing a mass-weighted aggregate payoff. So the mirror’s existence claim is existence of equilibrium in a \(K\)-group aggregate game. To ask for an algorithm, one must add a finite encoding for the utilities and action sets. The paper supplies none. With a chosen encoding, the question becomes computation of equilibria in that particular encoded game; the masses alone do not supply the pricing or other high-multiplicity structure that would make this a natural ChoCo problem.

The Theorem 1 mirror has the same problem. Its weighted Nash product is equivalent to maximizing a mass-weighted sum of log gains, subject to the individual gain constraints. For rational masses, that again corresponds to replicating identical players. It is a natural analytic restatement, but its central question—whether a profile of groupwise optima exists—is still an existence question, not a computational one. Moving to a genuinely continuous set of types might make the extension less reducible to finite replication, but it would require function-space assumptions and a representation of the type distribution and utilities. That would be a new analytic model, not a computational anchor supplied by this paper.

This is a strong case against spending ChoCo’s computational effort on these anchors: their best versions remain fixed-point existence questions, and the paper does not specify the data needed to classify their computational complexity. It is not a proof that no worthwhile continuous extension exists anywhere. The paper’s industry setting makes a large population with recurring firm types plausible, and an economist could reasonably want the analytic extension. The negative case is strongest if the programme’s scope remains computational; it weakens if the programme also values continuum existence theory.
