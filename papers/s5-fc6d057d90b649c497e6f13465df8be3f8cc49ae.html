The continuous mirror question

Given a finite rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported valuation types \(T\), a rational per-capita capacity \(b\), and a rational bundle menu \(q_r\) satisfying \(\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r q_r\le b\), decide whether there are per-capita payments \(p_r\) such that \(v_t(q_t)-p_t\ge v_t(q_r)-p_r\) for every \(t,r\in T\). This asks whether one payment menu makes truthful reporting optimal and prevents envy among present types.

The model it lives in

A market with a continuum of customers distributed by valuation type \(\mu\), divisible per-capita capacity \(b\), and one bundle and payment per reported type. Masses determine aggregate feasibility; common-payment feasibility is a finite system of type-level difference constraints.

The objection that survived

Because a unilateral report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, incentive and envy constraints coincide and the mixed cycles central to Theorem 3.5 disappear; the proponent acknowledges this loss but does not explain why the full interaction survives.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers common-payment implementation for a fixed type-mass society and allocation menu. It leaves the paper’s general characterization over allocation functions and valuation profiles, approximate tradeoffs, trust-based partition, and separation examples aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • If the allocation menu is given implicitly as a function of \(\mu\), what is the complexity of common-payment feasibility?
  • How do the approximate incentive and envy tradeoffs change when allocation feasibility depends on type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I can defend is narrow: a continuous mirror of the paper’s joint payment implementation question, not of its whole study of incentive compatibility and envy.

My sole anchor is Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper. Given an allocation function \(a\), it forms a directed graph \(G_a\) with nodes \((i,v)\). An envy-freeness arc from \((j,v)\) to \((i,v)\) has weight \(v_i(a_i(v))-v_i(a_j(v))\); an incentive-compatibility arc from \((i,(v'_i,v_{-i}))\) to \((i,(v_i,v_{-i}))\) has weight \(v_i(a_i(v))-v_i(a_i(v'_i,v_{-i}))\). The theorem says that one payment function makes \(a\) both incentive compatible and envy-free exactly when \(G_a\) has no negative cycle; shortest-path distances then provide suitable payment potentials. The paper uses this characterization in its subsequent constructions. This is a borderline anchor under your rule: it is a named, computationally used characterization, but it does not state a standard input-size classification such as polynomial time or NP-hardness. I would not present it as one.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Atomless Type-Mass Common-Payment Implementation. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation types. Think of a large service market with millions of customers but a small number of recurring valuation schedules. The service has divisible capacity \(b\) per capita, and the allocation rule gives every customer reporting type \(r\) the same bundle \(q_r\), with \(\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r q_r\le b\). Bundles and values are represented rationally. The input is \(\mu\), the valuation functions, and this allocation menu \((q_r)_{r\in T}\). The question is whether per-capita payments \((p_r)_{r\in T}\) exist so that truthful reporting is optimal for every type and no customer envies a customer of another present type. A solution is such a payment vector, or a certificate that none exists.

For types \(t,r\), the incentive constraint is
\[ v_t(q_t)-p_t \ge v_t(q_r)-p_r. \]
The envy constraint has the same form when type \(r\) is present. Thus payment existence is a finite system of difference constraints: equivalently, its type graph has no negative cycle. For an explicitly represented menu, this gives a polynomial-time test in the number of supported types and the input bit length. That is the positive result I would expect here: tractable, by the same cycle-and-potential structure as Theorem 3.5. It is not a claim that the paper proves this continuous result.

The high-multiplicity scenario is millions of agents sharing a modest number of valuation types, with fractional per-capita capacity making mass the natural population measure. The types include the valuation information the mechanism uses; grouping agents who share it is exactly the high-multiplicity assumption. The paper explicitly notes that its results extend to divisible allocations, which helps make the per-capita resource model recognizable to its authors. Fractional bundles are a co-modification needed to describe the scaled market; the population distribution remains the continuized object.

The scope is only the common-payment implementability question represented by Theorem 3.5. I would not use Claims 5.1–5.3 as anchors: they are claims rather than the required named theorem-level computational results. Further questions include whether the test remains efficient for a rule specified over *all* \(\mu\), how approximate envy and truthfulness trade off as in Section 4, and how finite-population effects change the result.

The soft spot is substantial. In an anonymous atomless model, one customer’s report does not change the population distribution. As a result, the incentive comparison and the envy comparison largely coincide. That removes much of the interaction the paper studies, and an author could reasonably say this is a local, simplified limit rather than a mirror of the paper’s full allocation-function problem. Also, the paper gives no named P/NP-style complexity theorem; the positive case rests on Theorem 3.5 as a computational characterization, not on a complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.5 is the only plausible anchor, but it is a characterization, not a complexity result for a specified computational problem. It says payment feasibility is equivalent to the absence of negative cycles in \(G_a\), then observes that shortest paths give payments. The paper does not specify a finite input representation or a complexity bound for constructing or checking that graph; its vertices range over valuation profiles, which may be infinite in number. So the theorem is computationally suggestive, but it does not establish the kind of named complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to continuize.

The service-market scenario is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Per-capita capacity and type-level fractional allocations are reasonable co-modifications, and I would not object to either. The problem is that the proposed model drops the interaction at the center of Theorem 3.5. For an anonymous atomless market at fixed \(\mu\), a single customer’s report does not change the population distribution. If report \(r\) gives bundle \(q_r(\mu)\) and payment \(p_r(\mu)\), then truthfulness for type \(t\) requires
\[ v_t(q_t(\mu))-p_t(\mu)\ge v_t(q_r(\mu))-p_r(\mu). \]
Envy-freeness for a type \(t\) customer comparing their outcome with a type \(r\) customer’s is the same inequality. The mixed IC/EF cycles that distinguish Theorem 3.5 therefore collapse into ordinary finite-menu payment constraints. The masses may matter for whether the allocation fits the capacity, but they do not drive the payment-compatibility question.

A stronger proposed mirror could let the allocation and payments depend on the whole distribution, \(q_r(\mu)\) and \(p_r(\mu)\), and ask for a rule that works across all societies. But a unilateral report still leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, so the constraints remain separate menu checks at each \(\mu\). Making an individual deviation change the state requires giving that individual positive mass; that turns the question into coalition or cohort incentive compatibility. Giving the individual a persistent, order-one effect instead preserves a finite set of strategic agents, not an atomless population. Either modification may define a useful new problem, but neither retains the paper’s individual-incentive interaction in a continuous population.

I would not claim this proves that no worthwhile mirror can be invented. The negative case is strongest against this sole anchor: its natural atomless version is a reasonable market question, but it loses the mixed-cycle phenomenon, while preserving that phenomenon requires changing what counts as a deviation or keeping the strategic population finite.
