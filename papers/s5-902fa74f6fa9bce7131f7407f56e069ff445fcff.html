The strongest possible positive lead is an extension of the paper’s core auction-design problem. Consider a large market of buyers with valuations drawn from a fixed finite set of valuation types, where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of buyers of type \(t\). Let supplies of the goods scale with market size so allocation remains meaningful. The question would be to choose a Groves redistribution rule over these type-mass profiles that preserves efficient allocation, truthfulness, individual rationality, and weak budget balance, while minimizing expected aggregate payments under a specified distribution over profiles. A solution is the redistribution rule satisfying those constraints and attaining the minimum.

That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting for repeated cohorts of buyers competing for standardized licenses or goods. But it is a proposed extension, not a mirror anchored in a result this paper proves. Its main weakness is also substantial: in a continuum, one buyer has zero effect on aggregate allocation, so the authors’ finite-agent incentive and payment structure may degenerate. The paper supplies no result showing that this continuum problem remains faithful to its original one, or classifying its complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point for the negative case is that there is no named computational result here to mirror. The paper reports a neural architecture, training procedure, and empirical comparisons; it proves no complexity bound, exact algorithmic guarantee, or hardness result. So the proposed large-market auction is an extension of the paper’s subject, not a continuous version of one of its computational results.

The best version of that extension would give bidders valuations from a finite set \(T\), represent the market by type masses \(\mu\), scale supply with market size, and seek an efficient, truthful, individually rational, budget-balanced redistribution rule minimizing expected per-capita payments. That is a coherent question for repeated markets. But if the mechanism depends only on \(\mu\), one bidder’s changed report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. The pivotal effect that gives finite-agent Groves payments their content disappears; truthfulness at the aggregate level is vacuous. Keeping an individual’s report as a separate input can restore a meaningful incentive question, but it changes the object into a direct mechanism over an aggregate market plus an individual report. With indivisible goods, feasibility and allocation at measure-zero individuals also need a new convention. Treating each positive-mass type as the strategic unit avoids that issue only by making cohorts, rather than buyers, the players.

Scaling supply makes allocation nontrivial, but it does not settle these incentive and accounting choices. Per-capita transfers, aggregate budget balance, and the unit of individual rationality all need to be specified anew. A study of one such model might be worthwhile, but it would be new large-market mechanism-design work rather than a mirror of this paper’s results. The paper’s distribution \(\rho\) over valuation profiles does not supply the missing population continuization: it is uncertainty over finite-agent profiles.
