The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t supply a compliant ChoCo anchor: the paper has no named result classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, FPT, or similar. Its theorems are about farsighted stability and network structure. So there are no per-anchor continuous computational questions to hand to a prover without inventing a result the paper does not contain.

The closest hooks are Theorem 5.3 and Theorem 5.6, both proved in this paper. Theorem 5.3 shows that a set of outcomes is farsighted stable when every outcome has the specified group structure induced by a group-optimal sequence, with zero transfers on missing links and balanced transfers for each agent. Theorem 5.6 says that, with no transfers and a winner-takes-all tournament, a stable set contains an efficient outcome if and only if the group-optimal sequence is \(\{n\}\), equivalently if \(n\) maximizes \(r(1,m)+v(m,n-m)\) over \(n/2<m\le n\). These are useful structural characterizations, but neither states a computational result.

There is a plausible population regime behind a *follow-on* model: a large pool of otherwise interchangeable R&D teams or firms competing in one market, with the same collaboration technology, effort costs, and tournament incentives. Each agent’s type would include those shared primitives; the population would have one type, or a small number, and many agents. A continuum version could represent collaboration by a symmetric effort kernel \(x(u,v)\) on a unit-mass population, with output based on \(\int_0^1 g(\min\{x(u,v),x(v,u)\})\,dv\). That fits the paper’s bilateral collaboration model better than treating fractional outcomes or noisy ballots as the continuous object.

The weakest point is central to Theorem 5.6: winner-takes-all prizes and ties are defined for finitely many agents. A group of mass \(\alpha\) tied for first could receive per-agent prize \(R/\alpha\) if the total prize scales with population size, but a measure-zero winner creates a singularity. And the paper’s farsighted blocking relation permits arbitrary finite coalitions; defining credible deviations by measurable coalitions in an atomless society needs new choices and proofs. The result’s condition on integer group sizes does not by itself settle those issues.

So the honest positive case is that the paper suggests a credible high-multiplicity economic model and a natural limit question about efficient stability. It does not yet provide a named computational anchor or a continuous problem whose complexity belongs in the programme’s trichotomy.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent concedes the decisive point: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 5.3 characterizes a farsightedly stable set; Theorem 5.6 characterizes when an efficient outcome belongs to one. Neither defines a computational problem or states its complexity. The model permits arbitrary functions \(f\) and \(g\), real-valued effort choices and transfers, and a solution that is itself a set of outcomes. It gives no finite input encoding, algorithmic task, or complexity parameters. So neither theorem is a ChoCo anchor as stated.

The population story is unusually strong, though. Identical firms in a large R&D market are a natural high-multiplicity regime, and a continuum model of them is plausible. Grant the strongest version: represent collaboration by a measurable kernel over a unit-mass population, scale tournament prizes with population size, rank agents by output quantiles, and allow deviations by measurable coalitions of positive mass. Those choices can make a coherent continuum economic model. They do not supply the missing computational question. The resulting object is a continuum network-formation game asking about stable sets and equilibrium structure—the kind of analytic stability question the programme distinguishes from computational complexity. To make it computational, one would have to choose an encoding for the payoff functions and kernels and specify a finite decision or optimization problem. That would define a new research project, not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The best mirror of Theorem 5.3 replaces integer group sizes with group masses and asks which component structure is farsightedly stable. The best mirror of Theorem 5.6 asks whether the complete collaboration network is stable, with the majority-coalition condition translated into an optimization over mass shares. Both are natural continuizations of the paper’s *mathematics*. But the first asks for a stability characterization of continuum networks; the second asks for a stability threshold under a continuum ranking rule. Neither becomes a complexity question until additional choices impose a finite input language and an algorithmic task. In the one-type version, the population distribution itself is just \(\mu=1\); the substantive state is the endogenous network kernel, not a distribution over social-choice types. Restricting to a finite collection of block structures could restore a finite-dimensional problem, but that restriction is extra structure whose computational interest would need its own case.

So the negative case is strong against counting this paper as a ChoCo mirror: its population can be continuized, but its results concern equilibrium stability, not computational complexity. It is not strong enough to establish that no continuous version could be worthwhile in any research setting. The proposed continuum-firm model is plausible; it simply does not, on the evidence of this paper, fill the programme’s computational gap.
