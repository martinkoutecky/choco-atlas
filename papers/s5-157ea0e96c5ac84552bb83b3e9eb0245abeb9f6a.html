The continuous mirror question

Given item classes \(M\), types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), a finite OR bid menu for each type, and rational per-capita supplies \(\kappa_j\), find a mass allocation maximizing total bid value. Each agent receives an integral feasible pack of pairwise-disjoint bids, while the aggregate use of each item class \(j\) is at most \(\kappa_j\). Require WPM: whenever positive mass receives a pack containing \((B',a')\), no supported type has a bid \((B,a)\) with \(B\subseteq B'\) and \(a>a'\). The decision version asks whether such an allocation has value at least \(R\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete OR bid menus and values, with population masses \(\mu_t\). Decision variables distribute each type’s mass among feasible integral bid packs; per-capita item capacities constrain aggregate use, and the objective maximizes total bid value subject to WPM.

The objection that survived

WPM depends only on which types have positive mass, so Proposition 1 itself adds no mass-dependent constraint; the aggregate computational content comes from winner determination under proportional capacities.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror carries Proposition 1’s final-allocation WPM guarantee into high-multiplicity winner determination. It does not establish runtime or approximation guarantees for the mass model, or cover Proposition 2’s anytime Weak-WPM guarantee, truthfulness, or the experimental comparisons.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of WPM-constrained high-multiplicity OR winner determination, including when there is one type and bids have equal-cardinality bundles?
  • Can the chapter’s greedy methods be extended to this mass model with a runtime or approximation guarantee in \(m\), \(\tau\), and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

I see one defensible, narrow mirror, anchored on Proposition 1. The chapter has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, or similar. Section 2.2 says WDPOR is generally NP-hard, citing Cramton et al. (2006), but that is an unnumbered claim attributed to another work. Proposition 1 is the chapter’s own numbered algorithmic guarantee, though its proof is cited to Fukuta and Ito (2007a), rather than given here.

Proposition 1 says that every proposed winner-determination algorithm in the chapter except the simulated-annealing-based algorithm produces a final allocation satisfying Winner-Price-Monotonicity (WPM) when it terminates. WPM requires that, for nonempty bundles \(B\subseteq B'\), if one bidder’s bid for \(B\) is strictly higher than another bidder’s bid for \(B'\), the latter bidder must not win \(B'\).

A plausible setting is a regional resource allocator serving many automated agents for recurring user or service profiles. An agent’s type would include its complete OR bid menu and bid values, so agents of one type are identical in every respect relevant to allocation. There could be tens of thousands of agents but only dozens or hundreds of such profiles. This fits the chapter’s ubiquitous-computing motivation and keeps its OR winner-determination problem intact. It does require a setting where resource stocks grow with population; the reason for that limitation is the main weakness of the mirror.

Here is the precise lead problem: WPM High-Multiplicity OR Winner Determination. An instance has resource classes \(M\), a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and for each type \(t\) a finite OR menu \(\mathcal B_t\) of bids \((S,a)\), where \(S\subseteq M\) and \(a\) is the bid value. It also gives a rational per-capita supply \(\kappa_j\) for each resource \(j\). In finite approximations, \(N\mu_t\) agents have type \(t\), and the stock of resource \(j\) is \(N\kappa_j\).

A type-level allocation is a distribution \(z_{t,P}\) over feasible OR bid packs \(P\): each pack contains bids with pairwise-disjoint bundles, so every person assigned that pack receives an integral, feasible bundle. The allocation must satisfy \(\sum_P z_{t,P}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,P}z_{t,P}\sum_{(S,a)\in P}\mathbf 1[j\in S]\leq\kappa_j\) for each resource. Its value is \(\sum_{t,P}z_{t,P}\sum_{(S,a)\in P}a\). The decision version adds a target \(R\) and asks whether a feasible allocation of value at least \(R\) exists, subject to WPM: whenever \(z_{t,P}>0\) and \((B',a')\in P\), no supported type \(u\) may have a bid \((B,a)\) with \(B\subseteq B'\) and \(a>a'\). A qualifying allocation is the witness; the optimization version maximizes the stated value.

This is still winner determination over OR bids, with mass describing how many indistinguishable agents receive each integral pack. It is not continuity of the outcome for its own sake. I expect hardness to transfer (Class B): bid conflicts live in the bundles and the item set, not in the number of distinct agents. For example, with one type and equal-cardinality bundles, WPM imposes no strict-subset restriction among distinct bids, while choosing a maximum-value compatible pack retains the set-packing difficulty. That is a reasoned expectation for the mirror, not a hardness theorem proved by Proposition 1. Further questions are whether WPM preserves useful approximation quality, and whether a compressed algorithm can run in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length.

The authors would likely recognize this as a version of their problem: it retains their OR winner-determination objective and the WPM guarantee they single out for their algorithms. The scope is that allocation problem and its WPM property, not truthful mechanism design or payments.

The soft spot is substantial. The chapter studies finite lists of bids, not a high-multiplicity model, and Proposition 1 gives no complexity or approximation guarantee for the mass formulation. Also, with a fixed stock of only 256 single-copy items, per-capita supply vanishes as the population grows. A convincing mirror therefore needs a resource-allocation scenario with replicated or population-scaled capacity, such as channels or service slots. The chapter’s setting makes that co-modification plausible, but does not establish that its algorithms or result extend to it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 1 is not a complexity result. It says that, once the chapter’s algorithms terminate, their allocations satisfy WPM. It does not classify the complexity of a computational problem or ask for an optimal WPM allocation. The chapter’s NP-hardness statement for WDPOR is unnumbered and attributed to earlier work. So the proposed mirror changes a postcondition of particular heuristics into a new constrained optimization problem.

WPM itself depends on which bids are present, not on how much population holds each type. Given the supported types’ bid menus, one can identify every bid that WPM forbids: a bid for \(B'\) is forbidden if some supported type offers a strictly higher bid for \(B\subseteq B'\). The same filter applies whether that type has mass \(0.5\) or an arbitrarily small positive mass. At zero mass, the constraint disappears. Thus WPM survives in the continuum as a support-level rule, but its content does not become an aggregate population question.

The strongest version of the mirror would define types by complete OR bid menus, scale resource supplies with population, and optimize over distributions of integral bid packs. That is a coherent high-multiplicity winner-determination problem. But its central computational question is the continuous relaxation of WDPOR; WPM is just a bid filter, and Proposition 1 provides no result about the relaxation, its pricing problem, or its complexity. That may be a worthwhile new project, but the chapter’s numbered result does not anchor it.

This defeats the proposed anchor as a mirror of the paper’s result. It does not establish the universal claim that no scenario could make high-multiplicity combinatorial auctions worth studying. The paper’s repeated-user motivations and the possibility of recurring, replicated resource demand leave that broader case plausible; the negative argument is weakest there.
