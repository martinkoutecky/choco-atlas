The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a task population version of Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper and expanded in Appendix D. The theorem assumes \(X\) and the experts’ reports \(Y^{[M]}\) are independent conditional on the ground truth \(Y\), that the neural network class contains the Bayesian posterior \(h^*\), and that some subset \(S\) of experts is mutually independent conditional on \(Y\), with \(Y^S\) sufficient for \(Y\). Under those assumptions, it proves that \((h^*,g^*,p^*)\) maximizes expected \(\mathrm{MIG}_f\) over the paper’s classifier and weighted-average aggregator classes and \(p\in\Delta C\); the maximum is the \(f\)-mutual information between \(X\) and \(Y^{[M]}\). It also proves that combining \(h^*\), \(g^*\), and \(p^*\) gives the Bayes-optimal data-crowds forecaster.

This is a named, computationally relevant result about the paper’s optimization objective. It is not a complexity-class result: the paper establishes no \(P\), NP-hardness, FPT, or similar classification, and gives no worst-case runtime for finding the maximizing parameters. If only results of those kinds qualify as anchors, this paper has no qualifying anchor.

A concrete mirror would treat the tasks as the population. Consider a large medical-image annotation service with a fixed panel of \(M\) experts and a classifier that operates on a finite encoded feature space \(\mathcal X\). A task type is \(z=(x,\ell)\), where \(x\in\mathcal X\) is the classifier input and \(\ell\in C^M\) is the vector of experts’ reports. Its mass \(\mu_z\) is the fraction of tasks with that signature. In a high-volume service, the number \(N\) of tasks could greatly exceed the support size \(\tau\); the model treats tasks with the same signature identically for its objective.

I would pose Promised Population Max-MIG as follows. The instance gives \(C\), \(\mathcal X\), \(M\), a choice of \(f\), and the positive rational masses \(\{(z,\mu_z)\}\). Fix the paper’s classifier architecture and weighted-average aggregator family
\[ g(\ell)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{M}W_j e(\ell_j)+b\right), \]
with \(p_c>0\). For \(K_p(a,d)=\sum_{c\in C}a_cd_c/p_c\), define
\[ J_f^\mu(h,g,p)= \sum_{z=(x,\ell)}\mu_z\,\partial f\!\left(K_p(h(x),g(\ell))\right) -\sum_{z=(x,\ell),\,z'=(x',\ell')}\mu_z\mu_{z'}\, f^\star\!\left(\partial f\!\left(K_p(h(x),g(\ell'))\right)\right). \]
The task is to find a global maximizer \((h,g,p)\), or, in a finite numerical version with fixed parameter bounds and \(p_c\ge\eta>0\), an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal one. The promise is that the task population has a latent ground truth satisfying Theorem 3.4’s assumptions and that the target parameters fit the fixed model bounds. The mass input is encoded by its support and binary rational weights, so the relevant parameters include \(|C|, M, \tau\), bit length, and model size.

This is recognizably their joint classifier-and-aggregator problem: the decisions, agreement objective, and latent information-intersection guarantee are retained. The paper itself uses an i.i.d. task distribution and an expected \(\mathrm{MIG}_f\) objective, so the mirror’s new question is the worst-case computation of an optimizer from a compressed finite-support mass description. It covers Theorem 3.4’s promised population guarantee; it does not cover the paper’s experimental accuracy claims or its comparison with MLE.

For the promised problem, I would provisionally expect Class A: the theorem gives a strong structural characterization, and the objective’s expectations can be evaluated from the support. That is a conjecture about what further algorithmic work might establish, not a tractability claim proved by the paper. Questions it opens include whether the maximizing parameters can be found efficiently from the aggregate law, whether the senior subset can be recovered without knowing its identities, and how an algorithm’s guarantee scales with \(\tau\) and finite-sample error.

The weakest point is substantial: Theorem 3.4 characterizes a population optimum but does not show that an efficient algorithm can find its parameters. Also, population expectations already appear in the paper’s theory, so the proposed mirror comes closest to restating that model. Its positive case depends on treating the computational problem over rational mass inputs as a new question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3.4, but it is a population optimality theorem, not a computational result in the programme’s sense. It identifies the maximizer of expected \(\mathrm{MIG}_f\) under latent assumptions; it gives no algorithm, runtime, or complexity classification for finding that maximizer. The paper’s experiments train finite networks, but they do not establish worst-case guarantees. The proponent’s proposed optimizer is therefore a new problem built around the theorem, rather than a computational result the theorem itself supplies.

The task population \(z=(x,\ell)\) makes the statistical objective precise, but it is already the kind of population the paper uses: tasks are drawn from a distribution, and its theory takes an expectation over that distribution. Encoding a finite sample as rational masses \(\mu_z=n_z/N\) gives a weighted empirical objective. When signatures repeat, the masses compress the sample; when high-dimensional examples are mostly unique, \(\tau\) is close to \(N\). The proposed pairwise sum is exactly the independent-draw population term. The finite-sample objective omits self-pairs, a normalization correction that vanishes with \(N\). None of this creates a new society of agents or a new source of tractability.

One can make the task-side question well-posed by fixing a finite feature space and network architecture, bounding parameters, and asking for an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal global solution. But then the central challenge is global optimization of a neural network objective. The mass representation only changes how examples are encoded; it does not bring the problem within the programme’s continuous-optimization toolkit in the way an LP pricing problem might. The latent promise also supplies the theorem’s guarantee, while the optimizer is computed from observed \((x,\ell)\) pairs. This could be a worthwhile learning-theory question, but it is a weak case for a continuous computational-social-choice mirror.

The more natural population to continuize is the experts themselves. That version has a deeper obstacle. In the paper, each task receives reports from a fixed panel of \(M\) experts, and the aggregator has an individual weight matrix \(W_m\) for each expert. If a growing crowd labels every task, the number of reports per task grows too, so the aggregate can concentrate and the statistical problem may degenerate. Keeping the number of reports per task bounded requires assigning tasks to experts, which changes the observation model and the joint learning problem. Also, grouping experts just by confusion matrix loses the arbitrary correlations that motivate Max-MIG; preserving those correlations can require encoding a large joint dependency structure, erasing the hoped-for small support. Restricting the model to exchangeable or independent expert types avoids that difficulty by narrowing the paper’s central setting.

So I would reject this anchor as evidence for a worthwhile mirror in ChoCo. The negative case is strongest against the task version: its population objective is already in the paper, and its new complexity question is about network optimization. It is less conclusive against a future expert population model. Such a model may be worth studying, but making it meaningful appears to require a new task-allocation and dependence model, rather than continuizing a result this paper proves.
