What the mirror covers

The proposed recognition and synthesis questions cover Theorem 1's characterization and Theorem 2's construction, but neither provides a surviving continuous-population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I can make is narrow: a continuous version of the paper’s constructive mechanism synthesis, for large constituencies whose delegates act on behalf of their populations. The paper contains no named theorem asserting a complexity class such as P or NP-hardness. Its algorithmic anchors are structural and constructive results, both proved in the paper.

Consider a federation choosing a location from a finite ordered set \(X=\{1,\ldots,M\}\). Each of \(\tau\) constituencies represents a large population with rational mass \(w_j>0\), where \(\sum_j w_j=1\). Its residents share a strict single-peaked preference, and a delegate with that preference makes decisions for the constituency. The society at a profile \(P\) is the distribution over constituency-preference types, with mass \(w_j\) on constituency \(j\)’s type. The regime is plausible when the number of residents is very large and \(\tau\) is small by comparison.

A useful rule is a mass-quota generalized median scheme. For each \(x<M\), choose a rational quota \(q_x\), with quotas nonincreasing in \(x\). A set \(S\) of constituencies is winning at \(x\) when \(\sum_{j\in S} w_j\ge q_x\); at \(M\), every nonempty set is winning. Given a profile \(P\), the rule selects the smallest \(x\) for which the constituencies whose peaks are at most \(x\) form a winning set. A delegate’s actions are the paper’s sequential choices between adjacent alternatives, \(x\) and \(x+1\). There is no social-cost objective: the computational task is to decide whether an obvious-strategy-proof implementation exists and, when it does, produce one.

The first anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that an onto social choice function on finite ordered alternatives with single-peaked preferences is obviously strategy-proof if and only if it is a generalized median voter scheme whose coalition system satisfies the increasing intersection property. In that property, for each alternative \(x<M\), let \(k_x\) be the largest size of a minimal winning coalition at \(x\), and let \(I_x^k\) be the intersection of the minimal winning coalitions of size at least \(k\). The requirements are \(|I_x^k|\ge k-1\) for every \(k\le k_x\), and, when \(k_x>1\), some \(i\in I_x^2\) must make \(I_{x+1}^1\cup\{i\}\) a minimal winning coalition at \(x+1\).

Its continuous mirror is Mass-Quota OSP Recognition. Given \(M\), the binary rational masses \(w_j\), and a monotone quota schedule \(q_x\), decide whether the induced rule has an obviously strategy-proof implementation for every profile of constituency preferences. A solution is a yes or no answer. I would investigate continuum-specific hardness first for the succinct quota input: testing the criterion entails understanding potentially many minimal mass-winning sets, with exact rational subset structure. That is a conjectured source of difficulty, not a hardness result in the paper. A further question is whether recognition can be done efficiently without enumerating those sets.

The lead anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. For every generalized median voter scheme whose coalition system satisfies the increasing intersection property, the paper’s algorithm constructs an extensive game form \(\Gamma_C\) that implements the rule in obviously dominant strategies. Its continuous mirror is Mass-Quota OSP Synthesis: given the constituency masses and an explicit description of the winning coalitions satisfying the property, output a finite perfect-information game form implementing the induced rule, together with truthful strategies for each preference. I expect this version to be tractable in \(M\), \(\tau\), the explicit coalition description, and the output size: the paper’s stage-by-stage construction works over adjacent alternatives and constituency delegates. Whether it can be done efficiently from a compact quota schedule, without first recovering the coalition structure, is a separate question.

The case depends on treating a constituency delegate as the strategic agent whose preference is shared by the represented population. This is plausible for delegated siting decisions, but it is the soft spot: the paper models individual agents, while the mirror gives a delegate control over an entire mass. If every resident instead acts independently in a nonatomic population, a single person’s deviation may have no effect on the outcome, and the paper’s individual obvious-dominance condition can lose its bite. So this is a credible mirror for mass-represented constituencies, not a continuization of the paper’s model of independently acting voters. It also cannot support a claim that discrete hardness dissolves: the paper supplies no named discrete hardness result to anchor that question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no numbered complexity result to continue: Theorem 1 characterizes which finite-agent rules are obviously strategy-proof, and Theorem 2 constructs an implementing game form. Neither gives a complexity bound or formulates a complexity problem with an input encoding. That does not rule out a new complexity question, but it leaves the proposed mirrors without an existing complexity result to extend.

The deeper difficulty for Theorem 1’s recognition mirror is that its increasing intersection property is about atomic agents. It counts minimal winning coalitions and identifies particular agents in their intersections. A distribution over preference types records masses, not which individuals belong to which winning coalitions. In an atomless population, an individual has zero mass and cannot change a mass-threshold outcome. Replacing coalition cardinalities by masses does not preserve the property’s structure: minimal winning sets and their intersections no longer identify the pivotal individuals the theorem uses.

The strongest repair is the proponent’s delegate model: let each positive-mass constituency act as one player. That makes the property meaningful, but the players are now weighted delegates. Their actions move entire blocs, and their boundaries and decision rights are part of the model. Rational weights can be rescaled to integer weights, so the recognition task is about a finite weighted voting game. It may be a worthwhile problem, but its strategic actors are not a high-multiplicity population of voters. The social distribution alone does not determine who has authority to act for each mass.

The same issue defeats Theorem 2’s synthesis mirror. Its construction sequences named agents through a finite game tree; each agent chooses between adjacent alternatives. A continuum of independently acting voters has no corresponding finite sequence of positive-mass moves. If a type’s whole mass chooses at one node, that type is acting as a coordinated bloc or through a representative. The construction then synthesizes a game for weighted delegates, rather than a continuous implementation for the paper’s voters.

Keeping a large but finite electorate does not bridge the gap cleanly. The empirical distribution \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) omits identity information that arbitrary coalition systems can use. Encoding each voter’s distinct role in the type space restores that information, but can make the support as large as the electorate. Restricting to anonymous rules makes the distribution sufficient, but then the continuum limit removes individual pivotality—the very feature on which obvious strategy-proofness rests. A group-level version could retain pivotality, but it would require a new definition of who acts, what a deviation means, and how obviousness is assessed.

So the negative case is strongest against treating either theorem as a continuous-population mirror: the paper’s central property and its construction depend on atomic strategic agency. It is not airtight against a separate programme on obvious strategy-proofness for delegated, weighted constituencies. That is the positive analyst’s best scenario, but it changes the strategic unit from voters to blocs; the paper does not supply a canonical reason to treat that as its high-multiplicity version.
