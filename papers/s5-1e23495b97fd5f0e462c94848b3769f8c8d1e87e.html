The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s decisive point is right: this paper offers no computational result to mirror. Equation (1) is an explicit probability formula; the appendices use it for sample calculations. The paper’s claims concern epistemic comparisons among institutions, and the Condorcet and Grofman–Dummkopf–Witkopf theorems are invoked as prior results. There is no complexity classification or algorithmic contribution here.

The proposed competence-type mirror is sensible as an epistemic model, but it does not supply the missing computational question. Let type \(t\) have mass \(\mu_t\) and competence \(p_t\). Under the paper’s independence and sincerity assumptions, the continuum puts a deterministic fraction \(q=\sum_t\mu_t p_t\) on the correct option. The majority is then determined by whether \(q\) is above or below \(1/2\). The finite-group uncertainty that the paper calibrates—\(P_N(n,p)\)—disappears in the limit; at \(q=1/2\), the limit alone does not resolve the outcome, so one must specify finite-size fluctuations and tie handling. Keeping a finite \(n\) restores a probability calculation, but that is a finite-population question with a type distribution, not a computational result from this paper being continuized.

A stronger proposed mirror might optimize the composition of a representative body, or model deliberation, evidence, and correlated votes over a population. Those could be worthwhile research questions. But the paper supplies no selection costs or design constraints for the first, and explicitly leaves the size of deliberative effects unmodeled. For the second, a marginal distribution of competence types does not encode common evidence, opinion-leader dependence, or strategic responses. Representing those dependencies would require additional joint structure and a new formal model. Such work may be continuous and computational, but it would not be a mirror of a named result here.

High voter counts do make a population regime plausible; that supports the subject matter, but it does not create a complexity problem. The honest negative case is therefore strong for ChoCo’s computational-mirroring programme: the closest mirror is either a limiting probability model whose finite-group uncertainty has vanished, or a new institutional-design problem requiring substantial additions absent from the paper. The case is not airtight against every adjacent research direction: a formal optimization model of institutional design could be worthwhile, and its authors might recognize it as related to their interests. Its weakness is that this would establish a new research agenda, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
