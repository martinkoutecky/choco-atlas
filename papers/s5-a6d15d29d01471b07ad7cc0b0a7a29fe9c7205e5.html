The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a continuous version of the paper’s coordinated-party model. Imagine a large membership body that votes on a slate of \(K\) appointments, with two caucuses and many members but only a few shared priority profiles. A type is a caucus and its complete vector of intensities across the slate; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of members of that type. Each member still has \(K\) indivisible votes. At type level, however, average allocations can be fractional, and the caucuses’ aggregate vote masses determine which nominations pass. The objective remains the paper’s: measure blocks and realized party or total welfare.

A precise follow-on question would be: given caucus masses \(\alpha_M>\alpha_m\) and a finite rational prior over pairs of intensity vectors \(v_M,v_m\in[0,1]^K\), decide whether there is a pure monotone Bayesian Nash equilibrium and, if so, compute one. In each state, the majority caucus favors every nomination and the minority opposes every nomination. Caucus \(p\) chooses an aggregate allocation \(x_p(v_p)\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^K\) with \(\sum_j x_{p,j}=K\alpha_p\); nomination \(j\) passes when \(x_{M,j}\ge x_{m,j}\), with ties passing. An equilibrium is a pair of allocation strategies that are mutual best responses for expected party welfare. Its block rate and total welfare are then determined by the induced outcomes.

This is a recognizable extension of the paper’s Model C, where party members have perfectly aligned interests and their aggregate behavior is represented by a party-level game. The closest named result is “Proposition: Monotonicity,” proved in the paper’s Appendix: for any \(N\), party sizes \(M,m\), number of nominations \(T\), and distribution \(\Gamma\), Model I has an equilibrium in monotone individual strategies, while Model C has an equilibrium in monotone group strategies. It is unnumbered in the text and establishes an equilibrium property, not a complexity classification. The block percentages and welfare comparisons are simulation findings, not named complexity results.

So there are no qualifying named computational anchors here, and the equilibrium question above is a new, unanchored research question, not a mirror of a numbered complexity result. I would leave its complexity class open: the paper supplies neither an algorithm nor a hardness argument for the mass-allocation game.

The main weakness is that the paper’s strategic incentives rely on finite voters’ chances of being pivotal. In a nonatomic population, one member cannot change a vote outcome. Modeling each caucus as a coordinated bloc preserves strategic influence, as in Model C, but narrows the mirror to a particularly cohesive regime. The paper offers much less support for a continuous mirror of its decentralized Model I.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper to mirror. The Monotonicity proposition is an equilibrium existence and structure result: it shows that certain models have equilibria with monotone strategies. The block rates and welfare comparisons are simulation findings. Neither gives a complexity classification or an algorithmic problem. The proposed equilibrium computation is therefore a new research question, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The strongest proposed version treats each caucus as a player choosing an aggregate allocation of its votes. That preserves the paper’s coordinated-party Model C, but population size then scales out: the vote budgets and the caucuses’ masses grow together, so dividing by total membership leaves the same two-player game, with the same nomination agenda and prior. Computing equilibria of that game might be worthwhile on its own. The continuous population adds only a mass ratio; it supplies no high-multiplicity computational gain.

A version with many preference types faces the deeper problem. In a nonatomic population, one member’s allocation changes the aggregate vote mass by zero. Their action cannot change whether a nomination passes, so the individual pivotal incentive that makes stored votes strategic disappears. Equilibrium no longer selects a meaningful pattern of vote concentration or predicts the paper’s block rates. Giving each type’s entire mass a coordinated collective action restores strategic influence, but adds a coalition assumption; with two caucuses, that returns to Model C, and with many types it defines a different game.

Fixing behavior by stipulating voting rules avoids that issue, but then the continuous calculation is an evaluation of weighted masses, block outcomes, and welfare. The paper’s simulations motivate that calculation, yet they do not identify a complexity problem for it. Optimizing the slate or adding a campaign intervention could produce one, but those would be new problems rather than mirrors of a named result here.

The negative case has a limit: a large association with recurring votes and cohesive caucuses is a plausible setting, so I cannot rule out every useful extension. But the paper’s own computational contribution gives ChoCo no complexity anchor, and the best proposed rescue either removes individual strategic incentives or scales down to its existing two player model.
