The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no eligible anchor: it states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Sections 5.1–5.4 report simulation findings—for example, that increasing diversity often improves information equality while slowing complex contagion—but those are not named computational results. So I cannot honestly supply a continuous problem per anchor or classify an anchored result in the programme’s trichotomy.

The strongest prospective mirror I can see is a continuous cohort network-design problem. Consider a large platform whose users fall into finitely many exchangeable types \(t\), such as group, degree or contact class, seeding status, and transmission parameters. The input gives each type’s population mass \(\mu_t\), per-capita link budgets, link costs \(\gamma_{tu}\), seed masses, and a specified finite-type mixing and contagion model. Choose nonnegative link masses \(y_{tu}\) between types. The objective is to minimize \(\sum_{t,u}\gamma_{tu}y_{tu}\), subject to an integrated disparity bound \(\int_0^1 |\Delta I(v)|\,dv\le\varepsilon\) and a minimum spread-efficiency requirement by a specified deadline. A solution is a feasible link-mass matrix and its induced type-level infection trajectory.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity setting: millions of users can share a small number of relevant profiles, and the decision concerns shares of links rather than individually targeted users. It also fits the paper’s interest in the equality–efficiency trade-off and its recommendation that platforms design connections to improve information access. The authors might recognize it as a computational version of their mechanistic-design aim.

The weak point is substantial: this requires a type-level mixing model that captures the paper’s sparse, growing networks and local contagion dynamics. The paper does not establish that such a model preserves its questions, and it offers no named computational result for this proposed optimization problem. I would treat it as a research extension, not as a supported continuous mirror of a result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. It reports simulation comparisons: for example, how homophily or diversification affects measured equality under selected spreading processes. It states no algorithmic decision problem, complexity bound, or numbered result about computation. So there is no anchor to place in the programme’s trichotomy. The proponent’s link-mass optimization is a newly proposed problem, not the continuous counterpart of a result in the paper.

The proposed formulation also loses the mechanisms behind the paper’s findings. The paper generates sparse networks sequentially: preferential attachment depends on current degrees, and diversification depends on the neighborhoods of already selected neighbors. A type-pair link mass \(y_{tu}\) does not determine those histories or local structures. Networks with the same type-pair edge totals can have different hubs, paths, and infected neighborhoods, and therefore different outcomes under both simple and threshold contagion. Making types rich enough to retain age, degree, and local neighborhood information may make the state sufficient, but its support can grow with the network. A finite-type closure avoids that growth by imposing a different mixing model; it may be coherent, but it no longer preserves the paper’s generative models.

The best faithful alternative would optimize the generator’s parameters—such as \(h\), \(\alpha\), and \(p_d\)—against an equality measure and a spread-efficiency constraint, using expected trajectories in a large-population limit. Group or cohort masses can make that a sensible population-scale question, and it aligns with the paper’s interest in mechanistic design. But it adds an optimization problem the paper never poses. To make it faithful, one first needs a limit model that retains the sparse network’s correlations and contagion dynamics; once that is available, optimizing a handful of generator parameters is a different task from the high-multiplicity complexity landscape the programme targets. Optimizing seed masses has the same issue: it turns an experimental condition into a decision variable, while the paper itself distinguishes its characteristic network question from seed-selection optimization.

So the negative case is strong for the claim that this paper offers no anchored continuous-complexity mirror. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous research could grow from it. The paper’s mechanistic-design aim makes a continuous network-design extension plausible; what the text does not establish is that such an extension would be a mirror of one of its results.
