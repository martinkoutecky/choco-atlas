The continuous mirror question

Given finite type and good-class sets \(V\) and \(G\), rational type masses \(\mu\in\Delta(V)\), rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{G}\), and additive values \(w_{v,g}\), compute finite-support rational masses \(x_{v,b}\ge0\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{G}\) such that \(\sum_b x_{v,b}=\mu_v\), \(\sum_{v,b}x_{v,b}b_g=\sigma_g\), and \(x_{v,b}>0\) implies \(u_v(b)\ge\alpha q_v(\sigma)\), for \(3/4<\alpha\le3/4+3/3836\), where \(q_v(\sigma)\) is the maximum guarantee achievable by a distribution over integral bundles with mean \(\sigma\).

The model it lives in

High-multiplicity fair division with continuum mass over agent types and integral bundle configurations: \(x_{v,b}\) is mass assigned to type \(v\) and bundle \(b\), \(\sigma\) is per-capita supply, and the objective is to maximize the common MMS approximation factor.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3 itself gives no compressed implementation, support-size bound, or running-time guarantee for computing \(q_v(\sigma)\) and the mass allocation; this limits the established algorithmic coverage but does not kill the mirror.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's \(3/4+3/3836\) approximation objective and its reduction-and-bag-filling allocation framework; it leaves the paper's auxiliary lemmas, non-existence bounds, and related variants untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can \(q_v(\sigma)\) and a finite-support \(x\) be computed from the compressed rational input without expanding a denominator-cleared population?
  • For fixed \(|V|\) and \(|G|\), is the configuration or column-generation pricing problem polynomial-time, with bounded support and effective rounding?
  • When \(|G|\) grows, does packing hardness survive in the continuous mirror, or does high multiplicity make part of Theorem 3 tractable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I would defend is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s indivisible-goods problem. It does not make goods divisible: each unit of population still receives an integral bundle. Only the population and the aggregate supply of repeated goods become continuous.

Take finite agent types \(V\) and finite good classes \(G\). A type \(v\in V\) is a complete additive valuation vector \(w_{v,g}\) over the good classes. The population is a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(V)\), and \(\sigma_g\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\) is the supply of goods of class \(g\) per unit of population. A bundle is an integral vector \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{G}\), with value

\[ u_v(b)=\sum_{g\in G}w_{v,g}b_g. \]

Thus, if the population has mass \(1\), there are \(\sigma_g\) goods of class \(g\) per capita. In a finite expansion with \(N\mu_v\) agents of type \(v\) and \(N\sigma_g\) goods of class \(g\), this is exactly the corresponding repeated-goods instance.

For each type \(v\), define its continuous maximin share by

\[ q_v(\sigma)=\sup\left\{q:\exists\lambda\text{ over integral bundles such that } \mathbb E_\lambda[b]=\sigma \text{ and } u_v(b)\ge q\text{ for }\lambda\text{-almost every }b\right\}. \]

The distribution \(\lambda\) is a distribution across a continuum of identical agents, not a lottery giving a fractional bundle to one named agent. Every unit of mass receives an integral bundle.

My lead problem is Continuous Approximate-MMS:

Given \((V,G,\mu,\sigma,w)\) and \(\alpha\le 3/4+3/3836\), compute a finite-support family \(x_{v,b}\ge0\) such that

\[ \sum_b x_{v,b}=\mu_v \]

for every type \(v\),

\[ \sum_{v,b}x_{v,b}b_g=\sigma_g \]

for every good class \(g\), and

\[ x_{v,b}>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_v(b)\ge \alpha q_v(\sigma). \]

The optimization version maximizes the common guarantee \(\alpha\). This is the exact population-level analogue of requiring every finite agent to receive at least an \(\alpha\)-fraction of their MMS value. The fairness condition is support-wise: it is not enough that the average value of type \(v\) is large.

The regime is a large population of households receiving a standardized catalogue of indivisible relief goods, school supplies, or household resources. There may be millions of households but only a small number \(\tau=|V|\) of complete valuation profiles, while each good class has supply proportional to population size. Two households have the same type only when they value every good class identically. This is precisely the high-multiplicity regime, not an assumption that named individuals have interchangeable preferences by fiat.

The finite-to-continuous bridge is direct. If \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are rational, choose \(N\) clearing their denominators. Then \(N\mu_v\) and \(N\sigma_g\) are integers, producing a finite instance with repeated agent and good types. Aggregating any finite allocation by type and bundle gives \(x\). Conversely, a rational \(x\) can be expanded into a finite allocation after multiplying \(N\). Establishing convergence, rounding rates, and finite-support bounds would be part of the continuous result, rather than being silently assumed.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that, for every additive-valuation fair-division instance and every

\[ \alpha\le \frac34+\frac{3}{3836}, \]

the algorithm \(\mathtt{mainApproxMMS}(\mathcal I,\alpha)\) returns an \(\alpha\)-MMS allocation. The algorithm first applies the paper’s reduction, normalization, and ordering transformations; obtains an ordered normalized irreducible instance; then runs one of two bag-filling procedures according to the size of \(N^1_1\); finally transfers the allocation back to the original instance. The paper proves this theorem itself rather than citing it from elsewhere.

This is a constructive approximation anchor, although I would not misstate it as an explicit polynomial-time theorem: the normalization step requires MMS values, and the paper notes that exact MMS computation is NP-hard while a PTAS is available. The continuous question therefore asks whether the paper’s constant-factor construction admits a genuinely compressed high-multiplicity implementation.

I would expect the fixed-\((|V|,|G|)\) version to be Class A. Its natural formulation is a configuration LP over integral bundles:

\[ \sum_b x_{v,b}=\mu_v,\qquad \sum_{v,b}x_{v,b}b_g=\sigma_g, \]

with forbidden configurations \(b\) removed whenever \(u_v(b)<\alpha q_v(\sigma)\). Column generation would expose the central pricing problem: given dual prices on good supplies and type masses, find an integral bundle with the best reduced cost subject to its MMS-value threshold. With a fixed number of good classes, this is a fixed-dimensional integer-optimization problem, and a finite-support theorem could make the entire mirror algorithmic.

If the number of good classes is allowed to grow with the input, I would expect a harder boundary. Bundle pricing and exact computation of \(q_v(\sigma)\) can encode packing or partition structure, so hardness may survive through the goods rather than through population multiplicity. That would not invalidate the mirror; it would identify where continuization stops helping.

The strongest plausibility point is that this is still the authors’ problem: additive valuations, indivisible bundles, MMS thresholds, and the same constant-factor allocation objective. It is not divisible-goods fair division, probabilistic social choice, or a mere average-welfare relaxation. The paper never studies distributions \(\mu\), per-capita supplies \(\sigma\), or allocations of mass over integral configurations, so this is not a novelty collision.

My weakest point is that Theorem 3 does not itself establish a compressed algorithm. The finite algorithm may depend on the expanded population, and its normalization step may remain computationally difficult. Thus the honest claim is not that the paper has already solved the continuous problem, but that its main constructive theorem supplies a precise and meaningful target: determine whether the \(3/4+3/3836\) guarantee survives in a genuine high-multiplicity population model, and whether the reduction-and-bag-filling insight can be replaced by a compact configuration or column-generation argument.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 3 is not actually a complexity theorem. It proves a finite-instance existence guarantee and presents a constructive procedure, but its normalization step requires MMS partitions, which the paper explicitly notes are NP-hard to compute. No running-time bound is established for the full algorithm, and nothing shows that its reduction and bag-filling process can be represented without expanding the population and the repeated goods.

For the proposed model, the existence guarantee is largely inherited for free. With rational \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), clear denominators and expand to \(N\) identical copies. Applying Theorem 3 to that finite instance and aggregating agents by type yields the continuous allocation. Thus the question “does a \(3/4+3/3836\)-MMS allocation exist?” adds no new mathematical content: it is a high-multiplicity lifting of the theorem. The genuinely new question would instead be whether the allocation can be computed from the compressed description \((\mu,\sigma,w)\), including the thresholds \(q_v(\sigma)\). But that is not a consequence of the paper and is a new configuration-optimization problem whose complexity, input encoding, and parameterization the proponent has not supplied.

The proposed model also moves substantially beyond a population continuization. To keep MMS nonzero while the population grows, it must scale the supply of goods and replace the paper’s individually specified goods by repeated good classes. That is a defensible high-multiplicity regime, but it changes the resource model as well as the population model. The fixed-\(|G|\) version may become tractable because it is fixed-dimensional integer/configuration optimization; the unrestricted version may retain packing hardness. Either way, the alleged continuous insight is not present in Theorem 3 itself.

Those objections do not establish that the mirror is unworthwhile. Indeed, they cannot honestly defeat the anchor: the proposed model has a natural standardized-household interpretation, preserves indivisible bundles and support-wise MMS guarantees, and is a legitimate high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem. The correct negative conclusion is therefore modest: the paper supplies no proved compressed computational result, only a guarantee that lifts to the continuum. A universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists would be overstated; Theorem 3 survives as a plausible, though underdeveloped, anchor.
