The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: matching with couples has a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Its Theorem 1, proved here, says that a singleton set \(V=\{\mu\}\) is a farsighted stable set if and only if \(\mu\) is a stable matching. Proposition 1 characterizes indirect dominance under an individual-rationality condition. Both are structural results; neither classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard, nor gives an algorithmic complexity result. The paper’s examples show that certain farsighted stable sets exist or fail to exist, but do not change that. So there are zero eligible anchors.

A plausible population regime is a large residency match with many couples and hospital positions but relatively few repeated preference profiles: \(n,m\) are large compared with the number of types. A couple type would include the joint ranking of the two partners’ hospital assignments; a hospital-position type would include its ranking of resident roles. Those descriptions must be complete for the preferences the model uses. This is credible as a regime for cohorts with shared rankings, and the paper itself discusses large couples markets. Each couple still receives a whole pair of assignments. Mass records the fraction of each type receiving each pair; it does not split a couple’s assignment.

One possible new, unanchored problem is: given finite sets of couple types \(C\) and hospital-position types \(Q\), rational masses \(\alpha_c,\beta_q\), the types’ strict preferences, and a proposed rational allocation \(x\), decide whether \(\{x\}\) is a farsighted stable set under a mass-based version of the paper’s deviation rules. Here \(x_{c,a,b}\) is the mass of couples of type \(c\) assigning partner 1 to position type \(a\) and partner 2 to type \(b\), with \(a,b\in Q\cup\{u\}\). Require \(\sum_{a,b}x_{c,a,b}=\alpha_c\); for each \(q\in Q\), the mass assigned to positions of type \(q\), counting both couple roles, must be at most \(\beta_q\). A deviation selects submasses of couples and positions, preserves feasibility, and lifts the paper’s enforcement rule: a new link requires both parties in the coalition, while breaking an old link requires at least one party. Each coalition member must prefer the terminal assignment to its assignment at that step of the finite deviation path. The answer is yes or no; there is no optimization objective in the source paper.

I would tentatively expect the singleton-recognition question to be tractable if the paper’s characterization of indirect dominance extends to these mass allocations: then recognition may reduce to checking finitely many type-level blocking conditions. That is a conjectural route, not a result of this paper. The central further question is whether the characterization survives when deviations involve positive submasses, and then whether stable allocations or DEM farsighted stable sets can be computed.

This candidate covers only the singleton characterization in Theorem 1. The authors could plausibly recognize it as their problem in a repeated-type market, provided identities do not affect preferences or enforcement. The weak point is that farsighted paths are defined over agents and their links: lifting them to positive-mass coalitions changes the model and needs its own justification. That limitation, together with the absence of any named computational result, means this is a promising research direction rather than an anchored ChoCo mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against starts with the anchor: this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes singleton farsighted stable sets; Proposition 1 characterizes indirect dominance; the remaining results are existence statements, examples, and structural observations. None gives an algorithm or a complexity classification. That leaves no named computational problem whose population-continuous version ChoCo could chart.

The proposed recognition problem takes Theorem 1 as a seed, but it is not a mirror of a computational task in the paper. In the paper’s finite model, the input is a matching among named couples and hospitals, and the theorem says when its singleton is farsightedly stable. Turning this into a mass-allocation decision problem requires defining a new market and new deviation rules. The proposed allocation \(x\) does not by itself settle what counts as a coalition of positive mass, how its members enforce a transition, or which hospital preferences govern those transitions.

There is a concrete obstacle to getting that market from the paper’s model while retaining a meaningful continuum. Each hospital has one position and strictly ranks named students. With hospitals fixed, growing the couple population makes the fraction that can be matched vanish. If instead hospitals scale with the population, the strict rankings over named students distinguish them: repeated couple types do not preserve all the preference comparisons that determine blocking and enforcement. Grouping students and positions into types could address this, but would require type-level priorities and scaled capacities. That may be a sensible new matching model; it is not a limit of the paper’s stated model, and the paper’s large-market discussion does not establish repeated types.

A stronger project could study farsighted stability in that redesigned mass market, perhaps including DEM sets. But it would be a new computational research direction, not a continuous mirror anchored in this paper. The negative case is strongest on that distinction and on the lack of computational anchors. It is not airtight: the paper’s interest in large couples markets makes a type-based extension plausible, and the authors might recognize it as a natural continuation.
