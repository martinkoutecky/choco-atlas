What the mirror covers

A candidate population analogue would recast Theorems 4.2.1 and 4.3.2 over distributions on weak-order types; it would not cover a computational result, and Theorem 2.1.2 is cited background.

The case FOR (proponent)

The most charitable continuous object is clear: in a large electorate, let each type be a complete weak order over the alternatives, and let its mass be the fraction of society holding that order. The aggregate preference relation would be the outcome. That is a plausible population regime, but it does not yield a qualifying continuous mirror of this paper: the paper asks axiomatic questions about social welfare functions, not computational questions about finding or optimizing over such a society.

The closest named results are Theorem 4.3.2, proved in this paper, and Theorem 4.2.1, also proved here. Theorem 4.3.2 says that if a social welfare function on at least three alternatives and at least two individuals satisfies Unrestricted Domain, IIA, Unanimity, and Non-Dictatorship, then some profile of weak orders is mapped to a total preference cycle spanning all alternatives. Theorem 4.2.1 says that under those same conditions, the function violates Transitivity. The paper also states Theorem 2.1.2, Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem, but cites it to earlier proofs rather than proving it here: no such function can satisfy Unrestricted Domain, Unanimity, IIA, Non-Dictatorship, and Transitivity simultaneously. These are existence and impossibility results, not results about computational complexity.

Theorem 4.3.2 might suggest a search problem: given a qualifying social welfare function, find a profile whose output is a total cycle. But the paper gives no encoding of a function as input, no complexity analysis of that search, and no continuous extension of the function from an \(N\)-person profile to a distribution over types. Moreover, its functions may distinguish individuals by their fixed positions. A distribution over weak-order types can lose that information; preserving it by making identity part of the type would generally eliminate the many-agents, few-types regime that motivates continuization.

So the paper contains no named computational result to anchor a continuous computational mirror. A large population with recurring preference types is a sensible adjacent scenario, and Theorem 4.3.2 supplies a compelling axiomatic question about that setting. Turning it into a computational problem would require adding the missing function representation and computational task—and would move the mirror beyond the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper’s results are axiomatic, not computational. Theorem 4.2.1 says that certain conditions on a social welfare function force an intransitive output; Theorem 4.3.2 strengthens this to a total cycle. Neither poses an algorithmic task or studies its complexity. Theorem 2.1.2 is an earlier impossibility result cited for context, not a computational result either.

There is a sensible high-multiplicity setting here: a large electorate can be represented by masses on weak-order types. In that setting, one could ask whether every aggregation rule satisfying the paper’s axioms must produce a total cycle for some distribution. That is a coherent continuous axiomatic question, but it still asks about the existence and properties of rules—not about algorithms for a society given as input. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, it is not a computational mirror.

The best attempt to turn the results into one would be: given a finitely represented aggregation rule, find a rational distribution of weak-order types whose aggregate is a total cycle. But the paper specifies no representation of a rule as an algorithmic input, and its theorems give no complexity claim for this search. An explicit table over profiles grows with the number of individually indexed voters; a rule defined on distributions needs a new representation and a continuous extension of the axioms. Choosing either would define a new search problem, rather than continuize a computational problem the paper studied.

So the negative case is strong as a verdict about this paper’s fit for the programme: its central results offer no computational problem to mirror. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could grow from the paper. A computational study of finding cycle witnesses for a specified class of aggregation rules might be worthwhile, but that would be an adjacent project, not a continuous mirror of Theorems 4.2.1 or 4.3.2.
