The continuous mirror question

Given goods \(J\) with rational per-capita supplies \(s_j\), and supported buyer types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, linear utilities \(u_{tj}\), per-capita budgets \(m_t\), and utility caps \(d_t\), find equilibrium prices \(p_j\) and bundles \(x_{tj}\) per unit of type mass. Each type chooses a utility-maximizing bundle at those prices up to its cap, spending the minimum needed if it reaches the cap; aggregate allocations satisfy \(\sum_t \mu_t x_{tj} \le s_j\), with equality whenever \(p_j>0\).

The model it lives in

A Fisher market with a finite distribution over complete buyer types. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), aggregate budget \(\mu_t m_t\), aggregate utility cap \(\mu_t d_t\), and aggregate allocation \(\mu_t x_t\); goods have per-capita supplies \(s_j\). The task is to return equilibrium prices and allocations, equivalently solving the type-weighted capped Eisenberg–Gale program with objective \(\sum_t \mu_t m_t \log U_t(x_t)\) and recovering prices from its dual.

What the mirror covers

Covers linear-utility M2 equilibrium computation, with existence and rationality support; leaves M1, the Leontief and CES M2 results, and the broader convex-duality contribution aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the paper's polynomial-time equilibrium method remain polynomial in the number of goods, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\) for rational type masses and per-capita supplies?
  • Can the common model with buyer utility caps and seller earning caps be solved efficiently for a society given by a finite distribution over types?
  • What finite encoding and algorithm extend this result from finite support to a genuinely continuous distribution of buyer types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s linear-utility version of M2: computing an equilibrium when buyers have utility limits. A plausible regime is a large market for standardized, divisible services, where many consumers fall into a relatively small number of exact preference and budget segments. A type includes the full linear utility vector, per-person budget, utility limit, and any other parameter the market uses. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of consumers in that segment. The regime is \(N\gg\tau\): millions of consumers, perhaps, but far fewer distinct complete types.

Continuous Buyer-Type M2 Equilibrium has as input a finite set of goods \(J\), rational per-capita supplies \(s_j\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. Each supported type \(t\) has utilities \(u_{tj}\), budget \(m_t\), and utility limit \(d_t\). The question is to find equilibrium prices \(p_j\) and a bundle \(x_t\) per member of each type. At prices \(p\), each type chooses a bundle within budget that maximizes utility up to its cap, spending as little as possible if it can reach the cap. Aggregate supply must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le s_j\), with equality for every good whose price is positive. This scaling of supplies is part of the mirror: a market serving \(N\) consumers has \(N s_j\) units of each good. The goods remain divisible, as in the paper; the continuization is of the buyer population.

The anchor is Theorem 9.1, cited from Jain (2004), not proved in this paper: given a convex set through a strong separation oracle, and a guarantee that it contains a point whose binary encoding length is at most \(\phi\), one can find a point in that set in polynomial time. The paper applies this result to equilibrium computation for linear-utility M1 and M2. For M2, its ingredients include the equilibrium-checking max-flow construction in §9.2, the separating oracle built using Theorem 8.3 in §9.3, and rationality from Lemma 7.3. Theorem 8.3, proved here, says that for an equilibrium price \(p\) and a non-equilibrium price \(q\), the excess-demand vector \(f_q\) and \(\Delta_q=f_q^\top q\) satisfy \(f_q^\top p>\Delta_q\). Lemma 7.3, also proved here, says that M2 with linear utilities has a rational equilibrium when its parameters are rational. These support the application; they are not separate complexity anchors.

In the type version, identical buyers can be aggregated: type \(t\) has total budget \(\mu_t m_t\), total utility limit \(\mu_t d_t\), and aggregate allocation \(\mu_t x_t\). Thus the market has \(\tau\) buyer rows rather than \(N\) named buyers. The resulting instance stays polynomial in the number of goods, \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). The underlying task is to output an equilibrium, not to optimize a new social objective. The paper’s capped Eisenberg–Gale potential has the type-weighted form \(\sum_t \mu_t m_t\log U_t(x_t)\), subject to the caps and aggregate supply constraints; it is a way to compute the equilibrium, not a replacement question.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A): the paper’s linear M2 algorithm applies after aggregation. Its further questions include whether the paper’s open common generalization—buyer utility limits together with seller earning limits—admits a similarly efficient equilibrium algorithm for a type population, and how far the finite-support algorithm extends when the population has a genuinely continuous type distribution. This mirror covers the paper’s linear M2 equilibrium computation, existence, and rationality results. It does not claim to cover its Leontief or CES results or its broader convex-duality contribution.

The weakest point is that Theorem 9.1 is a generic, externally proved point-finding theorem, not a numbered theorem in this paper explicitly classifying continuous-population market equilibrium as polynomial-time solvable. That conclusion comes from combining the theorem with the paper’s market-specific oracle and rationality results. Also, aggregating exact duplicate buyers reduces the model to a finite market with \(\tau\) buyers, so this is a strong case that the high-multiplicity version is natural and tractable, but it does not show that continuization dissolves a discrete hardness barrier. The paper supplies no comparable hardness anchor to test that possibility.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that its continuous version collapses exactly to a smaller finite M2 market. For each type \(t\), replace mass \(\mu_t\) by one buyer with budget \(\mu_t m_t\), utility limit \(\mu_t d_t\), and aggregate allocation \(y_t=\mu_t x_t\). Scale supplies per capita. Splitting \(y_t\) equally among the members of the type recovers their individual allocations, so the equilibrium conditions are preserved. Thus the proposed algorithm runs on \(\tau\) buyer rows; it does not need a new continuum method.

That makes this a low-novelty mirror, but it does not defeat it. The finite-support, rational-mass formulation is a well-posed high-multiplicity problem, and aggregation is precisely the compression that makes its input regime useful. The paper’s equilibrium oracle and rationality argument support the polynomial-time result. The broader literature also treats equilibrium computation for capped linear utilities, though its finite-buyer results do not themselves constitute a continuous-population treatment ([Bei et al., “Earning and Utility Limits in Fisher Markets”](https://doi.org/10.1145/3340234); [Garg, Hoefer, and Mehlhorn](https://www.algo.rwth-aachen.de/team/hoefer/papers/Garg23Satiation.pdf)).

A genuinely continuous distribution of buyer types would be a stronger mirror, but it would need a finite encoding—such as a parametric distribution or an oracle—that the paper does not supply. That limits the next question one can ask; it does not invalidate the finite-support mirror the proponent stated.

So I can argue that this is a thin extension whose answer follows almost immediately from the paper’s finite-market machinery. Under the programme’s stated standard, though, an easy or already-known answer does not sink a well-posed mirror. The proposed M2 anchor survives, and I cannot make an honest universal case that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror.
