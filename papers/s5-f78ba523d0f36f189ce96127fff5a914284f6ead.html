What the mirror covers

The proposed computation of benchmark disclosure would concern the equilibrium disclosure results; the paper's results on early contracting and welfare are not computational claims either.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive substrate is a large student cohort matched to a large job market. A type could be a school and ability class; its mass is that group’s share of students. Schools choose calibrated transcript policies, and their objective is to maximize the average desirability of the jobs their students receive. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the authors would recognize it as their subject.

But it is already the paper’s model: students and positions are represented by distributions, and the analysis studies equilibrium information disclosure and early contracting. The paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says that connected equilibria have the same desirability mapping when the aggregate ability and job distributions are preserved, even if students are reshuffled among schools. Theorem 2, proved here, says that benchmark disclosure removes positions’ incentive to make early offers in the two-period model. Theorem 3, proved here, says that under gradual information arrival, benchmark disclosure supports waiting, while greater disclosure makes some agents strictly prefer early contracting. These are equilibrium results, not complexity classifications or algorithms.

So I can’t honestly give a qualifying anchor or a continuous problem per anchor. A new problem about computing equilibrium disclosure from rationally encoded type masses might be worthwhile, but it would be a computational extension of an already continuous-population model—not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes connected equilibria; Corollary 1 concerns uniqueness; Theorems 2 and 3 establish incentives to wait or contract early. None poses a computational problem or gives a complexity classification, algorithm, or approximation result. The other analyst’s strongest proposed substrate—a large cohort of students and jobs—does not supply the missing anchor.

The closest candidate is Theorem 1: given encoded student and job distributions, compute the benchmark disclosure mapping. That could be a useful computational question, but the paper already models the population through distributions over ability and job desirability, and transcript structures as stochastic maps. Its equilibrium result is already about that continuous population. Restricting the inputs to empirical distributions from high-multiplicity finite cohorts would give a computational study of the paper’s model, not a continuous mirror of a discrete computational result in it. Adding a complexity target would make the question new; it would not make it a mirror.

The early-contracting results fare no better as anchors. Theorem 2 derives its conclusion from convexity and Jensen’s inequality; Theorem 3 adds continuous-time information arrival and a diffusion argument. Those are equilibrium analyses in an already continuous-population model. Treating the diffusion over time as the relevant continuization would change the wrong axis: the programme’s target is the population.

The negative case has a real limit: computing equilibrium disclosure for rational finite-support inputs might be worthwhile research. I cannot rule that out, and the paper’s constructive proof may not settle its complexity. But that would be a computational extension of an already continuous model, not a worthwhile *continuous mirror* of any named computational result in this paper.
