The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper that can serve as an anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says that if a valuation domain admits MD1 and MD2 transformations, every 2-cycle monotone allocation rule on that domain is truthfully implementable and satisfies revenue equivalence. It is a structural characterization, not a result about an algorithm or a complexity class. Corollary 1 similarly establishes implementability and revenue equivalence for a particular domain; it makes no computational claim.

A possible population story would involve many consumers of a divisible public good, with types given by their valuation functions and mass by their population shares. But the paper’s question is whether an allocation rule on a valuation domain can be implemented; the distribution of consumers across that domain does not enter its stated result. Treating the valuation domain or the divisible allocation set as the continuous object would change what is continuized.

So I cannot give a continuous problem per anchor, or a justified expected complexity class, without stretching the paper beyond its results. The required anchors are absent.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The opponent’s two named candidates, Theorem 1 and Corollary 1, are not computational results. Theorem 1 says that a property of a valuation domain makes \(2\)-cycle monotonicity sufficient for truthful implementation and revenue equivalence; Corollary 1 applies that implication to one domain. Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a computational problem over societies. The other numbered results are characterizations of implementability and revenue equivalence, not complexity claims. That leaves no paper result to mirror in ChoCo’s sense.

The strongest possible reinterpretation is a market with many consumers of a divisible good, whose valuation functions serve as types. But the paper’s conditions concern which allocation a mechanism assigns at each reported valuation profile. They do not use the population’s distribution over those valuations. Repeating agents with the same type, or replacing their counts with masses, therefore does not make the paper’s question about a continuous society. To make population composition matter, one would need to add an objective or constraints that depend on that composition, such as revenue maximization under a prior. The authors explicitly leave such design questions for future work; they are possible new projects, but not mirrors of this paper’s results.

A continuum-agent version could also be worth studying on its own. But a nonatomic population changes the strategic model, while keeping individually strategic agents preserves the paper’s pointwise incentive constraints. Either route requires a new problem, and the paper supplies no computational anchor for choosing it. The uncountable allocation sets and function spaces in its examples do not fill that gap: they continuize outcomes or valuations, while the programme’s target is the population. The negative case is strongest on that mismatch; it is not a claim that continuous mechanism design has no worthwhile questions, only that this paper does not pose one for ChoCo to mirror.
