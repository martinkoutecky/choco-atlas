What the mirror covers

The proposed direction concerns baseline information aggregation; it does not provide a computational mirror for the paper’s results on correlated signals, partisan bias, candidate entry, or primaries.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is the paper’s large-election regime: the number of voters grows while the number of distinct voter classes stays small. A possible mirror would take a voter’s type to include everything relevant to the model—signal technology, policy preferences, partisan bias, and turnout—and let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s share of society. The two candidates and their binary policy choices would remain finite; each voter type would have a ballot strategy, and the equilibrium outcome would be evaluated by whether the implemented policy matches the state. The question would be whether an equilibrium (or a sequence of finite-population equilibria approaching this model) achieves information aggregation. That continuizes the population, rather than the policy space.

But this is only a plausible research direction, not an anchor-based case under your criteria. The paper has no named computational result: none classifies an underlying problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise computationally complex. The closest result is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. In the baseline model, with \(\Pr(z=0)=\alpha<1/2\), candidates informed with probability \(\pi\), and office motivation \(\omega\), information aggregation is feasible under the paper’s voter-symmetric equilibrium concept if and only if
\[ \frac{\omega}{1-\omega}<\frac{2\alpha}{1-2\alpha}(1-\pi). \]
That is a game-theoretic condition on equilibrium incentives, not a complexity result. The other numbered propositions likewise concern equilibrium feasibility and policy outcomes, not algorithms or complexity classifications.

There is also a substantive obstacle to the proposed mirror: voters’ probability of being pivotal drives the paper’s swing-voter logic. In a straightforward nonatomic model, an individual voter has no effect on aggregate vote shares, which could remove that mechanism. Preserving it would require a careful finite-to-continuum equivalence argument.

So there is a credible population-continuum motivation here, but no qualifying computational anchor and no established computational mirror to hand to a prover. The paper itself studies what happens as the electorate grows; it does not chart the computational complexity of problems over a continuous society.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest on scope: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 is the closest anchor, but it gives a closed-form equilibrium condition for information aggregation, not a complexity classification or an algorithmic problem. The proposed mirror—whether equilibria aggregate information as the population grows—is already the paper’s Definition 1 and its main analysis. Replacing voter counts with masses would restate that large-electorate result, not open a computational question.

The best version of the mirror would put rational masses on voter signal types while keeping the two candidates and their platforms finite. That is a sensible model, but in the baseline paper voters are identical ex ante; their signals are random draws conditional on the state. The resulting type masses are therefore random, then become deterministic conditional on the state in the large-population limit. In an exact atomless model, an individual vote has no effect on the outcome, so the pivotal-voter incentives behind the paper’s voting behavior disappear. Add a finite-population or pivot-selection rule to recover those incentives, and the question returns to the paper’s equilibrium sequence and Proposition 1. Leave voters non-pivotal, and equilibrium selection needs new assumptions; the computational question then belongs to that enlarged model.

One could study equilibrium computation for richer distributions of voter information or preferences. But that would add a new research problem rather than mirror a result here, and the paper gives no reason to expect a useful complexity landscape from it. I do not count high multiplicity against the mirror; it supports the population regime’s plausibility. The honest limit of this negative case is that no computational mirror is anchored in this paper. I cannot rule out a worthwhile new model built from it, but that would be a different claim.
