The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result in this paper. Its strongest computational anchor is Theorem 1, proved here: for every resource-monotonic, anonymous, regular bilateral principle \(f\), the ascending algorithm gives an allocation rule extending \(f\); that rule is pairwise \(f\)-robust, and it is the unique rule with those properties. Pairwise robustness means that for each pair of citizens, no feasible change to just their allocations can move either allocation closer to the split prescribed by \(f\) for those two citizens and their combined allotment. The theorem gives a constructive algorithm and a uniqueness result, but no running-time bound. I would use it as an algorithmic anchor, not present it as a result in P.

The mirror I believe in is Egalitarian Network Allocation at High Multiplicity, an instance of Theorem 1 using the egalitarian bilateral principle specified in Example 1. Consider a water-shortage allocation with finitely many reservoirs or treatment plants \(S\), and a large population grouped into types \(T\). A type \(t\) consists of residents with the same per-person claim \(c_t\) and the same set \(A_t\subseteq S\) of plants they can receive water from. Its mass \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\) is its fraction of the population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each source \(s\) has capacity \(b_s\) per unit of total population. Thus the resource capacities scale with population size, as the programme’s scope permits.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: millions of residents may fall into a modest number \(\tau=|T|\) of classes defined by service connections and standardized water claims. For example, residents with the same connection to the network and the same claim are indistinguishable for this allocation problem. The type description includes every feature the problem uses; named individuals play no role.

The continuous problem is: given \(S,T,\mu,c,(A_t)_{t\in T}\), and \(b\), compute the per-person allocation \(q_t\) for each type and a supporting source-to-type flow \(x_{st}\). Require \(q_t\le c_t\), \(x_{st}=0\) when \(s\notin A_t\), \(\sum_s x_{st}=\mu_t q_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{st}\le b_s\). Assume the continuous analogue of the paper’s genuine-scarcity condition: for every nonempty \(U\subseteq S\), \(\sum_{s\in U}b_s < \sum_{t:A_t\cap U\ne\varnothing}\mu_t c_t\). The solution is the unique allocation prescribed by the high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s egalitarian network rule; the flow is a feasibility witness and need not be unique. There is no separate scalar objective in the paper’s problem: the rule selects the allocation through its egalitarian principle and pairwise-robustness requirement.

For this egalitarian case, the continuous question has a concrete replica interpretation. For any common denominator \(N\) of the masses, make \(N\mu_t\) identical citizens of each type and give source \(s\) capacity \(Nb_s\). Compute the paper’s finite-population rule and report each type’s per-person allocation. The continuous answer is the same for every such replication. Equivalently, it can be computed by the weighted ascending procedure: at each stage, over remaining types \(T_h\) and sources \(S_h\), find the largest \(\lambda_h\) such that, for every \(D\subseteq T_h\),
\[ \sum_{t\in D}\mu_t\min\{c_t,\lambda_h\} \le \sum_{s\in (\bigcup_{t\in D}A_t)\cap S_h}b_s. \]
Choose the largest tight type set, assign its members \(q_t=\min\{c_t,\lambda_h\}\), remove those types and their neighboring sources, and continue. This is the mass-weighted version of the paper’s ascending algorithm. The support representation uses \(\tau\) types rather than a potentially enormous number of citizens.

My expectation is Class A, tractable, although that is a follow-on complexity claim, not a theorem of this paper. For a fixed \(\lambda\), checking whether some type subset violates the displayed inequality reduces to a cut or maximum-closure computation. There are at most \(\tau\) allocation stages. The concrete question for a prover is whether this yields an exact algorithm polynomial in \(|S|,\tau\), and the binary input length \(L\), including the threshold searches over rational data. Further questions include whether the same type compression works for other bilateral principles, and which of them have a replica-independent mass-weighted extension.

The weakest point is that Theorem 1 is an axiomatic existence-and-uniqueness theorem with a finite ascending algorithm, not a hardness result or a proved complexity classification. The continuous mirror therefore does not test whether discrete hardness dissolves in the continuum. Its positive case is narrower: the paper’s underlying allocation problem remains the same, its agents become population mass grouped by complete types, and its constructive rule admits a precise high-multiplicity computation question. This covers the egalitarian instance of Theorem 1, not every principle or every result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case cannot rest on the population model being artificial. Many households can share a service zone, per-person claim, and set of connected sources; scaling source capacities with population makes that a sensible high-multiplicity instance. The proposed mirror fits the programme’s scope.

The stronger objection is to the anchor. Theorem 1 proves existence and uniqueness of a rule with pairwise robustness; it does not state a complexity result. Its distinctive pairwise condition also has no direct population-level meaning: changing the allocation of two named residents changes no positive mass. One could define a group-level version, but that would be a new axiom, not a computational consequence of Theorem 1.

The egalitarian mirror avoids that problem by asking for the weighted leximin allocation across types. But that is a weighted bottleneck-flow problem. The paper itself points to the computational literature on egalitarian flows, and Megiddo gives an algorithm for lexicographically optimal terminal flows in multi-terminal networks, with \(O(n^5)\) operation complexity ([Megiddo, “A Good Algorithm for Lexicographically Optimal Flows”](https://theory.stanford.edu/~megiddo/pdf/lexflow.pdf)). The mirror’s remaining distinction is the support-compressed representation with rational type masses. The proponent’s cut-based test suggests that this is a narrow weighted adaptation of known flow machinery, rather than a new complexity landscape.

That makes the case against useful but limited: the paper’s novel pairwise theorem does not transfer cleanly, and its strongest surviving mirror appears to be a compact formulation of a familiar flow computation. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The type-mass question is well-posed; the negative case is that its computational payoff may be too incremental to justify ChoCo’s attention.
