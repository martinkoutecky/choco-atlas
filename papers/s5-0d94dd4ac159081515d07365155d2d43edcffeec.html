The continuous mirror question

Given qualified candidates \(C\), spoiler candidates \(D\), target \(p\in C\), action limit \(k\), and a finite support \(T\) of complete fallback ballot types over \(C\cup D\), with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), decide whether some \(D'\subseteq D\), \(|D'|\le k\), makes \(p\) the unique fallback winner. For each proposed \(D'\), restrict ballots to \(C\cup D'\), then compute level scores from the masses of types that approve each candidate within their first \(i\) approved positions; use the paper's strict-majority threshold \(1/2\), and its total-approval fallback if no level has a strict majority.

The model it lives in

A finite rational distribution over complete approval-and-ranking ballot types on the potential slate \(C\cup D\). The chair chooses binary inclusion decisions for candidates in \(D\), subject to a cardinality limit; feasibility is determined by fallback's mass-weighted level scores and total-approval scores.

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover Theorems 3.1 and 3.2: constructive and destructive control by adding or deleting candidates. They leave Theorems 3.3 and 3.4 on voter control unexamined.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity when parameterized jointly by the action limit \(k\) and support size \(\tau\), including whether bounded \(\tau\) permits an exact algorithm?
  • What running-time bounds hold in the candidate count, support size \(\tau\), action limit \(k\), and mass bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is candidate control in a large fallback election whose voters fall into a small number of repeated ballot types. A type is a complete fallback ballot: an approval set and a strict ranking of its approved candidates. Its mass is the fraction of the electorate with that ballot. This fits settings such as a large election whose ballots are summarized by recurring approval-and-ranking patterns. The slate may be large, but many voters share each type.

My lead is Continuous Fallback Control by Adding Candidates. An instance has qualified candidates \(C\), spoiler candidates \(D\), a target \(p\in C\), an integer \(k\), and a finite support \(T\) of ballot types over \(C\cup D\), each with positive rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Ask whether there is a set \(D'\subseteq D\), \(|D'|\le k\), such that \(p\) is the unique fallback winner after adding \(D'\). For a ballot \(t\), its level-\(i\) approval contributes its mass to the candidates among the first \(i\) approved candidates. Apply the paper’s fallback procedure to these mass scores, with strict majority meaning score greater than \(1/2\); if no level has a strict majority, use total approval mass. A solution is the qualifying set \(D'\). The destructive variant asks whether such a set can make \(p\) cease to be a unique winner.

The anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper by a parameterized reduction from Dominating Set. It establishes that both constructive and destructive control by adding candidates in fallback voting are W[2]-hard, parameterized by the number of candidates added. The constructive continuous problem above mirrors its constructive result; its destructive variant mirrors the other result in the theorem.

A second, closely related anchor is Theorem 3.2, also proved here by reduction from Dominating Set. It establishes W[2]-hardness, parameterized by the number of candidates deleted, for both constructive and destructive control by deleting candidates. Its continuous mirror has a candidate set \(C\), target \(p\in C\), deletion limit \(k\), and rational-mass ballot types over \(C\). Ask whether deleting at most \(k\) candidates other than \(p\) makes \(p\) the unique winner; the destructive variant asks whether \(p\) is no longer a unique winner. After deletion, ballots are restricted to the remaining candidates, and winner determination uses the same mass-based fallback rule.

Both hardness results transfer directly. Given any finite election, assign each ballot type mass equal to its number of voters divided by the electorate size. Then every level score is the original count divided by the electorate size, and a strict majority of voters is exactly mass greater than \(1/2\). Adding or deleting the same candidates therefore produces the same winners. The paper’s reductions give polynomial-size rational-mass instances and preserve \(k\), so the continuous candidate-control problems are W[2]-hard parameterized by the action limit. This is a Class B case: the reduction’s combinatorics live in candidate choices and the graph encoded by those candidates, and the discrete instances embed exactly in the mass model.

The mirror is close to the authors’ problem: it keeps fallback ballots, the fallback winner rule, the chair’s candidate-control action, and the unique-winner goal. It changes the electorate representation to a distribution over complete ballot types. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and repeated ballots are already allowed in the paper’s election model.

The weakest point is that these results do not show continuous masses dissolving discrete hardness. Candidate actions remain discrete, and the transferred hardness is almost immediate from normalization. The W[2]-hard reductions also do not establish hardness when support size \(\tau\) is fixed. So the mirror is a strong, honest continuation of the paper’s computational question, but it does not promise a tractability gain. I would leave Theorems 3.3 and 3.4, on adding and deleting voters, outside this case: those actions directly select population mass, and their discrete hardness may behave differently when fractional mass is allowed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that both anchors change the electorate’s encoding while leaving the hard choice untouched. In continuous control by adding or deleting candidates, the chair still chooses a discrete subset of candidates. For any chosen subset, fallback scores are just weighted sums of ballot-type masses, \(\sum_t \mu_t a_{t,c,i}\), followed by the same level-by-level comparisons. The mirrors introduce no mass-allocation variables, pricing problem, or other obvious opening for continuous-optimization methods. Their computational core remains the candidate search from the original theorems.

That criticism applies to both anchors: Theorem 3.1’s candidate additions and Theorem 3.2’s candidate deletions. The paper’s reductions encode Dominating Set through candidate choices, so they do not suggest that population multiplicity is the source of hardness. A sceptic could therefore call these thin continuizations: useful as compressed-input questions, but less central to a programme whose strongest examples gain traction through continuous optimization.

But this does not defeat either mirror. A large electorate with recurring complete fallback ballots is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and neither candidate-control problem depends on tracking voter identities. For additions, types can describe ballots over the qualified and spoiler candidates; for deletions, ballots can be restricted to the surviving candidates. Both are recognizable versions of the paper’s problems. The discrete reductions transfer to rational masses, while the compressed input raises a separate question: how do complexity and algorithms depend on the support size \(\tau\) and mass bit length \(L\)? The paper’s W[2]-hardness in the action limit does not settle, for example, what happens when \(\tau\) is small. Exact hardness transfer is not a reason to dismiss that question.

I cannot honestly make the universal case the prompt demands. The paper has named computational results, a plausible repeated-ballot population regime, and no stated continuous computational treatment that already answers the mirrors. The best negative is about priority: these look more like high-multiplicity complexity questions than showcase continuous-optimization problems. That may make them less exciting than the bribery examples, but it does not establish that either is unworthy of ChoCo’s time.
