The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is continuous coalition manipulation. For a fixed ranking rule, let each type \(t\) be a sincere ranking and let \(\mu_t\) be its population share. Given the sincere winner \(w\), ask whether there is a challenger \(d\) and a mass transfer \(x_{t,r}\) of voters from sincere type \(t\) to reported ranking \(r\), using only types that prefer \(d\) to \(w\), such that \(d\) wins after the reports change. A large electorate with many repeated rankings and fixed \(m\) gives this a sensible high-multiplicity regime.

But that model has no qualifying named computational anchor in this paper. Propositions 1–22 establish structural claims about strategic voting, core equilibria, and nomination; the simulations estimate vulnerability rates. For example, Proposition 16, proved here, says that if sincere preferences contain a majority-rule cycle, no core equilibrium exists under the eight listed rules. That is a substantive result, but it does not classify a problem’s computational complexity or give an algorithmic runtime. So I can offer the mirror as a plausible research question, but I can’t honestly attach the required complexity prediction to a named result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s closest anchor is V1’s question of whether a coalition can change the winner to one all its members prefer, with Proposition 16 as an example. The strongest continuous version is well-defined: represent sincere rankings by rational masses \(\mu_t\), and ask whether mass from types preferring a challenger \(d\) to the sincere winner can be reassigned to reported rankings so that \(d\) wins. A large electorate with repeated ballot types is a sensible high-multiplicity setting. I don’t see a credible objection based on loss of individual identity or absence of meaningful mass.

The negative case is narrower: neither V1 nor Proposition 16 is a named complexity result. V1 reports simulated vulnerability rates and describes rule-specific routines, but gives no worst-case complexity classification or algorithmic guarantee. Proposition 16 is a structural claim about core existence under a majority-rule cycle. Its mass analogue may be worth stating, but it does not supply the required computational anchor. Asking for a complexity classification of the coalition-feasibility problem—or for the least mass needed to manipulate—would be a new computational question, not a continuous version of a complexity result established here. The nomination propositions have the same problem: they are analytical results, not complexity theorems.

So the strongest case against is that this paper is a poor match for a programme specifically seeking mirrors of named computational results. That does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: the continuous coalition-manipulation problem itself remains coherent and potentially interesting. The negative case is weak exactly there; it can reject the proponent’s anchor under the programme’s stated criterion, but it cannot honestly rule out the best re-modelled mirror.
