The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is an aggregate-performance mirror for large markets of repeated bilateral negotiations. It is plausible as a continuation of this paper’s work, but it is weaker than the kind of computational-complexity mirror ChoCo is looking for.

There are no eligible computational-complexity anchors in the paper: it proves no named result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The closest named results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved here. Theorem 1 claims that when all agents are rational type-I agents and this is common knowledge, the non-monotonic-offers protocol (N) is at least as good as the monotonic-offers protocol (M): agents can choose offers that maximize expected gain, and N avoids the risk of getting stuck at a valuation, so the paper argues N does at least as well in expected gain and success rate. Theorem 2 makes the corresponding claim for rational type-II agents, whose acceptance rule also considers whether an offer signals a local optimum. These are analytical protocol-comparison results, not computational results in the required sense. The simulation tables report performance measurements, but those are not numbered complexity results either.

A concrete continuous follow-up would be Aggregate N-versus-M Bargaining Evaluation\(_\infty\). Picture a large market for time-sensitive services, with millions of buyer-seller negotiations but relatively few recurring agent profiles. A buyer type would fully specify its role, valuation over the paper’s discrete periods, deadline, beliefs, and offer and acceptance policy; a seller type would specify the corresponding seller-side information. Rational masses \(\mu^B_b\) and \(\mu^S_s\) describe the fractions of each type, and a rational matching distribution \(\pi_{bs}\) describes the mass of negotiations between each buyer-seller type pair. The large number of agents and small number of types make high multiplicity plausible. This changes only the population scale: time, prices, and negotiation outcomes remain as in the paper.

For each protocol \(q\in\{M,N\}\), the problem is to compute the mass-weighted expected buyer gain and success rate across the matched negotiations, and decide whether N is at least as good as M on both measures. A solution returns those exact aggregate quantities and the comparison verdict. This is a population-evaluation question, not an optimization over offers or outcomes. It uses the finite-horizon, finite-state form of the paper’s bargaining process; with explicitly listed rational policies and transitions, I would expect the evaluation to be tractable by computing each type-pair process and summing by mass.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as an analogue of their protocol comparison: their simulations already aggregate success and surplus across many valuation pairs and strategy combinations, and their formal results compare N and M for agent classes. Under the theorem assumptions for every matched pair, the corresponding individual-level comparisons would carry over to the weighted population totals. That is a modest positive case, rather than a new complexity result. It covers the type-I and type-II protocol comparisons and the aggregate performance measured in the simulations; it does not turn the paper’s results into hardness or algorithmic findings.

The weakest point is that the formal problem in the paper is a two-agent negotiation. Replacing many repeated pairs with a distribution over finitely many types mostly aggregates pairwise behavior, and the main comparison may follow by summing the local results. The resulting continuous question is plausible, but its likely tractability and lack of a complexity anchor make this a weak fit for ChoCo’s central programme. It also raises natural follow-ups—how much mass of misinformed or manipulative agents defeats N’s advantage, and whether protocol choice can improve aggregate performance—but those are new questions, not results established by this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central difficulty is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–7 and Theorems 1–2 are claims about agents’ expected gain, offers, and success under two bargaining protocols. They do not classify the complexity of computing a solution to a problem. The simulations report aggregate performance, but they do not define a computational task either. Calling Theorem 1 or 2 an anchor does not change what kind of result it is.

The strongest plausible mirror is the proponent’s market of repeated bilateral negotiations. That is a sensible high-multiplicity setting: a type can include an agent’s valuation path, deadline, beliefs, and policy, and a matching distribution can specify how much mass of each buyer–seller type pair negotiates. For a fixed distribution \(\pi\), though, the aggregate quantities are just weighted sums of pair-level outcomes:
\[ G_q=\sum_{b,s}\pi_{bs}G_q(b,s),\qquad R_q=\sum_{b,s}\pi_{bs}R_q(b,s), \]
for protocol \(q\). This gives Theorems 1 and 2 a natural aggregate reading, and the simulations already report the corresponding kinds of averages. But the continuum is bookkeeping over independent dyads; the bargaining dynamics and the comparison remain pair-level. With explicitly represented finite-horizon policies and transitions, aggregate evaluation amounts to evaluating those dyads and summing their contributions. That is a reasonable follow-up, but it does not open the kind of complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.

A stronger computational variant might choose which protocol to use for each matched type pair, or choose the matching distribution to maximize aggregate surplus. With fixed pair-level outcomes, those are weighted assignment or linear-optimization problems. The interesting new structure would come from adding market capacities, queues, strategic responses to population composition, or a large policy-synthesis problem. The paper mentions queuing and manipulative agents, but does not model those interactions. Adding them could lead to worthwhile research; it would make the market or strategic-design problem the new subject, rather than provide a continuous mirror of the paper’s results.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit: neither theorem supplies a computational anchor, and the best population version aggregates isolated negotiations. It is weaker as a universal modeling claim. A repeated service market with recurring agent profiles is plausible, and the authors could recognize aggregate protocol evaluation as related to their work. I cannot rule out a worthwhile continuous study in every scenario; I can argue that the plausible ones either stop at aggregation or require introducing the coupled computational problem the paper itself does not study.
