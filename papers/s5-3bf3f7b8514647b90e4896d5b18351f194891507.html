The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), per-unit endowments \(b_t\), valuations \(v_t\), a shared production matrix \(A\), and a feasible proposal \((x^*,u^*)\) with \(Ax^*\leq\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t b_t\) and \(u_t^*\leq v_t^\top x^*\), decide whether there exist masses \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), not all zero, and a design \(y\geq0\) such that \(Ay\leq\sum_{t\in T}q_t b_t\) and \(v_t^\top y>u_t^*\) for every \(t\) with \(q_t>0\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution over complete stakeholder types, each carrying mass and per-unit resources and valuations. A blocking coalition selects positive mass from some types and pools its resources to choose a common public-goods design. Since each type is internally identical, any selected positive mass can be expanded to its full mass, so the blocking test reduces to enumerating nonempty type supports and solving a linear feasibility problem for each.

The objection that survived

The plausible federation may have few exact repeats once jurisdiction, budget authority, geographic access, and coalition rights are included in each type; the hardness reduction itself uses a growing set of distinct types.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Continuous NTU-LP Core Membership. It keeps the paper’s central decision question: can a proposed public-goods outcome withstand a coalition that pools its resources and chooses a design all its members strictly prefer?

Take a finite support \(T\) of complete stakeholder types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). A type \(t\) records its per-unit resource endowment \(b_t\), valuation vector \(v_t\), and any other characteristics relevant to coalition formation. The production matrix \(A\) and menu of public goods are shared. The proposed outcome consists of a feasible design \(x^*\), with \(Ax^*\leq \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t b_t\), and utility levels \(u_t^*\leq v_t^\top x^*\).

A coalition chooses masses \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), not all zero, and a common design \(y\geq 0\). It blocks the proposal if \(Ay\leq\sum_t q_t b_t\) and \(v_t^\top y>u_t^*\) for every type with \(q_t>0\). The problem is to decide whether a block exists; a blocking vector \(q\) and design \(y\) witness instability. This preserves the paper’s pooled-resource production model, public-goods design, and strict-improvement notion of objection.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a regional transit consortium with many small, separately organized municipalities or service districts. Their shared decision is still a transit service plan; their resource endowments and valuations describe contributions and access to the menu of routes. Districts with identical endowments, route valuations, and coalition rights belong to the same type, and their mass is their share of the consortium. The paper’s Chicago case itself has only 77 regions, so it is not this regime. But its player interpretation—regions pooling budgets and potentially organizing separately—carries over directly to a larger federation with many repeated stakeholder profiles.

My sole anchor, and the lead, is Theorem 3.9, proved in this paper. It states that, for a balanced NTU LP game represented by \(A,\{b_i\},\{v_i\}\), deciding whether a given \(u^*\in U(N)\) lies in the core is co-NP-complete, even when there is one resource, \(A\) is a row of ones, every player has \(b_i=1\) and \(v_i\geq0\), and every coordinate of \(u^*\) equals \(1\). The paper proves hardness by reduction from three-dimensional matching.

That anchor transfers to the mass model. For one of the theorem’s hard instances, let each paper player \(i\) become a type of mass \(1/|N|\), with the same valuation and unit endowment. Normalize the original design and utilities by \(|N|\); the proposed utility for each type is then \(1/|N|\). Every discrete blocking coalition gives a mass blocker by selecting the full mass of its types and scaling its design by \(1/|N|\). Conversely, if a mass blocker selects any positive amount of a type, extend it to that type’s full mass: the extra members have identical valuations and add resources, so the same design remains feasible and benefits them too. Scaling the design back by \(|N|\) gives a discrete blocking coalition. Thus the yes/no core-membership answers coincide on these instances, and the continuous problem is co-NP-hard. The nonnegative valuations also keep the instances in the paper’s balanced-game setting.

I expect hardness to transfer (Class B), because the reduction’s combinatorics are encoded in the type and good vectors. Divisible mass does not erase them: with identical agents within each type and pooled resources, any blocking support can be expanded to whole type masses. There is also an immediate parameterized direction. Enumerate each nonempty support \(S\subseteq T\) and solve an LP maximizing \(\epsilon\), subject to \(Ay\leq\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t b_t\) and \(v_t^\top y\geq u_t^*+\epsilon\) for \(t\in S\). A positive optimum detects a block, giving a \(2^\tau\operatorname{poly}(m,K,L)\) algorithm under the paper’s boundedness assumptions. So the mirror is co-NP-complete when support size varies, but fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau=|T|\).

This covers the paper’s core-membership hardness result only. It does not claim to mirror the cutting-plane implementation, the case study, or the structural non-emptiness results as computational complexity results. Further questions include whether transit-specific structure in the valuation vectors yields algorithms substantially better than support enumeration, and how other core variants change when coalitions can select only part of a type.

The weakest point is the regime’s exact-type requirement: many districts must share the same resource and valuation profiles, while real geography may give each district a distinct profile. Also, the reduction’s support grows with its 3DM instance, so it establishes neither hardness for fixed \(\tau\) nor an advantage from high multiplicity alone. The case survives as a faithful, narrow mirror: the paper’s coalition-stability question remains meaningful for a mass society, and its named hardness result transfers to that question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 3.9, and it is a strong one: the paper names a core-membership problem and proves it co-NP-complete. The best negative case is about whether a continuous population preserves the paper’s players and coalition rights.

In the paper, a player is a politically organized region. It controls a pooled budget and can join a coalition that proposes a different service plan. Recasting riders as the population would give individual residents the ability to take a pro-rata share of regional fare revenue and form their own service coalition. That is a substantial institutional change: residents do not individually control that budget or commission routes. To preserve the paper’s object, type would need to include jurisdiction, budget authority, geographic access, and coalition rights. In its Chicago application, those features make the 77 regions distinct. A larger federation of municipalities is a better candidate, but identical route valuations and budgets across many geographically distinct municipalities would be a demanding fit. This is the strongest objection I can make; it challenges the motivating regime, not the mathematical model’s possibility.

The theorem’s reduction also does not establish hardness specifically driven by repeated types: its node and edge players become distinct types, each with mass \(1/|N|\). The scaling argument appears correct, though. Any partial mass of a selected type can be expanded to its full mass without losing feasibility or strict improvement, so the continuous instance reproduces the discrete blocking-coalition question. That is a valid discrete-to-continuous identity, which the programme explicitly treats as a possible positive result. The fact that the reduction uses distinct types weakens the high-multiplicity motivation for this particular hardness result, but it does not defeat the mirror.

I found nearby work on cores with a continuum of players, but it studies a different coalition model and existence questions rather than this computational membership classification ([Kaneko and Wooders, “The core of a game with a continuum of players and finite coalitions”](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-4896(86)90032-6)). So “already done continuously” is not a sound objection here.

The honest negative case is therefore weak. It can question whether the paper’s institutional setting supplies a convincing high-multiplicity regime; it cannot rule out a larger federation of repeated stakeholder types, and it cannot defeat the formal continuous mirror of Theorem 3.9.
