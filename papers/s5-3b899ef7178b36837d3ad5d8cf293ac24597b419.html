The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), binary valuations \(v_{tI},v_{tj}\) for one pool of identical indivisible items and \(J\) divisible resource categories, and rational per-capita supplies \(q_I,q_j\), find feasible measures \(P_t\) over bundles \((k,d)\), where \(k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) and \(d\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^J\). Require \(P_t\) to have mass \(\mu_t\), require \(k=0\) or \(d_j=0\) whenever the type does not value that resource, and impose \(\sum_t\int k\,dP_t=q_I\) and \(\sum_t\int d_j\,dP_t=q_j\) for each \(j\). Minimize \(\sum_t\int (v_{tI}k+\sum_j v_{tj}d_j)^2\,dP_t\).

The model it lives in

The population is represented by type masses \(\mu_t\); each person’s bundle keeps an integral item count \(k\), while divisible amounts \(d_j\) and per-capita supplies scale with the population. The objective is the mass-weighted square of individual utility, not the square of type-average utility.

What the mirror covers

The mirror specializes Theorem 6.1 to square-sum fairness with identical indivisible items. It leaves the theorem’s full range of Φ, the MNW and egalitarian hardness results, and the algorithm for identical divisible goods unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can this problem be solved in time polynomial in τ, \(J\), and the input bit length?
  • Can a finite configuration formulation be given with a tractable pricing step?
  • How do solutions compare with finite cohorts whose type counts and supplies realize the rational masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I would defend is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s mixed-goods fairness problem. Picture a large public agency allocating standardized equipment and divisible support resources, such as training hours, across a much larger workforce. Workers fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of eligibility and role types. A type records the worker’s complete binary valuation profile: whether its members value the pool of identical indivisible items, and whether they value each divisible resource category. The number of workers \(N\) is large relative to \(\tau\).

Normalize the population mass to one. The input gives each type \(t\) a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and gives each resource category a rational per-capita supply. For the indivisible pool, every individual must receive an integer number \(k\geq 0\) of items. For divisible category \(j\), an individual may receive an amount \(d_j\geq 0\). Both are allowed only when the type values that resource. Supplies scale with the cohort: a per-capita indivisible supply \(q_I\) means \(Nq_I\) identical copies in an \(N\)-person realization.

An allocation is a family of measures \(P_t\) over individual bundles \((k,d)\), with total mass \(\mu_t\) for type \(t\). The measures let a type’s population be split among different bundles, while every individual bundle still has integral \(k\). Require \(\sum_t\int k\,dP_t=q_I\) and \(\sum_t\int d_j\,dP_t=q_j\) for every divisible category \(j\). If \(v_{tI}\) and \(v_{tj}\) are the binary values, utility is \(u_t(k,d)=v_{tI}k+\sum_jv_{tj}d_j\).

My lead question is Continuum Mixed-Goods Square-Sum Fair Allocation: given those rational masses, supplies, and binary type valuations, find feasible measures \(P_t\) minimizing \(\sum_t\int u_t(k,d)^2\,dP_t\). The objective counts the utilities of individuals across the population; it does not replace each type’s utilities by its average. In a finite realization, this is the paper’s square-sum objective up to a positive normalization factor.

The anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper by reduction from 3-dimensional matching. Its full claim is that, for any fixed symmetric strictly convex function \(\Phi\), finding a \(\Phi\)-minimizer is NP-hard even when the indivisible goods are identical, and hence finding a \(\Phi\)-fair allocation is NP-hard. Here “identical” means that each agent either values every indivisible good or values none of them. The square-sum objective is one fixed symmetric strictly convex choice, so this mirror asks what happens to a concrete member of the theorem’s family.

I expect this continuous problem to be Class A, at least when the number of types is moderate. The continuum lets each type’s mass divide among integral bundles, so the optimization can be expressed over bundle configurations. For the square-sum objective, the pricing question appears to depend on an individual’s total utility and the integer count \(k\); divisible resources contribute through their dual prices. That suggests a configuration LP or convex-optimization route. This is a forecast, not a consequence of Theorem 6.1: the pricing argument and polynomial bit complexity still need to be proved.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem. It retains the mixed divisible and indivisible goods, binary additive utility, and fairness objective. It changes the population to a distribution over complete valuation types and scales supplies with the population, as a high-multiplicity version requires. The indivisible constraint remains at the individual level, even though a fraction of a type’s mass may receive each bundle.

The mirror covers Theorem 6.1’s identical-indivisible-goods hardness, specialized to square-sum fairness. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s full range of \(\Phi\), its MNW and egalitarian results, or its polynomial-time result for identical divisible goods. Its weakest point is also the key reason to ask the question: the 3DM reduction uses many element- and hyperedge-specific agents and relies on indivisible assignment choices. In the continuous model, mass of one type can split across bundles, which may erase the reduction’s integral matching structure. The theorem therefore motivates this mirror but does not establish continuous hardness, nor does its reduction show hardness when \(N\) greatly exceeds \(\tau\).

Further questions follow naturally: whether the conjectured algorithm is polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of divisible categories, and the input bit length; whether its pricing step is actually tractable; and how its answer compares with large finite cohorts, both for exact solutions and additive approximation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that Theorem 6.1’s hardness construction does not establish a high-multiplicity regime. Its agents are mostly distinguished by their incidence in the 3DM instance: each hyperedge agent has its own divisible goods, and each element agent has its own good. The four auxiliary agents attached to a hyperedge share a profile, but that gives only constant multiplicity. In the reduction, the number of valuation types grows with the number of agents. So the theorem’s NP-hardness is not evidence that the problem remains hard, or even that its reduction has a useful limit, when many people share a small set of types.

That leaves a gap in the proponent’s motivation: the agency example is plausible, but it supplies a new application rather than showing that the paper’s hard instances have a continuous-population counterpart. The authors’ NP-hardness result alone cannot carry that argument.

But this does not defeat the better mirror. Give each type its complete binary valuation profile over the goods, let mass of a type split among bundles with an integral number of indivisible items per individual, scale supplies per capita, and minimize the mass-weighted square of individual utility. This preserves the distribution of individual utilities; it does not substitute type-average utility for individual utility. For finite realizations, the objective agrees with square-sum fairness up to normalization. The model is coherent, and a large population sharing a modest number of profiles is a sensible regime for standardized goods and services.

The lost matching structure in the paper’s reduction cannot serve as a deeper objection: whether it survives is an answer to the continuous question, not a reason the question is unworthy. Nor does the full-profile type space invalidate the mirror; it may make the paper’s reduction large in \(\tau\), but it still allows other instances with many people per type.

So I can make a case that the paper does not itself establish the mirror’s relevance, and that the proposed complexity forecast is speculative. I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. This anchor survives the strongest version of the objection.
