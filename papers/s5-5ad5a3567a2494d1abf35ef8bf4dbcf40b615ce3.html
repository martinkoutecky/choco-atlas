The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here, but the paper has no named computational result of the kind requested. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, characterizes the composition extension operator by four axioms: baseline truncation, truncation of excessive claims, baseline invariance, and polar baseline equivalence. Theorem 2 proves preservation of resource monotonicity, consistency, and resource-population uniformity; Theorem 3 proves preservation of specified pairs of monotonicity properties. These are axiomatic results, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. So none can honestly serve as a computational anchor.

The strongest plausible population regime is a large claims-adjudication process with many claimants but relatively few combinations of claim and baseline—for example, customers or creditors grouped into standardized entitlement tiers. A type would include every feature the rule uses, at least the claimant’s claim \(c_t\) and baseline \(b_t\). Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of claimants of that type. With total population normalized to one, let \(e\) be the per-capita endowment and require \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t=e\), where \(x_t\) is the award per claimant of type \(t\). This is sensible when the number of claimants is large and the number of distinct types \(\tau\) is small in comparison. It is a property of this regime, not of rationing problems in general.

A precise theorem-level mirror—not a computational anchor—would be the High-Multiplicity Composition Characterization. Its instances are finite-support rational distributions \(\mu\), claims \(c_t\), baselines \(b_t\), and per-capita endowment \(e\), with \(0\le e\le\sum_t\mu_t c_t\). Write \(q_t=\min\{b_t,c_t\}\) and \(Q=\sum_t\mu_tq_t\). For a weighted standard rationing rule \(R\), the proposed continuous operator returns
\[ R^c(\mu,b,c,e)= \begin{cases} R(\mu,q,e),&e\le Q,\\ q+R(\mu,c-q,e-Q),&e\ge Q. \end{cases} \]
The question is whether a rule \(S\) on these weighted instances satisfies the four corresponding type-level axioms if and only if it has this form for some standard rule \(R\). A solution would be a proof of that equivalence. The direct analogue of Theorem 1 should hold, though defining the weighted rule domain and its axioms carefully is part of the formulation. Because this is a characterization of rules rather than a complexity problem, it does not fall into the programme’s tractable/hardness trichotomy. It would prompt further questions about how compactly \(R\) is represented and the cost of evaluating \(R^c\) as a function of \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length.

The main weakness is that the proposed mirror continues the paper’s axiomatic question, not its computational analysis: the paper supplies no computational analysis to continue. Its allocation is already a divisible good, and applying the operator is a two-stage rule construction. Thus the population-continuous formulation is plausible and the authors could recognize it as an analogue of their central theorem, but this paper does not provide a named computational result on which to base the requested complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to continue. Theorem 1 characterizes an operator by axioms; Theorems 2 and 3 prove preservation of axioms. None asks for an algorithm, defines an input encoding, or establishes a complexity bound. That leaves no computational anchor for ChoCo’s tractability and hardness programme.

The proposed High-Multiplicity Composition Characterization is a sensible weighted reformulation, and its claims-adjudication setting is plausible. But its formula simply applies a weighted base rule on either side of the threshold \(e=Q\). The original proof already proceeds by those cases and the axioms. For an arbitrary rule \(R\), there is no representation from which to ask how long evaluation takes; for a specified, computable rule, the cost is inherited from that rule. The characterization adds no pricing problem, optimization task, or complexity question of the kind the programme is meant to chart.

The same problem applies to mirrors of Theorems 2 and 3: one can restate their monotonicity and consistency claims for masses, but they remain axiomatic preservation results. A more careful lift would also need to specify anonymity and invariance to splitting or merging types, since the paper allows rules on labelled populations and does not itself establish that outcomes depend only on type masses. Those additions may be reasonable, but they define a new axiomatic question rather than uncover a computational one in this paper.

So I would reject the proposed mirror as a ChoCo priority, not because large rationing populations are implausible or because divisible awards count as continuous populations. The honest limit of the negative case is that a weighted axiomatic extension could still be worthwhile to researchers in rationing theory. What the paper does not offer is a computational question for the continuization programme to answer.
