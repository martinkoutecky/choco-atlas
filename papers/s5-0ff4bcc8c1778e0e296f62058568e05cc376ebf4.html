The case FOR (proponent)

On the required anchor rule, this paper gives me no qualifying named computational result. Theorem 5.5, proved here, states that the order induced by the prescriptive CP-net matches the paper’s preference model for the norms. Proposition 5.6, also proved here, shows that a specified contrary-to-duty framework can be represented by a consistent CP-net. Both are semantic results, not complexity results.

The closest computational hook is the conclusion’s unnumbered discussion: it says that CP-net dominance and consistency can be NP-complete, and that dominance is linear for binary-variable tree networks and polynomial for polytrees, citing earlier work [2, 9]. Those claims suggest a mirror, but they are not numbered results of this paper, so they cannot serve as the requested anchors.

The strongest positive lead I can offer is Mass-Majority CP-Net Dominance. Fix a common set \(V\) of binary features. An instance consists of a finite support \(T\) of CP-net types over \(V\), each fully specified by its dependency graph and conditional preference tables, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and two outcomes \(u,v\). Each type is a complete preference description shared by the agents of that type; \(\mu_t\) is their fraction of the population. The question is whether
\[ \sum_{t:\,u\prec_{\mathrm{Ord}_{N_t}}v}\mu_t>\frac12. \]
Return yes exactly when more than half the population’s own CP-nets say that \(v\) dominates \(u\).

This is a plausible population-computational extension of the paper’s concern with representing a community’s preferences and testing dominance. Its expected general classification is hardness transfers: with support size \(\tau=1\), it reduces to ordinary CP-net dominance, whose NP-completeness the paper cites. The relevant combinatorics lie in the number of features \(m\), so replacing a single network by a population distribution does not remove that hardness. The paper’s cited tree and polytree cases also suggest a tractable regime: if every supported type has one of those graph structures, test dominance for each type and sum the corresponding rational masses. Natural follow-up questions include the complexity as a function of \(\tau\), graph structure, and mass bit length, and whether the majority relation can be represented compactly despite possible cycles.

The fit has a serious weakness: the paper’s CP-net represents a community’s normative preference model directly; it does not model a population of individuals with separate CP-nets or choose a majority aggregation rule. Mass-majority dominance adds that social-choice layer, so the authors might accept it as a follow-on population problem but not as the continuous form of a problem they actually studied. It also does not supply a valid numbered anchor under your criteria. Thus the positive case is a credible research lead, not an anchored mirror of this paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 5.5 is a semantic correspondence between norms and a CP-net; Proposition 5.6 shows that a contrary-to-duty framework can be represented consistently. Neither studies an algorithm or complexity. The conclusion’s claims about dominance and consistency are summaries of results cited from earlier work, not results established here.

The model’s exponential dimension is over features and possible worlds, not over a population of agents. A CP-net represents one community’s prescriptive preference model. The paper does not give individuals separate CP-nets or specify how to aggregate them. That distinction defeats the proposed Mass-Majority CP-Net Dominance mirror: it adds a population of people with personal CP-nets and a majority rule over their judgments. A more elaborate version that aggregates whole norm systems would still need to invent rules for reconciling conflicting prescriptions, indifference, and missing preferences. Those could be worthwhile research questions, but they are new aggregation problems, not continuous versions of a computational problem this paper studies.

A civic setting could make such an extension plausible: residents might hold different CP-net views, and a decision maker might aggregate them into public policy. But that scenario still supplies the missing social-choice layer from outside the paper. The proposed majority question is well-posed; its plausibility does not make it an anchored mirror. The negative case is strongest on that point. I cannot rule out an independently valuable project on populations of CP-net holders, but the paper itself gives no computational result for that project to continuize.
