The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying anchor under your rule. Its numbered sections report experiments, and Tables 1–3 give measured model performance, but it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. The deferred-acceptance algorithms are numbered, but they are procedures, not named complexity results of the required kind. So I cannot honestly make an anchor-based case or claim that a proposed question mirrors one of the paper’s formal results.

The strongest positive lead I can offer is a follow-up to the paper’s task of computing the proposer-optimal stable matching. Imagine a large labor market in which many workers share the same preference ranking over job categories, and many positions share the same ranking over worker categories. A type is the complete preference description over those categories; its mass is the fraction of the workforce or positions of that type. There are many individual workers and positions, but relatively few types. This is a different regime from the paper’s randomly generated individual rankings. Its Master-list experiments show interest in structured preferences, but do not themselves establish that the paper’s instances have small type support.

Call the proposed problem High-Multiplicity Proposer-Optimal Matching. An instance consists of finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) summing to one on each side, and a strict ranking of \(B\)-types for each \(a\)-type and of \(A\)-types for each \(b\)-type. The question is to compute a proposer-optimal stable mass matching: a matrix \(x_{ab}\geq 0\) with row sums \(\mu_a\) and column sums \(\nu_b\), such that no positive mass of an \(a\)-type and a \(b\)-type both prefer each other to their assigned types. “Proposer-optimal” means that, among stable matrices, each proposer type receives a distribution of partners that weakly stochastically dominates its distribution in every other stable matrix. The matrix is the solution; its fractional entries represent portions of cohorts matched across type pairs, while each unit of population still gets one partner.

I expect this problem to be tractable in \(\tau=|A|+|B|\) and the input bit length \(L\): a type-level deferred-acceptance procedure can propose and retain masses, with rejections moving to less-preferred types. That is a conjectured complexity direction for the proposed question, not a result of this paper. It is close to the authors’ underlying task—computing the proposer-optimal stable outcome—and the paper’s discussion of worker–task markets and preference ties makes the setting recognizable as a matching-market extension. The paper’s results about LLM accuracy do not transfer as complexity claims, though; this proposal mirrors the problem being benchmarked, not a theorem the paper proves.

The weakest point is that compressing agents into types changes the preference model: members of a type rank categories, rather than having distinct strict rankings over named individuals. The mirror therefore needs a convincing market where category-level preferences are genuinely the relevant data, such as a large cohort-to-role market. It should be judged as a plausible high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s central stable-matching task, not as an anchor-compliant case based on its named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror begins with the proponent’s own concession: the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its tables report LLM performance on benchmark tasks; they do not establish complexity bounds or algorithms for a computational problem. The deferred-acceptance procedure and its runtime appear as background. Under the requested anchor criterion, there are no qualifying anchors.

The proponent’s candidate—computing a proposer-optimal stable matching for worker and job types—is a plausible research question, but it is an extension of the paper’s setting. The paper gives each agent a strict ranking over named individuals. The proposed model ranks categories, lets a cohort split across partner types, and represents the outcome by a mass matrix \(x\). Those choices may be sensible for a cohort-to-role market, and the programme permits co-modifications required by continuization. They still change the preference domain and the matching object that the paper evaluates.

There is a fork here. Keeping the paper’s contribution means studying how LLMs perform on matching tasks; an aggregate-input benchmark would remain an empirical evaluation, not the complexity question ChoCo is organized around. Turning the candidate into a ChoCo complexity problem means studying stable mass matching, whose connection to the paper is its subject matter rather than one of its results. The paper’s measured LLM failures do not establish anything about the complexity of that problem.

I would not argue that cohort matching is an implausible regime, or that fractional mass outcomes are disqualifying. The proposed market could be worthwhile on its own, and a careful definition of stability and proposer-optimality may make it precise. That is the weak point in a universal negative: the material cannot rule out every worthwhile extension. The stronger conclusion is narrower: the proposed candidate does not supply a mirror of a named result, and the paper itself offers no qualifying anchor.
