The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 6

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 6 — survived: The atomless version retains the paper's truthful PF-allocation objective over a population distribution, although price taking removes SDM's finite-agent strategic effect.

The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) divisible items with unit supply, a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) additive valuation types \(v_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^m\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute a direct allocation rule for unit-budget agents in the atomless society. Require \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le1\), dominant-strategy truthfulness for an individual agent, and maximize the minimum represented-type ratio \(v_t\cdot x_t/(v_t\cdot x_t^*(\mu))\), where \(x^*(\mu)\) is the weighted PF allocation; determine the algorithmic complexity in \(m,\tau,L\).

The model it lives in

An atomless equal-income market over additive types: solve the weighted Eisenberg–Gale PF program on masses \(\mu_t\), obtain equilibrium prices, and assign each report a utility-maximizing bundle under budget \(1\). A single agent has zero mass and cannot move those prices; each represented type's truthful PF bundle is therefore best among budget-feasible reported bundles, with feasibility weighted by \(\mu_t\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6's SDM guarantee for additive valuations and equal budgets in a high-demand regime; it leaves aside Theorem 3's finite-population lower bound and the Partial Allocation results for broader homogeneous valuations.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an exact mechanism with dominant-strategy truthfulness be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and what bit-complexity assumptions are needed for its equilibrium prices and bundles?
  • Can approximate PF computation retain exact dominant-strategy truthfulness, or only approximate truthfulness?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named NP-hardness or complexity-class theorem in this paper. The best anchor is its named algorithmic mechanism result, Theorem 6, the Strong Demand Matching theorem. Proved here, it says that for additive linear valuations with equal unit budgets, SDM is truthful and guarantees every bidder at least \(\min_j p_j^*/\lceil p_j^*\rceil\) of their Proportionally Fair (PF) value, where \(p_j^*\) is item \(j\)’s PF price. In particular, if \(k=\min_j p_j^*\), the guarantee is at least \(k/(k+1)\).

A credible regime is a very large population competing for a fixed set of divisible resources: the paper’s privatization example has many citizens and 1,491 firms. Imagine that many citizens share one of a modest number \(\tau\) of complete valuation profiles over the firms. A type is that valuation vector together with the common unit budget; its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of citizens with that profile. Thus \(N\) can be far larger than both \(m\) and \(\tau\). The paper itself motivates SDM with bidders outnumbering items and high PF prices. This mirror keeps that problem—truthfully allocating resources to approximate PF—and makes the population, rather than the goods, the continuous object.

My proposed question is Atomless PF Truthfulness. Its input is \(m\) items, a finite set \(T\) of additive valuation types \(v_t\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^m\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\geq0\) summing to one. Each unit of mass has unit scrip budget. For \(N\) citizens and one physical unit of each item, use bundles scaled by \(N\); then feasibility is \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\leq1\), and the PF benchmark is

\[ x^*(\mu)\in\arg\max_{x\geq0}\left\{\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log(v_t\cdot x_t): \sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\leq1\ \text{for every }j\right\}. \]

The question is to compute a direct allocation mechanism for this society that maximizes the worst type’s ratio \(v_t\cdot x_t/(v_t\cdot x_t^*)\), subject to feasibility and dominant-strategy truthfulness for an individual agent. A solution is a bundle for every reported type, with a proof of feasibility and truthfulness and a guarantee on that ratio.

The expected answer is Class A, with ratio \(1\). Compute equal-income market prices \(p\) for the mass distribution and, for each report \(r\), give the agent a bundle \(x_r\) that maximizes \(v_r\cdot x\) subject to \(p\cdot x\leq1\). For types present in the society, choose these demands jointly so the market clears. In the atomless model, one agent’s report does not change the aggregate distribution or prices. A truthful type \(t\) receives a PF bundle that maximizes its value among all bundles with that budget; every other report also receives a budget-feasible bundle. So a lie cannot improve its value. The resulting allocation is PF, giving ratio \(1\), which is optimal. Computationally, the PF allocation and prices come from the weighted Eisenberg–Gale convex program, with size governed by \(m,\tau,L\); approximate convex optimization is the natural tractability route. Exact bit complexity and the incentive guarantee under approximate prices would need to be pinned down.

This matches Theorem 6’s rounding effect: for fixed normalized prices \(p_j>0\), the corresponding finite-population prices grow as \(p_j^{*,N}=Np_j\). Hence \(\min_j p_j^{*,N}/\lceil p_j^{*,N}\rceil\) tends to \(1\). In the continuum, the integer-capacity rounding disappears. The goods remain divisible; what has changed is that a type’s mass is allocated using market prices, and one agent no longer moves those prices.

The soft spot is also that last point. The proposed truthfulness is price-taking truthfulness in an atomless population. The finite-agent strategic effect that SDM handles with its price increases and integer capacities has vanished. The paper’s Theorem 3, which rules out guarantees above \((n+1)/(2n)+\epsilon\) for truthful mechanisms on all \(n\)-bidder additive instances, does not refute this fixed-type limit: its construction uses \(n\) distinct valuation patterns and a number of items growing with \(n\). But it does mark a boundary. This mirror covers Theorem 6’s additive, equal-budget, high-demand setting; it does not claim to mirror Theorem 3 or the Partial Allocation results for broader valuations. The further question is how quickly the price-taking result becomes a truthful finite-population approximation as \(N\) grows, especially when \(\tau\) grows too.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6 is the right anchor, and the proposed high-demand population is plausible. The strongest case against it is that its atomless limit removes the feature that makes SDM distinctive. With fixed type proportions, finite-population item prices scale as \(p_j^{(N)}=N p_j\). The integer capacity rule \(\lfloor p_j^{(N)}\rfloor\) then has only an \(O(1/N)\) effect per unit of population. In the atomless model there are no integer bidder slots to round at all. That is a genuine degeneration of SDM’s one-bidder-per-item construction.

The proposed replacement, price-taking PF truthfulness, is coherent, but it changes the strategic problem. A single agent’s report leaves the type distribution and market prices unchanged; truthfulness reduces to choosing the most valuable bundle from a fixed budget set. Continuum-economy theory has long studied this structure: Hammond and Córdoba describe how negligible individual influence makes Walrasian mechanisms strategy-proof in atomless economies. That is evidence of a shift into standard price-taking mechanism design, not a continuous-computation collision: their result does not supply the \(m,\tau,L\) algorithmic classification ChoCo seeks. But here the paper already gives the PF convex program and says how to compute it for affine valuations. The proposed mirror’s computational core is therefore the paper’s existing PF subroutine applied to weighted type masses, while its truthfulness comes from the atomless price-taking assumption. ([Hammond and Córdoba, “Asymptotically Strategy-Proof Walrasian Exchange”](https://web.stanford.edu/~hammond/cordham.pdf))

The best attempt to preserve SDM’s strategic content would ask how its truthful finite mechanisms behave when a single report changes the population by \(1/N\), or would give selected agents positive mass inside an atomless background. The first retains \(N\) and becomes a finite-population convergence question. The second makes the strategic part finite or turns a whole type cohort into a coalition. Neither is the proposed atomless, individual-truthfulness mirror; each puts the key strategic effect back into atoms.

I would argue against prioritizing this as a ChoCo mirror because the clean continuum version loses SDM’s distinctive incentive and rounding problem, while a version that preserves that problem must retain finite strategic mass. The case is not airtight: the weighted PF computation is still a well-posed computational question, and the authors may regard truthful allocation to a large population as their problem. So the strongest honest conclusion is a serious scope objection, not proof that no worthwhile mirror exists.
