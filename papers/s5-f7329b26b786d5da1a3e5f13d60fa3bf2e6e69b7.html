The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in the paper to mirror. Corollary 2 establishes the \(1/3\) lower bound on worst-case deviation; Example 1 shows it is tight. The propositions and other corollaries establish mathematical constraints on division functions. None gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or a computational problem with an input representation and parameters.

The proposed population of repeated three-agent teams does not make those results computational. Let a team’s full peer-evaluation profile be its type, with mass \(\mu_r\). If the objective remains the paper’s worst-case deviation over all consistent profiles, \(\mu\) has no effect: the same lower bound applies, and the same rule \(F\) attains it. If each team gets its own rule, applying \(F\) to each team also leaves no population-level optimization to solve.

A stronger version would give the profiles a distribution and ask for an exact, impartial rule minimizing average deviation on that distribution. That could be a sensible extension, especially given the paper’s observation that \(F\) does better on most profiles. But it changes the paper’s worst-case objective to a prior-weighted one. The paper neither defines that problem nor studies its computation; its algorithmic questions would depend on how the distribution and the class of allowed rules are represented. It is a possible new project, not a continuous mirror of Corollary 2 or Example 1.

Scaling a single project beyond three agents is a more ambitious route. Peer evaluations are relational: each report compares particular associates, and each agent’s payment must remain independent of that agent’s own report. A type model that merges people must preserve those relationships and the consistency conditions among reports. In a nonatomic limit, an individual report has measure zero, so impartiality may become vacuous unless the model retains pointwise relational information. Retaining it requires a new model and new results; the paper’s three-agent bounds do not transfer by simply replacing agents with mass.

The paper’s fixed dollar is divisible, but its population remains three identified people. Repeating the project makes the *number of projects* large while leaving each allocation problem unchanged. The honest weakness in this negative case is that a distribution-aware or large-team extension might be worth studying on its own. The case against a worthwhile continuous mirror is strong because that extension would supply a new computational question, rather than continuize a computational question this paper actually answers.
