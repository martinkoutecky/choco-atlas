Why no mirror

Theorem 2.3 is a clear computational result, but its individual incentive condition relies on a player changing the reported profile. In the atomless limit, a unilateral deviation changes no mass; the proposed repair instead makes a whole type cohort a strategic actor and adds a mass-level incentive requirement. Shared types establish high multiplicity, but do not by themselves establish collective control, so the proposed mirror does not survive the recognition test.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is Theorem 2.3, proved in this paper. It says that for multiparty set union with any number \(n\) of all-or-nothing players—each either submits their whole set or abstains—there is a mechanism that is truthful, Pareto efficient, and welfare maximizing, and that runs in polynomial time for every fixed \(n\). The paper contains no named hardness result, so this positive algorithmic result is the right anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a data-sharing consortium with many contributors holding recurring, validated packets of information: for example, incident reports or local measurements drawn from a finite catalogue \(U\). Let \(S\subseteq U\) be a complete type: its members hold the same packet and have the same preferences over information. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with support size \(\tau\). It is credible for the number of contributors to be very large while the number of distinct packets remains moderate. Each recipient still gets a whole set of information; it is the population mass that is continuous.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: Mass Set-Union Mechanism Design. An instance is a finite universe \(U\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) supported on \(\tau\) sets \(S\subseteq U\). Each member of type \(S\) may submit \(S\) or abstain. A report profile is a subdistribution \(\rho\leq\mu\), where \(\rho_S\) is the mass of type \(S\) that submits. The mechanism sees only submitted sets and their masses, not the true types of abstainers. For each submitted type \(S\), it returns a bundle \(Y_S(\rho)\) to participating members, with
\[ S\subseteq Y_S(\rho)\subseteq \bigcup_{R:\rho_R>0}R. \]
Members of the same submitted type receive the same bundle, as in the paper’s symmetric mechanism. Their information gain is \(g_i=|Y_S(\rho)\setminus S|\); abstainers gain zero. Utilities retain the paper’s exclusivity term:
\[ u_i=g_i-\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{j\ne i}g_j. \]

The mechanism is a rule computed from the observed report masses, not from hidden \(\mu\). For the continuous model, define truthful participation at the type-mass level: for every true \(\mu\), type \(S\), and feasible reports by other types, having all of type \(S\)’s mass submit must maximize that cohort’s average individual utility over every possible participation mass in \([0,\mu_S]\). At the truthful profile \(\rho=\mu\), the outcome must be Pareto efficient, and must maximize total information gain
\[ W(\mu)=\sum_{S\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_S\,|Y_S(\mu)\setminus S| \]
among outcomes of such truthful mechanisms. The computational question is whether a rule with these properties can be evaluated in time polynomial in \(|U|,\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\). A solution is a compact mechanism that returns the bundles for each observed report profile.

I would tentatively expect a Class A answer when \(\tau\) is moderate: the inputs are sets, rewards count new elements, and the outcome can be expressed through type-by-element allocation choices. That suggests looking for a compact optimization formulation or a pricing method. This is a hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 2.3: its runtime is polynomial for fixed *number of players*, and it does not establish a polynomial bound in continuous support size or mass bit length. Further questions include whether the discrete and mass versions have a two-way dictionary, how complexity changes when \(\tau\) varies, and whether the mass constraints admit a compact formulation.

The soft spot is the incentive notion. In an atomless population, one person’s unilateral deviation does not change aggregate reports, so ordinary individual dominant-strategy truthfulness can lose the externality that makes the paper’s problem interesting. The question above preserves that tension by allowing a positive mass of identical agents to change its participation rate, and by retaining the paper’s envy-based utility. This is a meaningful co-modification for cohorts governed by a shared data-sharing policy, but it is stronger than the paper’s unilateral truthfulness guarantee. The authors may accept it as a high-multiplicity extension; they may instead insist that only individual deviations count. That is the main risk to this mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2.3 is the strongest anchor: it gives a named polynomial-time mechanism, and repeated set types are plausible in a data-sharing setting. The best case against a mirror rests on what happens to its incentive condition in an atomless population.

A single member’s choice between submitting \(S\) and abstaining changes the reported mass by zero. If the mechanism depends on the report distribution, that choice leaves everyone else’s allocation—and the essential supremum of their gains—unchanged. The member’s own gain is \(g\geq 0\) when participating and \(0\) when abstaining, so participation changes utility by exactly \(g\). Thus truthful participation is weakly dominant for any rule that gives submitters nonnegative information gains. The individual externality that makes Theorem 2.3’s truthfulness argument substantive has vanished. This is a continuum-specific degeneracy: the theorem’s incentive constraint no longer restricts the mechanism in the way it does for individual players.

The proposed repair—requiring a positive mass of a type to deviate and maximizing that cohort’s average utility—restores an externality by making the cohort a coordinated strategic actor. But common information and preferences do not give a type cohort a shared decision right or a shared utility objective. The paper’s agents choose individually, and its truthfulness guarantee is individual dominant-strategy truthfulness. A mass-deviation condition is a coalitional incentive requirement with an added aggregation rule. It could be a worthwhile problem for organizations that truly decide participation collectively; it is not supplied by the high-multiplicity interpretation alone.

So the negative case is strongest for the paper’s actual incentive model: atomizing the players removes the strategic problem that Theorem 2.3 solves, while preserving it requires a different player model. I cannot make the universal case airtight. If the authors accept positive-mass cohorts as genuine decision-makers, the proposed coalitional mirror may survive; that institutional assumption is where this negative case is weakest.
