The strongest plausible mirror I can offer is therefore an unanchored extension of the paper’s allocation model. For each country \(n\), let \(\mu_t\) be the mass of recipient–donor pairs of type \(t\), where a type records the country and all clinical features needed to determine exchange compatibility. Let \(P\) be the feasible cycle and non-directed-donor chain patterns, within the paper’s length limits. Pattern \(p\) consumes \(a_{tp}\) units of each type and gives country \(n\) a transplant mass \(b_{np}\). Choose pattern masses \(x_p\geq 0\), subject to \(\sum_p a_{tp}x_p\leq\mu_t\). For each country coalition \(S\), define \(v_\mu(S)\) as the maximum transplant mass achievable by that coalition, respecting its standalone outcomes. Compute each country’s Shapley target from these coalition values. Given prior credits \(c_n\), choose a maximum-transplant allocation satisfying individual rationality that minimizes \(\sum_n |s_n(x)-(\psi_n+c_n)|\), where \(s_n(x)\) is country \(n\)’s transplant mass.

This could be a sensible high-multiplicity regime: many pairs in each clinical and country type, with a comparatively small support of types. It mirrors the paper’s compensation objective and dynamic credits; it does not continuize the countries or the Shapley coalitions. With fixed cycle and chain limits and an explicit set of type-level exchange patterns, the allocation step is an LP. Whether pricing those patterns is efficient, and how the cost of computing Shapley values grows with the number of countries, would be further questions.

The weak point is decisive for this task: the paper’s central evidence is simulation on small numbers of countries and a discrete compatibility graph. The fluid model is a plausible research extension, but it is not anchored by a named result the authors prove here.

The proposed mass-transfer model is the best extension to test that objection. It could be sensible if clinical types determine compatibility and many pairs share each type. But the paper’s input is a compatibility graph. A distribution over clinical types does not in general encode its pair-specific edges. To preserve an arbitrary graph, types may need to include detailed compatibility signatures, making the support grow with the pool and undermining the high-multiplicity compression. A bounded-type compatibility model avoids that problem, and with fixed cycle limits its pattern LP could be tractable. That makes it a plausible new research question; it does not supply a continuous counterpart to a result the authors proved.

Nor does the paper’s main empirical finding settle the matter. A mass model could study Shapley targets and allocation deviations, but the reported stability comparison comes from discrete, stochastic pool histories and integral matching choices. Its continuous analogue would need a new formulation and analysis.

So the paper does not support a green verdict based on a surviving anchored mirror. The broader claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous mirror is much weaker: the type-based mass model remains plausible, and I cannot honestly rule it out.
