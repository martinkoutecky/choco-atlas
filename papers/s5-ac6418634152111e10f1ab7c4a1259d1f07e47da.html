The continuous mirror question

Given a fixed finite set of complete agent types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a population scale \(n\) with \(n\mu_t\) integral, per-type allocations \(y_t\), primitive integer signature coefficients \(a_t\), capacities \(b_t\), and allowed type-pair weights \(w_{tu}\), choose coalition masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) and a feasible fractional \(b\)-matching \(z\), with \(\sum_{u\ne t}z_{tu}+2z_{tt}\le b_t x_t\). Minimize \(\sum_t y_t x_t-\sum_{tu}w_{tu}z_{tu}\), subject to \(\left|\sum_t a_t x_t\right|\ge 1/n\); the constantly many exceptional capacity-\(2\) agents may be represented as singleton types. Does this continuous separation problem admit an efficient algorithm, and how does its optimum relate to the discrete problem?

The model it lives in

A fixed role-template population with type masses \(\mu_t\), per-type allocations and signatures, and a fractional \(b\)-matching over allowed type pairs; optimize coalition excess subject to a population-scaled nonzero-signature threshold.

The objection that survived

Even after normalizing \(a\), the \(1/n\) threshold uses finite population scale and permits fractional signatures that are not on the discrete \(1/n\)-grid, so the mirror does not preserve exact nonzero avoidance.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.3 in a fixed role-template regime with constantly many exceptional capacity-\(2\) agents. It leaves the paper’s general \(b\)-matching equivalences, other game classes, general monotone-game results, and nucleolus instability result aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I see is narrow: mirror the \(b\)-matching separation problem in Theorem 3.3, proved in this paper. It says that LSA-MinExcess for \(b\)-matching games is solvable in polynomial time when \(b(v)\leq 2\) for every vertex and only constantly many vertices have \(b(v)=2\). In full, the problem takes a graph, edge weights, vertex capacities \(b\), an allocation \(y\), and a nonzero constraint \(a\); it asks for a coalition \(S\) minimizing \(y(S)-v(S)\), where \(v(S)\) is the maximum weight of a \(b\)-matching using vertices in \(S\), subject to \(a(S)\neq 0\) (or, equivalently, the paper’s linear-subspace avoidance constraint). The authors prove the polynomial-time result here. [Theorem 3.3 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29571)

A plausible regime is a large contract or labor market with many interchangeable participants in a small number of roles. A type records everything relevant: capacity, allocation rate, nonzero-constraint coefficient, contract weight, and its interaction with each other role. Most participants have capacity \(1\); only constantly many exceptional participants have capacity \(2\). As population size \(n\) grows, each ordinary role can contain \(\Theta(n)\) agents while the number of types \(\tau\) stays fixed. This is deliberately a role-block setting: if pair-specific relationships distinguish nearly every agent, the type count grows and the high-multiplicity case no longer applies.

I would name the mirror Thresholded Mass LSA-MinExcess for \(b\)-Matching. Its instance is a finite role template with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), population scale \(n\), type capacities \(b_t\), allocation rates \(y_t\), integer coefficients \(a_t\), and weights \(w_{tu}\) for allowed type pairs. Keep the constantly many exceptional capacity-\(2\) agents as explicit singleton coordinates. Choose coalition mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), and a fractional matching \(z_{tu}\geq0\) supported on allowed pairs, with
\[ \sum_{u\ne t}z_{tu}+2z_{tt}\leq b_t x_t. \]
The value of the chosen mass coalition is its maximum-weight feasible fractional matching. The question is to minimize
\[ \sum_t y_t x_t-\sum_{tu}w_{tu}z_{tu} \]
subject to \(\left|\sum_t a_t x_t\right|\geq 1/n\). A solution is an attaining mass vector \(x\) and matching flow \(z\). The \(1/n\) threshold expresses the smallest nonzero signature after scaling an integer coalition sum by population size; it also makes the continuous feasible region closed. With a fixed role template, this is a finite fractional matching optimization problem, so I expect this mirror to be tractable—Class A—by linear optimization.

The paper’s algorithm gives a useful reason to ask this question: its proof reduces the discrete separation problem to a shortest nonzero cycle instance with only constantly many relevant nonzero edges, then guesses those edges and completes the solution using odd joins. The mirror asks whether, after grouping genuinely identical agents and allowing fractional coalition mass, the resulting optimization simplifies to a type-level LP. That is a computational question about the population itself, not about continuous outcomes or stochastic profiles. It covers Theorem 3.3’s separation problem in the stated symmetric, high-multiplicity regime; a further question is whether its answer supports a type-level continuous MPS computation of the nucleolus.

I would not use the paper’s Theorem 4.1 as my lead hardness anchor. It is an oracle lower bound for general monotone games, and its construction singles out individual coalitions in a way that does not obviously survive aggregation into a fixed set of complete types. It is a real boundary question, but a weaker positive mirror.

The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 3.3 handles arbitrary discrete graphs and an exact nonzero-coalition condition, while this mirror restricts interactions to a finite role template and uses a fractional matching relaxation with a population-scaled threshold. In particular, the thresholded fractional problem is not already shown by the theorem to compute, or approximate, the discrete optimum. The positive case rests on whether the authors would recognize this as the right high-multiplicity version of their separation problem—and whether the LP relaxation preserves the feature that made their discrete algorithm work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s nonzero constraint depends on a discrete lattice, and the proposed continuous mirror loses that structure. In the role-template model, let \(B=\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\) be the feasible coalition masses, and let \(g(x)\) be the excess after optimizing the fractional matching. Both \(B\) and the matching value are continuous. If some \(x_1\in B\) avoids the subspace \(L\), then any unconstrained minimizer \(x_0\in L\) can be approached by \(x_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)x_0+\varepsilon x_1\), which lies outside \(L\). Thus the infimum over \(B\setminus L\) equals the ordinary minimum excess. If an ordinary minimizer already lies outside \(L\), the constraint changes nothing; if all minimizers lie in \(L\), the constrained problem has no attaining solution. Exact LSA therefore either adds no optimization content or breaks attainment.

The proposed threshold \(\left|\sum_t a_t x_t\right|\geq 1/n\) repairs attainment by restoring a minimum atom of size \(1/n\). But exact nonzero avoidance is invariant under replacing \(a\) by \(2a\), while this threshold is not unless the representation is normalized. A canonical normalization can address that issue, but the threshold still imports the integer lattice and a finite population scale. As \(n\) grows, the margin vanishes and the continuous problem returns to the degeneracy above. A fixed positive margin would give a coherent robust-coalition question, but it asks for a coalition measurably far from \(L\), which is a different condition from the paper’s “not in \(L\)” separation problem.

There is also a regime mismatch in the constant-exception result. If capacity-\(2\) vertices are repeated across a type, their number grows with the population, outside the theorem’s constant-number condition. Keeping only constantly many such vertices makes them zero-mass exceptions in the continuum; their effect must be retained as finitely many individual coordinates. And the role-template matching LP assumes homogeneous type-to-type interactions. In general \(b\)-matching instances, adjacency is part of what distinguishes vertices, so a fixed set of complete types cannot retain arbitrary graph structure.

These points defeat the proposed mirror of the theorem’s distinctive LSA condition, but they do not prove that every related continuous question is uninteresting. A fixed-margin version on homogeneous blocks could be a sensible, tractable new problem. Its weakness as a case for this paper is that it replaces the result’s defining nonzero constraint with a robustness margin and restricts the graph regime; its weakness as a case against *all* mirrors is that such a new problem might still interest the authors.
