The continuous mirror question

Given a finite supported set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), mutually acceptable type pairs, and each type's weak ranking of acceptable partner types, compute a stable allocation maximizing matched-pair mass, or report that none exists. Let \(x_{ts}=x_{st}\geq 0\) be the mass of pairs between distinct types \(t,s\), and let \(x_{tt}\geq 0\) be the mass of same-type pairs; these consume \(x_{ts}\) units of each distinct type and \(2x_{tt}\) units of type \(t\). Set \(a_{ts}=x_{ts}\) for \(s\ne t\), \(a_{tt}=2x_{tt}\), and \(u_t=\mu_t-\sum_s a_{ts}\), requiring \(u_t\geq 0\). For each acceptable type \(s\), define \(b_t(s)=u_t+\sum_{r:\,s\succ_t r}a_{tr}\). Stability requires \(b_t(s)=0\) or \(b_s(t)=0\) for each acceptable pair of distinct types, and \(b_t(t)=0\) for each type that accepts same-type partners. Maximize \(\sum_{t<s}x_{ts}+\sum_t x_{tt}\); study the problem parameterized by tree-cut width of the graph on supported types, with an edge for every acceptable pair of distinct types.

The model it lives in

The population is a distribution over complete preference and acceptability types, with rational mass \(\mu_t\) per type. Each unit of mass receives one partner or remains unmatched; \(x_{ts}\) records aggregate pair mass, and the objective is total matched-pair mass. Weak stability rules out any acceptable type pair for which positive mass on both sides strictly prefers each other to its assigned partners. The structural parameter is tree-cut width of the type acceptability graph.

The objection that survived

The theorem measures tree-cut width on the graph of individual agents, while the mirror measures it on the graph of preference types; those parameters can differ sharply, and the reduction's individual gadgets may disappear when agents are grouped.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s maximum-cardinality problem, in a market where the population is large but agents fall into a comparatively small number of preference and acceptability classes. Think of a large roommate-matching service whose users rank lifestyle or schedule profiles rather than named individuals. A type \(t\) is the complete profile relevant to matching; its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the population. Members of a type have the same acceptability and weak ranking of partner types, and the type masses sum to \(1\). This is a plausible regime for the authors: their related-work discussion already notes finite stable-matching settings where agents are grouped by shared preferences and the number of types is a parameter. That supports the regime; it does not answer the continuous question. ([Bredereck et al., Sections 1 and 4](https://arxiv.org/html/1911.09379v2))

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Max-SRTI\(_\infty^{\mathrm{tcw}}\). The input is the finite supported type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), mutually acceptable type pairs, and each type’s weak ranking of acceptable partner types, including its ranking of a different member of its own type if same-type matches are allowed. Let \(x_{ts}\geq 0\) be the mass of pairs between types \(t\) and \(s\); a same-type pair consumes two units of type mass. Thus \(2x_{tt}+\sum_{s\ne t}x_{ts}\leq\mu_t\). For each \(t\), let \(a_{tr}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) agents matched to type \(r\), and \(u_t\) its unmatched mass. For an acceptable partner type \(s\), define \(b_t(s)=u_t+\sum_{r:\,\mathrm{rk}_t(s)<\mathrm{rk}_t(r)}a_{tr}\): the mass of type \(t\) agents who strictly prefer \(s\) to their current assignment. Stability requires, for each acceptable pair of distinct types \(t,s\), that \(b_t(s)=0\) or \(b_s(t)=0\); a same-type pair is stable only if \(b_t(t)=0\). The task is to report that no stable allocation exists, or return a stable rational vector \(x\) maximizing \(\sum_{t<s}x_{ts}+\sum_t x_{tt}\). The parameter is the tree-cut width of the graph on supported types, with an edge for each acceptable pair of distinct types.

My lead anchor is Theorem 21, proved in the paper: “Max-SRTI parameterized by tree-cut width is W[1]-hard.” In its reduction from Clique, an input \((G,k)\), with \(n=|V(G)|\), becomes an SRTI instance \(H\) with \(\operatorname{tcw}(H)\leq\max\{k^2,10\}\); \(G\) has a \(k\)-clique exactly when \(H\) has a stable matching of size at least \(\kappa+k(k-1)n+\frac{k(k-1)}{2}+kC\), where \(\kappa\) and \(C\) are the gadget offsets defined in the reduction. Thus computing a maximum stable matching is W[1]-hard for that parameter. ([Bredereck et al., Theorem 21](https://arxiv.org/html/1911.09379v2))

The continuous question asks whether that parameterized hardness survives when the society is represented by type masses and a type’s mass may split among partner types. My expectation is Class A: an exact algorithm fixed-parameter tractable in type-graph tree-cut width, polynomial in the support size and the bit length of the rational masses. For a fixed choice of which side of each acceptable pair rules out blocking, the stability conditions become linear constraints, and the objective is linear. Bounded tree-cut width may let an algorithm manage those choices while carrying rational mass through the decomposition. That is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 21. Further questions include whether the continuous optimum agrees with the normalized optimum in sufficiently large finite clone markets, and how the answer changes if agents rank named individuals within a type.

The authors should recognize this as their maximum stable-roommates problem in a high-multiplicity regime: acceptability, weak preferences, one-partner capacity, blocking pairs, and maximum cardinality all remain central. The weakest point is that their hardness reduction encodes choices in individual agents and rank positions. Collapsing agents into shared preference types and allowing fractional assignments may destroy that encoding. The theorem anchors a worthwhile test of whether hardness dissolves; it does not establish that its reduction transfers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor is the paper’s Theorem 21: Max-SRTI is W[1]-hard parameterized by tree-cut width. The reduction makes that result a weak guide to a continuous version. Its clique choices are encoded by individual gadget vertices, incidence roles, and rank positions. In those constructed instances, agents are distinguished precisely by the relationships the reduction assigns them; treating each as its own type gives no meaningful multiplicity, while grouping them risks erasing the encoding.

That exposes a mismatch in the proposed mirror. Tree-cut width in the theorem belongs to the graph of individual agents. The proposed problem instead measures tree-cut width on a graph of preference types. These parameters can differ sharply: a small type graph can represent large cohorts whose individual acceptability graph is dense. So the proposed problem would test a new structural regime, not show whether the paper’s hardness result survives continuization. This is the strongest objection to the anchor, but it does not defeat the mirror: the authors could recognize maximum stable matching over type populations as their problem even if the parameter’s interpretation changes.

The stronger mass model itself looks coherent. With type masses and preferences over partner types, let \(x_{ts}\) be the mass matched between types \(t\) and \(s\). Capacity constraints handle unmatched mass and same-type pairs; stability can require, for every acceptable type pair, that at least one side has zero mass strictly preferring the other. Maximizing matched mass is then a computational question over a continuous population. The stability constraints are disjunctive, so optimizing them is not automatically a routine LP. That leaves a real algorithmic question.

I cannot make the universal case that no plausible scenario supports this model. Large cohorts with shared, standardized preferences are conceivable, and the paper itself notes work on agents grouped by shared preferences. That prior work supports the high-multiplicity regime; it is not a novelty collision. Nor is it an objection that the continuous answer might differ from the theorem’s answer, or that the reduction’s hardness may disappear.

So the negative case is limited: Theorem 21’s reduction does not itself supply a persuasive population-based application or carry its structural parameter over intact. But the better mirror survives that criticism as a distinct, well-posed computational problem. I do not see an honest basis for claiming that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
