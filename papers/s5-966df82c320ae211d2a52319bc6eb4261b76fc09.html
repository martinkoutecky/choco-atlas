The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a qualified one. The paper contains a natural continuous-population problem, and I believe the authors would recognise it as their matching problem. But it contains no named theorem proving membership in \( \mathrm P \), NP-hardness, FPT, or any other complexity classification. Thus the mirror is a credible new ChoCo problem, not a computational result already established by this paper.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper:

“There always exists a stable matching if firms have complementary preferences and firms’ acceptable sets form a balanced matrix.”

Here “complementary” means that if a firm chooses worker \(w\) from \(S\), it continues to choose \(w\) when the available set expands. The acceptable-set matrix has one row per worker and one column per firm-acceptable set; it is balanced when it contains no odd-order square submatrix having exactly two \(1\)s in every row and column.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Balanced Complementary Continuum Stable Matching. The input consists of finite worker types \(W\), finite firm types \(F\), rational masses \(\mu_w\) and \(\nu_f\), worker rankings over \(F\cup\{\varnothing\}\), and for each firm type \(f\) a preference over bundles \(S\subseteq W\). A type is a complete description: workers of type \(w\) have identical employer preferences, while firms of type \(f\) have identical bundle preferences, complementarities, and capacities. The acceptable-set matrix is promised to be balanced.

A solution is a vector \(x=(x_{f,S})\), where \(x_{f,S}\) is the mass of firms of type \(f\) receiving one worker of every type in \(S\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq W}x_{f,S}=\nu_f \]

for every \(f\), and

\[ \sum_{f\in F}\sum_{S\ni w}x_{f,S}\leq \mu_w \]

for every worker type \(w\). It must also be individually rational and admit no positive-mass blocking coalition: there must be no firm type \(f\), bundle \(S\), and \(\varepsilon>0\) such that \(\varepsilon\) mass of firms would strictly prefer \(S\) to their current bundles and at least \(\varepsilon\) mass of every worker type in \(S\) weakly prefers \(f\) to its current assignment.

The computational task is to construct such an \(x\), or report that none exists when the balancedness promise is removed.

This is genuinely a population continuization. The individuals are not being given fractional jobs or fractional firms: every underlying worker and firm still receives an integral assignment. Only the aggregate counts of indistinguishable workers and firms become masses. If all masses are rational, multiplying by a common denominator produces a finite high-multiplicity market, and normalising a finite stable matching gives a solution of the continuous problem. Conversely, a rational mass solution can be expanded into a finite matching. The relevant regime is a large standardised labour market: perhaps millions of workers in a few dozen skill-and-preference types, and many firms in a small number of technology types that repeatedly hire complementary teams such as an engineer-designer pair. A named individual is not the modelling unit; the type is.

The expected classification is Class A in the explicitly represented balanced regime. The paper’s proof already indicates why. It decomposes firms into single-acceptable-set components, constructs a divisible-worker market with Leontief-style team demands, obtains a stable fractional assignment, and then uses stable transformations plus the balanced-matrix polytope to reach an integral assignment. The same structure suggests a finite LP or flow-like computation on type masses, followed by a balanced-matrix realisation step. The paper also states that balancedness can be tested in polynomial time, although that fact is cited rather than established as a named complexity theorem here.

There are important qualifications. The paper does not provide an end-to-end polynomial-time algorithm for constructing the initial stable fractional matching, and its acceptable bundles may be exponentially numerous if given succinctly. The ChoCo problem would therefore need to distinguish explicitly listed bundles from implicit preference oracles and study the resulting pricing/separation problem. Removing balancedness would also generate a natural boundary question: do the odd-cycle obstructions in the paper completely characterise nonexistence for unequal type masses, or can the continuum create new obstructions?

The mirror is author-recognisable because the paper itself explicitly introduces a continuum market with divisible workers. That is also its main vulnerability: an exact restatement of the paper’s divisible-worker construction would be a novelty collision. The positive case survives only if the question is framed as the computational high-multiplicity problem—constructing, optimising over, rounding, and testing stable mass matchings—rather than merely repeating the paper’s existence argument.

So this mirror covers Theorem 1 emphatically, with possible extensions to the primitive-acceptable-set and technology-tree conditions in Theorems 2 and 4. Its weakest point is decisive under a strict anchor rule: Theorem 1 is an existence theorem, not a named complexity result. The fairest positive verdict is therefore that the paper supplies an unusually convincing model and a plausible Class A programme, but not itself a qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the mirror is decisive at ChoCo’s stated gate: this paper has no qualifying computational result to continuize.

Theorem 1 is an existence theorem for a finite matching market. It does not define a computational input/output problem, establish membership in \( \mathrm P \), prove hardness, give an approximation guarantee, or even formulate a decision problem. The statement that balancedness “can be tested in polynomial time” is a cited fact about matrices, not a complexity result proved or developed by this paper. Theorems 2 and 4 have the same defect: they are structural existence results and sufficient conditions, not computational claims.

The proposed continuous problem is therefore an invented problem adjacent to the paper, rather than a continuous counterpart of one of its computational results. That matters here because the programme is not asking merely whether a finite theorem can be rewritten with masses. It is asking for a computational complexity landscape.

The proposed formulation also has a more fundamental modelling gap. In the paper, \(S\subseteq W\) is a set of named, unit-demand workers. In the proposed mirror, \(S\) is apparently a set of worker types, with one worker of each type supplied to a firm. If \(W\) denotes types, then the paper’s preference over subsets of individuals has been replaced by a preference over type-bundles. A genuine high-multiplicity version generally needs bundles \(q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{\tau}\), type-level choice functions, and a new incidence or balancedness condition. The paper proves nothing about that extension. Restricting bundles to \(0\)-\(1\) type vectors makes the model coherent, but turns it into a new specialised team market rather than a faithful continuization of the stated matching problem.

The rational-expansion argument does not repair this. Once the new type-level model is defined, rational masses may indeed be expanded into clones, but that establishes a correspondence for the newly invented model; it does not turn the paper’s existence theorem into an algorithm. The proposed “report that none exists when balancedness is removed” is also a new problem: nonbalancedness is only a failure of a sufficient condition, not a characterization of nonexistence.

The paper itself already contains the closest continuous construction. In Section 3.3 and Appendix 5.2 it makes workers divisible, invokes existence of a stable fractional matching in the continuum market of Che, and then uses balancedness to recover an integral matching. I would not call this alone a collision—because it is a proof device rather than a high-multiplicity computational study, and analytic continuum matching is explicitly not disqualifying prior art. But it means the proposed mirror cannot claim that the continuous stable object is absent from the paper. Its genuinely new content would have to be an algorithmic, type-mass generalisation, and that content is not anchored by any named result here.

Theorem 2 does not rescue the case. It merely replaces acceptable sets by primitive acceptable sets after decomposing firms into independent components. The continuous question would still be the same newly invented aggregate-stability problem, with no computational theorem behind it. Theorem 4 is weaker still as an anchor: it proves that a technology-tree family yields a balanced matrix. It says nothing about computing a stable matching, optimising one, or classifying the complexity of the resulting market.

I would therefore reject all three proposed anchors under a strict ChoCo reading. This is not because the high-multiplicity labour-market scenario is implausible; it is plausible, and existing high-multiplicity matching work would support rather than undermine it. Nor is it because the continuous answer might coincide with the discrete one. The problem is that the paper supplies a structural existence theory and uses a divisible-worker relaxation, but supplies no computational result whose population continuization this programme could study.

The universal claim that no related continuous matching problem could ever be worthwhile is too strong: the type-mass algorithmic problem could be a good independent ChoCo project. But it is not a qualifying mirror of this paper’s results.
