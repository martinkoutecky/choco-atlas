The continuous mirror question

Given a finite school set \(S\), complete-ranking types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{T}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and rational capacity masses \(q_s\ge 0\) with \(\sum_sq_s=1\), compute the limiting normalized type-school assignment matrix \(y\) generated by common-lottery RP in all valid replications with \(n\mu_t\) students and \(nq_s\) seats, prove \(Y_n^{\mathrm{RP}}\to y\) in probability, and determine whether independent-school-lottery TTC/PRP has the same limit.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity school-assignment model with complete ranking types \(t\in T\), society mass \(\mu\), per-capita capacities \(q\), and aggregate variables \(y_{t,s}\) satisfying \(\sum_sy_{t,s}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_ty_{t,s}=q_s\); the objective is to compute \(y\) and test equality of the RP and PRP limits, not to assign fractional schools to individuals.

The objection that survived

The aggregate limit becomes a point mass and erases much of the lottery correlation and distributional content studied in the paper, while the mechanism equivalence is already exact at every finite replication.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow. This paper contains no named result establishing NP-hardness, membership in P, W[1]-hardness, FPT, or a comparable complexity classification. Its numbered results are equivalence theorems and combinatorial lemmas. Thus it cannot supply the programme’s preferred “does discrete hardness dissolve?” anchor.

The best available anchor is nevertheless Theorem 3, proved in this paper using Theorem 2. It states:

In a school-choice problem with \(n\) students and \(k\) schools, where school \(i\) has capacity \(q_i\) and \(\sum_i q_i=n\), the distribution of assignments produced by TTC is identical when all schools use one common random lottery and when every school uses its own independent random lottery.

This is a genuine mechanism-computation result, though not a complexity result.

My lead mirror would be Continuum Random-Lottery Evaluation and Equivalence. Consider a large centralized school system with a fixed set \(S\) of schools. A type \(t\in T\) is a complete strict ranking of the schools; students of the same type have no other relevant differences. The society is a mass vector \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}^{T}_{\ge 0}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). School \(s\) has per-capita capacity \(q_s\), with \(q_s\ge 0\) and \(\sum_sq_s=1\).

The decision variable is an aggregate assignment matrix \(y=(y_{t,s})\), where \(y_{t,s}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(s\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_s y_{t,s}=\mu_t \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_t y_{t,s}=q_s. \]

This does not give an individual a fractional school: every microscopic student receives one integral school, while \(y\) records the aggregate mass assigned to each type-school pair.

Define the common-lottery mechanism by taking \(n\mu_t\) students of each type and \(nq_s\) seats at each school, drawing one uniformly random order of the students, and assigning each student her most-preferred school with remaining capacity. Let \(Y_n^{\mathrm{RP}}\) be the resulting normalized type-school assignment matrix. Define \(Y_n^{\mathrm{IRP}}\) analogously using independent random priority orders for the schools, with TTC used exactly as in Theorem 3. The continuum problem is:

Given \((T,\mu,S,q)\), compute a matrix \(y\) such that, along all valid replications \(n\to\infty\),

\[ Y_n^{\mathrm{RP}}\longrightarrow y \quad\text{and}\quad Y_n^{\mathrm{IRP}}\longrightarrow y \]

in probability, and establish the equality of the two continuum mechanisms. A solution is an exact rational matrix \(y\), or an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation to it, together with the convergence guarantee.

This is plausibly tractable, hence Class A. For the common-lottery mechanism, one can compute \(y\) by a fluid sweep. While a set \(A\) of schools remains available, type \(t\) is processed at rate \(\mu_t\) and chooses its highest-ranked school in \(A\). Each school is removed when its remaining capacity reaches zero. There are at most \(|S|\) such events, so the resulting matrix is computable in time polynomial in \(|S|\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length. Theorem 3 then transfers the same continuum answer to the independent-lottery mechanism. The finite theorem gives equality at every valid \(n\); the new work is the population-limit formulation and its efficient evaluation.

The regime is credible for a large centralized school-assignment system: hundreds of thousands of students, a comparatively small number of schools, and a bounded collection of preference patterns induced by geography, programs, eligibility, or transportation. Capacities scale with the cohort, so \(q_s\) is the honest continuous quantity. This is precisely a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem, not a relaxation of the outcome alone. The lotteries remain random and the microscopic assignment remains indivisible.

The authors would likely recognize this as their problem: the same students, schools, strict rankings, capacities, common versus independent lotteries, and TTC assignment are retained. Only the population is represented by type masses and the output by aggregate assignment mass. It is also not a novelty collision: the paper proves a finite-population equivalence, whereas the mirror asks for the continuum object, its limiting interpretation, and an algorithm for computing the aggregate outcome.

The weakest point is substantial. The continuum lottery becomes deterministic at the aggregate level, so much of the paper’s distributional content washes out under the law of large numbers. Moreover, Theorem 3 already implies equality for every finite replication, making the equality part of the mirror close to inherited rather than newly difficult. This paper therefore offers a modest Class A mirror, not a strong complexity-landscape case. Further questions would concern finite-\(n\) convergence rates, positive-mass existing tenants, type-dependent priorities, quotas, and whether the fluid algorithm remains tractable when the number of schools or preference types grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against must begin with a scope problem: this paper contains no named computational result. Theorems 2–5 establish equality of distributions, and Lemma 1 is a permutation-counting identity; none gives a complexity classification, an optimization problem, or an algorithmic upper or lower bound. Thus Theorem 3 is not a computational anchor in the programme’s sense. The proposed task of computing a limiting assignment matrix is a new problem, not the continuization of a computational result in the paper.

There is also a more substantive modelling obstruction. The paper’s object is a probability distribution generated by permutations of named students: one permutation for Random Priority and independent permutations for the schools. A continuous society contains neither named students nor a canonical uniform random permutation. On an atomless population, a random priority order has no first student. One can add independent priority marks and process them by thresholds, but that construction immediately yields a deterministic fluid allocation: the mass of every type below any threshold is fixed in the limit. The lottery’s aggregate law collapses to a point mass \( \delta_y \).

The proponent’s finite-replication definition is mathematically coherent, but it changes the paper’s object from a distribution over assignments to a law-of-large-numbers calculation for \(y\). The equality between common and independent lotteries is already exact for every finite replication by Theorem 3. The limiting equality therefore adds no new mechanism-level content, while the computation of \(y\) is just the deterministic serial-dictatorship sweep. This is not objectionable merely because the answer is easy; the problem is that the continuous object has discarded the randomness and assignment correlations that the paper studies.

The obvious repairs do not restore a useful continuous mirror. Asking for fluctuations around \(y\) requires retaining the replication size \(n\), random priority marks, and usually correlations among named agents. Asking for the full distribution likewise requires identities that \(\mu\) intentionally forgets. Putting priority ranks or occupied houses into the type makes types essentially individual-specific; aggregating them again removes precisely the information needed. Scaling the number of schools or introducing a continuum of schools would be a new model rather than a population continuization of this paper.

The same issue affects the stronger variants: Theorems 2 and 4 concern alternative random priority constructions, and Theorem 5 links priorities to individually identified existing tenants and houses. With repeated tenant types, their aggregate limit again becomes deterministic; with genuinely individualized tenants, high multiplicity disappears.

I cannot honestly claim that no plausible high-multiplicity school-assignment scenario exists. Large school systems with repeated preference rankings are credible, and the proposed fluid limit is well-defined. But that is a modest high-multiplicity reformulation, not a worthwhile ChoCo computational mirror: the paper supplies no computational theorem to continue, and the only natural limit either becomes a deterministic bookkeeping exercise or requires reintroducing the finite identities that continuization was meant to remove.
