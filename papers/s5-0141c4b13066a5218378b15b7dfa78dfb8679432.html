The continuous mirror question

For fixed dimension \u(\(d\)\), an instance consists of rational ideal points \u(\(x_t \in \mathbb{Q}^d\)\) for \u(\(t \in T\)\), positive rational masses \u(\(\mu_t\)\) summing to \u(\(1\)\), and rational \u(\(\varepsilon \in (0,1/2]\)\). Let \u(\(L\)\) be the total input bit length and \u(\(\tau=|T|\)\). Define \u(\(W_{\mu,\beta}(p,q)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\mathbf{1}[\beta\|p-x_t\|_2<\|q-x_t\|_2]\)\) and \u(\(D_{\mu,\beta}(p,q)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\mathbf{1}[\beta\|p-x_t\|_2>\|q-x_t\|_2]\)\), with ties contributing to neither. Set \u(\(\beta_\mu(p)=\sup\{\beta: W_{\mu,\beta}(p,q)\ge D_{\mu,\beta}(p,q)\text{ for every }q\}\)\) and \u(\(\beta(\mu)=\sup_p\beta_\mu(p)\)\). Does there exist an algorithm, polynomial in \u(\(\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\)\) for fixed \u(\(d\)\), that outputs a point \u(\(p\)\) with an explicit exact representation of polynomial bit length and \u(\(\beta_\mu(p)\ge(1-\varepsilon)\beta(\mu)\)\)?

The model it lives in

A finite-support distribution of voter ideal points, with type masses \u(\(\mu_t\)\); proposals \u(\(p\)\) are evaluated against every competitor \u(\(q\)\) by comparing the total mass won and lost under the paper's scaled-distance rule, and the objective is to maximize \u(\(\beta\)\). The computational target is a near-optimal proposal from the succinct support and binary-encoded masses.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.4's approximation algorithm for maximizing \u(\(\beta(p,V)\)\). It leaves the exact optimization algorithm, the dimension-dependent universal bounds, and the guaranteed-\u(\(\beta\)\) point constructions unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the candidate construction and competitor-depth tests be weighted by rational type masses to give running time polynomial in \u(\(\tau\)\) and \u(\(L\)\), independent of any common-denominator electorate size?
  • Can the resulting approximation algorithm guarantee a polynomial-bit exact representation of its output proposal?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is high-multiplicity \(\beta\)-Plurality Optimization. The type is a voter’s ideal point \(x_t\in\mathbb{Q}^d\); mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population at that point. This is plausible for a large electorate modeled as a modest number \(\tau\) of policy-position blocs: \(n\) can be very large while \(\tau\ll n\). In the paper’s model, ideal location fully determines how a voter compares proposals, so people at the same location are indistinguishable for this problem.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved in the paper. It says that, for a multiset \(V\) of \(n\) voters in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), the authors can compute a point \(p\) that is a \((1-\varepsilon)\beta(V)\)-plurality point in time
\[ O\!\left(\frac{n^2}{\varepsilon^{3d-2}}\log\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{d-1}}\log^2\frac1\varepsilon\right), \]
with \(d\) fixed. Here \(\beta(V)\) is the best plurality margin achievable by any proposal. The paper proves this algorithmic result; it does not give a hardness result to anchor a hardness-dissolution story. ([Theorem 3.4 in the paper](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol164-socg2020/LIPIcs.SoCG.2020.7/LIPIcs.SoCG.2020.7.pdf))

The mirror can be stated as a prover-ready problem. An instance is a fixed dimension \(d\), a rational \(\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)\), and a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) rational ideal points \(x_t\), with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Let \(L\) be the total bit length of the points and masses. For proposals \(p,q\in\mathbb{R}^d\) and \(0<\beta\le1\), define
\[ W_{\mu,\beta}(p,q)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[\beta\|p-x_t\|_2<\|q-x_t\|_2\right], \quad D_{\mu,\beta}(p,q)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[\beta\|p-x_t\|_2>\|q-x_t\|_2\right]. \]
Ties contribute to neither mass, as in the paper. Call \(p\) a \(\beta\)-plurality point if \(W_{\mu,\beta}(p,q)\ge D_{\mu,\beta}(p,q)\) for every \(q\). Set \(\beta_\mu(p)\) to the supremum of such \(\beta\), and \(\beta(\mu)=\sup_p\beta_\mu(p)\). The task is to output a point \(p\) with \(\beta_\mu(p)\ge(1-\varepsilon)\beta(\mu)\), with running time polynomial in \(\tau,L,1/\varepsilon\) for fixed \(d\), independent of the population size or common denominator of the masses. The output should have an explicit exact representation of polynomial bit length.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). The paper’s algorithm builds candidate proposals around voter locations and tests whether a competitor can capture more than half the electorate by weighted range counting. With a distribution, candidate construction can use the \(\tau\) support points, and the tests can sum rational type masses instead of counting listed voters. The key complexity question is whether those steps, including their geometric precision and tie handling, can be carried out in time polynomial in \(L\). Expanding the masses to a finite electorate proves the same semantic instance is represented by a multiset, but its denominator can be exponential in \(L\); Theorem 3.4’s stated dependence on \(n\) alone does not answer the support-sized bit-complexity question.

This mirror keeps the paper’s spatial voting rule, Euclidean preferences, proposals, and robustness objective intact. The population becomes the distribution; the continuous proposal space is already part of the paper’s model and remains in place. The scope is specifically the optimization algorithm in Theorem 3.4, not a claim about every result in spatial voting games. A natural next question is whether exact computation of \(\beta(\mu)\), or the same approximation when \(d\) is an input, remains tractable.

The soft spot is that the paper already allows multisets and coincident voter locations, so the mirror is close to its existing model. Also, blocs sharing exactly one ideal point are a credible stylization, not a description of every electorate. But the paper states its running time in the number \(n\) of listed voters, not in \(\tau\) and the mass bit length \(L\); it does not establish a support-sized algorithm for binary-encoded multiplicities. That distinction leaves a real computational question, even if the eventual weighted adaptation proves straightforward.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchor is that its continuous society has exactly the same voting behavior as a finite multiset in the paper. For rational masses \(\mu_t\), choose a common denominator \(N\) and set \(n_t=N\mu_t\). Then every weighted win or loss mass is the corresponding voter count divided by \(N\). The plurality condition, \(\beta(p,\mu)\), and the optimum \(\beta(\mu)\) are unchanged. Since the paper already allows multisets and coincident voter locations, the mirror adds no new preference structure, objective, or voting behavior; it repackages the same geometric problem.

The stronger mirror asks whether Theorem 3.4’s approximation can run in time polynomial in support size \(\tau\) and mass bit length \(L\), rather than in the expanded electorate size \(N\). The theorem’s \(n\)-based bound does not answer that: \(N\) can be exponential in \(L\). This is a real encoding gap. Still, the paper’s candidate construction and geometric depth tests appear to depend mainly on distinct locations; aggregating duplicates and using rational weights for depth calculations looks like a natural adaptation. If so, the result would establish a succinct-input version of an existing geometric algorithm, rather than open a different computational problem. That may make it a narrow contribution, but it does not make the question meaningless, and ease of the eventual answer is not a sound objection under this programme’s standard.

A diffuse distribution over ideal points could avoid the exact finite-multiset correspondence, but it would be a substantially broader input model than the paper’s finite voter sets and the programme’s finite-support societies. I see no fundamental reason it could not be worthwhile either. The honest negative case is therefore weak: the paper’s theorem is a genuine computational anchor, and the best objection is about incremental value, not a defect in the mirror. Under the programme’s own criteria, the support-sized weighted version remains a sensible question.
