The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(t\in T\) with masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), initial opinions \(s_t^0\), stubbornness values \(\alpha_t\), a row-stochastic influence matrix \(K\), and parameters \(\beta,\rho,k,p>0\), set \(A=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_t)\) and \(B=[I-(I-A)K]^{-1}\). Choose defender opinions \(s\) and adversarial attacker masses \(a_u\) and incidence masses \(q_{tu}\), subject to \(0\le s\le s^0\), \(\sum_t\mu_t(s_t^0-s_t)\le\beta\), \(0\le a_u\le\mu_u\), \(\sum_u a_u\le\rho\), \(q_{tu}\ge0\), \(\sum_t q_{tu}\le k a_u\), and \(\sum_u q_{tu}\le\mu_t/p\). With \(h_t=\sum_u q_{tu}/\mu_t\) and \(\Delta K(q)_{tu}=q_{tu}/\mu_t-h_tK_{tu}\), find the \(s\) minimizing the worst-case value of \(\sum_t\mu_t(BAs)_t+p\sum_t\mu_t(B(I-A)\Delta K(q)BAs)_t\), and return an optimal \(s\), a worst-case response, and the value.

The model it lives in

A finite-role FJ population in which members of each type share initial opinion, stubbornness, and influence profile; persuasion is uniform within each type, while attacker choices and target links are represented by mass variables. The objective is the paper’s first-order robust total opinion with a per-capita control budget.

The objection that survived

Uniform control within a role rules out splitting otherwise identical users into different post-control opinions, and the proponent does not show how the theorem extends when those within-role distributions are allowed.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Robust FJ Opinion Control. The population consists of many users in a small number \(\tau\) of repeated network roles. A type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), initial opinion \(s^0_t\), stubbornness \(\alpha_t\), and a baseline influence profile \(K_{tu}\): a member of type \(t\) puts fraction \(K_{tu}\) of their influence on type \(u\). Types are complete for this model; users sharing a type agree in all those respects. A plausible regime is a large platform whose users fall into a modest number of belief and exposure roles, with millions of accounts but far fewer roles.

The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It gives a convergence bound for Algorithm 2 on the paper’s first-order robust defense problem (26): after \(T_{\max}\) iterations, the best value found is within the theorem’s explicit bound of the optimum. In the paper’s notation, the bound is \(\bigl(\mu^2+\xi_2N(1+2mkp^2(1/\alpha_{\min}-1)^2)\bigr)/(2\xi_1)\), where \(\xi_1=\sum_{i=1}^{T_{\max}}1/\sqrt{i}\), \(\xi_2=\sum_{i=1}^{T_{\max}}1/i\), and \(\alpha_{\min}=\min_i\alpha_i\); it tends to zero as \(T_{\max}\) grows. This is an optimization guarantee, not a polynomial-time complexity result, and it applies to the paper’s first-order approximation.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. The instance gives the role masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, \(s^0_t\in[0,1]\), \(\alpha_t\in(0,1)\), a row-stochastic influence matrix \(K\), a per-capita persuasion budget \(\beta\), an attacker-mass limit \(\rho\), a per-attacker target limit \(k\), and perturbation strength \(p\). Let \(A=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_t)\), \(D_\mu=\operatorname{diag}(\mu_t)\), and \(B=[I-(I-A)K]^{-1}\).

The defender chooses post-persuasion opinions \(s_t\), with \(0\le s_t\le s^0_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t(s^0_t-s_t)\le\beta\). The adversary chooses attacker mass \(a_u\) in each source type and nonnegative incidence mass \(q_{tu}\), the mass-normalized number of attacks from type \(u\) to targets of type \(t\). Require \(0\le a_u\le\mu_u\), \(\sum_u a_u\le\rho\), \(\sum_tq_{tu}\le k a_u\), and \(\sum_uq_{tu}\le\mu_t/p\). The last constraint carries over the paper’s limit \(p|A_v|\le1\); attacks are spread uniformly within a type, so each member of type \(t\) receives \(h_t=\sum_uq_{tu}/\mu_t\) attack links. Define \(\Delta K(q)_{tu}=q_{tu}/\mu_t-h_tK_{tu}\). The question is to find the defender’s \(s\) minimizing the worst-case first-order total opinion
\[ \sum_t\mu_t(BAs)_t +p\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(B(I-A)\Delta K(q)BAs\bigr)_t \]
over all feasible \(a,q\), and to return an optimal \(s\), a worst-case response, and the value.

This mirrors the paper’s robust control question: the defender spends persuasion budget to reduce total equilibrium opinion, anticipating an adversary who selects attackers and targets. The co-modifications are population-scaled budgets and aggregate attack incidence. They make sense in a high-multiplicity regime: a campaign can control a fraction \(\rho\) of accounts, while each account still has a bounded number \(k\) of target slots. The first-order objective is linear in \(s\) for any fixed attack plan. In this role-based model, the attacker’s response is a linear optimization over a finite flow polytope, so I would expect the mirror to be Class A for approximate optimization, with work governed by \(\tau\) and the desired accuracy. The paper does not prove that continuous result; it supplies the finite-network algorithmic anchor and motivation.

The mirror covers the paper’s first-order robust defense problem and Theorem 2’s convergence result. It does not claim to settle the exact perturbed-resolvent game or the paper’s other analyses. The continuous state opinions in the FJ model are not themselves the continuization here: the population is the object made continuous.

The weakest point is the role assumption. In an arbitrary social graph, users’ full influence profiles may all differ, making \(\tau\) close to the population size. The case is strongest for block-regular or repeated-role networks, where type-level influence profiles are genuinely shared. A useful next question is whether the finite theorem’s guarantee can be transferred to that regime with bounds depending on \(\tau\), and whether the exact, unlinearized adversarial game remains tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is that Theorem 2 is a weak anchor for a complexity mirror. It bounds the convergence of a subgradient method on the paper’s first-order surrogate; it does not classify the problem by polynomial time, approximation, or parameterized complexity. Its iteration bound depends on \(\alpha_{\min}^{-1}\), which may be exponential in the bit length of a rational input. There are also problems in the printed proof: Algorithm 2 sets \(\eta=\eta_0/\sqrt{k}\), where \(k\) is the target limit, while the convergence argument uses a step size indexed by the iteration; and the gradient-norm proof drops cross terms when bounding squared norms. These may be repairable errors, but the stated theorem does not cleanly establish the computational guarantee the proposed mirror claims to carry over.

The proposed finite-role model also restricts the defender’s choices. In the paper, \(s_i\) is chosen separately for each user. Choosing one value \(s_t\) for every member of a role rules out splitting otherwise identical users into differently persuaded subgroups. That matters because the adversary selects particular users based on their opinions and influence. Allowing the split means tracking the distribution of post-control opinions within each role; the attacker’s selection problem then depends on that distribution, not just a vector of role averages. A finite-\(\tau\) LP for the uniform-control version would therefore solve a restricted policy problem, not automatically mirror the paper’s problem.

There is a related tradeoff in the network model. If the network is irregular, users’ influence profiles and their positions in paths through the graph affect the perturbed equilibrium. Aggregating attacks into \(q_{tu}\) preserves first-order effects under strong symmetry assumptions, but the exact perturbed resolvent depends on where those attacks land and how they interact. Keeping that information can make the type set nearly as large as the population. The first-order model avoids this by using an approximation; the paper gives no uniform error guarantee showing that it remains accurate as the population grows or across the perturbation strengths used in its experiments.

This is the strongest negative case, but it does not meet the universal burden. A block-regular network with role-wide control is a plausible regime, and there the type masses and attack incidences can represent the first-order problem cleanly. Moreover, the resulting robust optimization may be tractable by LP duality. Neither individual identity dependence nor the existing continuous-opinion literature rules out that mirror. So the proposed model overstates what Theorem 2 supports, but a convincing continuous mirror survives for a narrower, symmetric regime; I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile scenario exists.
