Why no mirror

Lemma 7 is a computational result, and the proposed cohort intervention problem is well-defined. But each finite player in the paper responds to node-specific neighbors and can affect the aggregate herd-immunity threshold; in the proposed model, an individual is nonatomic and interacts through type averages. That changes the equilibrium problem, so the Stackelberg objective alone does not make Lemma 7’s anchor a continuous analogue.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Lemma 7, proved in this paper. It says that, given a budget \(k\) and target \(N\), finding a set \(S\) of at most \(k\) nodes to force pro-vaccine, so that the worst Nash equilibrium consistent with those forced choices has at most \(N\) anti-vaccine nodes, is NP-complete. The paper sketches a reduction from Target Set Selection, whose hardness it cites. This is the paper’s intervention problem in computational form, and it is a better anchor than a general claim about continuum-agent games: it gives us a specific decision to continueize.

I would name the mirror Continuous-Type Stackelberg Vaccination Control. Its instance is a population of total mass \(1\), divided among \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type completely describes the properties the game uses: its vaccination utility \(C_t\) and its contact profile with the other types. Let \(a_{ts}\) be the per-capita contact intensity from type \(t\) to type \(s\), and let \(\alpha\), \(\delta\), and \(\gamma\) be the peer, herd-immunity benefit, and threshold parameters from the paper.

An action remains binary: each person chooses pro-vaccine or anti-vaccine. The variable \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) is the mass of type \(t\) choosing anti-vaccine, and \(Q=\sum_t q_t\) is total anti-vaccine mass. A type-\(t\) person’s utilities at aggregate profile \(q\) are

\[ u_t(0;q)=C_t+\alpha\sum_s a_{ts}(\mu_s-q_s), \qquad u_t(1;q)=\alpha\sum_s a_{ts}q_s+\delta\,\mathbf{1}_{\{Q<1-\gamma\}}. \]

Thus the peer term is the expected exposure to people with the same sentiment, and herd immunity depends on the population-wide fraction. People of one type may choose different binary actions; \(q_t\) records their share. If both actions have positive mass among the unforced people of a type, those people must be indifferent. This makes fractional *mass* possible without making vaccination itself a fractional action.

Given a campaign budget \(\kappa\) and target \(\nu\), choose \(z_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), with \(\sum_t z_t\le\kappa\), to force mass \(z_t\) of each type pro-vaccine. The continuous problem asks whether there is such an intervention for which at least one pure equilibrium exists and every pure equilibrium consistent with the intervention has \(Q\le\nu\). A solution is the intervention vector \(z\). The universal condition states the worst-equilibrium guarantee directly, including cases where a continuum model’s equilibrium set does not have an attained maximum.

The plausible regime is a very large population—say, a city or region with millions of residents—whose relevant contact patterns can be represented by a much smaller set of cohorts. Members of a cohort share vaccination utility and have approximately the same relative exposure to each other cohort; the cohorts might reflect age, school, workplace, or community contact patterns. The input then has \(n\gg\tau\), with \(\tau\) in the tens or hundreds rather than one type per resident. A public-health planner’s budget and target are naturally expressed as shares of residents. The authors could recognize this as their Stackelberg question about reducing the largest anti-vaccine equilibrium, applied to a high-multiplicity society.

For the unrestricted-\(\tau\) problem, my tentative expectation is Class C: selecting intervention mass while guarding against all equilibria may retain hard combinatorics in the interactions among types, even when the population within each type is divisible. That is a hypothesis to test, not a hardness claim supplied by Lemma 7: its graph reduction does not automatically establish hardness for this continuous model. With fixed \(\tau\), I would expect a tractable parameterized regime, since there are finitely many pure, anti, and indifferent response patterns to examine, each expressible with linear constraints. The resulting questions include whether that yields an algorithm fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), whether hardness persists when \(\tau\) grows, and when a continuous intervention can be rounded to a discrete campaign with a controlled loss.

The mirror covers Lemma 7’s Stackelberg intervention result. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s other equilibrium, social-optimum, or empirical results. Its soft spot is the contact model: replacing individual neighborhoods with type-level contact intensities loses local topology, and some graphs cannot be represented faithfully with a small number of types. That limits the regime, but does not erase it. Large populations with repeated contact roles are a credible high-multiplicity setting, and the intervention remains the paper’s intervention question rather than an unrelated outcome-space or dynamics problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 7 is the paper’s one clear computational anchor: choosing at most \(k\) people to force pro-vaccine so as to bound the worst consistent equilibrium is NP-complete, via Target Set Selection. The strongest case against a mirror is that the reduction uses the identities and exact neighborhoods of those people. The network is not incidental population data: it determines who can trigger a cascade and which intervention sets work.

That makes the proposed cohort model a substantial change. If a type must preserve everything the game uses, its members need the same relevant contacts. In many networks that leaves nearly one type per person, so \(\tau\) is not small relative to \(n\). If instead a type records only average exposure to other types, then the model replaces local neighborhoods with aggregate contact rates. That may be a useful mean-field vaccination game, but it no longer captures the network structure that makes the paper’s intervention problem hard. The paper’s empirical networks do not establish a regime with many people per such exact type.

The stronger alternative is a population indexed by network positions \(x\), with a contact kernel \(W(x,y)\), and a planner choosing a measurable set of positions to intervene on. That preserves much more of the topology and gives a coherent continuous control question. But a general kernel needs an encoding before worst-case computational complexity is defined. Restricting to rational step kernels supplies one; then the steps are types, and the contact matrix can still encode the whole network. This avoids the loss from crude cohort averages, but it does not establish that the resulting question is simpler or distinct enough to justify this programme’s finite-support continuization framework. Keeping individual positions also makes the connection to high multiplicity less direct: fixed-size seed sets have vanishing mass, while positive-mass interventions are a different budget regime.

This is a serious limitation of the proposed mirror, but it does not defeat every version of it. A large, exchangeable population divided into a modest number of contact-role types is plausible, and fractional intervention by type is a sensible analogue of the paper’s planner problem. The paper does not establish that regime, and the type-block model discards much of its graph structure; still, the question remains well-posed and one the authors might recognize. So the strongest honest negative is that the mirror’s fidelity and its high-multiplicity advantage pull in opposite directions—not that no worthwhile mirror exists.
