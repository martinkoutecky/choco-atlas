The continuous mirror question

Given a finite tree \(T\), a candidate set \(C\) with subtrees of \(T\), a committee size \(k\), and a finite support of voter types \(t\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each represented by a voter subtree that induces its approval set \(B_t \subseteq C\), is there a committee \(W \subseteq C\) with \(|W|=k\) such that \(\sum_t \mu_t \mathbf{1}[B_t \cap W \ne \varnothing] \ge q\), for a given rational threshold \(q\)? Consider both restrictions from Theorem 5: candidate subtrees are singletons, or voter-type subtrees are singletons; for Corollary 1, additionally require a singleton candidate at every tree vertex.

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete approval types on a fixed tree representation; the decision variables are the selected candidates, the objective is the mass of types approving at least one selected candidate, and the question is whether a size-\(k\) committee reaches threshold \(q\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5 and Corollary 1, including their stated singleton-subtree restrictions. It leaves the tractability and integrality results for VCI and LC elections, and the domain-structure results, outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the problem admit polynomial-time or fixed-parameter algorithms when the support size τ is bounded?
  • How do approximation guarantees depend on τ and \(k\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: winner determination for Thiele rules is NP-complete on the tree representation domain, even for Chamberlin–Courant (CC), both when candidate subtrees are singletons and when voter subtrees are singletons. The paper’s other hardness anchor, Corollary 1, is also proved here: CC winner determination remains NP-complete when candidate subtrees are singletons and every vertex of the tree is a candidate.

The natural population mirror is Continuous Tree-CC Winner Determination. Its input is a finite tree \(T\), candidates \(C\) with fixed subtrees of \(T\), a finite support of voter types \(t\), positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a committee size \(k\), and a rational threshold \(q\). Each type has a complete approval set \(B_t\subseteq C\); the instance supplies or promises a tree representation in which that set is induced by a voter subtree intersecting exactly the approved candidates’ subtrees. Types with the same approval set can be merged. The question is whether there is a committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|=k\), such that
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[B_t\cap W\ne\varnothing]\ \ge q. \]
A solution is the committee \(W\). The objective is exactly CC’s per-person utility, averaged over the population. For Theorem 5’s two restrictions, require respectively that all candidate subtrees or all voter-type subtrees are singletons.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for approval-based committee selection: many residents, members, or stakeholders share one of a modest number of recurring eligibility or constituency patterns on a shared service or organizational tree. A person’s type is their full approval set; the tree is the structural explanation of those approvals. The candidates and the size-\(k\) committee remain discrete, and each person still receives utility \(0\) or \(1\). Only the population becomes a distribution over types. If the support has size \(\tau\), any such rational distribution can also represent a population with \(N\gg\tau\): replicate the represented type counts by a common scale factor without changing the distribution or the winner.

The continuous question retains Theorem 5’s hardness. In its candidate-singleton case, take the paper’s set-cover star: one voter type per element, mass \(1/n\), and one candidate at each leaf for each set. An element type approves the leaves for sets containing that element. Setting \(q=1\) asks whether a size-\(k\) committee covers every positive-mass type, exactly the set-cover question. The voter-singleton case has the same correspondence: put each element at a leaf as a voter type of mass \(1/n\), and make each set’s candidate subtree intersect exactly the leaves for its elements. These reductions show why the mass model does not wash away the combinatorics: hardness lives in the incidence structure across types and candidates.

For Corollary 1, the corresponding problem, Continuous All-Nodes Tree-CC, adds the restriction that every tree vertex has a singleton candidate. Its continuous reduction also mirrors the paper’s construction. With \(n\) element voters, \(m\) set leaves, and \(L\) dummy voters per leaf, aggregate each leaf’s \(L\) identical dummy voters into one type of mass \(L/(n+mL)\), and give each element type mass \(1/(n+mL)\). Set \(q=(n+kL)/(n+mL)\), as in the paper’s target score normalized by population size. A committee reaching \(q\) must select \(k\) leaf candidates and cover all element types. Selecting the center instead of a leaf cannot reach the threshold. Thus the stricter all-vertices-are-candidates version remains NP-hard too.

I expect both mirrors to be Class B: discrete hardness transfers. Their decision versions are in NP: a proposed committee can be checked by summing the rational masses it represents. The hard families have support that grows with the set-cover instance, so this does not show hardness for fixed \(\tau\); it shows that the high-multiplicity formulation is not automatically easy when the number of distinct types is part of the input. Further questions include whether bounded \(\tau\) or restrictions on tree branching give fixed-parameter or polynomial algorithms, and how approximation behaves as a function of the support and committee size. The paper’s scope also includes tractable path-like domains, but this case deliberately mirrors only its tree-hardness results.

The strongest point is that the underlying social-choice question is unchanged: choose a size-\(k\) committee to maximize the share of voters receiving at least one approved candidate, with the same tree structure and the same CC utility. The weakest point is the scenario’s specificity. A large constituency with recurring ballots is plausible, but a shared tree representation of approvals is a restrictive assumption. Also, the hardness reductions use support that grows with the instance, so the case is for the continuous-population problem with variable support—not for every setting where a population happens to be large.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that the hardness reductions do not show a benefit from smoothing population granularity. In both parts of Theorem 5, each set-cover element contributes a distinct approval type, so the support size \(\tau\) grows with the universe. Duplicating every element many times makes the electorate large but leaves that support growing. The result therefore says little about regimes with many people and a fixed, small set of recurring approval patterns. That is a real limitation on what the reductions establish, but it does not defeat the mirror: the programme treats \(\tau\) as an input parameter, and a continuous problem with variable support is still in scope.

Corollary 1 has the same limitation. Aggregating its \(L\) dummy voters per leaf gives a mass type, but the element types carrying the set-cover incidence remain. Its threshold \(q=(n+kL)/(n+mL)\) faithfully expresses the normalized CC score. And the stronger mirror need not use the \(q=1\) reduction, where the exact masses do not affect whether every type is covered. It can ask for a size-\(k\) committee covering at least a specified share \(q\) of an arbitrary rational population. Then the masses directly affect the objective, and the question is a natural weighted CC problem.

There is also a plausible high-multiplicity setting: many residents or stakeholders share recurring eligibility patterns along a service or organizational tree, while a committee selects a fixed number of initiatives. Their approval set is their complete type. Repeated types can occupy the same tree representation, so neither singleton-subtree restriction rules out multiplicity. Replacing named voters with masses is therefore not a distortion of the problem.

I cannot honestly defeat either anchor under the stated criteria. Theorem 5 and Corollary 1 assert computational complexity results, and their continuous-population counterparts are well-posed. Expanding rational masses into repeated voters confirms the high-multiplicity connection; it is not an objection here. The negative case is limited to saying that these reductions establish hardness as support grows, not tractability or hardness for fixed small support. That caveat does not make the mirrors unworthy of study.
