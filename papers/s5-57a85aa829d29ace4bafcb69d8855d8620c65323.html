The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), committee size \(k\), approval-type set \(T\), and rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), compute \(x\in[0,1]^C\) with \(\sum_{c\in C}x_c=k\) that is Pareto-optimal for \(u_t(x)=\sum_{c\in A_t}x_c\), satisfies fractional AJR+ via \(\frac{\sum_t\rho_tu_t(x)}{\|\rho\|}\geq k\|\rho\|\) for every nonzero \(\rho\leq\mu\) supported on types approving any \(c\) with \(x_c<1\), is fractionally core-stable against deviations of total mass at most \(k\|\rho\|\), and satisfies GRP through a maximum flow with source capacities \(k\mu_t\), approval arcs, and unit candidate capacities.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population has mass \(\mu_t\) of each complete approval type \(t\). The decision variable is the candidate-level fractional committee \(x\), supported by type-level Lindahl prices and a flow; the computational objective is feasibility of all four properties.

The objection that survived

The paper never states a polynomial running-time or exact bit-complexity bound for computing the zero-respecting Lindahl equilibrium and carrying out the completion, so the expected Class A result remains unproved.

fatal: False

Open questions for a prover

  • What bit-complexity guarantees hold for the convex-program equilibrium and the resulting flow completion?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity approval-population mirror of the paper’s incompatibility results, with Theorem 3 providing the positive algorithmic companion.

There is one limitation up front: the paper contains no named theorem proving NP-hardness, membership in P, W[1]-hardness, FPT, or another standard complexity classification. Its named results are structural impossibility, existence, and optimization guarantees. Thus this paper cannot supply a conventional complexity anchor by itself. The mirrors below are nevertheless precise computational feasibility problems whose continuous-population versions are not studied in the paper.

Let \(T\) be a finite set of approval types, with \(A_t\subseteq C\) the approval set of type \(t\), and let \(\mu\) be a probability distribution on \(T\). A unit-mass continuum of voters contains mass \(\mu_t\) of type \(t\). For a fractional committee \(x\), define \(u_t(x)=\sum_{c\in A_t}x_c\). A lottery over integral committees has marginal committee \(x\).

The high-multiplicity regime is concrete. Take the paper’s construction with \(w>50\), \(k=2.2w\), and \(11w\) approval types, but replace every type by \(q\) indistinguishable voters. Then \(n=11wq\), while the number of types is only \(\tau=11w\), and \(q\) can be arbitrarily large. Each voter’s complete approval set is determined by their type. Candidates corresponding to five type-roles reproduce the paper’s cross-group candidates. This is a standard repeated-ballot regime: large blocs of voters submit identical approval ballots.

My lead mirror is Continuous JR/WPO Implementation. The instance consists of \(C\), \(k\), \(T\), and \(\mu\). An integral committee \(W\) is continuum-JR if, for every candidate \(c\), the mass of voters who approve \(c\) but approve no member of \(W\) is less than \(1/k\). The question is whether there exists a lottery supported entirely on continuum-JR committees whose marginal \(x\) is weakly Pareto optimal: there must be no feasible fractional committee \(y\) with \(u_t(y)>u_t(x)\) for every type of positive mass.

This directly mirrors Theorem 1, proved in this paper: there is an instance in which every integral JR committee is strictly Pareto dominated by a fractional committee; equivalently, no lottery satisfies both ex-ante weak Pareto optimality and ex-post JR.

The paper’s proof survives the high-multiplicity translation. In the lifted construction, every five type-roles have a common cross-group candidate, so a JR committee can leave fewer than five type-blocks uncovered. The counting argument bounding same-group candidates is unchanged after multiplying all voter counts by \(q\). For the resulting underrepresented group, the paper’s \(1/6\)-allocation to each of the eleven same-group candidates preserves or improves every type’s utility while freeing committee mass, which can then be spread over cross-group candidates to improve every type strictly. Thus the continuous problem has genuine NO instances.

This is not a claim that fractional committees are new—the paper already studies them. The continuization is the replacement of the electorate by \(\mu\), and the question is whether the finite incompatibility is caused by named-voter granularity. In this construction it is not: the obstruction survives when each relevant voter role is occupied by an arbitrarily large population.

A second, independent mirror is Continuous AJR/JR Lottery Implementation. The input is again \((C,k,T,\mu)\). The lottery must be supported on continuum-JR committees, while its marginal \(x\) must satisfy continuum AJR: for every subpopulation \(\rho\leq\mu\) of mass \(\|\rho\|\), if \(\|\rho\|\geq \ell/k\) and the types in its support have at least \(\ell\) common approved candidates, then \(\frac{\sum_t\rho_tu_t(x)}{\|\rho\|}\geq\ell\). The solution is a valid lottery or a certificate that none exists.

This mirrors Theorem 2, proved here: there is an instance where no lottery satisfies both ex-ante AJR and ex-post JR. In the lifted gadget, each six-type same-group block has mass \(6/(11w)\), and duplicated same-group candidates give it two common approved candidates. AJR therefore requires average satisfaction at least \(6/5\). Summing these requirements over the eleven blocks forces all committee mass onto same-group candidates, because each such unit contributes mass \(6/(11w)\) of satisfaction while each cross-group candidate contributes only \(5/(11w)\). Hence every cross-group candidate has lottery probability zero. But a committee containing only same-group candidates cannot satisfy JR by the paper’s counting argument. Again, the obstruction is population-level and remains after arbitrarily many voters of each type are added.

The positive companion is Continuous Fair Fractional Completion. Given \((C,k,T,\mu)\), output \(x\in[0,1]^C\) with \(\sum_cx_c=k\) satisfying all four continuum properties:

  • Pareto optimality over type utilities;
  • fractional AJR+, meaning that for every \(c\) with \(x_c<1\) and every nonzero subpopulation \(\rho\) supported on types approving \(c\), \(\frac{\sum_t\rho_tu_t(x)}{\|\rho\|}\geq k\|\rho\|\);
  • fractional core stability, meaning no coalition of mass \(\|\rho\|\) can obtain a bundle of total mass at most \(k\|\rho\|\) that strictly improves every participating type;
  • GRP, expressed by the type-compressed flow network with source-to-type capacity \(k\mu_t\), infinite arcs from type \(t\) to approved candidates, and unit-capacity candidate-to-sink arcs.

This mirrors Theorem 3, proved in the paper: every approval-based committee instance admits a fractional committee simultaneously satisfying Pareto optimality, fractional AJR+, fractional core stability, and GRP. Its proof uses the Lindahl-redistributive flow-completion rule, initializing a Lindahl allocation and completing it through max-flow updates.

The natural expectation is that this is Class A for an explicitly represented type set: the individual voter network compresses to capacities \(k\mu_t\), the Lindahl step is a convex program, and the completion step uses max flow and greedy score ordering. The paper itself proves existence and gives the construction, but does not state a complete polynomial-time bound for the continuous type-compressed problem. That running-time and bit-complexity analysis would be a genuine ChoCo contribution.

The weakest point is therefore clear. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are highly engineered impossibility constructions, and a referee might regard the five-subset candidate gadget as a formal rather than sociological population model. More seriously, the paper supplies no standard complexity-class result, so one cannot honestly claim that it already establishes a Class A/B/C classification. What it does supply is a strong test case: its impossibilities survive high multiplicity, while its Lindahl-and-flow construction suggests a tractable continuous-population formulation. The next questions are whether these type-distribution problems admit polynomial algorithms, whether approximate JR changes the impossibilities, and how the convex hull of continuum-JR committees can be characterized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case starts with a limitation the proponent concedes: this paper has no named theorem, lemma, or corollary giving a complexity classification. Its results are impossibility statements, existence theorems, and fairness characterizations. That matters under ChoCo’s remit. One can attach a computational decision problem to almost any existence theorem, but doing so does not make the source paper itself a computational anchor.

For Theorem 1, the proposed “Continuous JR/WPO Implementation” is mathematically coherent, but it is not a computational mirror of the theorem. The theorem says that, for one finite approval instance, the convex hull of JR committees can miss the fractional weak-Pareto frontier. Replacing voter counts by masses merely turns that same statement into a weighted approval-profile statement. For rational \(\mu\), multiplying by a common denominator recovers a finite election exactly; for approval utilities, all relevant information is already contained in the masses of approval types.

A stronger formulation could ask whether the convex hull of continuum-JR committees intersects the global fractional Pareto frontier, or could optimize the Pareto violation over that hull. That may be a worthwhile new polyhedral problem, but it is a problem about the geometry of implementable committee outcomes. The paper supplies neither an algorithmic result nor a complexity obstacle for it. The high-multiplicity lift therefore establishes that the impossibility is compatible with repeated ballots, not that this paper has opened a computational population-continuum problem.

The same objection applies to Theorem 2. The proponent’s AJR/JR mirror is a legitimate weighted version of the finite theorem, and the gadget can plausibly be lifted by treating each original voter role as a repeated approval type. I do not object that high multiplicity is artificial: repeated approval ballots are a perfectly sensible regime. But the resulting question is again just feasibility of a weighted committee polytope subject to fractional AJR inequalities. Quantifying over subpopulations \(\rho\) does not create an infinite-dimensional problem here. With finitely many candidates, there are finitely many approval types, and every such coalition is summarized by its mass on those types.

A better version could ask for the complexity of deciding whether a lottery supported on continuum-JR committees satisfies continuum-AJR, or for the minimum achievable AJR violation. Those would be reasonable ChoCo projects, but they are new projects rather than computational consequences of Theorem 2. The theorem itself contributes only a particular infeasible family. Its survival under cloning is evidence that the family is robust, not evidence that the paper contains a worthwhile continuous-complexity phenomenon.

Theorem 3 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but it too falls short as a mirror of this paper. In the type-compressed formulation, the source capacity for type \(t\) is simply \(k\mu_t\). Aggregating the individual voter network by approval type gives exactly the same flow problem: identical voters can be merged, and any type-level flow can be split back among their copies. Personalized Lindahl prices can likewise be symmetrized across identical voters. Thus the proposed continuous algorithm is not a new continuous-population method; it is the paper’s finite construction with repeated identical rows replaced by one capacity.

That does not make the formulation meaningless. A running-time and bit-complexity analysis in \((m,\tau,L)\) could be useful, especially if the authors’ convex-program and flow-completion argument genuinely yields a polynomial algorithm. But that would be a new algorithmic paper built on Theorem 3, not a computational result already present here. The theorem as written proves existence through a constructive proof, without stating the required complexity bound or identifying a separation problem, hardness boundary, or high-multiplicity algorithmic gain.

The negative case is therefore not that these mirrors are incoherent. In fact, the proponent is right that Theorems 1 and 2 admit sensible repeated-ballot interpretations, and Theorem 3 has a natural type-compressed formulation. The honest objection is narrower: every proposed anchor turns an already finite, approval-type-summarizable fairness statement into a new computational question, but none of the paper’s named results is itself computational. Under ChoCo’s standard, that is a serious failure of provenance. It prevents this paper from supplying a strong continuous-complexity mirror, even though it could motivate a worthwhile follow-up programme.
