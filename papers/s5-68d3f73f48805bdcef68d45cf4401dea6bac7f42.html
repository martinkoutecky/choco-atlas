What the mirror covers

No result is covered: Theorem 5 and the other distortion guarantees do not state computational results. The suggested finite-type worst-case optimization would be a new task over a population model the paper already treats distributionally.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant positive case: the paper has no named result classifying a problem’s computational complexity or giving its algorithmic tractability. Its theorems concern distortion guarantees.

The closest tempting anchor is Theorem 5 (Appendix D.2), proved in this paper. It says that for every \(\beta>0\) and \(m\ge3\), Borda—and therefore RLHF—cannot guarantee distortion below
\[ \max_{0<\gamma<1} \frac{\beta}{2}\frac{1+e^{-\beta}}{1-e^{-\beta}} \left(1-\gamma+ \frac{\sigma(\beta\gamma)-1/2}{\sigma(\beta)-1/2}\right). \]
This bound is strictly above the voting-rule-independent lower bound for every \(\beta\), and is at least \((1-o(1))\beta\) as \(\beta\to\infty\). But “distortion” here measures lost average utility, not computational hardness or runtime; the theorem does not classify a computational problem.

A natural population regime would be many users sharing a small set of complete utility-vector types, with each type’s mass representing its fraction of users. Yet the paper already models users through a distribution \(\mathcal D\) over utility vectors, defines welfare by expectation over that distribution, and studies the \(n\to\infty\) limit. The proof of Theorem 5 itself uses a distribution over three utility types. So the proposed mirror would largely restate the paper’s existing population model. For instance, computing the worst Borda distortion over a finite menu of utility types could be a worthwhile new optimization problem, but it is not a computational result this paper establishes.

Thus there are zero eligible anchors and zero continuous problems per anchor. The strongest honest conclusion is that this paper already works with a continuous population for its distortion analysis, but does not provide the named computational result needed for an anchored ChoCo mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper establishes no computational problem to mirror. Theorem 5 bounds Borda’s distortion; it does not give a complexity result for a search or optimization problem. And the population axis is already continuous in the paper: users are drawn from a distribution \(\mathcal D\) over utility vectors, welfare and comparison rates are expectations over \(\mathcal D\), and the theorem studies the \(n\to\infty\) limit. Replacing repeated users with type masses would restate that model.

The proposed rescue—compute worst-case distortion over a finite menu of utility types—does not supply a missing mirror. For a fixed finite type list, welfare and Bradley–Terry win rates are finite sums; Borda follows directly, and Maximal Lotteries is a finite zero-sum game. Theorem 5’s own construction already uses three utility types. Optimizing worst-case distortion over mixtures of at most \(\tau\) types could be a worthwhile new nonlinear optimization problem, but the paper does not formulate that computational task. It would be a new question on a population model the paper already treats continuously, not a continuization of one of its results.

So the negative case is strong against an anchored mirror of this paper: there are no eligible computational anchors, and the population is already represented as a distribution. It is weak as the literal universal claim that no worthwhile adjacent computational question exists. The paper’s model could inspire new optimization problems, but calling those mirrors would stretch the programme’s scope.
