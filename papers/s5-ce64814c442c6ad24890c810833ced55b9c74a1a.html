The anchor — algorithmic

Lemma III.1

Every anchor argued

  • Lemma III.1 — survived: The opponent challenges the cohort update, but with identical clones and divisible resources the cohort ideal can represent the average of individual-target ideal allocations.

The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses μ_t, linear utility vectors u_t, disagreement utilities d_t, per-capita asset supplies b_k, and ε > 0, start from a feasible utility profile q. For each type r, find a feasible type-level allocation y^r maximizing its per-capita utility while keeping every other type s at q_s; then average these allocations with weights μ_r to obtain the next allocation and profile. Does this weighted Raiffa iteration reach an ε-Pareto-efficient profile, and how many rounds are required in terms of K, τ, input bit length, ε, and the smallest positive type mass?

The model it lives in

A continuum population with finite support over complete player types, where each type includes its utility vector and disagreement utility. Type-level allocations x_{tk} satisfy ∑_t μ_t x_{tk} = b_k, and each type receives utility ∑_k u_{tk}x_{tk}. Each round computes one restricted ideal allocation per type and averages them by mass. For n_t = Nμ_t identical clones, the cohort ideal gain is the single-clone ideal gain divided by n_t, so its mass-weighted midpoint gain equals the finite procedure’s 1/N increment.

The objection that survived

The paper does not establish a round bound for the weighted iteration, and the effect of nonunique ideal allocations on its convergence still needs analysis.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is the paper’s multi-asset Raiffa procedure, and specifically its approximation result. One qualification: the paper has no numbered theorem saying that a problem is in P or NP-hard. Its complexity bounds are given in prose in Section IV-B. My single named anchor is therefore Lemma III.1, proved in this paper: the stated Raiffa procedure, which repeatedly computes each player’s restricted ideal point and then applies the midpoint rule, produces an \(\varepsilon\)-Pareto-efficient solution when it stops with every player’s ideal utility within \(\varepsilon\) of the current midpoint.

A natural mirror is High-Multiplicity Raiffa Approximation. An instance has \(K\) divisible assets and a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) complete player types. Type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t>0\), utility vector \(u_t\), and disagreement utility \(d_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each asset has per-capita supply \(b_k\), so total supply scales with population size. An allocation \(x_{tk}\) gives each member of type \(t\) that type’s per-capita share of asset \(k\); feasibility requires \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tk}=b_k\). The type’s utility is \(v_t=\sum_k u_{tk}x_{tk}\).

Starting from a feasible disagreement profile \(q^0=d\), each round computes an ideal allocation for every type \(r\): maximize that type’s per-capita utility while holding every other type at its current utility \(q_s^j\). Write the resulting ideal utility as \(I_r(q^j)\). Average these ideal allocations with weights \(\mu_r\) to obtain the next feasible allocation and profile:
\[ x^{j+1}_{tk}=\sum_{r\in T}\mu_r y^{r,j}_{tk}, \qquad q^{j+1}_t=q^j_t+\mu_t\bigl(I_t(q^j)-q^j_t\bigr). \]
Given a rational \(\varepsilon>0\), the problem is to return this allocation and profile once \(\max_t(I_t(q^j)-q^{j+1}_t)\le\varepsilon\). The output is a feasible type-level allocation whose utility profile is within \(\varepsilon\) of the Pareto frontier.

The regime is a large pool of claimants—say residents sharing a municipal allocation of several resource classes—with many people but relatively few distinct combinations of utilities and fallback options: \(N\gg\tau\), with about \(N\mu_t\) people of each type. Per-capita supplies scale with \(N\), so the bargaining problem does not collapse as the population grows. The assets are divisible in the paper already; the continuization is the population. In the finite replicated model, each person’s ideal plan contributes \(1/N\) to the midpoint. With symmetric clones and population-scaled supplies, a type’s cohort ideal gain is averaged with weight \(\mu_t\). That makes the weighted update a plausible high-multiplicity form of the paper’s restricted-ideal-point/midpoint procedure.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, at least with a running-time bound that may depend on \(\mu_{\min}^{-1}\), where \(\mu_{\min}=\min_t\mu_t\). Each round requires \(\tau\) explicit LPs with \(O(K\tau)\) allocation variables, so pricing over an exponential outcome space is not the obstacle. The key complexity question is how many rounds are needed as a function of \(K,\tau\), the binary input length, \(\varepsilon\), and the smallest type mass. A polynomial bound in the encoded input would give the strongest tractability result; a naive dependence on \(1/\mu_{\min}\) could be large when a type has a tiny rational mass.

The mirror covers Lemma III.1’s Raiffa approximation result and the paper’s unnumbered LP-based complexity discussion. I would not use the paper’s Talmud-rule properties as extra anchors: they are not named computational results of the kind required here.

The weakest point is the finite-to-continuous bridge. The paper’s finite procedure targets one named player at a time; the mirror expresses those repeated, symmetric targets as type-level cohorts. The population-scaled, mass-weighted construction gives a concrete reason to expect the right limit, but proving that it reproduces the discrete procedure—including how nonunique ideal allocations affect the limit—is part of the research question. Lemma III.1 supports an approximation mirror; it does not itself establish a polynomial-time bound for this continuous version.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror does not derive from Lemma III.1. The lemma says the finite-\(N\) Raiffa procedure returns an \(\varepsilon\)-Pareto-efficient point when its stopping condition is met. It gives no bound on how many iterations are needed. Section IV-B’s running-time expression includes \(J\), the number of iterations, without bounding \(J\) in terms of the input or \(\varepsilon\). So the paper supplies an approximation guarantee for a procedure, not a high-multiplicity complexity result.

There is also a specific error in the claimed finite-to-continuous bridge. Let type \(t\) have \(n_t\) identical players, with \(\sum_t n_t=N\), and let each currently receive utility \(q_t\). In each of the \(N\) ideal allocations, one named player is the target and every other player is held at \(q\). Averaging those allocations, each player’s utility rises by \((I_t^{(N)}-q_t)/N\), where \(I_t^{(N)}\) is the ideal utility of one targeted clone. The proposed update instead raises the per-capita utility of the whole type by \(\mu_t(I_t-q_t)\). That corresponds to targeting the entire type cohort in one ideal allocation. It changes the bargaining unit from individuals to blocs; it is not the limit of the procedure the lemma analyzes.

A repaired mirror could target one representative at a time and derive the limit of those clone-level ideal allocations. But that requires specifying how asset supply and utilities scale, and what happens to an individual’s ideal allocation as the population grows. With fixed total resources, the per-round individual gain can vanish; with per-capita resources, a single targeted player’s ideal allocation can draw on society-scale resources. Either choice may define a worthwhile new problem, but neither is established by Lemma III.1. A bloc-based repair is also conceivable, though it asks about a different Raiffa variant.

That defeats the proponent’s anchor as stated, including its claimed derivation from finite replication. It does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. An exact clone-limit or a bloc-based bargaining model could still be coherent, and the paper’s references to generalized Raiffa solutions make the latter harder to dismiss outright. The honest negative case is therefore weak beyond the proposed mirror: the paper does not itself give the needed complexity result, and the proponent’s formula changes the procedure, but I cannot rule out a better recognized mirror.
