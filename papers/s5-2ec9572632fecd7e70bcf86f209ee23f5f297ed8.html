The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to one, a downward-closed set \(\mathcal Q\subseteq\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{T}\) of feasible ride templates with \(1\le\sum_t q_t\le 3\), and costs \(c_t(q)\) for each represented type, decide whether there are masses \(x_q\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{q\in\mathcal Q}q_tx_q=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), with no RSIE move. A move exists if two distinct rides have active patterns \(q,r\), a type \(t\) has \(q_t>0\), \(q-e_t\) and \(r+e_t\) are feasible, \(c_t(r+e_t)<c_t(q)\), and \(c_s(r+e_t)\le c_s(r)\) for every type \(s\) with \(r_s>0\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity ride-matching model in which a type records all trip, feasibility, and cost features relevant to the paper’s game. Each ride remains an integral group of at most three riders; \(x_q\) is the mass assigned to template \(q\), and type masses are conserved. Stability excludes a type’s move from an active source ride to an active receiving ride when the mover benefits and the receiving riders accept.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s RSIE-existence problem, because RSIE is one of its proposed ridepooling equilibrium notions, rather than the traditional Strong Equilibrium that the authors argue is a poor fit for real services.

The anchor is Theorem 3.6, proved in this paper: given a CTG instance, deciding whether a Ridepooling Semi-individual Equilibrium exists is NP-complete, even when no feasible group contains more than three players. The theorem concerns the underlying equilibrium problem, not an encoding. Its proof refers to the reduction arguments used for Theorem 3.4(a), which establishes the corresponding NP-completeness for Strong Equilibrium.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is recurring commute or event-shuttle demand: many riders travel from a small set of pickup zones to a small set of destinations within common departure windows. A type records everything that affects feasibility or a rider’s costs—origin and destination class, time window, service requirements, and relevant preferences. Suppose there are \(N\) riders but only \(\tau\) such types, with \(N\) much larger than \(\tau\). Each feasible ride still contains an integral group of at most three riders; mass represents the share of demand served by each ride pattern, not a fractionally occupied vehicle.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover:

Continuous RSIE-Existence (lead). The input is a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and a downward-closed set \(\mathcal Q\) of feasible ride templates. Each template \(q\in\mathcal Q\) is an integer vector giving the number \(q_t\) of riders of each type in that ride, with \(1\le\sum_t q_t\le 3\). For every feasible \(q\) and type \(t\) represented in it, the input gives the individual cost \(c_t(q)\) to a rider of type \(t\) in that ride. The question is whether there are nonnegative ride masses \(x_q\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{q\in\mathcal Q} q_t x_q=\mu_t\qquad\text{for every }t\in T \]
such that no RSIE move is possible. The solution is such a vector \(x\); the objective is the yes/no existence decision.

Precisely, a move exists if there are source and receiving templates \(q,r\), a type \(t\) with \(q_t>0\), and positive mass on both templates, such that \(q-e_t\) and \(r+e_t\) are feasible,
\[ c_t(r+e_t)<c_t(q), \]
and every type \(s\) represented in the receiving ride satisfies
\[ c_s(r+e_t)\le c_s(r). \]
A sufficiently small mass of riders of type \(t\) can then leave rides of pattern \(q\) and join rides of pattern \(r\); each receiving rider is willing to accept them, and the moving riders strictly benefit. The source rides become rides of pattern \(q-e_t\). This carries the paper’s RSIE test over at the population level: one rider’s move becomes a move by a small positive mass of otherwise identical riders.

I expect this problem to be continuum-specifically hard (Class C), as a conjecture, not a result. Fractional assignment means Theorem 3.6’s finite NP-hardness does not transfer automatically: a continuous solution can split a type across ride templates, so its reduction’s discrete choices may disappear. But stability still depends on which templates have positive mass. A forbidden move can rule out pairs of active templates, leaving a combinatorial support-selection problem even though the mass-balance equations are linear. Whether that support structure really yields hardness, or admits a tractable formulation, is the prover’s question.

The mirror is plausible because the paper already defines feasibility, costs, and individual moves at the ride-group level; aggregating repeated, indistinguishable requests preserves those ingredients. It covers Theorem 3.6’s RSIE-existence result only. It does not mirror the paper’s cost-sharing protocols, price-of-stability results, or simulation findings. Natural follow-ups are the complexity in \(\tau\) and the input bit length, whether minimizing total system cost over continuous RSIE assignments is tractable, and how continuous RSIE relates to discrete RSIE as cohort sizes grow.

The weakest point is the regime assumption. In the paper’s stated mobility model, exact origins, destinations, and departure times can make nearly every rider a different type. The mirror is convincing only in a setting where many riders really do share the complete features that determine ride feasibility and costs. If coarsening those features changes those quantities materially, then the proposed types would not be faithful.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.6 is a genuine computational anchor, so the strongest objection is that its hardness is tied to an instance regime that the proposed continuum does not preserve. The reduction inherited from Theorem 3.4 uses individually ranked groups: passengers have different preferences over possible co-travellers, and the construction encodes an exact-cover instance in those distinctions. In a high-multiplicity model, passengers with the same complete type must share costs and feasibility. Keeping the reduction’s distinctions therefore makes the number of types grow with the number of passengers; merging them removes the structure that carries the reduction. That undercuts the proponent’s Class C conjecture, though it does not settle the continuous problem.

The paper’s transport setting makes this a real modelling concern. A passenger’s exact origin, destination, and departure time can change which rides are feasible and what each co-traveller costs. Grouping people into a small number of zones or time windows may erase those differences. Exact duplicates are possible, but if many requests truly have identical matching-relevant features, the resulting repeated-pattern model may be much more symmetric than the hard instances in the theorem. The paper offers little evidence that its hard regime has a plausible small-type counterpart.

That is the best negative case, and it does not defeat the improved mirror. A demand cohort with a small set of complete trip types is plausible. For each ride pattern \(q\), let \(x_q\) be its mass; conservation equations account for each type’s demand. If patterns \(q\) and \(r\) both have positive mass and a type-\(t\) passenger can move from one to the other, the corresponding infinitesimal pattern change captures repeated copies of the paper’s RSIE move. Finite vehicle capacity does not prevent this: each ride remains an integral group, while the mass counts repeated rides. The resulting existence problem is well-defined and could be hard for reasons of its own.

Nor does adding the objective of finding the least-cost stable assignment defeat it. That is still a recognizable population-level question about the same ride-matching and acceptance dynamics. The paper’s finite-instance results do not answer it, and the absence of hardness transfer is not a reason to dismiss it under ChoCo’s criteria.

So I can challenge the claimed *hardness expectation*, but not the continuous RSIE question itself. The missing evidence is that low-support types are a useful regime for this problem; that is a weakness in the case for the mirror, not a fundamental obstruction. I would not give a confident negative verdict on this anchor.
