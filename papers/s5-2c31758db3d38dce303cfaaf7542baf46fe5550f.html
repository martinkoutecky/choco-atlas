What the mirror covers

The candidate analytical extension covers the convergence and truth-learning questions in Lemma 1 and Theorems 1–2; it does not cover the experiments or establish computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous-population analogue of this paper’s central question, but the paper contains no named computational-complexity result to anchor it. Its numbered results concern limiting behavior and sufficient conditions for learning, not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest anchors are ineligible under that standard. Lemma 1 says traditional social learning drives beliefs on hypotheses outside the network’s weighted KL-minimizing set to zero. Theorem 1 derives expected log-belief ratios for symmetric two-community stochastic block models and gives a sufficient condition on \(\delta\) for the two communities to favor their own hypotheses. Theorem 2 gives a sufficient internal-versus-cross-community information condition under which some \(\delta\) makes each community favor its own hypothesis. These are substantive results, but they analyze learning dynamics; they do not classify a computational problem. The introduction’s claim that full Bayesian inference is generally NP-hard is cited background, not a numbered result proved here about the paper’s core problem.

The strongest *candidate mirror* would take types to be community-specific likelihood models, with mass \(\mu_t\) representing each type’s share of a large population. Because the interaction graph matters to the paper’s conclusions, the instance would also need a normalized interaction kernel between types. The evolving decision variable would be a belief vector for each type, updated by the paper’s adaptive Bayesian step and geometric averaging; the question would be whether, for a given \(\delta\), each type’s expected steady-state belief favors its own hypothesis. A natural regime is a large network of repeated sensors in a few geographic or operating communities, where many sensors share the same observation model and interaction pattern.

That is a credible continuous-population formulation of the paper’s *analytical* question. It does not supply the required complexity anchor, and turning it into a tractability question would go beyond the results stated here. So I would not present it as a qualifying continuous mirror for this task.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational-complexity result to mirror. Lemma 1 is an almost-sure convergence statement; Theorems 1 and 2 give conditions for expected log-belief ratios to favor each community’s truth. The introduction’s claim about the hardness of full Bayesian inference is cited background, not a result about the learning strategy studied here. So the proponent’s candidate—whether each type’s expected steady-state belief favors its own hypothesis—is a continuous version of the paper’s analytical question, not a computational mirror of a result in the paper. Under ChoCo’s scope, that distinction is decisive.

The population regime itself is credible. Repeated sensors with shared likelihood models and community structure are a natural high-multiplicity setting, and I would not object that mass is meaningless. But that does not rescue any of the three anchors. For Lemma 1, the natural population formulation replaces agents by community or type masses in the weighted KL objective, \(\min_\theta \sum_t \mu_t D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_t\|L_t(\theta))\). That is still a convergence and model-selection statement. For Theorem 1, masses and a type-level interaction kernel yield the same kind of expected-sign condition for the symmetric case. For Theorem 2, the asymmetric case likewise becomes a condition on type masses, information levels, and cross-community influence. None asks an algorithmic-complexity question.

A more elaborate mirror could retain the distribution of individual belief states, rather than only expected log-ratios, or ask a designer to choose \(\delta\) to meet a target error rate. Those may be useful research questions. But the former changes the paper’s stated criterion from the sign of an expected ratio to a population distribution of outcomes; the latter adds an optimization objective the paper does not study. Both are new questions, not continuous versions of Lemma 1, Theorem 1, or Theorem 2. The paper’s stochastic block model and large-population approximations make an analytical population limit plausible; they do not turn its learning guarantees into computational results.

There is also a modeling tradeoff in the proponent’s type-and-kernel proposal. If types preserve the realized network details that affect each agent’s updates, the number of distinct types may grow with the network. If types collapse agents to communities and replace realized links with an average interaction kernel, the model becomes a mean-field analysis of expected behavior. That is a defensible analytic model, but it does not supply the missing computational anchor.

I can make a strong case that this paper has no qualifying mirror within ChoCo’s computational programme. I cannot honestly prove the broader claim that no continuous-population study of these learning dynamics would ever be worthwhile. The sensor scenario is sensible, and a parameter-design problem could be valuable; the material here simply does not establish either as a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.
