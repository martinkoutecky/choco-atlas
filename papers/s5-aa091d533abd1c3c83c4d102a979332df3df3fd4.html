What the mirror covers

The proposed reading concerns Propositions 1 and 2; neither is a computational result, and the paper has no qualifying computational result to cover.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper to anchor a continuous computational mirror. Its numbered results are analytical impossibility results, not claims about an algorithm, computational complexity, or parameterized complexity.

The closest results are Proposition 1 and Proposition 2, both proved in the paper. Proposition 1 says that if two Paretian social observers are continuously linear in mixture on a subset that is socially ambivalent for observer A, and A is at least as inequality averse as B, then their social evaluation functions are ordinally congruent. Proposition 2 says that if two Paretian observers are time consistent, A has a socially revertible state, and A is at least as inequality averse as B, then their social evaluation functions are ordinally congruent after the initial period. These are genuine impossibility results, but they do not assert that any computational problem is hard or tractable.

A population-continuum extension is imaginable: types could be agents’ preference processes, mass could represent their population shares, and one could ask whether Paretian, time-consistent social evaluation can express differing inequality aversion. But that would be a new axiomatic question, with no computational anchor or specified algorithmic input. In the paper, the society formally consists of two individuals; the states, periods, lotteries, and mixture operation are parts of the outcome and uncertainty model, not a continuous population. Treating any of those as the population would miss the programme’s scope. So I can’t give a qualifying continuous problem per anchor here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that Proposition 1 and Proposition 2 are not computational results. Proposition 1 characterizes when two Paretian social observers’ preferences must coincide under mixture linearity and social ambivalence. Proposition 2 gives a related impossibility result from time consistency and a socially revertible state. Neither studies an algorithmic problem: the paper specifies no computational input, intervention to optimize, or complexity claim.

One could imagine agents drawn from a distribution over preference processes. But asking whether such a continuum permits different degrees of inequality aversion would still be a new axiomatic question. The mixture operation in Proposition 1 and the states of uncertainty in Proposition 2 belong to the outcome and decision models; treating them as population mass would change the subject. Even making the two-person society into a continuum would require reformulating the paper’s axioms and proving new results, not mirroring a computational one.

A finite encoding could also invite a new algorithmic question, such as checking whether specified preferences satisfy the paper’s assumptions. That would be a separate model-checking problem whose computational content depends on choices absent from the paper. It would not make either proposition a continuous computational mirror. This case does not rule out worthwhile research inspired by the paper; it rules out a ChoCo mirror of its results. The proponent’s two named propositions therefore do not survive the programme’s computational gate.
