The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), target \(p\), fixed rational \(\alpha\), a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), an eligible-pool capacity \(\nu_t\) for each ranking type measured in units of baseline mass, and rational budget \(\kappa\), decide whether there are additions \(0\le x_t\le\nu_t\) with \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\) such that \(p\) maximizes the Copeland-\(\alpha\) score under \(\mu+x\). Pairwise margins are \(\Delta_{\mu+x}(a,b)=\sum_t(\mu_t+x_t)(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a])\), and the signs of those margins determine wins, losses, and ties.

The model it lives in

Ranking types \(t\) carry baseline mass \(\mu_t\) and eligible capacity \(\nu_t\); choose fractional additions \(x_t\) so that \(p\) is a Copeland-\(\alpha\) winner of \(\mu+x\), subject to \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\). Equivalently, minimize \(\sum_t x_t\) subject to that winner condition and the capacity bounds.

What the mirror covers

Covers the constructive, nonunique-winner voter-addition case of Theorem 4.22; it leaves the theorem’s other variants and the paper’s remaining bribery and control results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does this mass-addition problem admit a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\), or is it \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard?
  • Can the outcome-pattern search over pairwise wins, losses, and ties be reduced to a tractable formulation, or can fractional additions encode continuum-specific hardness?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with fractional control by adding voters, anchored on Theorem 4.22, proved in this paper by reductions from X3C. The theorem says that for every rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), Copeland\({}^{\alpha}\) is resistant to both constructive and destructive control by adding voters, in both unique-winner and nonunique-winner models, and for rational and irrational voter preferences. I would mirror its constructive, nonunique-winner case with rational rankings: deciding whether at most \(k\) eligible voters can be added to make \(p\) a winner is NP-hard and susceptible, hence resistant in the paper’s terminology.

Call the continuous problem Mass-CCAV\({}^{\alpha}\). An instance gives candidates \(C\), a target \(p\in C\), a fixed rational \(\alpha\), and two finitely supported distributions over strict rankings of \(C\): \(\mu\), the current electorate, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and \(\nu\), the available pool’s type capacities, measured relative to the current electorate’s mass. The rankings are the types; \(\tau\) is the size of their combined support, and all masses and the budget \(\kappa\) are binary rationals. Choose additions \(x_t\) with \(0\le x_t\le\nu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\). In the resulting society \(\eta=\mu+x\), define the pairwise margin \(\Delta_\eta(a,b)=\sum_t\eta_t\bigl(\mathbf{1}[a\succ_t b]-\mathbf{1}[b\succ_t a]\bigr)\). Candidate \(a\)’s score is the number of candidates it defeats plus \(\alpha\) times the number it ties: \(\mathrm{score}^\alpha_\eta(a)=|\{b\ne a:\Delta_\eta(a,b)>0\}|+\alpha|\{b\ne a:\Delta_\eta(a,b)=0\}|\). The question is whether some feasible \(x\) makes \(p\) a maximizer. A solution is that mass vector; the optimization version minimizes \(\sum_t x_t\).

This fits a large association, union, or party election where outreach mobilizes a fraction of each preference group from an eligible pool. Millions of members may share a few hundred observed rankings, so the relevant regime has a large population and moderate support \(\tau\). A ranking is a complete type for this problem because the paper’s control action uses preferences and no other individual attributes. The masses are deterministic population fractions, not a noise model or a lottery. The paper’s own turnout examples make the interpretation recognizable: “add \(k\) voters” becomes “bring out \(\kappa\) of the current electorate.”

My provisional expectation is Class C, though I would ask a prover to settle it rather than claim it. The theorem’s X3C reduction selects whole voters, one per set. With fractional additions, that all-or-nothing choice can become a fractional-cover system, which may have a solution even when the original X3C instance does not. So Theorem 4.22’s hardness does not automatically transfer. On the other hand, for any fixed pairwise outcome table, the mass constraints are linear: each margin is linear in \(x\), and the target table fixes which margins are positive, negative, or zero. Thus one can test a table by linear programming. The unresolved combinatorics is choosing among the \(3^{\binom{|C|}{2}}\) possible pairwise outcome tables that make \(p\) a Copeland winner. This gives an exact algorithm exponential in \(|C|\) and polynomial in \(|C|,\tau,L\) per table, while leaving open whether the table choice admits a polynomial method or yields continuum-specific hardness.

The soft spot is the same feature that makes the question interesting: fractional turnout changes the paper’s action model, and its reduction depends on indivisible voter choices. The authors should still recognize the mirror as CCAV, with turnout measured in population mass; that co-modification follows directly from making the population continuous. This case covers only the constructive voter-addition result, not the paper’s other control and bribery results. Its further questions include whether the destructive mass-addition version behaves differently, whether type-specific turnout costs preserve an optimization formulation, and whether the outcome-table search can be replaced by a polynomial separation method.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4.22’s constructive control by adding voters. I cannot make a convincing case that its continuous mirror is unworthy. The paper asks whether a chair can select eligible voters to change the result; in a large electorate with repeated preference types, selecting a fraction of each eligible type is a coherent mass-control question. The type can include a ranking, eligibility, and any mobilization cost the model uses. No individual identity is needed for the paper’s action.

There is a real modeling choice about normalization: if the current society has mass \(1\) and additions have mass \(x\), the resulting society has mass \(1+\sum_t x_t\). Normalizing it back to \(1\) leaves every pairwise margin’s sign unchanged, so it leaves the Copeland winner unchanged too. That choice does not collapse the question. Nor does fractional turnout make the mirror a scope violation: it is a natural co-modification of voter control when the population becomes continuous.

My best objection is about the likely payoff from continuous optimization. For a fixed pattern of pairwise wins, losses, and ties, the mass constraints are linear. But the algorithm must also choose a pattern that makes \(p\) a winner, and there are exponentially many patterns as the candidate set grows. The paper’s X3C reduction also uses set-specific voter types, so its combinatorial choice remains visible in the support. This suggests that continuity may yield a useful formulation without an obvious polynomial-time algorithm.

That is a limited objection. A difficult outcome-pattern search is still a well-posed computational mirror, and finding continuum-specific hardness is within the programme’s stated aims. The X3C reduction does not show that fractional turnout is uninteresting; its all-or-nothing choices are precisely what the continuous version would test. The paper does not already solve that version, and its existing high-multiplicity context would support its plausibility rather than count against it.

So the negative case is weak at its central burden. The best criticism is that this mirror may offer less immediate algorithmic leverage than an LP whose pricing problem is tractable. It does not defeat the question in realistic high-multiplicity turnout settings, and I see no sound basis for claiming that no worthwhile scenario exists.
