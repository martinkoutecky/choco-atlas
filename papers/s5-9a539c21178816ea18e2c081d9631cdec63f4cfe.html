The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s qualitative causal-network aggregation, anchored on Theorem 1. The scenario would be a large, standardized network of causal reviewers—say, teams assessing the same public-health variables—with many reviewers sharing one of \(\tau\) recurring causal graphs, where \(n\gg\tau\). A type is an acyclic directed graph over the fixed variable set; its mass is the fraction of reviewers who hold that graph. The collective decision is another graph.

Theorem 1, stated in this paper and derived from the cited impossibility result of Dietrich and List (2010a), says that when there are at least three variables, no causal judgment aggregation rule can satisfy all four conditions: universal domain (accept every profile of individually acyclic causal relations), aggregate acyclicity, unbiasedness (each edge decision depends only on individual judgments about that edge and treats yes and no symmetrically), and nondictatorship (no fixed individual’s graph always determines the result).

A precise population version is a rule-existence question. Let \(\mathcal D_m\) be the set of acyclic directed graphs on \(m\ge3\) variables. Does there exist a function \(F\), defined on every finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal D_m\), that returns a graph in \(\mathcal D_m\), satisfies continuous versions of universal domain, acyclicity, and edgewise unbiasedness, and is nondictatorial when applied to the empirical distribution of any labeled finite electorate? Here \(\mu_D\) is the share of the population with graph \(D\). The answer is no: any such \(F\) induces a rule on finite profiles, and Theorem 1 rules that out. This is a faithful population formulation of the paper’s qualitative aggregation problem, and the authors would likely recognize the underlying question.

The limitation is central: Theorem 1 is an axiomatic impossibility result, not a computational complexity result. The population formulation preserves that impossibility, but it does not create a new algorithmic problem or a P/NP classification. The paper’s discussion of reducing the number of probability inputs in the quantitative stage is likewise an input-size observation, not a named algorithmic result. Under ChoCo’s computational scope, then, this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. The mirror covers only Section 3’s qualitative stage; it does not cover the one-stage pooling results or the later aggregation of conditional probabilities.

The population model does suggest a computational follow-up the paper itself makes recognizable: given rational masses over DAG types, find an acyclic aggregate graph minimizing expected edge disagreement,

\[ \min_{D\in\mathcal D_m}\sum_{G\in\mathcal D_m}\mu_G\,|E(D)\triangle E(G)|. \]

That formalizes the paper’s distance-based compromise rule as a population problem. It could be a genuine computational-social-choice question, but the paper gives no numbered complexity result for it, so I would not use it as an anchor or claim a complexity classification.

The weakest point is that distributional input erases individual identities, making nondictatorship automatic for the induced anonymous rule; the resulting theorem mostly carries over from finite profiles rather than revealing a new continuous phenomenon. The large standardized-reviewer scenario is plausible, though less natural than a mass electorate. So this is a real, author-recognizable continuous-population analogue, but a weak case for ChoCo’s computational programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the level of the proposed anchor: Theorem 1 is an axiomatic impossibility result, not a computational result. It classifies which aggregation rules can satisfy four conditions; it gives no algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or approximation guarantee for a continuous-population model. The paper’s discussion of reducing the number of probability inputs is also not a complexity result. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, neither supplies a qualifying result to mirror.

The proposed distributional question is a faithful restatement of Theorem 1. Restrict a rule on DAG distributions to rational empirical distributions of finite electorates, and it induces a rule on finite profiles. The impossibility therefore carries straight over. Moreover, a rule that sees only the distribution cannot name a dictator among labeled individuals, so nondictatorship is automatic; the substantive obstacle remains the original axiomatic conflict. This is a sensible question, but its answer adds no computational landscape.

There is a plausible high multiplicity setting here: many reviewers assessing the same variables might share one of a small number of causal graphs. Individual identity is not a decisive objection, since the graph can be a complete type for the qualitative aggregation task. That makes the direct mirror coherent, though it does not turn Theorem 1 into a computational anchor.

The stronger computational candidate is to compute the paper’s distance-based compromise: given masses \(\mu_G\) over DAGs, find an acyclic \(D\) minimizing \(\sum_G \mu_G |E(D)\triangle E(G)|\). For each possible edge \(e\), let \(p_e\) be its mass in the input society. The objective depends only on these edge marginals: including \(e\) costs \(1-p_e\), while omitting it costs \(p_e\). Thus the problem reduces to maximizing \(\sum_{e\in E(D)}(2p_e-1)\) subject to acyclicity. It is a genuine computational question tied to a rule the paper discusses, and the population scenario is plausible.

That candidate is also the negative case’s weak point. It introduces the computational task that the paper did not analyze; it does not mirror Theorem 1’s result. And although the full distribution over graph types collapses to edge frequencies for this objective, that alone does not show the question is unworthy: a weighted population input can still be a sensible object for computational analysis. I cannot honestly rule out this stronger mirror on the grounds available here. The negative case defeats the stated anchor as a ChoCo computational result, but it does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
