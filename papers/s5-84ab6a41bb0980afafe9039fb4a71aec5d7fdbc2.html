The continuous mirror question

Given finite supports of ordered time-window classes \(f\) and value-budget classes \(e\), with masses \(\nu_f\) and \(\rho_e\) matched according to the secretary model so that \(\mu_{f,e}=\nu_f\rho_e\), and supply of \(q\) units per person, construct a randomized online mechanism for finite populations of size \(N\) with \(qN\) indivisible units. Each realized bundle must be integral, reports of \((a,d,v,b)\) must be individually truthful in every finite implementation, allocations must respect true time windows and supply, and payments must respect budgets. Require expected revenue relative to the paper's offline heterogeneous-price benchmark to approach a \(32\)-approximation as the large-market parameter \(\epsilon\) tends to zero, while the mechanisms admit a limiting mass-policy interpretation.

The model it lives in

A secretary market whose unit-mass society has complete types \(t=(f,e)\) and distribution \(\mu_{f,e}=\nu_f\rho_e\). Supply scales as \(qN\) in finite implementations; each advertiser receives an integer bundle, while type-level average allocations may be fractional. The decision is a randomized online allocation-and-payment policy, evaluated by revenue against the offline heterogeneous-price benchmark and required to preserve individual truthfulness in finite populations.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's revenue guarantees for indivisible and divisible goods, and Theorem 9's liquid-welfare guarantee; it leaves the general transformation techniques and other valuation settings aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the sample-price and allocation rules be expressed as a mass-flow policy whose finite implementations preserve individual truthfulness and the \(32\)-approximation?
  • Can the policy and benchmark be computed in time polynomial in the supported type count, number of window classes, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is an online ad market with a very large population of campaigns but a modest number of recurring campaign types. A type is a complete combination of an arrival/departure window, value per item, and budget. For example, many advertisers might share the same campaign window and the same value and budget tier. Let \(\nu_f\) be the population mass in each time-window class \(f\), and \(\rho_e\) the mass in each value-budget class \(e\). Under the paper’s secretary assumption, economic types are randomly matched to time windows, so the limiting mass of type \((f,e)\) is \(\mu_{f,e}=\nu_f\rho_e\). There are \(N\) actual advertisers but only \(\tau\) supported types, with \(N\gg\tau\). Tied arrival times can be ordered randomly, as in the paper’s tie-breaking extension.

The population has unit mass, with supply scaled to \(q\) items per person: a finite approximation has \(qN\) items. This scaling is a co-modification that keeps per-capita supply meaningful as \(N\) grows. In the indivisible case, each advertiser still receives an integer bundle, possibly by lottery; a type’s average allocation can be fractional. The policy sees reports as advertisers arrive, and must allocate within their true time windows, respect budgets, and preserve individual truthfulness. The mass distribution defines the environment and benchmark; the policy must work from the public type domain, supply, and online reports, as in the paper’s prior-free setting.

Lead: Continuum Secretary Revenue. The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that Rev Indiv is truthful and gives a \(32\)-approximation to optimal revenue for indivisible items as \(\epsilon\) tends to \(0\); Rev Div is truthful and gives a \(16\)-approximation for divisible items in the same large-market limit. The proof is the authors’ result, though it uses earlier results cited from Abrams and Borgs et al.

The continuous problem is: given a finite type domain, ordered time-window classes, per-capita supply \(q\), and a secretary-order population, find a truthful online policy. On each arrival the policy outputs a possibly randomized bundle \(X_i\) and payment \(P_i\). It must satisfy supply feasibility, deliver only during the advertiser’s true window, keep payments within budgets, and make truthful reports optimal—including reports about arrival and departure times. Evaluate revenue per person against the limit of the paper’s offline heterogeneous-price revenue benchmark for the same population and supply. For indivisible goods, bundles remain integer-valued per advertiser; for divisible goods, they may be real-valued. The target is the respective \(32\)- or \(16\)-approximation as the individual mass tends to zero and the paper’s large-market parameter tends to zero.

I expect this question to be Class A, tractable. The paper already supplies truthful online mechanisms, and its revenue analysis is driven by random sampling, prices, and a large-market condition. The continuous formulation asks whether those policies have a well-defined mass-flow version and whether their guarantees carry through the limit. That is a substantive limit and implementation question, not a claim that Theorem 2 already proves a continuous theorem.

Second anchor: Continuum Online Liquid Welfare. Theorem 9, also proved in this paper, gives a concrete result: with the paper’s mixing probability set to \(1/10\) and \(\gamma=1.0001\), Liquid Div is truthful and gives a \(2443\)-approximation to optimal liquid welfare. This is the paper’s divisible-item, budgeted online setting.

The continuous problem is: for the same finite time-window and economic type domain, find a truthful online allocation-and-payment policy for divisible supply \(q\) per person. Its benchmark is the offline mass optimum
\[ \mathrm{LW}^{*}(\mu,q) = \max_{\substack{x_t\ge 0\\ \sum_t\mu_t x_t\le q}} \sum_t \mu_t\min(v_t x_t,b_t), \]
where \(x_t\) is the type’s average allocation. The policy’s objective is expected liquid welfare per person,
\[ \sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\min(v_tX_t,b_t)\right], \]
while preserving online feasibility, budget feasibility, and individual truthfulness. The target is a constant-factor guarantee, with \(2443\) as the direct analogue of Theorem 9’s stated bound. I also expect Class A here: the paper’s mechanism combines sampling and price thresholds, and the continuum model makes those prices and allocations distribution-level quantities. The no-large-market guarantee in Theorem 9 makes this a useful second question, though the limit still needs its own proof.

The strongest case is Theorem 2: its large-market condition already points toward the regime where individual contributions vanish, while its indivisible-goods result keeps the mirror from being mistaken for outcome-space continuization. Theorem 9 adds a distinct objective and a concrete mechanism-design result. Neither anchor is a hardness result; this paper offers algorithmic mechanism results rather than a comparable named hardness theorem.

These questions cover the paper’s revenue and liquid-welfare results for online allocation. They do not purport to mirror every technique in Section 2, arbitrary valuation classes, or all online-auction models. Nor is the mirror merely “the goods are divisible”: the defining change is that the advertiser population is represented by mass. Divisibility in the liquid-welfare anchor is retained from the paper.

My weakest point is the limit itself. A continuum has no literal uniform random permutation of all its agents, and truthfulness can become vacuous if it is defined only for zero-mass deviations. The questions therefore require a mass-flow interpretation grounded in finite secretary markets: a solution should give a limiting policy and finite implementations that retain the paper’s individual-level truthfulness. The paper’s large-market theorems make that bridge credible, but do not establish it automatically. A useful next question is whether the resulting policies and benchmarks can be computed in time polynomial in the number of supported types, time windows, and input bit length.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that these mirrors promise very little new computation. The advertiser-cohort scenario is sensible: many campaigns can share a window and an economic type, and the paper’s tie-breaking extension accommodates tied arrival times. Individual incentives also survive through finite implementations. So neither lack of multiplicity nor the continuum’s lack of a literal random permutation is a sound objection.

For Theorem 2, the proposed target is already the regime of the theorem. With bounded budgets and revenue growing proportionally to the number \(N\) of advertisers, \(\epsilon=b_{\max}/\mathrm{OPT}\) tends to zero as \(N\) grows. The paper proves the \(32\)- and \(16\)-approximation guarantees in that limit. In a finite-support mass model, the sample prices and random partitions have direct type-level counterparts; computing a uniform-price benchmark amounts to checking prices at the supported values. Asking whether those same guarantees carry to the limit is therefore mainly a limit and implementation exercise, not a new complexity landscape. A stronger question—finding the best truthful online policy for a given distribution—could be interesting, but it changes the target from the theorem’s constructive approximation guarantee to a new mechanism-optimization problem.

Theorem 9 has a sharper mismatch. With the paper’s fixed supply of one item, per-person supply tends to zero as \(N\) grows, so the proposed positive-supply-per-person benchmark does not arise from that limit. Scaling supply to \(qN\) repairs this, and the paper explicitly says its single divisible item model is equivalent by normalization to \(m\) divisible items. But then the finite approximations are already covered by the theorem’s mechanism and guarantee. Taking their limit may be worth formalizing; it does not, by itself, create a new computational question.

The best strengthened version would optimize over all truthful online policies on a finite-support population model, with explicit report spaces, timing information, and incentive constraints. That could be worthwhile research. I cannot honestly defeat it on the grounds available here: it is a coherent new problem. The negative case is strongest against the two mirrors as stated—their specific guarantees are already asymptotic or transfer through the paper’s normalization—not against every continuous model of online auctions. So the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists is weak, especially for the revenue result.
