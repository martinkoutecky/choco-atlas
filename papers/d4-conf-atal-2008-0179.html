It says that for two weighted voting games with the same quota and mean weight and approximately the same variance, Algorithm 1’s *approximate* Shapley value for a player does not decrease when that player’s weight increases, does not increase when it decreases, and stays the same when it is unchanged. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 does not provide a computational result to continuize. It proves monotonicity for Algorithm 1’s Gaussian approximation to a player’s Shapley value, under assumptions on the games’ means and variances. Its conclusion is about that approximation, not the complexity of computing Shapley values or solving the inverse problem. The exact \(\#\mathrm P\)-completeness claim is background attributed to prior work. The paper’s own \(O(n^2)\) bound is per iteration; it gives no bound on the iterations needed to reach a specified error, and convergence is assessed experimentally. So the proponent is right that there is no qualifying named complexity result to anchor.

The best alternative mirror would group shareholders or voters into types with identical target powers and other relevant parameters, then ask for voting weights that achieve a target distribution of power. That is a coherent high-multiplicity scenario. But in a continuum, each individual has zero mass, so their unscaled Shapley value vanishes. Recovering a nonzero quantity requires a normalized limit, such as \(n\phi_i\). That asks about a limiting power density rather than the paper’s finite vector of individual Shapley values; the finite-player pivotality that drives its computation is precisely what the limit may erase. This is a substantial reason not to count that model as a mirror of the paper’s result.

It is not a proof that the normalized inverse problem would be uninteresting. A cohort model could still make it worthwhile, and the authors might recognize it as their problem. So the negative case is strong against an *anchored* mirror of this paper, but weak against the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous mirror. The proponent raises no surviving named-result anchor; the broader inverse-design question remains plausible.
