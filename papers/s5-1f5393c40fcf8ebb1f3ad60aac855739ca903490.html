The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 1

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 1 — survived: The opponent engaged this anchor but did not defeat the equilibrium mirror; the added population regime limits its reach rather than its plausibility.

The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), complete type data \((e_t,v_t)\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), can an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) output a compactly represented mass allocation \(x_{t,b}\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^m\), satisfying \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}b_gx_{t,b}=q_g\), where every allocated unit of category \(g\) goes to a type maximizing \(v_t(g)/e_t\), and \(f(\rho(x))W(x)\ge W^*\)? Here \(W(x)=\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}\log(e_t+\sum_g b_gv_t(g))\), \(W^*\) is its maximum over feasible allocations, \(\rho(x)=\max_{t,b:x_{t,b}>0}\sum_g b_gv_t(g)/e_t\), \(f(r)=(r+r^3/3)/\ln(1+r)\) with \(f(0)=1\), and the input satisfies \(\sum_t\mu_t\log e_t\ge 0\).

The model it lives in

The population is a unit mass of complete types \(t\in T\), each with mass \(\mu_t\), endowment \(e_t>0\), and additive valuations \(v_t(g)\ge 0\) for \(m\) standardized categories. Category \(g\) has supply \(q_g\) per capita; finite approximants use populations for which type counts and category supplies are integral. The decision variables \(x_{t,b}\) give the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\), with type-mass and supply constraints as stated in the mirror question. The objective is per-capita Nash welfare \(W(x)\); the candidate assigns each category only to types maximizing \(v_t(g)/e_t\).

The objection that survived

The repeated cardinal types and per-capita supplies describe a restricted standardized-resource regime that the paper does not empirically establish; this limits the mirror’s reach.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s welfare approximation for the decentralized equilibrium rule. It leaves the hybrid-process result, the fairness guarantees, and the stochastic convergence analysis unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the paper’s approximation bound extend to this mass allocation with the sharper factor \(f(\rho(x))\), and can the floor-and-ceiling product allocation be output in a compact encoding?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a narrow mirror of Theorem 1, proved in the paper: assuming \(\sum_i \log e_i\ge 0\), define \(f(x)=(x+x^3/3)/\ln(1+x)\) and \(z=f(\max_i v_i(G)/e_i)\). The theorem says that the decentralized allocation \(\DEC\), which gives each good to an agent maximizing \(v_i(g)/e_i\), satisfies \(z\,\mathrm{NW}(\DEC)\ge \mathrm{NW}(\OPT)\), where \(\OPT\) maximizes Nash welfare. The paper proves this guarantee; its theorem numbering and statement are also visible in the [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.07821). This is an approximation result, not a named complexity-class result. The paper mentions that exact Nash-welfare maximization is NP-hard, citing prior work, but I would not present that as a hardness theorem proved here.

A plausible regime is a large, recurring community distribution program for standardized resource categories such as food or medicine. Let there be \(N\) residents, with \(N\) much larger than the number \(\tau\) of complete types. A type \(t\) specifies both an endowment \(e_t\) and the resident’s valuation \(v_t(g)\) for every category \(g\). Residents of a type are identical for everything the model uses; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the population. There are \(Nq_g\) copies of category \(g\), for a fixed per-resident supply \(q_g\). This scales supply with the population while preserving integral bundles for each resident.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover: Per-capita decentralized Nash-welfare approximation. Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), endowments \(e_t\), valuations \(v_t(g)\), and per-capita supplies \(q_g\), find a feasible mass allocation \(x_{t,b}\). Here \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\) is an individual bundle and \(x_{t,b}\) is the fraction of the whole population consisting of type \(t\) residents who receive it. Feasibility means
\[ \sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,b} b_gx_{t,b}=q_g\quad\text{for every }g. \]
Its per-capita Nash welfare is
\[ W(x)=\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}\log\!\left(e_t+\sum_g b_gv_t(g)\right), \]
and \(W^*\) is the maximum over feasible allocations. Require \(\sum_t\mu_t\log e_t\ge0\), the mass-weighted counterpart of Theorem 1’s assumption.

The proposed solution must send each category only to a type maximizing \(v_t(g)/e_t\): if \(x_{t,b}>0\) and \(b_g>0\), then \(t\in\arg\max_s v_s(g)/e_s\). Define its maximum load ratio as
\[ \rho(x)=\max_{t,b:x_{t,b}>0} \frac{\sum_g b_gv_t(g)}{e_t}. \]
The continuous question is whether one can compute such an \(x\) satisfying \(f(\rho(x))W(x)\ge W^*\), with \(f(0)=1\) by continuity. This is a load-sensitive continuous extension: Theorem 1 itself uses the maximum value of *all* goods available to an individual, while \(\rho(x)\) measures the bundles actually assigned. The paper does not prove this mass version, but its proof’s per-agent inequalities extend naturally when summed over type mass.

I expect this problem to be tractable (Class A). Choose a ratio-maximizing type for each category, then distribute that category’s per-capita supply across that type’s mass using integral bundle counts whose mean is \(q_g/\mu_t\). Rounding each count between its floor and ceiling gives a finite-support mass allocation, representable compactly as a product distribution. The type scans and rounding use time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the rational input bit length. The proof obligation is the welfare guarantee, not computing \(W^*\).

The mirror is credible because it preserves the paper’s underlying question: how well does its decentralized, endowment-aware rule approximate maximum Nash welfare? It preserves integral bundles for individual residents, and the types contain the endowments and valuations that determine their choices. It covers Theorem 1’s welfare guarantee, not the paper’s hybrid-process result, fairness theorems, or convergence dynamics.

The weak point is that exact shared cardinal valuations and endowments may be a demanding assumption in a real community, and residents in the paper’s process interact locally in random groups. This mirror drops those dynamics and asks only about the equilibrium allocation, which is where Theorem 1’s guarantee lives. Further questions include whether a sharper guarantee can depend on average rather than maximum bundle load, and whether exact continuous Nash-welfare maximization has a tractable pricing formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the anchor is that Theorem 1 studies a static, item-by-item rule: each good goes to an agent maximizing \(v_i(g)/e_i\). The theorem does not analyze the complexity of a consequential allocation problem; its local-exchange process disappears from the result, and the rule itself is a direct scan. The proposed mirror must add per-capita supplies and distributions over integral bundles to make a population limit meaningful. That is a narrow regime, and it no longer captures settings where goods differ individually or the network’s local interactions determine outcomes.

That objection does not defeat the mirror. Standardized relief supplies and a large population with repeated, complete valuation and endowment types are plausible. The bundle distribution preserves integral allocations for individuals, while mass records how many people receive each bundle. The fact that the paper also studies heterogeneous instances does not make this regime-specific mirror illegitimate.

Nor does the theorem’s lack of a named complexity-class result settle the matter: it gives a numbered approximation guarantee for a polynomial-time rule. The natural continuous question is well-defined, and the proof’s weighted-sum structure appears to carry over to type masses and bundles. The dynamic process may be difficult to continuize faithfully, but this anchor deliberately concerns the equilibrium guarantee, which does not depend on those dynamics.

So my honest negative case is weak. The restrictive assumptions and modest computational scope are reasons to question the mirror’s priority, but they do not show it is not worthwhile. I cannot defeat this anchor on the grounds available; it survives.
