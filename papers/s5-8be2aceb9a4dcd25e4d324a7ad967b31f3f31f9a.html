The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper never states a computational problem. Its named results—including May’s theorem on p. 15 and the later Fishburn and Houy characterizations—identify voting rules by the axioms they satisfy. Example 2 shows that quorum can break monotonicity. None gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or a computational problem whose complexity is at issue. That leaves no computational result here to hand to a prover.

The closest candidate, May’s theorem, does not become an eligible anchor by writing votes as masses \(p_+,p_0,p_-\). One could ask which aggregation rules on those masses satisfy continuous versions of anonymity, self-duality, and monotonicity. That would continuize the paper’s axiomatic question, which is outside this programme’s scope. The stronger monotonicity conditions also concern a change by one participant; in an atomless population that change has zero mass. Replacing it with a positive-mass shift would define a new axiom, not recover a computational result from the paper. This addresses the best version of the proposed May mirror, not just the suggestion to calculate a rule’s output.

One could invent a computational task around the procedures described—for example, evaluate a majority or weighted-majority rule from rational type masses, or synthesize a rule satisfying a chosen bundle of properties. But the first is a new evaluation task, and the second turns the paper’s axiomatic analysis into a new computational problem with a representation that the paper does not supply. Neither is a continuous mirror of a result here. Questions about individual pivotality or voting power would be further away: they track the influence of particular states, a feature that aggregation into mass types can erase.

The finite, heterogeneous nature of international organizations adds support but is not decisive by itself: members may share vote types, so a high-multiplicity scenario can be imagined. The firmer point is that even a plausible continuous population supplies no computational result from this paper to mirror. A nearby research question might be worthwhile on its own; presenting it as this paper’s continuous computational counterpart would not be justified.
