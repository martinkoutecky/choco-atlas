The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a natural high-multiplicity version of the paper’s party elections. Consider a large district with a fixed candidate slate and many voters, where agents are indistinguishable whenever they submit the same complete ranking. A type is then a ranking \(t\in L_C\), and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters with that ranking. In a regime with many voters but relatively few distinct ballots, \(\tau\ll n\), this is a plausible description of an election—not an artificial smoothing of it.

A precise follow-up problem would be Continuous Allocation-Proportionality Evaluation for OWA Rules. Its input is \(C,P,a,k\), scoring and OWA vectors \(s,z\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) rankings. The winning committees are \(W_R(\mu)=\arg\max_{S\in\binom Ck}\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{j=1}^k z_j s_{\operatorname{pos}_{t,j}(S)}\). For a winning committee \(S\), report its party seat shares \(q_i(S)=|S\cap a^{-1}(i)|/k\), the aggregate party scores \(\psi_i(\mu)=\bigl(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{c\in a^{-1}(i)}s_{\operatorname{pos}_t(c)}\bigr)/\|s\|_1\), and the paper’s chosen deviation measure or quota-compliance result. The committee is still selected by the rule’s OWA score; proportionality remains a measured consequence, as it is in the paper.

This is recognizable as the same party-election question with the electorate represented by type masses. Proposition 1, proved in this paper, gives the clearest bridge: under plurality scoring and party-list ballots, the aggregate party score equals that party’s voter share, \(\psi_i=n_i/n\). Its direct mass version replaces \(n_i/n\) with the total mass of types whose top-ranked candidate belongs to party \(i\). That is a useful identity, but it is not a computational-complexity result.

That limitation is decisive for the requested anchors: the paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity. Proposition 1 is an identity about party shares; Lemma 1 gives properties of the score surrogate. The comparisons among SNTV, k-PAV, k-Borda and other rules are experimental findings, not numbered computational results. So there are no eligible anchors, and the proposed evaluation problem cannot honestly be presented as a mirror of a qualifying result from this paper.

As a prospective complexity question, exact winner determination on these mass inputs would likely inherit any hardness of the corresponding discrete rule: a finite profile embeds as \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). The combinatorics of choosing a \(k\)-committee remain. That is a reason to study the mirror, but the paper supplies no hardness anchor for it. The weakest point in the positive case is therefore that the paper studies proportionality of outcomes, while its continuous version mostly replaces a ballot list with a histogram; it neither analyzes the resulting computation nor shows that continuity changes the difficulty.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the positive case’s clearest anchor, but its mass extension does not produce a computational result. For plurality, it becomes
\[ \psi_i(\mu)=\sum_{t:\,\text{top}(t)\in a^{-1}(i)}\mu_t, \]
so the identity with party mass follows directly from the definition. Lemma 1’s normalization, additivity, and invariance properties likewise extend by replacing ballot counts with weights. These are legitimate continuous statements, but neither result asks or answers a complexity question.

The proposed “Continuous Allocation-Proportionality Evaluation” is well defined, including for multiple districts. Given a winning committee, its seat shares and the paper’s deviation measures can be computed from the type masses. Finding a winning committee is a computational question, but the paper’s contribution is not a result about that computation: it is an experimental comparison of rules under statistical cultures. A continuous input turns those experiments into weighted-profile evaluations; it does not mirror a computational theorem in the paper. The finite-profile embedding explains the relationship between the models, but does not supply the missing anchor.

A stronger proposal could study worst-case allocation disparity over all mass distributions, or expected disparity under a continuous version of a spatial culture. Those are possible research questions. The former shifts the paper’s empirical comparison into an optimization problem; the latter needs a probability model over societies as well as a continuous population. Neither follows from Proposition 1 or Lemma 1, and the paper gives no reason to think either is a computational gap its results expose. That is the strongest case against treating this paper as a continuization target.

It does not establish the universal claim, though. A large electorate with relatively few distinct rankings is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the proposed weighted-profile question preserves the paper’s central allocation-proportionality comparison. So the negative case is strong on the narrow point that this paper offers no named computational result to mirror, but weak as a claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
