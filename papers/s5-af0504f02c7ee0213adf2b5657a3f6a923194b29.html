The continuous mirror question

Given rational type data \(T\) with \(|T|=\tau\), masses \(\mu_t\), repeated-agent columns \(a_t\), objective coefficients \(v_t\), and population-scaled resources \(b\), define \(Z^\ast=\arg\max\{\sum_{t\in T}v_tz_t:\sum_{t\in T}a_tz_t\le b,\ 0\le z_t\le\mu_t\}\). Compute \(Y_\infty=\{t:z_t=\mu_t\ \forall z\in Z^\ast\}\), \(N_\infty=\{t:z_t=0\ \forall z\in Z^\ast\}\), and \(M_\infty=T\setminus(Y_\infty\cup N_\infty)\), with an exact complexity bound.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic high-multiplicity selection ILP in which type \(t\) carries mass \(\mu_t\), selected mass \(z_t\) satisfies \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\), aggregate constraints are \(\sum_t a_tz_t\le b\), and welfare is \(\sum_t v_tz_t\). An exchangeable lottery gives each type-\(t\) agent selection probability \(z_t/\mu_t\).

The objection that survived

The aggregate face does not retain the multiplicities of labelled optimal solutions, so it cannot by itself mirror the paper’s uniform or RSD lottery rules.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s partitioning and preprocessing in a repeated-type, population-scaled regime, and motivates continuous leximin and Nash extensions. It does not cover exact uniform or RSD lottery semantics, the full auxiliary-integer ILP class, or the paper’s axiomatic and experimental results.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact bit-complexity of computing \(Y_\infty,N_\infty,M_\infty\) from the rational aggregate LP?
  • Under what conditions do finite repeated-agent partitions converge to the continuous type-level partition as \(N\to\infty\)?
  • When do continuous leximin or Nash solutions admit finite-\(N\) lotteries with explicit approximation guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is a single lead anchor: Proposition 1. The paper contains no numbered theorem proving hardness or polynomial-time solvability for fair integer programming itself. Its statements that kidney exchange and total tardiness are NP-hard cite external results, so I would not present them as anchors from this paper. Proposition 1 is therefore the cleanest named computational result.

Proposition 1, proved in the paper, says the following. For an ILP \(\xi\) with \(n\) binary variables representing agents, let \(Y\) be the agents selected in every optimal solution, \(N\) those selected in none, and \(M\) those selected in some but not all optimal solutions. The paper proves that this partition can be found by solving at most \(n+1\) ILPs, each differing from \(\xi\) by at most one added constraint. First solve \(\xi\) and obtain an optimal solution \(x^\ast\). For each agent \(i\), solve a copy with \(x_i=1-x_i^\ast\). If that copy is infeasible or has a strictly smaller optimum, \(i\) belongs to \(Y\) or \(N\), according to \(x_i^\ast\); if it retains the original optimum, \(i\in M\). The result is an oracle-reduction, not a claim that arbitrary ILPs are in P, but it is precisely the preprocessing on which the paper’s fair-distribution methods depend.

The continuous mirror I would propose is Continuous Forced/Mixed-Type Partitioning for Fair High-Multiplicity Selection.

Consider a large selection programme with \(N\) agents and a fixed finite set \(T\) of complete agent types, \(|T|=\tau\ll N\). A type specifies everything relevant to the ILP: the agent’s constraint column \(a_t\), objective contribution \(v_t\), and canonical utility. For example, this could be a national grant or admissions programme with millions of applicants but only a few dozen combinations of eligibility, resource consumption, priority, and fairness category. Agents of the same type are genuinely interchangeable; if they differ in price, budget, or eligibility, those differences create different types.

Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\), so the finite population contains \(N\mu_t\) agents of that type. The repeated-agent finite problem is the ILP over selected counts \(q_t\):

\(\displaystyle \max\left\{\sum_{t\in T}v_tq_t:\sum_{t\in T}a_tq_t\le Nb,\ 0\le q_t\le N\mu_t,\ q_t\in\mathbb Z\right\}.\)

The continuous society is obtained by normalizing \(q_t\) by \(N\). Its aggregate optimal face is

\(\displaystyle Z^\ast=\arg\max\left\{\sum_{t\in T}v_tz_t:\sum_{t\in T}a_tz_t\le b,\ 0\le z_t\le\mu_t\right\}.\)

Here \(z_t\) is selected mass, not a fractional individual decision. Each individual agent is still either selected or not selected in any realized allocation; because the population is nonatomic, selecting mass \(z_t\) from type \(t\) is possible even when \(z_t\) is fractional. An exchangeable lottery gives every agent of type \(t\) selection probability \(z_t/\mu_t\).

The continuous problem is: given rational \((a_t,v_t,\mu_t,b)\), output the exact partition of types into

\(\displaystyle Y_\infty=\{t:\ z_t=\mu_t\text{ for every }z\in Z^\ast\},\)

\(\displaystyle N_\infty=\{t:\ z_t=0\text{ for every }z\in Z^\ast\},\)

and

\(\displaystyle M_\infty=T\setminus(Y_\infty\cup N_\infty).\)

Thus \(Y_\infty\) consists of types whose entire mass is selected by every optimal aggregate plan, \(N_\infty\) of types whose mass is never selected, and \(M_\infty\) of types whose agents can be treated differently across optimal plans. This is the precise continuous question corresponding to Proposition 1.

It is solvable in Class A. Solve the aggregate LP once to obtain \(z^\ast\). If \(0<z_t^\ast<\mu_t\), then \(t\in M_\infty\). If \(z_t^\ast=0\), maximize \(z_t\) over the optimal face; the result distinguishes \(N_\infty\) from \(M_\infty\). If \(z_t^\ast=\mu_t\), minimize \(z_t\) over the optimal face; the result distinguishes \(Y_\infty\) from \(M_\infty\). Hence at most \(\tau+1\) LPs are needed, with running time polynomial in the number of types, constraints, and encoding length.

This is a genuine high-multiplicity mirror rather than merely another fractional outcome model. The population is the continuous object, while the underlying selection remains dichotomous at the individual level. The vector \(q/N\) is the finite high-multiplicity bridge, and the LP above is its aggregate limit. It also preserves the paper’s central concern: whether an agent or group is wrongly treated as always or never selected when other optimal solutions exist.

The original authors should recognize this as their problem. Their Proposition 1 is explicitly about identifying the agents in \(Y\), \(N\), and \(M\) before computing leximin, Nash, or other fair distributions. In the high-multiplicity regime, quotienting interchangeable agents into types is the natural version of exactly that operation. It is also consistent with the paper’s emphasis on the projection \(S_x\) and on fairness over the convex hull of optimal selections.

This mirror covers Proposition 1 and the fair-distribution pipeline’s essential preprocessing step, not every result in the paper. It naturally generates further computational questions: continuous leximin selection over \(Z^\ast\), maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\log(z_t/\mu_t)\) for a Nash-style rule, and finite-\(N\) approximation guarantees relating type-level probabilities to lotteries over discrete optimal solutions.

The weak point is that this mirror deliberately excludes genuinely indivisible global auxiliary decisions. If the model contains an integer facility-opening variable or another non-scalable combinatorial object, the aggregate problem may remain an MILP rather than an LP. It also mirrors a preprocessing proposition rather than the paper’s most substantive fairness rules. But for large selection programmes with repeated agent columns and population-scaled capacities, that restriction describes a credible instance regime, and the resulting question is recognisably the paper’s fair-selection problem with the population—not merely the outcome—continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Proposition 1 is only marginally a computational anchor. It is an oracle-counting observation: it identifies \(Y\), \(N\), and \(M\) after solving up to \(n+1\) arbitrary ILPs. It gives neither a polynomial-time algorithm in the ordinary input model nor a complexity classification. Even granting that it qualifies, however, the proposed mirror changes the object whose computation is interesting.

Proposition 1 concerns labelled coordinates of an implicitly represented set \(S(\xi)\) of optimal integer solutions. Its content is that one must determine, for each named agent \(i\), whether some optimal solution contains \(x_i=1\) and another contains \(x_i=0\). In the proposed high-multiplicity regime, agents of one type are interchangeable by construction. If \(q_t\) is the number selected from type \(t\), then every permutation of those agents is feasible whenever one such selection is feasible. Consequently, the individual partition disappears:

\[ q_t=0\text{ in every optimum}\Rightarrow\text{all type-t agents are in }N, \]

\[ q_t=N\mu_t\text{ in every optimum}\Rightarrow\text{all are in }Y, \]

and every intermediate type count makes all members of that type belong to \(M\). The proposed LP therefore computes ordinary coordinate extrema on an aggregate optimal face. That is mathematically sound, but it removes the identity-level problem that Proposition 1 addresses rather than continuizing it.

The loss becomes clearer for the paper’s actual target, lotteries over optimal solutions. The face

\[ Z^\ast=\left\{z:\sum_t a_tz_t\le b,\ 0\le z_t\le\mu_t,\ \sum_t v_tz_t=z^\ast\right\} \]

records possible type-level selection masses, not a distribution over the underlying optimal solutions. Different lotteries can have the same \(z\). The uniform rule, for example, depends on the number of labelled micro-solutions realizing each type-count vector; a vector \(q\) can carry multiplicity proportional to

\[ \prod_t {N\mu_t\choose q_t}, \]

which the aggregate LP does not retain. Random Serial Dictatorship depends on the sequential interaction of individual labels and is likewise not determined by the face alone. Thus the proposed mirror preserves a preprocessing statistic while discarding the lottery object on which the paper’s fairness framework is built.

One can repair this by defining only type-anonymous fairness, such as maximizing

\[ \sum_t \mu_t\log\!\left(\frac{z_t}{\mu_t}\right). \]

But that is a new aggregate fairness problem. It is a reasonable one, yet it is no longer a direct mirror of the paper’s distribution rules unless one separately proves that the relevant finite lotteries converge to it, including their support and multiplicity semantics.

The proponent’s other repair options do not give a clean escape. Retaining the auxiliary integer variables \(y\) makes the limit a mixed-integer problem rather than the advertised LP; scaling and fractionalizing \(y\) changes the feasible outcomes. Retaining individual-specific constraints makes the type count grow with the population, destroying the claimed high-multiplicity compression. Introducing configuration or lottery variables may produce an interesting new research programme, but its difficulty lies in reconstructing the omitted optimal-solution structure, not in Proposition 1’s population partition.

Still, this negative case is not decisive. For the restricted anonymous resource-allocation regime, the proposed \(Y_\infty,N_\infty,M_\infty\) problem is a legitimate high-multiplicity Class-A analogue, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as their preprocessing question. The honest conclusion is therefore not that no continuous mirror exists. It is that this anchor supports, at most, a thin type-level LP-face mirror—not a continuization of fair integer programming’s central lottery and implementation problem.
