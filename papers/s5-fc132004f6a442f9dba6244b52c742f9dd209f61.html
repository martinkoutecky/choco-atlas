The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has a natural population-level interpretation: in a large electorate, types could record whether a voter is allied or floating, which candidate they support, and their voting cost; each type’s mass would be its share of society. Polls reveal support shares, and one could ask how poll information changes equilibrium turnout, winning probabilities, and welfare. That is a plausible follow-on question about this paper’s subject.

But the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Its \(TR1\)–\(TR5\) are equilibrium comparative-statics predictions, and \(ER1\)–\(ER6\) summarize experimental findings. None is a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifying a computational problem as tractable or hard. So there is no paper result to restate as an anchor, and I can’t responsibly give the requested anchor-specific continuous problem.

The main obstacle for even the follow-on question is preserving pivotality: in a nonatomic population, one voter’s decision has no effect on the outcome. The paper mentions Poisson games as an approximation for large electorates, but does not develop that into a computational result. My positive case is therefore limited to a plausible high-multiplicity setting; it is not an anchored continuous mirror of a named result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s admission is the strongest point against the mirror: the paper offers no computational result to continue. Its \(TR1\)–\(TR5\) are equilibrium predictions for a game with \(E=12\); its \(ER1\)–\(ER6\) are experimental findings from a small set of electorates. None asks for an algorithm or classifies a computational problem. A continuous model of poll effects could be a sensible follow-on, but it would not be a mirror of a named result in this paper.

Turnout is the most promising candidate, and the population setting is natural: masses of allied and floating voters with different support could respond to polls. But the paper’s strategic mechanism depends on a voter’s chance of being pivotal. In a nonatomic electorate, an individual has no effect on the outcome, so with a positive voting cost the original incentive to turn out disappears. A Poisson or finite-population model can preserve pivotality, but keeps the strategic object finite. A logit or mean-field model can supply a nontrivial response rule, but then the computational question comes from that added behavioral model, not from a computational result in this paper.

The same issue carries through to the other outcomes the proponent names. In a deterministic continuum, once turnout masses are specified, the winner follows from aggregate votes and welfare is an aggregate of payoffs and voting costs. Polls can still change those quantities if the model supplies a turnout response, but computing that response is the unresolved modelling choice. Asking which poll policy maximizes welfare would be a plausible new design problem—the authors do care about policy—but it is not the paper’s comparison of poll and no-poll treatments, and it needs a new policy space and equilibrium specification.

Allied status, candidate support, and voting cost can be encoded in types; the repeated-election distinction between stable allies and floating voters does not make mass representation inherently nonsensical. So I would not argue that no high-multiplicity scenario exists. The stronger negative is narrower: even the best such scenario does not yet yield a ChoCo-style computational mirror. To make one, we would have to add a rule that preserves pivotal incentives or replaces them, then define an algorithmic task around that rule. That could be worthwhile work, but it would be a new computational model inspired by the paper.

The negative case is therefore strongest on the programme’s fit and weakest as a universal claim. A large-electorate model of poll effects is plainly plausible. What is missing is a computational problem from this paper that survives continuization as the authors’ problem, rather than an analytic or experimental question rebuilt around a different model.
