The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive hook is a large configuration service: many customers share one of a small number of complete LPOD contexts, such as the same available components, default rules, and ordered preferences. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), and \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of that type assigned to feasible answer set \(S\). With shared capacity limits, one could ask whether all the mass can be assigned among each type’s preferred answer sets while respecting those limits. This is a plausible population problem around the paper’s configuration applications, but the capacity constraints and allocation question are additions; the paper does not study them.

I cannot honestly anchor that mirror in a qualifying result. That is a named expressiveness result, not a complexity result about a problem in the sense requested. The computation section describes a generator–tester implementation and says that a *separate* cited paper contains complexity results; it gives no numbered complexity result to use as an anchor here.

So the positive case is only a plausible new research direction, not a theorem-backed mirror of this paper. Its weakest point is that the paper’s agents are configurators making individual choices; turning them into a high-multiplicity population with shared capacities changes the problem. Under the required anchor standard, this paper supplies no named computational complexity anchor for a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s concession is decisive: this paper contains no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem to anchor a mirror. Proposition 2 is a non-translation result: it rules out an extended logic program whose answer sets match the preferred answer sets of every LPOD. It says nothing about an algorithm’s running time or about a population-level problem. The computation section points to complexity results in a separate paper, but they are not results of this paper. So the proposed configuration-service scenario may be a research direction, but it does not mirror a computational result here.

The strongest version of that scenario is plausible: let each customer type be a complete LPOD context, and let a service assign customers among their preferred configurations subject to shared capacity limits. That gives a meaningful population and makes mass assignment natural. But the capacities couple customers in a way the paper never models. Without them, the service solves each type’s LPOD independently; mass just records how many times each case occurs. With them, the central problem becomes capacity-constrained allocation among configurations. That may be worth studying, but it is a new problem introduced around the paper’s examples, not a continuization of one of its results.

Proposition 2 does not supply the missing anchor. A distribution over LPODs could ask what share of a batch admits a matching translation, but that merely aggregates a per-program expressiveness question. If the question instead concerns preserving collective assignments under shared capacities, it again depends on an added allocation model. Neither formulation turns Proposition 2 into a computational complexity result about a continuous society.

I cannot rule out that population-level configuration allocation is worthwhile in its own right. The negative case is weaker on that broader claim. But under the requested standard—an anchor in this paper and a continuous computational question built around it—no proposed mirror survives.
