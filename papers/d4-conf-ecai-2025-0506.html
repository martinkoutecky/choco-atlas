What the mirror covers

The proposed question concerns Theorem 17 alone; it leaves the agreement, convergence, and other distributional results unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I can make is narrow: Theorem 17, proved in this paper, gives an exact decision characterization. It states that an unbiased single-peaked distribution for \(k\)-approval exists if and only if \(k\) divides \(m\). Here “unbiased” means every candidate has the same expected \(k\)-approval score. The paper also notes that the general existence question can be decided by solving a linear system. Theorem 17 is not a conventional hardness or parameterized-complexity result, but its characterization yields a direct polynomial-time decision procedure.

A continuous version is: given \(m\) candidates on a fixed axis \(x_1>\cdots>x_m\) and \(k\in[m-1]\), decide whether there is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete single-peaked rankings such that
\[ \mathrm{sc}_{\mu}(x)=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[r_t(x)\le k]=\frac{k}{m} \quad\text{for every candidate }x, \]
and, if so, return a sparse distribution as a solution. Types are complete rankings; mass is population share; the decision variable is the distribution itself. When \(k\mid m\), partition the axis into \(m/k\) consecutive blocks of \(k\) candidates and put mass \(k/m\) on a single-peaked ranking whose top \(k\) candidates are each block. Every candidate then receives the same score. When \(k\nmid m\), Theorem 17 rules out such a distribution. This mirror is tractable: the theorem gives the divisibility test, and the constructive witness has polynomial size.

The plausible regime is a large synthetic electorate drawn from a fixed single-peaked axis, with voters grouped by identical rankings and the generator designed to avoid favoring any candidate in expected \(k\)-approval score. The paper itself studies synthetic cultures and unbiased distributions, so the authors would readily recognize the question. This anchor covers Theorem 17 only; it does not mirror the paper’s main agreement and finite-election convergence results. The mirror question is a feasibility problem, not an optimization problem, and its expected class is tractable.

The serious weakness is that this may be a collision, rather than a new continuous mirror: the paper already represents cultures as distributions over rankings and Theorem 17 already asks whether such a distribution exists. Treating those masses as demographic shares leaves the equation and result unchanged. So this is the best affirmative foothold, but it does not show that continuization opens a missing computational question or dissolves discrete hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor is already continuous in the paper. For a mass distribution \(\mu\) over single-peaked rankings, the aggregate \(k\)-approval score of candidate \(x\) is exactly the expected score under the paper’s culture \(\pi=\mu\):
\[ \mathrm{sc}_{\mu}(x)=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[r_t(x)\le k]. \]
Because each ranking awards exactly \(k\) points, the scores sum to \(k\). They are all equal precisely when each is \(k/m\). That is the paper’s definition of an unbiased distribution and exactly the question answered by Theorem 17. The theorem already gives the divisibility test and, when \(k\mid m\), a rational witness with polynomial support. So the proposed “continuous version” does not expose a missing computational question; it restates one the paper asks over masses already.

The most plausible stronger variant would start from a particular society \(\mu_0\) and ask for the minimum-cost mass transfer to an unbiased society. That could be a worthwhile new problem, but it adds a starting point, transfer costs, and an optimization objective absent from the paper. Its appeal would come from those additions, not from continuizing Theorem 17. The paper’s main agreement and convergence results are probabilistic bounds, not discrete computational problems waiting for a high-multiplicity version.

This makes the negative case strong for the only computational anchor offered. The universal claim is harder to defend: I cannot rule out a useful new intervention problem built around these distributions. But that would be a new research direction, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s result.
