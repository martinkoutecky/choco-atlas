The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition in this report that asserts a complexity or algorithmic classification. The numbered formal statements I find are Definitions 1–3. Some abstracts make computational claims, but none meets your required anchor standard. So I can’t honestly give an anchor-driven case.

The strongest positive lead is Ratliff’s Definition 3, which is the report’s own proposed committee-selection method, not a proved complexity result. It takes \(n\) candidates and \(N\) voters, each ranking their top \(k\) committees of size \(m\). It builds a complete weighted graph on the candidates, including a loop at every vertex. For each voter, it adds \(k-r+1\) to every edge between candidates in that voter’s \(r\)-th ranked committee, including the candidates’ loops. The selected committee is the \(m\)-vertex induced subgraph of maximum total weight.

A continuous version keeps that objective and replaces voter counts with population masses. Let \(V=[n]\), and let each type \(\theta\) be an ordered list \(Q^\theta_1,\ldots,Q^\theta_k\) of distinct \(m\)-candidate committees. The instance gives a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and \(n,m,k\). For \(i\leq j\), define

\[ w_{ij}(\mu)=\sum_{\theta\in T}\mu_\theta \sum_{r=1}^{k}(k-r+1)\mathbf{1}\!\left[\{i,j\}\subseteq Q^\theta_r\right], \]

where \(i=j\) represents the loop contribution. The problem, Continuous Top-\(k\) Committee Selection, is to find a set \(C\subseteq V\), \(|C|=m\), maximizing

\[ \sum_{\substack{i,j\in C\\i\leq j}} w_{ij}(\mu). \]

Any maximizing committee is a solution. Its input parameters include \(n,m,k,\tau=|T|\), and the bit length \(L\) of the masses. This continues the population: committee outcomes remain discrete.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large professional association choosing a fixed-size board from a standing candidate pool. Members may share a modest number of recurring discipline, region, or policy-based rankings of whole committee slates, with \(N\gg\tau\). That regime preserves the paper’s central motivation: voters care about committee composition, so their preferences are recorded over groups rather than inferred from rankings of individual candidates. The report’s Wheaton example itself is a small election with many distinct rankings; the larger association scenario is a separate, plausible setting for the same mathematical problem.

There is an exact dictionary for rational instances. If a finite electorate has \(n_\theta\) voters of each type and \(N\) voters total, set \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/N\). Every committee score is then the discrete score divided by \(N\), so the maximizing committees are unchanged. Conversely, rational masses have a common denominator and can be represented by integer type counts. I would provisionally expect hardness to transfer if this underlying finite committee optimization is shown hard: the population change alters the coefficients, while the choice among \(m\)-subsets remains. The report does not establish that classification. Further questions include whether the problem has an exact or parameterized algorithm in \(m\), \(k\), or \(\tau\), and whether rounding a society’s masses gives useful approximation guarantees.
