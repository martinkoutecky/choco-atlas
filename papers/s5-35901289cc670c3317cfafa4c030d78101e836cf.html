The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(E\) of complete cohort or contract types, rational supplies \(\mu_e\), the two preference preorders, the partition \(E=E_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}E_2\), and two endpoint-supply-respecting polymatroids \(P_D,P_H\) arising from clone-symmetric matroid constraints, decide whether there is an \(x\in P_D\cap P_H\) with \(0\le x_e\le\mu_e\) such that no type \(e\) with \(x_e<\mu_e\) weakly blocks on both sides and, for \(e\in E_2\), strongly blocks on at least one side. A weak block on side \(S\) is a feasible positive-mass addition \(x+\epsilon\mathbf 1_e\in P_S\), or exchange \(x+\epsilon(\mathbf 1_e-\mathbf 1_f)\in P_S\) for some \(f\ne e\) with \(x_f\ge\epsilon\) and \(e\succeq_S f\); a strong block uses \(e\succ_S f\) for an exchange. Output such an \(x\) when one exists.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity cohort market with a small support of complete match or contract types, rational type supplies μ, and side-specific clone-compatible matroid constraints represented by polymatroids. The decision variable \(x_e\) is matched mass of type \(e\); stability uses the paper's \(E_1/E_2\) rule and feasible infinitesimal additions or exchanges on each side.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s common-independent-set stability problem, with the elements of \(E\) treated as complete types of candidates or match opportunities. A type records every feature the model uses: its place in both preference orders and how it interacts with both feasibility systems. In a large placement or matching market, many individuals can share one such type, so the input is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over a support of \(\tau\) types, with \(N\gg\tau\). The two matroids encode the two sides’ collective constraints, and the paper’s \(E_1,E_2\) partition retains its different stability standards. This is plausible when the market has a small menu of profiles and common side-specific priority orders; it is not a claim that every matching market has that regime.

The lead question is Continuous Non-uniform Stability for Common Polymatroid Allocations. Its input is a finite support \(E\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_e\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), the two preorders \(\succeq_D,\succeq_H\), the partition \(E=E_1\mathbin{\dot\cup}E_2\), and two rational rank functions \(r_D,r_H\) given with polynomial-time exact separation oracles. A type \(e\) includes all attributes relevant to the two rank systems. The decision variable is \(x_e\), the mass of type \(e\) assigned or matched. Feasible allocations lie in
\[ P_D\cap P_H,\qquad P_S=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^{E}:x(F)\le r_S(F)\text{ for all }F\subseteq E\}, \]
where the rank functions include the type-supply bounds \(x_e\le\mu_e\). There is no welfare or cost objective: as in the paper, the question is whether a stable allocation exists, and a solution is such an \(x\).

Here is the blocking rule, so the mirror is a problem rather than just an angle. For a type \(e\) with unassigned mass, say \(e\) weakly blocks \(x\) on side \(S\) if either some \(\epsilon>0\), with \(\epsilon\le\mu_e-x_e\), permits \(x+\epsilon\mathbf 1_e\in P_S\); or some \(f\ne e\) and such an \(\epsilon\le x_f\) permit \(x+\epsilon(\mathbf 1_e-\mathbf 1_f)\in P_S\) with \(e\succeq_S f\). It strongly blocks if the same holds with direct addition, or with an exchange satisfying \(e\succ_S f\). An allocation is non-uniformly stable when no available type weakly blocks on both sides, with the additional strong-block condition on at least one side for types in \(E_2\), exactly as in the paper. This replaces a selected unit by a positive but arbitrarily small mass of identical units; the population mass is the continuous object, and the fractional allocation follows from representing that mass.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that Algorithm 4 correctly decides whether two matroids on a common ground set, with the paper’s two preorders and \(E_1,E_2\) stability rule, have a non-uniformly stable common independent set, and finds one when it exists. Under the paper’s polynomial-time independence-oracle assumption, this is a polynomial-time algorithm in \(|E|\). That is the theorem’s full computational result; I find no named hardness result in this paper to anchor on. The complexity claims about other stable-matching variants in the introduction describe cited work, not additional results proved here.

I would expect this continuous problem to be tractable (Class A). The reason is specific to this paper: its algorithm is built around rank, closure, critical subsets, and matroid exchanges—the same structure that describes feasible infinitesimal additions and swaps in the proposed mirror. The theorem does not establish that extension. The prover’s question is whether those exchange arguments can be carried over to rational mass vectors with running time polynomial in the support and bit length, rather than in the number of represented individuals.

The mirror covers Theorem 1 only. The paper’s cited prior algorithms and its technical lemmas support the modeling context, but I would not present them as separate anchors. The main follow-up questions are whether every rational continuous solution has a discrete realization after scaling the masses; whether the paper’s global side-specific preorders and matroid constraints remain meaningful for the proposed type regime; and whether exact separation of fractional blocking directions can be done in polynomial time.

My weakest point is the co-modification from matroid-independent sets to rank-constrained mass vectors, together with the infinitesimal version of blocking. The authors could reasonably say that this is a new polymatroidal stability problem, not simply their original one with a larger population. The case survives because the changed objects preserve the paper’s central structure—rank feasibility, the same two preference orders, the same \(E_1/E_2\) distinction, and add-or-exchange blocking—and because those changes are what make the high-multiplicity population explicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives a polynomial-time decision algorithm for the existence of a non-uniformly stable common independent set, and finds one when it exists. There is only one result here to assess; the cited algorithms in the introduction are not additional results of this paper. So the negative case cannot rest on the absence of a complexity result.

The concern is whether a distribution over the paper’s ground set is a population. In the matching interpretation, elements of \(E\) are possible matches or contracts, while the people are their endpoints. A mass \(\mu_e\) for each contract type does not, by itself, describe how many people of each type are available: contract masses must be consistent with both sides’ supplies and with which pairs can form. Nor do arbitrary matroids and global preorders automatically supply that missing population structure. A faithful cohort model would need endpoint-type supplies, feasible flows, and blocking deviations that both sides can actually carry out. Those are substantial additions to the paper’s model.

The proponent’s best repair is to model symmetric copies of ground elements and take a normalized limit, with clone-compatible matroid constraints represented by polymatroids. That can make the mass vector and its feasibility constraints coherent. But it narrows the setting to specially structured matroid pairs, and the resulting blockers concern infinitesimal changes to a fractional allocation. The paper’s theorem concerns absent atomic elements and single-element exchanges. The continuous problem may be mathematically worthwhile, but its population interpretation and its blocking rule would need to be established; they do not follow just from Theorem 1.

A more conventional high-multiplicity matching model—many agents in a few cohorts—could repair the population interpretation. Yet it would require preserving the endpoint supplies and cohort-specific preferences, which the theorem’s arbitrary pair of matroids and global preorders do not encode. That is the strongest reason against this anchor: the theorem abstracts away the very agent structure needed to make matching multiplicities meaningful. A mass model over interchangeable resource items remains possible, but it is a less direct case for continuizing a society.

This is not a decisive objection. Clone-symmetric matroid instances or matroid-constrained cohort markets could provide a sensible regime, and the paper’s exchange structure gives a reason to investigate them. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile mirror. The negative case is limited: the proponent has proposed a plausible new polymatroid stability problem, but has not yet shown that it is the high-multiplicity version of the problem Theorem 1 actually studies.
