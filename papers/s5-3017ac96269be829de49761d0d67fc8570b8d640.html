The continuous mirror question

Fix \(k\ge3\). Given a finite set \(T\) of complete patient-donor types, a type-level directed compatibility relation \(A\subseteq T\times T\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), compute the maximum total mass that can be assigned to vertex-disjoint exchange cycles of lengths at most \(k\). Equivalently, choose \(x_p\ge0\) for every compatible cyclic type pattern \(p\) of length \(2\le |p|\le k\), subject to \(\sum_p a_{p,t}x_p\le\mu_t\) for each \(t\in T\), maximizing \(\sum_p |p|x_p\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity kidney-exchange pool has types representing complete compatibility twins, mass capacity \(\mu_t\) for each type, and decision variables \(x_p\) assigning mass to integral cycle patterns. The objective is maximum covered patient-donor mass, subject to type-capacity constraints; for fixed \(k\), this is an explicit LP over at most \(\sum_{\ell=2}^{k}\tau^\ell\) patterns.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous mirror of Theorem 3, the paper’s strongest computational result.

Theorem 3, proved in this paper rather than cited, states that for every fixed integer \(k\ge3\), max size \(\le k\)-way exchange is APX-complete. The input is a simple directed graph \(D=(V,A)\); a feasible exchange is a vertex-disjoint collection of directed cycles of length at most \(k\); and the objective is to maximize the number of covered vertices. The paper proves APX-hardness by an L-reduction from APX-complete vertex-disjoint triangle packing: Lemma 1 orients a 3-partite graph cyclically, and Lemma 2 subdivides arcs so that triangles become directed \(k\)-cycles. Thus the theorem is a genuine hardness result for bounded-length kidney exchange, not merely an encoding or an experimental observation.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuous Maximum-Mass \(\le k\)-Way Exchange\(_\infty\). Fix \(k\ge3\). An instance consists of a finite set \(T\) of patient-donor types, a directed compatibility relation \(A\subseteq T\times T\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). A type is a complete compatibility description: two pairs of the same type have identical incoming and outgoing compatibility and are indistinguishable for the objective. A feasible cycle pattern is a cyclic word \(p=(t_1,\ldots,t_\ell)\), \(2\le\ell\le k\), such that \(t_i\to t_{i+1}\in A\), including \(t_\ell\to t_1\). Repeated type labels are allowed when there is enough mass of that type.

For each pattern \(p\), choose \(x_p\ge0\), interpreted as the mass of exchanges using that pattern. If \(a_{p,t}\) is the number of times type \(t\) occurs in \(p\), feasibility is

\[ \sum_p a_{p,t}x_p\le \mu_t \qquad\text{for every }t\in T. \]

The objective is

\[ \max \sum_p |p|x_p. \]

A solution is the vector \(x\), or an equivalent measurable partition of each type’s population into cycle roles. Every individual still participates in an integral cycle; only the population of such cycles is continuous. Thus this is not divisible transplantation or a fractional kidney. It is precisely the paper’s cycle-packing problem with individual vertex capacities replaced by aggregate type masses.

The regime is a large, periodically cleared kidney-exchange pool: perhaps tens of thousands of incompatible pairs but only tens or hundreds of compatibility classes. Types could record the features actually used by the programme—blood-group class, sensitization category, donor-recipient compatibility class, geography, or risk band. The key assumption is not that all patients are medically identical, but that many pairs share the same complete description for this optimization problem. This is a natural setting because pairs are pooled before optimization and have no arrival-time identity in the paper’s model.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A: exactly tractable for every fixed \(k\). There are at most

\[ \sum_{\ell=2}^{k}\tau^\ell \]

cycle patterns, where \(\tau=|T|\). Hence the problem is an explicitly representable linear program of polynomial size in \(\tau\) for fixed \(k\), and can be solved in polynomial time in the input length.

This is a meaningful continuization of Theorem 3, not a weakened problem chosen merely because it is easy. The compatibility relation, cycle-length restriction, vertex-capacity interpretation, and maximum-transplant objective are unchanged. The only change is that a type contains many interchangeable agents, so the numbers of cycles are real-valued masses rather than integers. In the paper’s APX-hardness reduction, the obstruction is exactly integral vertex-disjointness. After continuization, the reduction produces fractional triangle packing, which is an LP. The discrete APX-hardness therefore dissolves for the intended high-multiplicity regime.

The high-multiplicity bridge is also concrete. If type masses have common denominator \(N\), then \(N\mu_t\) is the number of discrete pairs of type \(t\). A basic optimal LP solution uses at most \(\tau\) positive cycle-pattern variables. Rounding those variables down to multiples of \(1/N\) gives a feasible discrete exchange losing at most \(k\tau\) transplants. Thus the mirror supplies both an exact fluid answer and an additive approximation for sufficiently repeated populations.

This mirror covers Theorem 3 and deliberately does not claim to mirror the paper’s parameterized Theorem 7 or its approximation analyses. It raises natural extensions: type-level arc weights, type-level cycle-risk weights, variable \(k\), unbounded cycles, altruistic chains, and robustness to failures of a positive mass of pairs. For fixed \(k\), weighted versions with weights depending only on the cycle’s type pattern remain LPs; variable-length versions may require a genuine pricing or separation analysis.

The weakest point is medical realism. In actual kidney exchange, HLA compatibility and late clinical discoveries may be highly pair-specific, producing almost one type per pair and weakening the \(n\gg\tau\) story. But that is a limitation of the proposed application regime, not of the mathematical mirror: the paper itself abstracts to a directed compatibility graph, and a large cohort governed by repeated compatibility classes is a legitimate high-multiplicity realization of that graph model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proponent’s anchor is that the proposed type regime is medically and structurally fragile. A type must include the complete compatibility signature: all incoming and outgoing compatibilities, together with any weights or risks used by the objective. In genuine kidney-exchange data, HLA and crossmatch information is often pair-specific, so these signatures may be nearly unique. If one instead groups pairs by coarse features such as blood group or sensitization band, compatibility is no longer determined by type, and the proposed cycle-pattern LP permits exchanges that may not exist among the actual pairs.

That gives a sharp modelling dilemma. Exact compatibility signatures produce mostly singleton types, so there is little population multiplicity. Coarse clinical types produce many agents per type, but they describe a different exchange problem unless compatibility is genuinely type-based. The proposed “large pool with tens of compatibility classes” is therefore not supported by the paper’s empirical model; it is an additional, potentially artificial assumption.

The better version of the mirror—defining types as exact compatibility twins—does evade that objection. But it also reveals its limitation. The continuous instance is then merely a blow-up of a smaller directed graph: each type is a clone class, and all the combinatorial structure resides in the type graph. For fixed \(k\), the continuous problem has only \(\sum_{\ell=2}^{k}\tau^\ell\) cycle patterns and is an explicit linear program. It requires neither column generation nor a nontrivial pricing theorem. The mirror therefore turns Theorem 3 into a standard fractional bounded-cycle-packing formulation, rather than exposing a substantial new continuous-computational phenomenon.

That is a legitimate criticism of priority, but not a decisive objection under this programme’s rules. The programme explicitly regards a tractable high-multiplicity version as valuable, even when discrete hardness disappears, and it does not require the continuous formulation to preserve the paper’s APX-hardness. The rounding argument also gives a real bridge back to repeated finite instances. There is no identity-dependent objective, no vanishing cost, and no loss of integral cycles: only the population of interchangeable pairs is fractional.

So the honest negative conclusion is limited. The kidney-exchange interpretation is weak unless the authors can document genuine repeated compatibility signatures, and the resulting fixed-\(k\) LP may be too elementary to deserve major attention. But those points do not defeat the anchor itself. A valid high-multiplicity scenario exists, and Theorem 3 has a coherent continuous mirror. I cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
