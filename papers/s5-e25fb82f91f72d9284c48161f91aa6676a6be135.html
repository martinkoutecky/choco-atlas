The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete applicant types \(T\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), type preferences and school priorities, and per-capita capacities \(q_c\) and reserve quotas \(q_c^R\), compute the MR and JSA mass assignments by type. For instances satisfying high competitiveness, \(\sum_{t\in T_m}x^{MR}_{t,c}\le q_c^R\) for every school \(c\), decide whether for every disadvantaged type and every preference cutoff the mass assigned to schools at least that preferred under JSA is at least the corresponding mass under MR.

The model it lives in

Use finite-support applicant types carrying preferences, disadvantaged status, and school priorities, with total mass one and rational per-capita school capacities and reserves. Represent each mechanism by an assignment matrix \(x_{t,c}\); split type mass at capacity cutoffs in deferred acceptance on the expanded general-seat and reserved-seat instance, then compare each disadvantaged type’s assignment distributions by first-order stochastic dominance.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the deferred-acceptance representations for MR and JSA and the MR/JSA welfare comparison under high competitiveness. It leaves the DISC results, other mechanism properties and comparisons, empirical analyses, and the asymptotic sufficient conditions in Theorems 4.3 and 4.4 outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Specify when splitting a type at a priority cutoff is the correct high-multiplicity interpretation of the paper’s strict individual priorities and tie-breaks.
  • Prove that the mass version of high competitiveness implies the stated type-level stochastic-dominance comparison, and bound the algorithm in terms of \(m\), τ, and input bit length.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuum version of the paper’s MR-versus-JSA comparison. The natural regime is a large admissions cohort with many applicants but relatively few complete applicant types. A type would include disadvantaged status, the applicant’s school ranking, and their priority at each school. Mass is the fraction of applicants of that type; capacities and reserved seats become per-capita shares. A continuum allocation may split a type’s mass across schools.

The closest result to anchor this in the paper is Theorem 4.2, proved here: if, under minority reserve, the disadvantaged mass assigned to each school is at most that school’s reservation quota, then the joint seat allocation matching weakly dominates the minority reserve matching for every disadvantaged student. This is a strong substantive bridge to a population model. But it is an outcome theorem, not a computational-complexity result, so it does not meet the prompt’s anchor requirement. Theorem 4.3 and Theorem 4.4 give asymptotic conditions for high competitiveness; they also do not classify a computational problem. Propositions C.2 and C.3 relate the finite mechanisms to deferred acceptance on expanded instances, but do not establish a named complexity result.

A plausible new question, if the authors accepted the continuum extension, would be the Continuum MR/JSA Dominance Problem: given rational type masses, school rankings and priorities over types, per-capita capacities, and per-capita reserve quotas, compute the stable mass allocations under MR and JSA and decide whether JSA’s assignment distribution for every disadvantaged type first-order stochastically dominates MR’s. That means that, at every preference-rank cutoff, at least as much of the type’s mass receives a school at least that preferred under JSA. The solution would be the two allocation matrices and a yes/no dominance result. I would expect this to be tractable for finite support, via a type-level deferred-acceptance or flow formulation, but that would be a new result, not one established by this paper. Its scope is specifically the MR/JSA comparison under high competitiveness.

The mirror is plausible because the paper’s own NYC setting has about 27,000 applicants competing for seats at eight schools, and the proposed question preserves the mechanisms and the individual-welfare comparison at the center of Theorem 4.2. The weak point is that the theorem compares each individual’s assignment, while a continuum type can split across schools. First-order stochastic dominance is a defensible mass-level analogue, but it is an extension the authors would need to accept and prove. So there is a credible continuous-population research direction here, but no anchor-compliant positive case based on a named complexity result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational-complexity result for ChoCo to mirror. Theorems 3.6 and 3.7 and Theorem 4.2 establish welfare comparisons; Propositions 3.4 and 3.8 establish stability properties; Theorems 4.3 and 4.4 give probabilistic sufficient conditions. The computations use deferred acceptance and compare its outcomes on data. Theorem 4.2 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but turning it into a continuous question would be a new computational problem, not a continuous counterpart to a complexity result in the paper.

The proposed MR/JSA problem is coherent, but its natural finite-support version looks like a direct extension of the machinery already in the paper. Appendix C represents both mechanisms as deferred acceptance on expanded instances. With finitely many types and rational masses, a mass version can process proposals by type and split mass at capacity cutoffs; first-order stochastic dominance can then be checked at the finite school-rank cutoffs. That may be useful, but it gives little reason to expect the complexity landscape ChoCo is designed to chart. The paper has already isolated the matching structure; the proposed question mostly asks to run it on masses.

The difficulty is that the paper’s relevant types must include priority as well as preferences and group. In its NYC setting, priority depends on test score and a lottery tie-break, so applicants are largely distinguishable by the very data that determine admission. Treating score bands as types discards priority distinctions at school cutoffs and can change assignments. Keeping exact priority positions avoids that distortion, but makes the number of types grow with the applicant population, so it does not provide a high-multiplicity regime. A continuous priority quantile would preserve the ordering, but it moves to a distribution over a continuum of priorities rather than the finite-support rational-mass input proposed by ChoCo. In that atomless model, individual deviations and priority changes have zero mass; the proposed stochastic-dominance comparison is a new distributional welfare notion, rather than the paper’s comparison of the same students across mechanisms.

Theorem 4.3 and Theorem 4.4 do not solve this problem for the proponent. They already study large random markets, but their results are asymptotic statements about when high competitiveness holds, not computational questions over a supplied population distribution. The number of schools grows in those theorems, while the NYC case motivating the paper has eight. Turning the results into an algorithmic question over arbitrary type distributions would again be a new model, not a mirror of a computational result. And the high-multiplicity literature cited by the paper is supporting evidence that such models can make sense, not a novelty objection.

Still, the universal negative case is weak. A market with many applicants in finitely many preference and priority classes, with capacities and reserves scaled to population, is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Mass assignments that split a class at a cutoff are interpretable, and the MR/JSA welfare comparison has a natural stochastic-dominance analogue. Theorem 4.2’s argument may carry over to that setting. I can argue that this would add little to ChoCo’s computational programme; I cannot honestly rule out that it is a worthwhile continuous mirror the authors would recognize.
