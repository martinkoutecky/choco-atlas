The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), positive rational voting weights \(a_t\), and rational quota fraction \(\lambda\in(0,1)\), form finite weighted voting games with \(M\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\) and quota \(\lambda M\sum_t\mu_t a_t\), for admissible replication sizes \(M\) at which the masses and quota align with the integer weight lattice. For each game, let \(\eta_t(M)\) count coalitions of the other voters in which a type-\(t\) voter is critical, and set \(\Pi_t(M)=\frac{M\mu_t\eta_t(M)}{\sum_s M\mu_s\eta_s(M)}\). Prove the limit of \((\Pi_t(M))_{t\in T}\) exists independently of the admissible replication sequence, and compute it to additive error \(\varepsilon\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over voter types \(t\), each with voting weight \(a_t\). The observable is each type’s share of total Banzhaf swings in growing finite replications of that society; the computation aggregates pivotality across same-type voters before taking the limit.

The objection that survived

The proposed output is a limit of finite pivotal-coalition counts, not an index defined directly on an atomless society; independence from the admissible replication sequence remains to be proved.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the aggregate Banzhaf-share vector converge to the same limit along every admissible replication sequence?
  • Can the limit be computed with polynomial bit complexity in the support size, input bit length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), including a proof of the proposed scalar-root formula?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is for Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper: for a weighted voting game with \(k\) possible voter weights, all Banzhaf indices can be computed in \(O(n^k)\) time. It is an algorithmic anchor, but it describes exactly the regime that has a natural high-multiplicity interpretation: many voters, with only a few distinct weight types. The paper’s two-weight result, Theorem 4.2, is a special case, so I would not add it as a separate anchor.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Limit-BI. An instance is a finite support \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), positive rational voting weights \(a_t\), and a quota fraction \(\lambda\in(0,1)\). A type is a shareholder with voting weight \(a_t\); \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of the shareholder population. The continuous society has total weight \(W=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t\), and its quota is \(\lambda W\).

The requested solution is the vector of aggregate Banzhaf power shares by type. Define it through finite approximations: take population sizes \(M\) for which each \(M\mu_t\) and the quota \(Q_M=\lambda MW\) are integral, after scaling weights to integers and aligning the quota with their common lattice. There are \(M\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\). For a voter \(i\) of type \(t\), let \(\eta_t(M)\) be the number of coalitions of the other voters whose weight is below \(Q_M\) but reaches at least \(Q_M\) when \(i\) joins. The type’s aggregate normalized Banzhaf power is

\[ \Pi_t(M)= \frac{M\mu_t\,\eta_t(M)} {\sum_{s\in T}M\mu_s\,\eta_s(M)}. \]

Limit-BI asks for the limit of this vector as \(M\) grows, returned to additive error \(\varepsilon\). The coalition remains the action being measured: in continuous coordinates it selects mass \(x_t\) from each type, and passes the decision threshold when \(\sum_t a_t x_t\geq\lambda W\). The power measure is the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s own count of coalitions in which a voter is critical, aggregated over voters who are identical by type.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. For integer-scaled weights, a standard asymptotic coefficient calculation suggests a one-dimensional computation. Find \(z>0\) satisfying

\[ \sum_t \mu_t a_t\frac{z^{a_t}}{1+z^{a_t}}=\lambda W. \]

Then the limiting type shares are given by normalizing \(\mu_t h_t(z)\), where

\[ h_t(z)=\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{a_t}z^r}{1+z^{a_t}}. \]

At the midpoint quota, \(z=1\), so the shares simplify to \(\Pi_t=\mu_t a_t/W\). The general-quota formula and its approximation guarantees are new work, not results claimed by this paper. They make the expected direction concrete: a scalar root computation rather than enumeration of coalitions. Further questions include proving the limit is independent of the admissible replication sequence, establishing bit-complexity and error bounds, and identifying what changes when the support size grows rather than staying small.

The scenario is a large company with many shareholders holding one of a small number of standardized voting blocks. Voters with the same block are indistinguishable for this problem; their number can be very large while the number of types remains small. This is close to the paper’s own joint-stock-company motivation. The mirror also preserves the paper’s notion of voting power rather than making outcomes continuous: it changes the population description and reports influence at the type level.

The weakest point is that an atomless voter cannot individually swing a threshold decision. The mirror therefore has to report aggregate type power as the limit of finite Banzhaf indices; it cannot promise a nonzero continuum index for each individual. That aggregation is natural because same-type voters have identical indices, but a referee could reasonably ask whether the limit is the right continuous observable. The paper’s hardness result, Theorem 5.8, is not a stronger anchor for this particular case: its 2-unbalanced instances encode complexity in many distinct weights, and repeating those voters to create a high-multiplicity regime generally breaks the class condition. I would not present that theorem as evidence that hardness transfers here.

This case covers Theorem 4.3’s bounded-number-of-weight-values result, not the paper’s full classification. The bridge is strongest precisely where the theorem has few types and many voters.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.3 is the hardest anchor to defeat. It is a named computational result, and its \(k\) weight classes are exactly \(k\) types with potentially large multiplicities. The paper itself points to joint-stock companies, and many shareholders holding standardized voting blocks is a credible regime. Neither “no computational result” nor “no sensible high-multiplicity scenario” works here.

The strongest objection is that the Banzhaf index counts coalitions of individual players, while a distribution \(\mu\) over weight types does not itself provide a way to count—or randomly sample—subcoalitions. In an atomless society, adding one individual has zero mass and cannot change the threshold. The proposed mirror therefore gets its nonzero observable from a sequence of finite societies, then aggregates and normalizes pivotality by type. That is a meaningful asymptotic statistic, but it is not a Banzhaf index directly defined on the continuous society; away from a half quota, it describes the relative composition of exponentially rare pivotal coalitions.

That objection does not defeat the proponent’s best repair. Fixing rational masses, primitive integer weights, and an aligned quota makes the replication limit precise, while type aggregation addresses the vanishing power of any one atomless voter. If the limit and its approximation error can be established, it supplies a computational question about large populations with a small number of types. The paper’s focus on exact finite computation gives some reason to doubt that its authors would regard this asymptotic approximation as the same problem, but it is not enough to rule out a worthwhile mirror.

So the negative case is weak at its central point: the anchor survives. The strongest honest reservation is about whether a limit of finite coalition counts is an object of the continuous model, rather than a distinct asymptotic voting-power problem. That reservation deserves attention, but I cannot support the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile continuous mirror.
