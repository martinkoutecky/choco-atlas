What the mirror covers

The proposed computation covers evaluating the threshold rule over ranking types with population masses; it does not computationally mirror Theorem 4.1's axiomatic characterization.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 4.1, proved here, characterizes delegation rules: a rule satisfies Pareto optimality, consistency, support neutrality, and strategy-proofness if and only if it is a threshold rule for some \(f:\mathbb Z_{++}\to(1/2,1]\) with \(f(t+1)\ge (f(t)+1)/2\). That is an axiomatic characterization, not a result about whether a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise. Proposition 3.1 establishes that threshold rules are well-defined, but likewise makes no complexity claim. The paper’s central results are axiomatic, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The most plausible computational add-on would take a large society whose agents share rankings from a small set of types. Let \(\mu\) give each reported ranking its population fraction, and, given \(f\), ask for the threshold delegation: the smallest \(t\) whose \(t\) most-supported rankings have cumulative mass at least \(f(t)\), returning those rankings as the delegates. This is a sensible continuous-population scenario, with \(\tau\) distinct rankings potentially far fewer than the number of residents. But it merely evaluates an already specified rule, essentially by sorting the \(\tau\) masses. It is not a named result of the paper, nor does it mirror the theorem’s axiomatic question. I therefore would not present it as an anchored mirror or assign it a meaningful place in the tractable/hardness trichotomy.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive negative is the lack of a computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.1 characterizes the delegation rules satisfying four normative axioms; Proposition 3.1 establishes that the threshold rules are well-defined. Neither makes a claim about algorithmic complexity.

A continuous society is quite natural here: many people can share a small set of rankings, and the paper’s rules depend on their support. The strongest direct computational question is to take a mass distribution \(\mu\) and a threshold function \(f\), then compute the resulting delegation. Sort the \(\tau\) supported rankings by mass and scan cumulative support until it reaches \(f(t)\). That takes \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) mass comparisons and \(O(\tau)\) threshold checks, apart from reading the rankings and doing exact arithmetic. There is no search over all \(m!\) rankings: Proposition 2.1 ensures delegates are reported rankings. This evaluates a specified rule; it does not mirror the theorem’s axiomatic classification or yield a computational landscape.

Trying to preserve Theorem 4.1 as the anchor leads to the same problem. Every rational distribution over rankings is the normalized support of some finite profile, and the threshold formula extends directly to real-valued masses. That makes the continuous-domain extension immediate. The resulting classification remains axiomatic, while evaluating the rule remains simple.

A more ambitious proposal might add costs for shifting population mass between rankings and ask for the least-cost way to produce a desired delegation. That could be a worthwhile adjacent control problem. The paper supplies no such costs, target, or intervention objective; its strategyproofness condition is a normative property of a rule. Adding those ingredients creates a new problem rather than a continuous mirror of a named result here. I cannot prove that no adjacent model could interest these authors, but the paper itself offers no substantive computational anchor for ChoCo.
