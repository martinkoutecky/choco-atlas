The continuous mirror question

Given a fixed model set \(C\), target \(p\), and task types \(T\) with strict rankings \(\pi_t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-unit training costs \(\gamma_t\), and budget \(\beta\), is there a choice of trained mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t\gamma_t x_t\le\beta\) such that \(p\) is a Borda winner? For each type, training its chosen mass replaces \(\pi_t\) by \(\pi_t^p\), the ranking with \(p\) moved to the top while preserving the other models’ order; the resulting score is \(S_c(x)=\sum_t((\mu_t-x_t)b_{\pi_t}(c)+x_t b_{\pi_t^p}(c))\), and the condition is \(S_p(x)\ge S_c(x)\) for every \(c\in C\).

The model it lives in

A fixed set of models and target, with a distribution over task types defined by each type’s ranking and per-unit training cost. Decision variables \(x_t\) transfer task mass from its original ranking to the ranking with the target first; minimize \(\sum_t\gamma_t x_t\) subject to the Borda winner inequalities.

The objection that survived

The proponent identifies modularity and task-local training effects as conditions, but gives no evidence that real benchmark federations with repeated ranking-and-cost types satisfy them; the proposed regime remains plausible rather than demonstrated.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Borda hardness result in Corollary 3.3; Theorem 4.5 supplies a parallel mean-win-rate mass formulation. The arithmetic-mean and median results, pairwise-majority formulation, and empirical suite measurements are not covered.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a federation of \(N\) equal-mass tasks, how large can the gap be between the mass LP optimum and the minimum whole-task selection cost, as a function of \(m\), \(\tau\), and input bit length?
  • Which benchmark construction regimes make repeated ranking-and-cost types and task-local training effects plausible at scale?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a continuous task population: keep the model set fixed and make the benchmark’s task mass the continuous object. That follows the paper’s own identification of datasets as voters and models as candidates; it does not continuize model scores or leaderboard outcomes.

The lead anchor is Corollary 3.3, which states that the Benchmark-Specific Training Problem is NP-hard under Borda count. The corollary is proved in this paper: its proof uses the paper’s shift-bribery correspondence and cited NP-completeness results for Borda shift bribery, including the all-or-nothing-price case. The paper’s Proposition 3.2 supplies the bridge: under a monotone ordinal benchmark operator, choosing datasets to train on corresponds exactly to shift bribery with all-or-nothing prices.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large benchmark federation with many separately scored task units—for example, across languages, domains, templates, or data sources. Fix the competing models. A task’s type records its complete ranking of those models and its per-unit training cost; tasks of the same type have the same ranking and cost. Training can move the target to the top on the selected task mass, as in the paper’s maximal-gain assumption. The total number of task units can be large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct types is much smaller. The paper’s particular suites of 24 and 57 tasks do not themselves establish this regime; the federation is the scenario that makes the mirror plausible.

Call the lead problem Borda Task-Mass Shift Bribery. Its input is a fixed set \(C\) of \(m\) models, a target \(p\), a finite support \(T\) of strict-ranking types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a rational cost density \(\gamma_t\) for each type, and a budget \(\beta\). Let \(\pi_t\) be type \(t\)’s ranking and \(\pi_t^p\) the ranking after moving \(p\) to the top while preserving the relative order of all other models. Choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), the mass of type \(t\) trained on, with \(\sum_t\gamma_t x_t\le\beta\). The resulting Borda score of model \(c\) is
\[ S_c(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\bigl((\mu_t-x_t)b_{\pi_t}(c)+x_t b_{\pi_t^p}(c)\bigr), \]
where \(b_\pi(c)\) is the Borda score in ranking \(\pi\). The question is whether there is such an \(x\) for which \(S_p(x)\ge S_c(x)\) for every \(c\in C\); a solution is the vector \(x\). Equivalently, minimize \(\sum_t\gamma_t x_t\) subject to those inequalities.

This is a linear program with \(\tau\) variables and \(m-1\) winner constraints, so with explicit support it is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the rational input length \(L\). The expected outcome is Class A: allowing divisible task mass removes the discrete selection step behind the NP-hardness. The costs are per unit of mass; in a finite benchmark with \(N\) equal-mass tasks, they correspond to costs normalized by \(N\).

A second, related anchor is Theorem 4.5, proved in the paper and used in its computation. It gives an exact binary program for \(k_{\mathrm{win}}\), the minimum number of datasets whose training makes the target top under mean win rate: minimize \(\sum_D x_D\) subject to \(\sum_D q_D(A)x_D\ge m\Delta_{\mathrm{win}}(A_1,A)\) for every rival \(A\), with \(x_D\in\{0,1\}\). The theorem is a formulation result; the paper states immediately afterward that solving it is NP-hard in general, since a special case is Set Cover.

Its continuous counterpart is Mean-Win-Rate Task-Mass Robustness. Use the same population types, and let \(w_t(a)\) be model \(a\)’s win rate on type \(t\). Define the baseline gap against rival \(a\) as \(d(a)=\sum_t\mu_t(w_t(a)-w_t(p))\), and let \(q_t(a)\) be the reduction in that gap from shifting type \(t\) to put \(p\) first. Choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) to minimize the manipulated mass \(\sum_t x_t\), subject to
\[ \sum_t q_t(a)x_t\ge \max\{0,d(a)\}\qquad\text{for every }a\ne p. \]
A feasible vector makes \(p\) top-ranked under mean win rate. This is again an LP, now with one constraint per rival, and is expected to be Class A. Its multiple simultaneous rival constraints preserve the central robustness question in Theorem 4.5; it is not merely a pairwise simplification.

Both questions cover the paper’s ordinal manipulation results, with the first directly mirroring Corollary 3.3 and the second mirroring its global mean-win-rate robustness problem. They do not claim to continuize the paper’s arithmetic-mean or median results, experiments, or every benchmark-manipulation setting. For a finite benchmark of \(N\) equally weighted tasks, the type masses are multiples of \(1/N\); requiring each \(x_t\) to use those same increments recovers the discrete selection constraint. This gives a concrete bridge to study, including how much rounding a continuous optimum costs.

The weak point is whether benchmark tasks really form a high-multiplicity population. The paper’s small suites are not such a population, and in practice task rankings and training costs may be very diverse; training can also affect other tasks. The mirror is strongest for a federation of modular, separately scored tasks with repeated ranking-and-cost profiles and task-local training effects. That is a conditional regime, not a claim about all benchmarks. It remains credible because the paper itself makes tasks the voters, and its shift-bribery equivalence makes moving from finitely many task voters to mass over task types a recognizable continuation of the authors’ problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has two named computational results to mirror, so the strongest negative case cannot be that it is merely axiomatic or analytical. The concern is whether its task units can honestly be treated as a population.

The paper’s benchmark instances contain 24 or 57 distinct tasks. Those are curated measurement instruments, not obviously members of a large, interchangeable population: “22% of voters” has a familiar meaning, while “22% of benchmark tasks” may just reflect how a designer chose to weight the suite. Treating tasks as a population risks turning benchmark composition into the object of study. Counting model entries does not help: models are alternatives in this setup, not the population being continuized.

That concern applies to both anchors. Corollary 3.3 gives a real NP-hardness result for Borda benchmark-specific training. Its proposed mass version has variables \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) and linear winner constraints, so it is a well-posed relaxation. But to make it an instance of the paper’s manipulation problem, fractional mass must stand for training on a fraction of otherwise equivalent, separately scored tasks. Splitting a task into examples does not automatically supply those units: training on examples need not produce independent, task-local score changes. Conversely, duplicating identical tasks may amount to changing their weight in the benchmark rather than revealing a natural task population.

The same issue reaches Theorem 4.5. Replacing its binary variables with masses yields a covering LP, with constraints \(\sum_t q_t(a)x_t\ge d(a)_+\) for each rival \(a\). That preserves the global mean-win-rate robustness question. Yet its population interpretation still needs divisible or repeated task units whose manipulation effects are interchangeable. In the paper’s setting, the units are named datasets selected whole.

The best response is a federation of many separately scored modules across languages, domains, or templates, with repeated ranking-and-cost profiles. That is a credible high-multiplicity regime if the modules are sufficiently independent and their task mass is meaningful. In that regime, both continuous questions survive; the second is especially hard to dismiss because the paper already provides the covering formulation that becomes an LP.

So the negative case is weak where it matters most. The fixed suites do not themselves establish a natural task population, and a task-mass model could be benchmark reweighting in disguise. But the federation scenario answers that objection well enough that I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
