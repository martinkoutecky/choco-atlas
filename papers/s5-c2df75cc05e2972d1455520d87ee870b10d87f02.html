The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that there is no qualifying anchor here. Theorem 1 proves almost-sure concentration of beliefs; Theorem 2 gives a high-probability convergence bound; and Theorem 3 is a general concentration inequality. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s update rule is an algorithm in the everyday sense, but its results analyze the dynamics rather than ask whether a social-choice task is tractable. Under the stated anchor rule, there is no continuous computational question to attach to a named result.

The strongest alternative would model a continuum of agent types, with each type recording its signal distribution, likelihoods, prior, and communication role, and an interaction kernel describing how beliefs are aggregated. That could be a sensible model for repeated cohorts in a sensor or social network. Its natural questions—whether beliefs concentrate and at what rate—are analytic learning questions, however, much like the paper’s own results and its suggested extension in the number of agents. To make this a ChoCo problem, one might instead optimize the network or decide whether a target rate is achievable. But the paper does not formulate those tasks: evaluating its displayed bound is direct arithmetic, while rate optimization or network design adds a new objective.

So the negative case is strong for rejecting this paper as a ChoCo mirror, but it does not establish that no continuous-agent study of the paper’s dynamics could be worthwhile. The honest objection is the absence of a computational anchor, not that a plausible continuum model cannot be built.
