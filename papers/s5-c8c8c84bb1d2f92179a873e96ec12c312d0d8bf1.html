The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible mirror here, but it comes with a decisive qualification: this paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Theorem 2, Corollary 2, Proposition 1, and Lemmas 1–4 are asymptotic equilibrium, stability, convergence, and concentration results. None says that an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or gives an algorithmic formulation whose complexity is analysed. DA is described algorithmically, but no numbered result establishes its complexity. Under the strict anchor rule, therefore, there is no eligible computational anchor, and I would not pretend otherwise.

The strongest positive case is nevertheless a genuine computational extension of the paper’s central object. Consider a large admissions system with \(C\) schools and \(N\gg\tau\) applicants, where applicants fall into \(\tau\) complete types. A type \(t\) contains its utility vector \(u_t\), priority-score vector \(s_t\), acceptable colleges, and true ranking \(\rho(t)\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of applicants of type \(t\), and let school capacities be \(NS_c\). This could describe repeated cohorts facing discrete priority bands and a finite set of preference profiles. The important point is that the mass is population mass: each finite applicant still receives one integral school, while \(z_{t,c}\) records the aggregate mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(c\).

For a report \(R\), cutoff vector \(p\), and type \(t\), let \(M(t,R;p)\) be the highest-ranked college in \(R\) satisfying \(s_{t,c}\ge p_c\), or the outside option if none exists. If \(y_{t,R}\) is the mass of type \(t\) submitting \(R\), aggregate demand is

\[ D_c(p;y)=\sum_{t,R} y_{t,R}\mathbf{1}\{M(t,R;p)=c\}. \]

The applicant-proposing mass version of DA generates the least market-clearing cutoff vector \(p(y)\). This gives a precise continuous population model whose variables are report masses \(y_{t,R}\), cutoffs \(p\), and assignment masses \(z_{t,c}\). It is not merely fractionalising outcomes: the continuous object is the society.

My lead candidate is a computational version of the paper’s Theorem 2, proved here: “Any regular robust equilibrium is asymptotically stable.” Define \(f_t(p)\) as type \(t\)’s most-preferred feasible college at \(p\). Call a report \(R\) a stable-response report if \(M(t,R;p)=f_t(p)\). A regular mass-report profile requires \(y_{t,\rho(t)}\ge \gamma\mu_t\) for every type \(t\), and it must be implementable by finite economies with \(N\mu_t\) agents whose maximum unilateral regret tends to zero.

The continuous problem is:

\[ \textsc{Continuum Robust-DA Instability}: \]

given \((T,\mu,u,s,S,\gamma)\), find a regular asymptotically robust report profile \(y\) and its DA cutoffs \(p(y)\) maximizing

\[ I(y)=\sum_{t,R}y_{t,R}\mathbf{1}\{M(t,R;p(y))\ne f_t(p(y))\}. \]

Equivalently, decide whether \(I(y)>\varepsilon\) is possible. Theorem 2 predicts that, under the paper’s full-support and regularity conditions, the optimum asymptotically collapses to \(0\): robust mistakes may be widespread, but the mass receiving a non-favorite feasible college vanishes. Corollary 2 strengthens this: for any regular robust equilibrium, the mass receiving a different assignment from truthful DA also converges to \(0\).

This is a plausible mirror because it preserves everything essential: applicant-proposing DA, priority scores, strategic misreports, cutoffs, robust best responses, and stability. It does not replace the authors’ problem by ordinary fractional matching. The new question is computational: given a high-multiplicity population description, can one compute the cutoff fixed point, certify robustness, and calculate the worst possible instability? With fixed \(C\) and explicitly represented reports, aggregate DA itself should be tractable; the difficult part is the equilibrium/search layer. With variable \(C\), the \(C!\) report space and discontinuous cutoff responses could create genuinely difficult cases.

A second, weaker candidate follows Theorem 1, proved here: there exists a cutoff vector \(p\) such that, for arbitrarily small \(\delta,\gamma>0\), types within \(\delta\) of a cutoff report truthfully, while all other types mix truthful reporting with probability \(\gamma\) and a non-WTT stable-response report with probability \(1-\gamma\). Corollary 1 states that there is a robust equilibrium in which every applicant uses a non-WTT, hence non-truthful, strategy with probability arbitrarily close to one.

Its computational mirror is \(\textsc{Non-WTT Mass Maximization}\): choose \(y\) and the induced cutoffs \(p(y)\), subject to regularity, DA market clearing, and the requirement that types with \(|s_{t,c}-p_c|\le\delta\) for some \(c\) report truthfully, while other types may use only stable-response reports. Maximize

\[ \operatorname{NWTT}(y) =\sum_{t,R\text{ non-WTT}} y_{t,R}. \]

The paper’s Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 predict \(\operatorname{NWTT}(y)\to1\) as \(\delta,\gamma\to0\), provided the type distribution is sufficiently smooth that the cutoff-boundary mass vanishes. This is a particularly clean continuous question: the decision variable is mass assigned to reports, and the objective is the population share making mistakes. It is also faithful to the paper because it does not assume mistakes disappear; it asks how much mistaken behaviour can coexist with the same aggregate outcome.

The authors would recognise both questions as their problem. The paper itself explicitly interprets \((\eta,S)\) as a continuum economy, and its proofs revolve around continuum demand functions and cutoff convergence. The mirror therefore should not be sold as discovering continuum matching. Its contribution would be to take the paper’s continuum limit and make it an explicit high-multiplicity computational input, asking for exact algorithms, approximation guarantees, or complexity classifications.

The weakest point is substantial. The paper already writes down the continuum population model, so a naive mirror would be a novelty collision. The only defensible positive case is the additional computational layer. Moreover, the paper’s full-support assumption is doing real work: its Example 1 shows that repeated types with discontinuous priorities can produce large rejection chains and destroy price-taking. Thus the finite-type mirror needs an analogous smoothness or non-pivotality condition; it cannot simply assert that every repeated-type admissions market inherits Theorem 2.

So my honest position is: the continuous-population mirror is substantively plausible and preserves the paper’s question very well, especially through \(\textsc{Continuum Robust-DA Instability}\). But the paper offers no eligible named computational anchor, and the underlying continuum model is already present. Under the programme’s strict grading rule, this is a promising computational continuation of the paper, not yet a fully anchored positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s entry gate: this paper has no eligible computational anchor. Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Theorem 2, Corollary 2, Proposition 1, and Lemmas 1–4 concern equilibrium, convergence, concentration, and stability. None asserts membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), NP-hardness, fixed-parameter tractability, or provides an algorithmic complexity result. DA is described as a mechanism, but its computational complexity is not a theorem of the paper. The proposed problems would therefore be new computational projects inspired by the paper, not continuous mirrors of computational results in it.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 2 is also not yet a well-defined high-multiplicity problem. The paper’s robust equilibrium is a property of an infinite, identity-indexed strategy profile \( \bsigma=(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\ldots) \): for every \( \epsilon>0 \), there must be a threshold \(K\) after which every finite random economy is an interim \( \epsilon \)-BNE. A mass vector \(y_{t,R}\) does not encode that sequence, the identity-specific strategies, or the rate and mode of convergence. The phrase “implementable by finite economies with maximum unilateral regret tending to zero” hides the entire equilibrium definition rather than giving a finite certificate for it.

This is not a cosmetic concern. The paper’s own appendix constructs a regular asymmetric strategy profile for which cutoffs fail to converge along the full sequence and only converge on a subsequence. Thus a single report-mass vector need not determine a limiting cutoff \(p(y)\) at all. Imposing symmetry and finite types would repair that defect only by discarding the asymmetric strategy profiles and the subsequence argument that are part of Theorem 2. Replacing robust equilibrium by a fixed-\(N\), finite-\(\epsilon\) equilibrium would produce a legitimate new matching-game problem, but not a mirror of the theorem’s asymptotic object.

There is a second mismatch. In a literal continuum game, one agent has zero mass, so a unilateral report cannot change aggregate demand or cutoffs. The finite-economy random fluctuations and the uniform effect of unilateral deviations—precisely the difficult content of Proposition 1—disappear. If those effects are retained by defining the continuum question through finite approximating economies, the model again becomes a quantified family of finite games with strategy functions over a continuous type space, not the finite-dimensional population LP suggested by \(y\).

Theorem 1 fares no better as a computational anchor. Its construction relies on the absolutely continuous distribution \( \eta \) over utility and score vectors: the mass of the cutoff-neighbourhood \( \mathcal{T}^{\delta}(\overline p) \) vanishes as \( \delta\to0 \). In a finite type space, score atoms can lie exactly at \( \overline p_c \), so the boundary mass need not vanish and the claimed near-unit non-WTT mass need not follow. To preserve the theorem, one must retain a continuous score distribution. But then complete types are generally uncountable, and the input is an analytically specified measure or density, not a finite high-multiplicity vector \( \mu\) over \( \tau \) types.

If one instead fixes a finite type set, rational capacities, a tie-breaking rule, symmetry, and a finite-\(N\) approximation, then maximizing

\[ \operatorname{NWTT}(y) \]

is a possible new extremal problem. But its defining constraints are no longer Theorem 1’s robust-equilibrium statement; they are an added finite model. Under the paper’s own assumptions, the intended answer is already supplied qualitatively: types away from the cutoff use non-WTT stable responses with probability approaching one. The remaining computation is mainly to find the cutoff and count boundary types, not to expose a new computational phenomenon analogous to the pricing problems in the continuization programme.

The honest limitation is that “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” cannot be proved from a modelling paper. A carefully engineered finite-type, symmetric, approximate-equilibrium variant could become a respectable follow-up. But the proponent has not produced such a well-posed computational object. The paper already contains the continuum population model analytically, while supplying no named computational result; the two proposed anchors either collapse into that existing asymptotic analysis or require enough extra structure that they cease to be mirrors of the paper.
