The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under the anchor rule: this paper has no numbered result that classifies a computational problem as hard, tractable, FPT, or similar.

The nearest results are Theorem 5 and Corollary 2, both proved here. Theorem 5 says that on a random \(d\)-regular graph, if the initial blue colors are independent with probability \(P_b\leq 1/2-\epsilon\), then for sufficiently large \(d\geq c/\epsilon^2\), the majority process reaches an all-red state in \(O(\log_d\log n)\) rounds with high probability. Corollary 2 says that for sufficiently large constant \(d\), a random \(d\)-regular graph is immune with high probability: its smallest dynamic monopoly has linear size. These are results about the behavior of a process on random graphs, not computational-complexity results about a problem. The paper’s mention of an NP-completeness result for consensus time is attributed to Kasser et al.; it is not a numbered result of this paper.

A plausible *follow-on* mirror would make a network itself continuous: a \(d\)-regular graphing \((X,\mu,R)\), with population mass \(\mu\), and a measurable seed set \(D\). One could ask for the infimum of \(\mu(D)\) such that, when \(D\) starts blue, majority dynamics reaches an all-blue state almost everywhere in finite time regardless of the initial colors outside \(D\). Corollary 2 motivates asking whether that infimum is positive. But the paper does not establish a complexity result about this question, and its finite random-graph theorem does not by itself transfer to a graphing. So this is a reasonable research direction, not an honest anchored continuous mirror of a named computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper proves no computational result to mirror. Theorem 5 is a high probability statement about how quickly majority dynamics settles on a sampled finite graph. Corollary 2 bounds the minimum dynamic monopoly in random regular graphs. Lemma 4 supplies a structural bound used in those arguments. None classifies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. The NP-completeness result about consensus time is cited as prior work by Kasser et al., not proved here.

The proposed graphing version of Theorem 5 is a coherent research question, but it changes the task into analyzing dynamics on an infinite or atomless network. The theorem concerns a global finite-graph event—every vertex becomes red within \(O(\log_d\log n)\) rounds, with high probability over the graph and initial coloring. A population distribution over finitely many types does not encode the adjacency that drives each update. Keeping the adjacency in a graphing preserves the dynamics, but the resulting question is about network limits and convergence, not a complexity question about a society represented by type masses.

Corollary 2 has the more promising mirror. One could ask for the infimum mass of a measurable seed set that forces blue consensus in a \(d\)-regular graphing. That is a legitimate continuous optimization question, and it is the strongest version of the proponent’s dynamic-monopoly idea. But the paper gives an asymptotic bound on that quantity, not an algorithm or a complexity classification. To ask for an algorithm, one must additionally specify how graphings are represented and how their adjacency is encoded. In a bounded-degree graph, vertices of the same broad type can have different neighbors; encoding enough neighborhood structure to make their updates identical can make the types effectively individual. A repeated-block network model could avoid that problem, but it would add the structure that makes the optimization tractable or meaningful.

So the negative case is strong against treating either result as an anchored computational mirror. It is weaker against the broader claim that *no scenario* could yield worthwhile work: a repeated-role network or an effectively encoded graphing could support a new continuous optimization problem. That would be a plausible project adjacent to this paper, but the paper itself does not establish the computational question the continuization programme is meant to chart.
