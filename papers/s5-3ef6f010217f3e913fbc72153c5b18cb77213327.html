The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) non-rival services, supported monotone submodular valuations \(v_1,\ldots,v_\tau:2^{[n]}\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\), and rational cohort shares \(\mu_i\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), choose a menu of lotteries \(X_i\) over integral bundles and expected payments \(p_i\) to maximize per-capita revenue \(\sum_i\mu_i p_i\), subject to \(v_i(X_i)-p_i\ge v_i(X_j)-p_j\) for all \(i,j\) and \(v_i(X_i)-p_i\ge 0\) for all \(i\). For support size \(\tau=\mathrm{poly}(n)\), can this be approximated within any \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) factor in polynomial time, given value-oracle, demand-oracle, or explicit valuation access?

The model it lives in

A society of \(\tau\) customer types with masses \(\mu_i\), each type described by its complete valuation \(v_i\), faces a common direct menu. The decision variables are lotteries \(X_i\) over integral service bundles and expected payments \(p_i\); the objective is per-capita revenue \(\sum_i\mu_i p_i\), subject to type-by-type incentive compatibility and individual rationality.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s hardness for the single-bidder MDMDP with a polynomial-size support of monotone submodular valuations; it leaves the SADP hardness, the general MDMDP-to-SADP reductions, and the fractional max-min fairness results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does fixed support size \(\tau\) admit better algorithms?
  • How do shared capacity constraints change the complexity?
  • What changes for additive valuation types?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest mirror is a narrow one: Cohort Submodular Revenue. Think of a large digital-services market in which a provider sells bundles from a catalog of \(n\) non-rival services. Each customer’s complete type is a monotone submodular valuation \(v:2^{[n]}\to\mathbb R_{\ge 0}\). There are \(N\) customers but only \(\tau\) distinct valuation types, with \(N\gg\tau\). Customers of the same type have identical values and face the same menu. The society is the distribution \(\mu\) over those types, where \(\mu_v\) is the fraction of customers of type \(v\). The provider can serve each customer independently, so each has access to the same catalog; this is the necessary per-capita supply assumption.

The continuous problem takes \(n\), the supported valuations \(v_1,\ldots,v_\tau\), and their rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\), summing to \(1\). It asks for a direct menu assigning each reported type \(v_i\) a lottery \(X_i\) over integral bundles and an expected payment \(p_i\), maximizing per-capita revenue
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i p_i. \]
A feasible solution must be Bayesian incentive compatible and individually rational:
\[ v_i(X_i)-p_i\ge v_i(X_j)-p_j\quad\text{for all }i,j, \qquad v_i(X_i)-p_i\ge 0\quad\text{for all }i. \]
Here \(v_i(X_j)\) is the expected value of the lottery. Each customer still receives an integral bundle; lotteries make the allocation averaged over a type cohort fractional. The input parameters are \(n,\tau\), and \(L\), the bit length of the masses and valuation descriptions. Valuations may be supplied through value or demand oracles, or as explicit Turing-machine descriptions, matching the paper’s input models.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. Its full claim is that both \(\mathrm{SADP}(2^{[n]},\text{monotone submodular functions})\), for \(k=\mathrm{poly}(n)\), and \(\mathrm{MDMDP}(2^{[n]},\text{monotone submodular functions})\), for \(k=|T_1|=\mathrm{poly}(n)\), cannot be approximated within any \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) factor using polynomially many value-oracle queries, or polynomially many demand-oracle queries. With explicit access to the input functions, no polynomial-time algorithm achieves such an approximation unless \(\mathrm{NP}=\mathrm{RP}\). I rely on the MDMDP part: the underlying problem is to find a revenue-maximizing BIC, IR mechanism for one bidder whose valuation is drawn from a finite support of monotone submodular types. The SADP clause is part of the theorem, but SADP itself is an algorithm-design subproblem, not the population problem I am proposing.

The bridge is unusually clean. Given a hard one-bidder MDMDP instance, use its type distribution as the cohort masses \(\mu_i\). The one-bidder mechanism and the per-customer menu have the same incentive and participation constraints, and their expected revenue and per-capita revenue are the same expression. Conversely, a menu for the cohort market is a feasible one-bidder mechanism for that distribution. Thus an algorithm for the continuous problem would give the same approximation for the hard MDMDP instance, in each of the theorem’s oracle and explicit-input regimes. For finite markets with cohort counts \(N_i\), this identity holds whenever \(\mu_i=N_i/N\); the continuum allows the cohort masses to be treated directly.

I expect hardness to transfer. The hard combinatorics in Theorem 3 live in the number of goods and the valuation functions, not in the number of customers. Replacing customer counts by rational cohort masses does not soften the menu-design problem: it leaves the optimization exactly intact. This is a Class B-style mirror. The theorem’s stated hardness needs \(\tau=\mathrm{poly}(n)\), so it does not establish hardness when the number of valuation types is fixed. Follow-up questions include whether fixed \(\tau\) is tractable, whether restrictions such as additive valuations change the result, and what happens when customers compete for shared capacity rather than receiving independent catalogs.

The scope is just this revenue-maximization result in a replicated-catalog market. It does not mirror the paper’s general MDMDP-to-SADP reductions or its fractional max-min fairness result. The authors could plausibly recognize the proposed problem: it is their single-bidder Bayesian menu problem interpreted as a per-capita market of customers with those valuations. The key distinction is that \(\mu\) describes actual shares of a large society, rather than uncertainty about one buyer. Their existing result already answers the computational question for this particular mirror, but it does not make the population finite; it supplies the hardness result that carries over.

The weakest point is also that close correspondence. Because the per-customer market decomposes and each customer faces the same menu, the continuous problem is mathematically identical to the paper’s single-bidder MDMDP. That makes the scenario plausible and the hardness argument strong, but leaves little new continuum-specific structure to analyze. It is a convincing mirror for a high-multiplicity regime, not a claim that this paper studies population limits or that its hardness is caused by population size.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the strongest anchor, and the proposed cohort market is its cleanest mirror. Under its stated assumptions, though, that clean correspondence is also the problem: it identifies the cohort problem with the paper’s existing one-bidder MDMDP. Given valuation types \(v_1,\ldots,v_\tau\) and shares \(\mu_i\), both optimize
\[ \sum_i \mu_i p_i \]
over menus satisfying \(v_i(X_i)-p_i\ge v_i(X_j)-p_j\) and \(v_i(X_i)-p_i\ge 0\). The paper already takes those rational weights as the bidder’s type probabilities. Replicating customers and normalizing revenue adds no population variable or new constraint; even finite cohort counts \(N_i\) give the same instance with \(\mu_i=N_i/N\). Lotteries over integral bundles are already part of the paper’s model. This is a model collision: the proposed population interpretation restates an object the paper already analyzes. The objection is the identity of the formulations, not the fact that Theorem 3 happens to prove hardness.

The theorem’s SADP clause offers no separate population anchor. SADP optimizes differences between functions over feasible outcomes; its hard instance concerns finding hidden subsets of the goods. It supplies no agent population or type masses to continuize.

A stronger repair would give customers a shared capacity constraint, so allocations to one cohort affect what remains for others. That creates genuine population-level coupling, but it also breaks the exact one-bidder identification: the objective and incentive constraints now sit inside a coupled allocation problem. Theorem 3’s hardness does not settle that problem. It may be a worthwhile extension of mechanism design, but the proponent would need to formulate and motivate it as a distinct mirror; the separable cohort story does not establish it.

So the negative case is strong against the specific anchor and the natural independent-catalog mirror. It is not an honest proof that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. Shared-capacity markets leave a plausible opening, and the paper’s broader multi-bidder framework makes that opening hard to dismiss.
