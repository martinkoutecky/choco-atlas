What the mirror covers

The proposed question concerns the 2BPB equilibrium and its induced outcomes; it does not turn the simulation comparisons into guarantees or cover a welfare-optimal mechanism-design problem.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that reservation allocation has a natural high-multiplicity regime. Think of a large recurring class or appointment system in which many participants share a complete type: the same immediate-value distribution \(F\), future value \(v^2\), present-bias factor \(\beta\), and belief \(\hat\beta\). In a cohort-based system, the number of participants \(n\) can be large while the number of such types \(\tau\) stays modest. Let \(\mu_\theta\) be each type’s population share, and let resource supply scale with the population, so the mechanism allocates a fixed fraction \(\kappa\) of society. Individuals still receive either a resource or none; only the type-level allocated mass can be fractional.

There is a strict problem with making this a case anchored in the paper’s named computational results: the paper has no named computational complexity result. Its strongest candidate is Theorem 1, proved here. It states that under assumptions (A1)–(A3), 2BPB has a dominant strategy in which an agent bids her maximum acceptable penalty \(\zeta_i\); if assigned a resource with minimum permitted penalty \(\underline z\), her dominant second bid is \(\arg\max_{z\geq\underline z}\hat u_i(z)\); and the mechanism satisfies voluntary participation and no deficit. That is a mechanism and incentive result, not a claim that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. Proposition 1, also proved here, says dominant-strategy bids under 2BPB or the \(m+1\) price auction are monotone in the agent’s subjective present-bias factor. It is not a computational result either. The simulations do not supply a numbered computational anchor.

A plausible *unanchored* continuous question would be to compute the induced 2BPB equilibrium for a high-multiplicity cohort. Give an instance as a finite rational distribution over types \(\theta_j=(c_j,p_j,w_j,\beta_j,\hat\beta_j)\) from the paper’s \((c,p)\) model, with masses \(\mu_j\) summing to \(1\), and a rational resource-supply fraction \(\kappa\). Ask for the assigned mass \(q_j\) of each type and its second-stage penalty. The solution must allocate mass up to \(\kappa\) to the types with the highest \(\zeta_j=\sup\{z:\hat U_j(z)\geq0\}\), split mass at a tied cutoff if needed, and give each assigned type a penalty maximizing \(\hat u_j(z)\) subject to the cutoff. It can also report aggregate utilization and welfare using the paper’s type-level utilization and welfare formulas. This computes the mechanism’s outcome; it does not optimize welfare, which Theorem 1 does not claim 2BPB does. For this finite-support \((c,p)\) version, the expected answer is tractable by sorting the type bids and computing the cutoff penalties.

That candidate covers the DSE mechanism and its induced outcomes, not the paper’s simulation claims as guarantees and not a welfare-optimal mechanism-design problem. Its weakness is substantial: it is not a compliant per-anchor question under the requested anchor rule, and the finite-support outcome computation appears to follow directly from sorting. The population model fits well, but the paper supplies no named computational result to support a formal positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the anchor itself: the paper has no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1 proves a dominant strategy and voluntary participation and no deficit for the 2BPB mechanism; Proposition 1 gives a comparative static about bids. Neither specifies a computational problem, an input encoding, or a complexity claim. The simulations are not such a result either.

There is a sensible high-multiplicity setting here: many participants in recurring classes or appointments can share complete types, with resource supply scaled to a fixed population fraction. In that setting, the proponent’s best candidate is to compute the 2BPB outcome for a finite rational distribution over types. The mass version is coherent: rank types by their \(\zeta\) bids, split mass at the cutoff if necessary, then choose each assigned type’s preferred penalty and aggregate welfare and utilization.

But that candidate is a new implementation question, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper. For the paper’s explicit parametric types, it follows the mechanism’s scalar bids and cutoff allocation. For arbitrary value distributions \(F_i\), the paper supplies no finite input representation from which to state a bit-complexity question. Adding one would make a different computational project. A richer optimization problem over mechanisms would be further from the named results still.

So the negative case is strong under the required anchor rule, but weak as a universal claim: I cannot honestly argue that high multiplicity is implausible here, or that the proposed mass implementation is ill-posed. It may be worth studying in a broader programme. The case against is that it does not give this paper a qualifying computational mirror.
