The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest on the ground the proponent identified: the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 characterizes incentive compatibility, no externalities, and no change on a specified domain. Corollary 1 gives an existence condition; Corollary 2 gives an impossibility condition. None defines an input representation or classifies the complexity of computing or deciding anything. The paper’s continuum discussion is about economic existence, not the computational questions ChoCo targets.

The best candidate mirror would take a finite-support distribution over complete preference types and ask whether an anonymous, efficient, incentive-compatible mechanism exists, or how to compute one. But preserving Theorem 2’s question requires preserving its domain of possible utility reports and its minimally comprehensive neighborhoods. A finite list of types and rational masses does not encode those domains. Restrict reports to a finite, encoded type set and the question becomes a different mechanism-design problem; retain the paper’s rich utility domain and the mechanism is a function over an effectively infinite space, not a finite instance of the kind the paper analyzes. Computing an efficient allocation for one reported profile is a possible new question, but it does not mirror the paper’s characterization of mechanisms.

There is also a deeper mismatch in the continuum version. The paper’s incentive constraints concern a single person changing their report. In an atomless society represented by a distribution, one person has zero mass, so their report leaves the distribution unchanged and the incentive constraint can become vacuous. Treating a positive mass of people as deviating gives a coalition or group-manipulation problem instead. Either might be worth studying, but neither is the paper’s individual incentive-compatibility result.

This is not an objection that the high-multiplicity regime is implausible: public-goods economies with many people are a natural setting, and the paper itself discusses continuum-agent economies. Nor does the paper’s analytic continuum work settle a computational question. The weaker point is that there is no computational result here to anchor such a question, and the most promising attempt to construct one either changes the strategic problem or lacks a natural finite input model. I do not see a strong basis for claiming that every such newly formulated problem would be unworthy; that universal claim is less secure than the conclusion that this paper offers no worthwhile *computational mirror of its results*.
