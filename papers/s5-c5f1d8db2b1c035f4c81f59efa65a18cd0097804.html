The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying computational anchors in this paper. Its named results concern existence and uniqueness of the MSS, structural properties, and equivalences between solution concepts; none asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar. For example, Theorem 3.1, proved here, establishes that every social environment has a nonempty MSS. Theorem 3.6, also proved here, gives uniqueness when the dominance correspondence is lower hemi-continuous. Neither is a complexity result. The paper makes the state space potentially infinite, but keeps the population finite.

The nearest positive lead is Theorem 4.11, proved here: in the finite Shapley–Scarf housing matching environment—with strict preferences and coalitions able to reallocate their houses without changing outsiders’ assignments—the unique MSS is exactly the set of Pareto-efficient house allocations. The paper derives this using the core characterization and the top trading cycle algorithm. This is a substantial result about the underlying problem, but it is not an admissible complexity anchor under your instructions.

A plausible continuized question suggested by it is Mass Housing-Exchange Blocking. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete preference types over a finite set \(H\) of housing categories. The input gives rational type masses \(\mu_t\), housing supplies \(\nu_h\), and an assignment \(x_{t,h}\), with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,h}=\nu_h\). Each individual receives one house; \(x\) records the aggregate mass assignments. Decide whether a positive-mass coalition can reassign its houses so every participant strictly prefers the new house, while preserving supplies and leaving nonparticipants’ assignments alone. A blocking reassignment is a certificate; if none exists, \(x\) is Pareto-efficient against such mass exchanges.

A credible regime is a large housing pool whose residents share relatively few preference rankings over a modest set of housing categories, so the population is large while \(|T|\) is comparatively small. The type must include every attribute relevant to preferences and exchange rights. This is a plausible population-level analogue of the paper’s housing problem, and the blocking question appears suited to a circulation or linear optimization formulation. I would tentatively expect tractability, with follow-up questions about whether the continuum MSS exists and coincides with mass-Pareto-efficient assignments.

That is a research lead, not a result-backed ChoCo case: Theorem 4.11 says nothing about the complexity of this continuous problem, and does not establish that the continuum MSS has the same characterization. That missing bridge is the weakest point—and the reason I cannot honestly provide a continuous problem *per qualifying anchor* for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is decisive: this paper states no computational complexity result. Theorem 3.1 proves existence of an MSS; Theorem 3.6 gives a uniqueness condition; and Theorem 4.11 identifies the MSS with Pareto-efficient housing allocations. None claims an algorithmic running time, hardness, or parameterized complexity. Theorem 4.11 uses the top trading cycle algorithm in its proof, but does not analyze the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s distinctive contribution is a stability characterization for finitely many agents, even when the state space is infinite.

The proposed mass housing-exchange question is plausible as a new research problem. But it adds an input of rational masses and supplies and asks whether an aggregate reassignment blocks an allocation. That is not a complexity question asserted by Theorem 4.11, nor does the theorem establish that the continuous MSS has the same characterization. A population continuum would also require specifying which positive-mass coalitions can move and how they may exchange houses. Those choices may be sensible, but they define a new model and computational question rather than continuize a result in this paper.

The boundary matters here: the paper already allows continuous states, while keeping the population finite. Replacing its finite agents and coalitions with a mass distribution would change the primitives on which its results rest. That change could inspire worthwhile work, especially for housing markets, but under the programme’s stated test it does not supply a qualifying mirror. I cannot honestly argue that no such aggregate problem could ever be worthwhile; the negative case is strongest and firmest on the narrower point that this paper gives no computational result to mirror.
