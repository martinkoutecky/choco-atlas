The continuous mirror question

Given common finite variables \(V\) and domains, a finite support \(\Theta\) of conditionally acyclic TCP-net types, rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to \(1\), and outcomes \(a,b\in\mathcal{D}(V)\), compute the exact value \(D_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta\,\mathbf{1}[a\succ_\theta b]\), where \(a\succ_\theta b\) means that type \(\theta\) entails \(a\) is strictly preferred to \(b\).

The model it lives in

A population of users choosing among the same product attributes, represented by a distribution \(\mu\) over complete TCP-net preference types. The computation sums the masses of types whose networks entail the same outcome comparison.

The objection that survived

The population share is a batch statistic, and the paper supplies no collective choice rule; the template scenario makes the statistic plausible, while its decision value remains an extension.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 5’s dominance inference for a pair of outcomes. It leaves Theorem 1’s satisfiability guarantee, general consistency, and the constrained search for Pareto-optimal outcomes outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding \(a\succ_\theta b\) for conditionally acyclic TCP-nets?
  • Can structure in the supported types yield exact or approximate algorithms whose running time is polynomial in the input size and support \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or otherwise. Its strongest named computational anchors are instead a consistency guarantee and a characterization of dominance. I would build the positive case around the latter, while being clear that neither result settles the complexity of the underlying problem.

Consider a large product configurator serving many customers choosing among the same product attributes. It could offer a small library of preference templates: each template specifies a complete TCP-net, including its conditional preference tables and importance relations. Customers using the same template are the same type for this query; \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction using template \(\theta\). In a large deployment, the customer count could vastly exceed the number \(\tau\) of templates. The paper itself motivates TCP-nets with configuration applications, so asking how its reasoning behaves across a population is a plausible extension of its problem.

My lead is TCP-Dominance Mass. An instance consists of common variables \(V\) and domains, a finite support \(\Theta\) of conditionally acyclic TCP-net types over \(V\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to one, and two feasible outcomes \(a,b\). The task is to return the exact rational value
\[ D_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta\,\mathbf{1}[a\succ_\theta b], \]
the share of the population whose TCP-net entails that \(a\) is strictly preferred to \(b\). A solution is that exact mass, with the type-by-type entailment answers determining the sum. Restricting types to conditionally acyclic TCP-nets ensures they are satisfiable under Theorem 1.

The anchor is Lemma 5, proved in this paper. It says that, for a TCP-net \(N\), \(a\succ b\) is a consequence of \(N\) if and only if there is an improving flipping sequence from \(b\) to \(a\). Every step in that sequence either changes one variable to a value preferred according to its CPT given the unchanged parent values, or changes two variables: improving the more important variable while worsening the less important one, with the relevant importance relation holding in the same selector context. The authors explicitly leave the precise complexity of TCP-net dominance testing open. The mirror keeps that underlying question intact: it asks for the population mass of types answering yes to the very entailment test Lemma 5 characterizes.

I expect this problem to inherit the complexity of individual dominance reasoning, rather than acquire a new obstacle from the continuum. At \(\tau=1\), it is exactly the individual query; for general \(\tau\), it aggregates up to \(\tau\) such queries with rational weights. The paper does not establish whether individual dominance is tractable or hard, so I cannot honestly assign the mirror to a settled complexity class. Its conditionally acyclic restriction gives a meaningful domain, but Lemma 5 itself does not promise that dominance is easy there. Further questions include whether useful TCP-net structure makes the mass computable efficiently, and whether approximate mass estimation is easier than exact computation.

A second mirror is TCP-Coherence Mass. The input is a rational distribution over TCP-net types on common variables and domains; the output is the exact mass of types whose qualitative preference and importance statements admit a satisfying total preorder. A type is satisfiable in precisely the paper’s sense: some total preorder over outcomes satisfies all its CPT and importance constraints.

Its anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: every conditionally acyclic TCP-net is satisfiable. Thus, when every support type is conditionally acyclic, the answer to TCP-Coherence Mass is exactly \(1\). For unrestricted types, a single type of mass \(1\) recovers the individual satisfiability question, so the population representation alone should not dissolve any hardness that question may have. The paper supplies a tractable structural guarantee, not a complexity classification for general TCP-net consistency.

These mirrors cover the paper’s coherence and dominance reasoning, not its whole constrained-optimization section. That section presents a search algorithm for finding Pareto-optimal feasible outcomes, but the excerpt gives no numbered complexity result for it to anchor a separate continuous question.

The soft spot is the population layer. The paper reasons about one decision maker, while these mirrors ask for population shares; moreover, exact agreement on a complete TCP-net may leave \(\tau\) large unless preference templates are genuinely reused. The case holds in a setting where a configurator actually uses a small shared library of templates: every agent’s type retains the full preference description the paper reasons about, and mass records how prevalent each type is. It is a credible population-scale extension of the paper’s computational questions, though the paper itself does not make that population extension.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered result classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1 is a satisfiability guarantee for conditionally acyclic TCP-nets; Lemma 5 characterizes when one outcome is entailed to be preferred to another. Neither supplies a complexity result for a continuous version to mirror. The paper does mention dominance complexity as an open question, but that makes it a candidate for new analysis, not a computational result established here.

TCP-Dominance Mass is the stronger proposed mirror, and its product-template scenario is plausible: many customers might share a preference template. But the query changes the paper’s task. The paper asks what a single decision maker prefers, or which feasible outcomes are optimal for that person. The mirror asks for a statistic about a population: what fraction entails one comparison. For explicit support of size \(\tau\), that statistic separates into \(\tau\) individual entailment queries and rational additions. The masses record how often each answer occurs; they do not enter the preference reasoning or connect the agents’ choices. A majority threshold could turn the statistic into a collective decision, but the paper supplies no such rule. That would add a social-choice model rather than continue its existing one.

The more ambitious mirror would be to find a feasible configuration that is best for the population. Here the obstacle is more fundamental than the literal form of a TCP-net. Its semantics gives qualitative constraints on possible rankings, not numerical utilities or a shared scale for comparing how much different people value outcomes. A population objective therefore needs an additional choice: majority over entailed pairwise comparisons, a Pareto criterion, a rule for choosing among each network’s satisfying rankings, or something else. Those choices can produce different answers, and none follows from the paper’s model. A configurator could certainly study them, but that would be a new collective-preference problem.

TCP-Coherence Mass has a sharper fit problem. An unsatisfiable TCP-net does not describe a coherent preference relation for an agent, so it is not naturally a voter type in the paper’s semantics. Asking what fraction of submitted descriptions is satisfiable could be useful for auditing an elicitation system, but the population is then a collection of reports, not a society of agents with the preferences the paper models. Restrict to conditionally acyclic types and Theorem 1 makes the mass exactly \(1\); include arbitrary networks and the query is again a weighted batch of individual consistency tests.

The negative case is not airtight. A market with a small library of repeated preference templates is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the share of customers who entail a comparison might be useful. But the proposed mirrors turn the paper’s individual reasoning into population reporting without deriving a population-level decision from its semantics. Making that decision meaningful requires substantial new modeling choices. That is the strongest reason these anchors do not establish a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
