The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives, a committee size \(k\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete rankings with encoding length \(L\), can one compute in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) a size-\(k\) committee \(X\) satisfying \(D_\mu(X)=\sup_{(v_t\in P_t)_t}\frac{\max_{a\in C}\sum_t\mu_t v_t(a)}{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{b\in X}\sum_t\mu_t v_t(b)}\le C\max\{k,m^2/(k\sqrt{k})\}\) for a universal constant \(C\), where \(P_t\) is the set of unit-sum cardinal utility vectors consistent with ranking \(t\)?

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over ranking types. The rule observes only \(\mu\); for evaluating distortion, each type has an average utility vector \(v_t\in P_t\), and the committee’s welfare is computed from the mass-weighted sums \(\sum_t\mu_t v_t(a)\). The algorithm returns a size-\(k\) committee whose uniform lottery over members has the stated distortion guarantee.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 4’s committee construction and distortion guarantee, with Theorem 3’s lower bound as context; leaves the randomized positional scoring results, experiments, and explainability assessments outside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the approximately stable committee subroutine and greedy welfare construction be implemented directly on rational type masses with runtime polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), while retaining Theorem 4’s guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible regime is a large recommendation or participatory decision platform that repeatedly chooses from a fixed slate of \(m\) options. Millions of users submit complete rankings, but the same ballot patterns recur, so the population can be represented by \(\tau\) ranking types with \(\tau\ll n\). The mass \(\mu_t\) of a type is its share of users. This is more convincing for recurring, low-stakes choices than for a small committee, and it matches the paper’s ordinal information model: the voting rule sees rankings, while cardinal utilities remain hidden.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper in Section 4 (with its construction using the approximately stable committee result cited as Theorem 18, reference [40]). It proves that, for committee size \(k\), there is a polynomial-time computable random \(k\)-committee member rule with distortion \(O\!\left(\max\{k,m^2/(k\sqrt{k})\}\right)\). The bound is minimized at \(k=m^{4/5}\), giving \(O(m^{4/5})\). The rule constructs a committee and then chooses a member uniformly at random. The paper has no named NP-hardness result for me to anchor on; its Theorem 3, for example, is a distortion lower bound, not a computational-hardness result.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. The input is \(m\), \(k\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete rankings of the alternatives, with masses encoded in \(L\) bits. For each ranking \(t\), let \(P_t\) be the set of normalized cardinal utility vectors consistent with that ranking. The rule sees only \(\mu\). For any choice of type-average utilities \(v_t\in P_t\), define \(W_v(a)=\sum_t\mu_t v_t(a)\). For a size-\(k\) committee \(X\), define its robust distortion by
\[ D_\mu(X)=\sup_{(v_t\in P_t)_t} \frac{\max_{a\in C} W_v(a)} {\frac{1}{k}\sum_{b\in X}W_v(b)}. \]
The task is to return a size-\(k\) committee \(X\) such that \(D_\mu(X)\le C\max\{k,m^2/(k\sqrt{k})\}\) for a universal constant \(C\), in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The solution is the committee; the implemented voting rule draws uniformly from it. Allowing each ranking mass to contain different hidden utilities preserves the paper’s distortion model: only the ranking distribution is input to the rule, and the guarantee must withstand every compatible utility assignment.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, though the continuous algorithm is a question rather than a consequence of Theorem 4’s stated runtime. The paper’s construction combines stability, plurality mass, and a greedy welfare argument—all quantities that appear amenable to weighted versions over the \(\tau\) types. But “polynomial time” on an explicit \(n\)-voter profile does not by itself give time polynomial in \(L\): scaling rational masses to an integer electorate can produce an \(n\) exponentially large in \(L\). Establishing a support-based weighted construction is the actual computational contribution the mirror would ask for.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it keeps their alternatives, rankings, committee rule, hidden-utility model, and distortion objective. The rational-mass version also has a precise bridge to their setting: scaling by a common denominator yields a finite electorate with exactly those proportions. The mirror’s added question is whether the result can be computed from the compressed distribution rather than by expanding that electorate. Theorem 3, proved here, provides useful context: every random \(k\)-committee member rule has distortion \(\Omega(\max\{k,m^2/k^2\})\), hence at least \(\Omega(m^{2/3})\). This leaves a natural question about closing the gap to Theorem 4’s \(O(m^{4/5})\) guarantee, including for mass-weighted algorithms.

This case covers Theorem 4’s committee-selection result, not the paper’s separate randomized positional scoring bounds, experiments, or the full design of explainable randomized rules. Its weakest point is the relation between ballot types and cardinal preferences: two users with the same ranking may have different latent utilities. If “type” must include every such latent utility, the hidden population may have many more types than the rule’s observable ranking support. I think the mirror remains faithful because the paper itself gives the rule only rankings and measures distortion against all compatible utilities; the continuous question retains that uncertainty instead of giving the rule extra information.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 defeats the strongest easy negative: it is a numbered computational result, and large platforms with recurring rankings are a credible high multiplicity regime. Nor does the hidden-cardinal-utility issue defeat the proposed model. Within each ranking type, averaging agents’ utilities preserves the social-welfare vector, and the average remains consistent with that ranking. The type-level uncertainty therefore captures the paper’s distortion benchmark.

The best case against is narrower: the proposed mirror may amount to a weighted implementation of the paper’s existing construction. The committee algorithm aggregates rank counts and uses greedy choices; those quantities can be computed directly from rational masses. If the approximately stable committee subroutine also admits a support-weighted implementation, the distortion proof appears to carry over with the same bound. The unresolved point is bit complexity: scaling rational masses to voter counts can produce an electorate too large to expand, so an explicit-profile algorithm polynomial in \(n\) does not by itself give an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). Showing that the weighted subroutine avoids this expansion is real work, but it may be a modest algorithmic lift rather than a new complexity landscape.

A stronger question—finding the committee with minimum worst-case distortion—would go beyond Theorem 4’s guarantee, which constructs a committee with a stated bound. That could be interesting, but it would be a new optimization objective, not a reason the stated mirror fails.

So the negative case is weak. The only serious objection is that the continuous result may follow from a routine weighted adaptation; the binary-encoding issue prevents me from calling that settled. Under the programme’s criteria, Theorem 4’s mirror survives.
