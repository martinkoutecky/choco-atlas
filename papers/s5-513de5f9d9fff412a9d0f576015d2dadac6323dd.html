Why no mirror

Theorem 1 proves representational properties of LUAV but gives no computational result. Continuous LUAV winner determination is a coherent new question, but the proponent explicitly acknowledges that it is not anchored to a computational result in this paper. Bit (a) therefore fails.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror studies selecting a committee by LUAV’s lexicographic representation objective over a distribution of approval types. It does not turn the paper’s α-unanimity, approval-basedness, or JR guarantees into computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind: no theorem or other numbered result says that a problem is tractable, hard, or has a specified complexity. Its main named result, Theorem 1, is proved in this paper and says that LUAV is \(\alpha\)-unanimous for every \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), is approval-based, and satisfies justifed representation (JR). In full, JR means there is no group of at least \(n/s\) voters who all approve some common candidate while none approves a member of the selected committee. Theorem 1 is a result about the rule’s properties, not its computational complexity, so it cannot be a qualifying anchor under the stated requirement.

The strongest plausible positive case is nevertheless a continuous winner-determination problem for the exact LUAV rule defined in the paper. Consider a large membership association choosing an \(s\)-person committee from a slate of \(k\) candidates to develop policy proposals. Its members submit approval ballots; many members share a ballot, so the number \(n\) of members may be far larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct ballots. A type is a nonempty approval set \(A_i\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}\), and its mass \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of members with that ballot. The committee remains an ordinary size-\(s\) subset of candidates: only the population becomes continuous.

Call the proposed problem Continuous LUAV Winner Determination. An instance consists of \(k\), \(s\), and the \(\tau\) supported approval types \(A_i\), each with a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_i\), where \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). For every size-\(s\) committee \(C\), define
\[ d_\alpha(\mu,C)=\sum_{i:\,|A_i\cap C|/\min(|A_i|,s)\ge\alpha}\mu_i. \]
Let \(\Lambda_s=\{r/t:1\le t\le s,\ 0\le r\le t\}\), ordered increasingly. The task is to return a size-\(s\) committee maximizing the vector \((d_\alpha(\mu,C))_{\alpha\in\Lambda_s}\) lexicographically, using the paper’s fixed priority order if a single tie-broken winner is wanted. This is the mass version of LUAV’s own definition: the objective is the population’s representation profile across agreement thresholds, not a fractional committee, lottery, or other continuous outcome.

The connection to the paper is direct: the ballots, committee size, representation measure, and lexicographic objective are unchanged. Replacing ballot counts by masses also gives the high-multiplicity dictionary: every finite profile maps to \(\mu_i=n_i/n\), and any rational mass input can be scaled to integer type multiplicities. The paper’s stated contexts—committees intended to include members’ opinions in pre-proposals—make a large organization with recurring ballot types a plausible regime. Theorem 1’s three guarantees also have natural mass analogues, but those are axiomatic consequences, not computational results.

For variable \(s\), I would expect hardness to transfer. The first positive threshold in \(\Lambda_s\) is \(1/s\), and for every nonempty ballot \(A_i\),
\[ \frac{|A_i\cap C|}{\min(|A_i|,s)}\ge\frac1s \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A_i\cap C\ne\varnothing. \]
Thus \(d_{1/s}(\mu,C)\), the first nonconstant lexicographic coordinate, is exactly the mass of types covered by the selected candidates. Maximizing it is weighted Maximum Coverage: candidates are the selectable sets, and ballot types are the elements they cover. This yields the familiar NP-hardness when \(s\) varies, already on finite profiles; it is hardness from the candidate-selection combinatorics, not from population multiplicity. For fixed \(s\), enumerating the \(\binom{k}{s}\) committees and evaluating their vectors gives a polynomial-time algorithm. Follow-up questions include the parameterized complexity in \(s\), approximation guarantees that respect the lexicographic objective, and the complexity of selecting among committees tied on maximum covered mass.

That is a credible mirror of the paper’s central rule, but it does not satisfy the required anchor condition: the Maximum Coverage implication is derived from LUAV’s definition, not asserted by a numbered computational result in the paper. My weakest point is therefore decisive for grading: I can give a precise, plausible continuous computational question and a likely hardness classification, but I cannot honestly claim that this paper supplies the required named computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Its only numbered result, Theorem 1, proves that LUAV satisfies \(\alpha\)-unanimity, is approval-based, and satisfies JR. Those are axiomatic properties. A mass version would replace voter counts by sums of type masses and prove corresponding properties; that would continueize an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The best repair is the proposed Continuous LUAV Winner Determination problem: represent a large association’s recurring approval ballots by rational masses, then choose a size-\(s\) committee maximizing the lexicographic representation vector. This is a sensible high-multiplicity model. The committee stays integral, the mass measures representation, and neither voter identity nor a degenerating limit creates an obstacle. I cannot dismiss it on those grounds.

But the repair changes the research question. The paper defines LUAV and proves what its winners guarantee; it does not analyze how hard it is to find a winner. A hardness result for the mass version—such as the proposed Maximum Coverage connection—could be worthwhile work inspired by the paper, but it would be a new computational result, not a continuous mirror of one the paper establishes. Adding manipulation, control, or another computational problem would move still farther from its stated results.

So the negative case is strong at the programme’s anchor gate: there is no qualifying computational result here, and the mass analogue of Theorem 1 is axiomatic. It is weak as the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous question can arise from this paper. Winner determination has a plausible continuous formulation; the argument against it is that it is an unanchored new project, not that its model is unsound.
