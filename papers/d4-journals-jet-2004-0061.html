What the mirror covers

The paper’s axiomatic characterization results are not computational anchors; the proposed population-level policy comparison is an extension and has no computational result here.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is the paper’s motivating labor market: many workers in a few sectors face a small number of macroeconomic states. A type could be a worker’s complete state-contingent income profile; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the workforce with profile \(t\). For two policies \(f\) and \(g\), fix explicitly represented ex ante and ex post welfare functionals \(I_a\) and \(I_p\), and a weight \(\alpha\). A natural computational question is whether
\[ U_\alpha(f)\ge U_\alpha(g),\qquad U_\alpha(h)=\alpha(I_p*I_a)(h)+(1-\alpha)(I_a*I_p)(h), \]
where the functionals operate on the finite state set and the mass distribution over income types. The answer is an exact comparison of the two policies. This asks about a continuous population while keeping uncertainty finite, and it evaluates the kind of uncertain income-policy rankings the authors study.

But this is a proposed extension, not a valid anchor under your rules. The paper contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or an algorithmic formulation that it computes with. Theorem 1, proved here, is an axiomatic representation of welfare orderings; Theorems 4–6, also proved here, characterize subclasses through further axioms. None gives a complexity result. The paper’s continuous real-valued incomes and continuous preorder do not make its finite set of individuals \(K\) a continuous population.

So I cannot supply a continuous problem *per qualifying anchor*, or honestly assign this proposed comparison to Class A, B, or C based on the paper. Its weakest point is decisive for the requested exercise: the authors might recognize the population-level policy comparison as a natural extension of their subject, but it is not a computational problem this paper names or solves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The workforce example is the strongest possible setting for a population mirror: workers can be grouped by their complete state-contingent income profiles, with \(\mu_t\) recording each profile’s share. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and treating workers this way does not itself distort the paper’s question.

But the proposed comparison \(U_\alpha(f)\ge U_\alpha(g)\) is an extension, not a mirror of a computational result. The paper’s numbered results characterize welfare orderings through axioms; none states an algorithm, a complexity bound, or the complexity of a computational problem. Its continuous preorder and real-valued incomes concern the welfare model, while the set of individuals remains finite. Replacing that set with a distribution over profiles would make the population continuous, but it would not turn the paper’s axiomatic theorems into computational results.

One could make the comparison computational by specifying how the welfare functionals and policies are represented, or add a policy-optimization problem. Those choices might define worthwhile research questions, but their computational content would depend on the added encodings and feasible policy space. The paper supplies no such problem to continuize. So the negative case is strong for this paper as an anchor; it does not show that the proposed population-level welfare computation could never be worthwhile as a new project.
