What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation tracks Theorem 1’s maximum and minimum rate classification; Section 3.3’s condition-checking discussion is not a separate named anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is for a continuous smoothed-paradox classification. It is narrow: it mirrors the paper’s characterization of when doctrinal paradox is likely, rather than trying to continuize all of judgment aggregation.

Take a large online consultation on \(p\) linked propositions. Its agents fall into a small number \(\tau\) of judgment-generation types—for example, cohorts exposed to the same briefing or survey channel. A type \(t\) is a complete probability distribution \(\pi^t\) over the \(2^p\) possible premise judgments. This retains dependence between an agent’s judgments on different premises. The society is a mass distribution \(\mu\) over these types: \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\). With population scale \(N\), type \(t\) represents \(N\mu_t\) agents, each of whom independently realizes a judgment according to \(\pi^t\). Thus \(N\) is the replication scale, while the society is described by type shares.

Here is the problem I would hand to a prover. Given a finite rational type set \(T=\{\pi^1,\ldots,\pi^\tau\}\), a logical connection \(f\), a quota rule \(r_{\vec q,\vec d}\), and population scale \(N\), define
\[ \mathcal M_N(T)=\left\{\mu\in\Delta(T):N\mu_t\in\mathbb Z\text{ for every }t\right\}. \]
For each \(\mu\in\mathcal M_N(T)\), draw \(N\mu_t\) independent judgments from each type \(t\), apply the quota rule, and check whether the resulting premises and conclusion violate \(f\). Determine the asymptotic classes of
\[ D_N^{\max}=\max_{\mu\in\mathcal M_N(T)}\Pr[\text{paradox}] \quad\text{and}\quad D_N^{\min}=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal M_N(T)}\Pr[\text{paradox}]. \]
A solution gives the applicable rate and necessary-and-sufficient conditions for it. The adversary’s decision variable is the mass assigned to each type; the objective is the probability of an inconsistent collective judgment. The model keeps the paper’s independent noise, but makes population composition a distribution over types.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, “Smoothed Likelihood of Doctrinal Paradox”—proved in this paper. It states that for any closed, strictly positive set \(\Pi\) of distributions, any logical connection \(f\), and any quota rule, the maximum likelihood is \(0\) if \(\kappa_1\) holds; otherwise it is \(\exp(-\Theta(N))\) if \(\kappa_2\) holds; otherwise it is \(\Theta(N^{-1/2})\) if \(\kappa_4\) holds; and otherwise it is \(\Theta(1)\). For the minimum, the corresponding cases are \(0\) if \(\kappa_1\) holds; otherwise \(\exp(-\Theta(N))\) if \(\kappa_3\) holds; otherwise \(\Theta(N^{-1/2})\) if \(\kappa_4\) holds; and otherwise \(\Theta(1)\).

Here, \(\kappa_1\) says no \(N\)-agent profile can produce a paradox. \(\kappa_2\) says that for every distribution in \(\operatorname{conv}(\Pi)\), every *effective refinement* of its quota outcome is logically consistent. A refinement agrees with the quota outcome on untied propositions; it is effective if some \(N\)-agent profile realizes it. \(\kappa_3\) asks for at least one distribution in \(\operatorname{conv}(\Pi)\) for which all effective refinements are consistent. \(\kappa_4\) says the conclusion is equivalent to one premise or its negation, with the corresponding quota thresholds matching that relationship. The theorem’s conditions allow the rate to depend on \(N\); the paper’s own example has different behavior for odd and even \(N\).

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: \(N\) can be very large while the number of distinct judgment-generation types \(\tau\) is modest. The paper’s finite-agent model already permits each agent to have a different distribution from \(\Pi\); grouping agents who share the same \(\pi\) is exactly a high-multiplicity description of that regime. Existing high-multiplicity structure supports the mirror’s plausibility. The authors could reasonably recognize it as their likelihood question with type shares made explicit.

I expect the rate-classification problem to be Class A, at least for fixed \(\tau\). The paper’s Section 3.3 gives algorithms for checking the theorem’s conditions for finite rational \(\Pi\), polynomial in \(m=2^p\) (and in \(\log N\) for \(\kappa_1\)) when \(\tau\) is fixed. An FPT-in-\(\tau\) classification looks plausible from its fixed-dimension integer-programming step, but that parameterized bound is an extrapolation, not a named result of the paper. Natural follow-up questions include whether the classification remains tractable when \(\tau\) grows, what happens without strict positivity, and whether the same rate picture extends beyond quota rules.

The weak point is substantial: this paper has no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, or similar. Theorem 1 is a probability characterization, not a computational-complexity theorem. Also, the paper already permits arbitrary agent-specific distributions and its proof works through their convex hull, so grouping repeated distributions may look close to a repackaging. Finally, in a literal infinite population, independent noise makes the aggregate judgment proportions converge to their expectations; the nontrivial likelihood rates concern finite-\(N\) fluctuations. The case survives only if the object of study is the high-multiplicity family of societies described by mass shares, with \(N\) retained as its replication scale.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only anchor, and it classifies asymptotic probabilities. It does not classify the complexity of a computational social choice problem. Section 3.3 does give algorithms for checking its conditions, but those algorithms operate on the paper’s finite sets of judgment distributions. That leaves the main ChoCo question—what becomes of a computational task when the population is its input—without a result to mirror.

In fact, the proposed mass formulation reproduces the paper’s likelihood problem. Fix \(T=\{\pi^1,\ldots,\pi^\tau\}\). An \(N\)-agent assignment \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\in T^N\) has an empirical type distribution \(\mu\in\mathcal M_N(T)\), and every such \(\mu\) corresponds to an assignment. Because quota aggregation and the paradox event are anonymous, reordering the assignments does not change the probability. Thus the proponent’s \(D_N^{\max}\) and \(D_N^{\min}\) are exactly the paper’s max and min likelihoods with \(\Pi=T\). Theorem 1 already classifies them; for finite rational \(T\), Section 3.3 already gives polynomial-time checks of its conditions when \(\tau\) is fixed. Writing the assignments as type masses compresses the input, but does not create a new continuous computational question.

This is a specific collision with the paper’s model, not a general objection to high multiplicity. The proposed society type—an agent’s judgment-generation distribution—is a sensible complete type for this probability calculation. The catch is that the paper already allows each of the \(N\) agents to have exactly that type, and studies the resulting likelihood. Here \(\mu\) is the empirical distribution of those types, not a new population object with new computational structure.

A more genuinely continuous \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\) needs a different definition of the random outcome when \(N\mu_t\) is not integral. If agents contribute fractional mass, a continuum law-of-large-numbers interpretation makes aggregate vote shares deterministic; the paper’s nontrivial rates come from finite-\(N\) fluctuations. Retain those fluctuations and the model returns to the paper’s integer-count family. One could instead study the mass shift needed to change the paradox probability, but that would introduce a new intervention problem and a probability objective absent from the paper’s results.

The negative case is limited: a large consultation with recurring response types is plausible, and a new intervention question might be worth studying independently. But the case offered here does not establish a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper’s result. Its strongest anchor is already covered by the paper’s finite-agent formulation, while its computational content is the tractable checking of that same characterization.
