What the mirror covers

The proposed extension uses Theorem 2's four-agent, eleven-good instance. It leaves aside Theorem 1's asymptotic goods bound and Theorems 3–4's chores bounds; these too are allocation-guarantee impossibility results rather than computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The closest positive mirror is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: \(\alpha^{\mathrm{MMS}}\le 20/21\), witnessed by a four-agent, eleven-good instance in which every agent’s MMS is \(21\), yet every allocation gives some agent value at most \(20\). It is the best candidate because its four agents have only two valuation types, so those types can be given positive mass in a large population.

There is an important qualification: Theorem 2 is an impossibility result about what every allocation can guarantee. Theorem 1 and Theorems 3–4 give similar allocation-guarantee bounds. None of them asserts a computational-complexity result such as NP-hardness, polynomial-time solvability, or parameterized complexity. So, under your anchor rule, this paper has no eligible named computational result. What follows is a plausible continuous-population analogue of Theorem 2, not a qualifying computational anchor.

Take the two valuation vectors \(v_1,v_2\) from Theorem 2 as the agent types, each with mass \(1/2\). Treat the eleven goods as item categories, with supply \(q_j=1/4\) of category \(j\) per unit of population. A mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{11}\) receives an integral bundle; only the mass assigned to each bundle can be fractional.

For type \(t\), define its continuous MMS \(M_t(q)\) as the largest \(z\) for which there is a finite-support distribution \(p_{t,b}\) over integral bundles satisfying
\[ \sum_b p_{t,b}=1,\qquad \sum_b p_{t,b}b=q, \]
and \(p_{t,b}>0\) only when \(v_t\cdot b\ge z\). The continuous allocation problem is to maximize \(\alpha\) over masses \(x_{t,b}\ge0\) such that
\[ \sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_j=q_j\ \text{for every }j, \]
with \(x_{t,b}>0\) only for bundles worth at least \(\alpha M_t(q)\) to type \(t\). A solution is a finite-support allocation of all population mass and all item supply.

For this instance, the continuous optimum is \(1\). Type \(v_1\) has the four value-\(21\) bundles listed in the paper, and type \(v_2\) has its own four value-\(21\) bundles. Assign mass \(1/8\) of each type to each of its four bundles. Each type then uses mass \(1/8\) of every item category, so combined use is \(1/4\) per category, exactly the supply. Every assigned mass unit receives an integral bundle worth \(21\). Also, \(M_t(q)=21\): the witness bundles attain \(21\), and the average value available per agent is \((1/4)\cdot84=21\). In finite replications, this allocation is realized by taking \(4r\) agents and \(r\) copies of each item category, for even \(r\), and assigning \(r/2\) agents of each type to each of that type’s four bundles.

Thus the discrete \(20/21\) obstruction disappears in this replicated regime. As an existence question, the mirror has an exact, constructive answer. Its broader algorithmic version—computing continuous MMS benchmarks and allocations for variable numbers of item categories and types—would need a separate complexity analysis; this paper does not provide one. The weakest point is that replication and type-level mass split the goods in a way the four-agent instance cannot. That co-modification is natural for a high-multiplicity mirror, but it means this is not a continuous version of the paper’s finite-instance obstruction unchanged.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, but it does not meet ChoCo’s computational criterion. It proves an extremal existence statement: there is a four-agent instance for which every allocation leaves someone below \(20/21\) of their MMS. It does not classify the complexity of finding an allocation, computing MMS, or deciding whether a target guarantee is attainable. The exhaustive enumeration in the appendix verifies this one small instance; it is not a complexity result. The other numbered theorems are also impossibility bounds, not complexity claims.

The proposed continuous model is sensible. Its two valuation types and per-capita supplies make a natural high-multiplicity allocation problem, and assigning fractional mass to bundles while keeping each bundle integral respects indivisibility. But the mirror it establishes is still an existence comparison. To turn it into a computational question, one would need to generalize to variable numbers of types and item categories and ask for algorithms or complexity bounds. That may be worthwhile research, but it is a new computational problem rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. The paper itself says its focus is on guarantees “without computational restrictions.”

So the strongest negative case is a scope objection: this paper offers no eligible computational anchor for ChoCo. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous study of MMS is possible. The replicated instance makes the high-multiplicity regime particularly plausible; it just does not supply the missing computational result.
