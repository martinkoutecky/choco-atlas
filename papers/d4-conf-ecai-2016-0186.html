What the mirror covers

The proposed question draws on Theorem 1 and its supporting Proposition 1, while leaving the empirical ECR, SACR, and EEE comparisons aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow: a continuous mirror of the paper’s truthful threshold auction. The paper proves no numbered \(P\), NP-hardness, or parameterized-complexity result, so I would not present its empirical rankings as complexity anchors. Its strongest named anchor is the paper’s own Theorem 1, proved here. It says that a threshold-based online auction is truthful when agents may misreport valuations and may delay arrival or leave early, but may not arrive early or stay late. In that auction, resource-specific thresholds determine which resources an agent can lock; at departure the agent receives the locked resource giving them the highest utility, and pays the second-highest sample bid if they arrived by the cutoff, or the highest sample bid otherwise. The supporting Proposition 1, also proved here, establishes the arrival-departure priority property used in the truthfulness proof.

Here is the regime in which I think that result has a credible mirror: a large crowdsourcing platform matching many experts to repeated task templates. A type is an expert’s full valuation vector for the resource classes and their arrival and departure window. Experts share a type only when those details match. There may be millions of experts but relatively few distinct types; each resource class has a supply that scales with the cohort. That last change is deliberate: keeping the paper’s fixed number of one-off resources while making the population mass one would make the available supply vanish. Repeated task slots are a plausible way to scale supply with the population.

My proposed problem is Mass Threshold Auction Synthesis. An instance has a finite horizon \(H\), \(k\) ranked resource classes, rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), a finite type set \(\mathcal T\) of rational \((v,a,d)\) vectors, and a threshold fraction \(\alpha_j\) for each resource class. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal T\), with total mass one. The mechanism sees reported mass only as it arrives. It may split a type’s mass fractionally across outcomes, but each infinitesimal agent receives at most one resource, only during their reported availability, and total assigned mass of class \(j\) cannot exceed \(q_j\). The question is whether one can construct a threshold-based online allocation and payment rule, using the first \(\alpha_j\) fraction of arrivals to set each class’s mass threshold, that is feasible and dominant-strategy truthful for every type and every permitted report \((\hat v,\hat a,\hat d)\) with \(a\leq\hat a\leq\hat d\leq d\). A solution is the rule together with a proof of feasibility and truthfulness; a negative answer should give a mass profile and profitable deviation. The underlying question is about dynamic assignment and incentives, not about any encoding used to represent the mechanism.

I would expect this finite-support version to be tractable, if its feasibility and incentive constraints admit a polynomial-size LP or an efficient separation procedure. The paper’s proof gives a concrete reason to investigate that route: threshold payments and the arrival-departure priority argument already organize the discrete truthfulness proof. This would mirror only Theorem 1, not the paper’s empirical claims about ECR, SACR, or EEE. Further questions include whether the construction extends to unrestricted rational valuations, and whether manipulation of arrival and departure creates a hardness boundary.

The weak point is that the mirror scales resource supply and uses fractional mass allocation, while the paper’s theorem concerns indivisible resources assigned to individual agents. Also, truthfulness for an infinitesimal agent may be easier because that agent cannot change the aggregate threshold. If the intended market has genuinely scarce one-off resources, this mirror may not be their problem. For a repeated, high-volume assignment market, though, it is a plausible continuization of the paper’s dynamic assignment and incentive question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the paper’s named results do not establish a computational result. Proposition 1 proves arrival-departure priority for a specified auction; Theorem 1 uses that property to prove truthfulness. Neither poses a complexity question about finding, optimizing, or classifying algorithms. A continuous version of the same proof would be an incentive theorem for a modified mechanism. That may be useful mechanism design, but it does not by itself supply the computational question ChoCo is set up to study.

The proposed repeated-task scenario is plausible. Large cohorts can share complete types, and scaling supply with population size is a reasonable co-modification. So I would not reject it on grounds of multiplicity, identity, or the change from one-off resources. Its difficulty is that the proposed *synthesis* question must add a computational objective to create a ChoCo mirror. If the threshold rule is fixed, checking truthfulness is a new theorem about that rule. If thresholds are to be optimized for welfare, the question also needs an objective and a model of future arrivals; those are additional design choices, not results anchored by Theorem 1. In either case, the theorem itself supplies no complexity landscape to continue.

The continuum version also changes what the truthfulness proof is about. An infinitesimal agent cannot change aggregate cutoffs or sample order statistics through a unilateral report. The mass mechanism may still have meaningful individual allocation and payment incentives, but the finite auction’s strategic effects have changed. A careful mass-auction model could address that change; it would be a new mechanism-design problem, rather than a direct computational mirror of the theorem.

That leaves a real limit to the negative case: I cannot rule out a worthwhile computational problem built from continuous online assignment and truthful threshold synthesis. The repeated-task setting makes that direction credible. The case against is narrower: this paper does not provide a named computational result that motivates such a mirror, and its truthfulness theorem alone does not establish that the proposed synthesis question is the paper’s continuous-population complexity problem.
