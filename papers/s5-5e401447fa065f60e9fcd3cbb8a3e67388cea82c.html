What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high traffic highway observed over a day or year. Let a type be an origin–destination trip \([h,k]\) with a common toll \(q_{hk}\), and let \(\mu_{hk}\) be its share of all trips. With \(V\) trips, the corresponding toll entry is \(t_{hk}=V\mu_{hk}q_{hk}\); the output is the revenue allocated to each segment. The number of vehicles can be enormous while the number of trip types is at most \(n(n+1)/2\). This is a plausible population regime, and the paper’s toll matrix already records tolls collected from users grouped by trip. The paper’s authors would likely recognize this as their allocation setting.

But that is only a plausible population interpretation, not an eligible computational anchor. Theorem 4.1, proved here, says the SPS method is the unique method satisfying efficiency, the inessential segment property, weighted segment symmetry, and covariance. Theorem 5.1, also proved here, gives the analogous characterization of SCS using efficiency, linearity, the inessential segment property, weak segment symmetry, and indifference to individual extensions. Theorem 2.2, attributed to Wu et al. (2024), identifies SES with the Shapley value for the segments allocation game; Theorems 4.2 and 5.3 identify SPS with the \(\tau\)-value and SCS with the average tree solution. These are axiomatic or game-theoretic results, not complexity claims, algorithms with running-time bounds, or computational formulations.

The paper gives explicit allocation formulas and applies them to AP68, but it does not name a computational result about solving an underlying problem. Moreover, representing toll totals as trip masses times toll rates appears to preserve the same aggregate input the paper already uses; the formulas then directly calculate the allocations. There is no evident computational question here whose difficulty might change under continuization.

So the positive case supports the *plausibility* of a high-multiplicity traffic scenario, but it cannot supply a per-anchor prover question or a tractability forecast under your rules. The paper contains no eligible named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.1 characterizes SPS, and Theorem 5.1 characterizes SCS, through axioms. Theorem 2.2 identifies SES with the Shapley value; Theorems 4.2 and 5.3 identify SPS and SCS with other game solutions. None states a complexity bound or studies an algorithmic problem. The AP68 section compares allocations; it does not analyze computation. So the proponent’s trip-type scenario supports a plausible population interpretation, but it does not anchor a continuous computational question in any of the results they cite.

The stronger version of that scenario would take origin–destination trips as types, assign each type a mass and toll rate, and ask for the segment allocation. But the paper’s toll matrix already records total revenue by trip, and its methods give allocation formulas from that aggregate input. Turning the input into masses and per-trip rates does not expose a population-size computation that the paper studies. One could add a new task—such as choosing among allocation methods under a specified fairness objective—but the paper does not define that optimization problem. It would be a new computational question, not a mirror of its characterizations or value identities.

I would not claim that no plausible high-multiplicity highway scenario exists. Traffic over a day or year is a credible one, and the paper’s trip-level aggregation makes it natural. That is the negative case’s weakness: the paper gives little basis for the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror could be formulated. Its firmer point is narrower: the proposed case has no eligible computational anchor, and rescuing it requires adding a computational task the paper itself does not pose.
