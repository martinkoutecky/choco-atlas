The continuous mirror question

Given vendors with rational base prices and bundle-discount schedules whose demand thresholds are population shares; a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete valuation types \(\theta\); and an aggregate allocation \(x_{\theta,a}\) of each type’s mass to whole choices \(a\in S^c\), promised to maximize welfare, compute budget-balanced transfers among positive- and negative-surplus cohorts \(g=(\theta,a)\). Let \(m_g=x_{\theta,a}\), and let \(\sigma_g=v_\theta(a)-p_x(a)-\max_{a'\in S^c}(v_\theta(a')-b(a'))\), where \(p_x(a)\) is the market price under the allocation and \(b(a)\) is the sum of base prices for choice \(a\). Require each payer’s total transfer to be at most \(m_g\sigma_g\), each deficit cohort to receive \(m_h(-\sigma_h)\), and transfers to go only from a positive-surplus cohort buying the full bundle from an active-discount vendor \(s\) to a negative-surplus cohort whose choice includes an item from \(s\). Deviators pay base prices and receive no discount, as in the paper’s rule. Return the resulting per-person price adjustments.

The model it lives in

A society is a finite-support distribution over complete valuation-function types; allocations assign mass to indivisible vendor choices, and discounts trigger on per-capita demand. A max-flow over vendors and recipient vendor-subsets computes the rational transfers, which can be distributed among cohorts to recover per-person price adjustments. The network has \(O(M^c)\) vendor-subset structure and polynomial complexity in the explicit rational input for fixed \(c\).

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 5 and Section IV’s computation of rational, stabilizing prices, with Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 as existence support. It leaves computing a welfare-maximizing allocation in Section V unresolved.

Open questions for a prover

  • What finite-instance to mass-instance correspondence preserves discount thresholds, allocations, and the paper’s stability rule?
  • What rounding guarantees preserve stability when discrete counts approximate masses near discount thresholds?
  • Which stronger deviation rules that let buyers retain discounts after deviating still admit efficient mass-price algorithms?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest affirmative case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s price computation, given a welfare-maximizing allocation. The anchor is Proposition 5, proved in this paper (Section X): for a fixed market and SWM allocation, the paper’s map from feasible flows in its network to rational group transfers is a bijection, and a maximum flow yields rational, stabilizing group transfers. Section IV uses those transfers to compute buyers’ prices. As supporting results, Theorem 1 proves that rational, stabilizing transfers exist for every SWM allocation, and Corollary 1 gives the corresponding existence of rational, stabilizing prices. Proposition 5 is not phrased as a complexity-class theorem; it is the numbered constructive result the paper computes with. Section IV-A gives its Ford–Fulkerson running time under integer-capacity assumptions.

A credible high-multiplicity setting is a large group-buying campaign for standardized products, with millions of potential buyers but relatively few exact valuation profiles—for example, members of procurement or benefit programs using the same price caps for the same vendor bundles. A type \(\theta\) is the buyer’s complete valuation function \(v_\theta:S^c\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), including her values for every possible vendor assignment. Let \(\mu_\theta\) be the fraction of the audience with that type; there are \(\tau\) supported types, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the number of buyers. Vendors’ volume thresholds must be expressed as audience shares, so a threshold means, for instance, that a stated fraction of campaign demand must select each item from that vendor. Prices per purchase stay as in the paper. Each buyer still chooses a whole bundle; only the aggregate allocation of a type may split across choices.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Mass Rational Stabilizing Pricing. The instance gives the vendors’ base prices and discount schedules, the type distribution \(\mu\), and an aggregate allocation \(x_{\theta,a}\) over complete choices \(a\in S^c\), where \(\sum_a x_{\theta,a}=\mu_\theta\). The allocation is promised to maximize social welfare under the market’s aggregate-demand discounts. The task is to find budget-balanced price adjustments that make this allocation stable under the paper’s deviation rule: a deviating buyer pays base prices and receives no discount.

For each positive-mass cohort \(g=(\theta,a)\), let \(m_g=x_{\theta,a}\), let \(\pi_x(a)\) be its market price under the allocation, and let \(b(a)\) be the sum of base prices for its chosen items. Its surplus over its best deviation is
\(\sigma_g=v_\theta(a)-\pi_x(a)-\max_{a'\in S^c}(v_\theta(a')-b(a'))\).
A positive-surplus cohort can contribute at most \(m_g\sigma_g\). It may contribute only if it buys the full bundle from a vendor \(s\) whose discount is active. A negative-surplus cohort needs \(m_g(-\sigma_g)\); it may receive a contribution from \(s\) only if its current choice buys at least one item from \(s\).

The decision variables are nonnegative money transfers \(\lambda_{g,h}\) from positive-surplus cohorts \(g\) to negative-surplus cohorts \(h\). Require that each payer’s total is at most \(m_g\sigma_g\), each needy cohort receives exactly \(m_h(-\sigma_h)\), and \(\lambda_{g,h}=0\) unless the sender and recipient satisfy the vendor conditions just stated. The resulting per-person price adjustment is the net transfer divided by cohort mass. The solution is such a transfer scheme: it balances the budget, respects rationality, and leaves every cohort at least as well off as its best permitted deviation. Equivalently, aggregate cohorts by the paper’s \(P(s)\) and \(N(X)\) groups; the feasible-transfer question becomes its max-flow problem.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, for explicit input and fixed or modest \(c\): the flow network has \(O(M^c)\) seller-subset structure, while the type support affects the cohort totals. Exact rational-capacity max flow gives polynomial bit complexity in the explicit input size. The result is plausible as a mirror because it retains the paper’s allocation, bundle discounts, surplus limits, rationality condition, and stability notion. Continuizing changes buyer counts to masses; it does not turn the products or each buyer’s choice into fractional outcomes. Proportional allocation of transfers within the cohorts can also carry over the paper’s fairness rule.

This case covers Proposition 5 and the paper’s conditional computation of rational, stabilizing prices from an SWM allocation. It does not claim to make computing an SWM allocation tractable. That is the main weakness: the pricing result assumes the hard part of the market problem has already been solved. The paper says in Section V that deciding whether an allocation with welfare at least a given value exists is NP-hard, but that claim is unnumbered and attributed to Lu and Boutilier, so it is not my anchor under the requested rule. A second weakness is that the mirror needs volume thresholds to scale with audience size; fixed absolute thresholds would lose their intended meaning in a population limit.

The natural follow-up questions are whether rational finite-market instances and these mass instances admit a precise high-multiplicity correspondence, how rounding affects stability near discount thresholds, and whether stronger deviation rules that let buyers retain access to discounts have tractable mass-price solutions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that all three anchors reduce to one narrow step: after a welfare maximizing allocation is already known, aggregate subsidy needs and surpluses and route them through a flow network. Proposition 5 makes that correspondence explicit; Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 establish existence for the same transfer problem. The network’s structure is driven by vendors and vendor subsets, while the buyer population contributes capacities. A continuous version would therefore change the capacities to rational masses without exposing a new population-level algorithmic structure. It also leaves the paper’s hard allocation problem untouched.

That is a limited computational payoff, but it does not defeat the mirror. The best version is coherent: a large standardized purchasing campaign can have a small number of complete valuation types, with discount thresholds stated as shares of the campaign. Each person still chooses whole bundles; the type distribution and allocation record masses. The transfer flow then has rational capacities, and type-level price adjustments can be recovered from the aggregate transfers. Neither fractional masses nor exact thresholds create a fundamental obstacle.

Nor does atomlessness by itself make stability vacuous. The paper’s deviation rule denies a deviator the discount, which can be part of the campaign’s signup terms even when one person cannot change aggregate demand. If instead the mirror required a person’s exit to change discount eligibility for everyone, that assumption would need reconsideration; it is not necessary for the price-computation mirror.

So the negative case is that this may be a modest extension of a familiar flow computation, conditional on the paper’s difficult optimization already being done. That is a fair reservation about its payoff, but it is not a strong objection under the programme’s standard. The paper has named constructive results and an algorithm to mirror, and a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Proposition 5 survives the best case against; Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 survive with it. I cannot honestly sustain the requested universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
