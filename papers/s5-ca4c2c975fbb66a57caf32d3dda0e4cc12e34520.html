The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case has a hard limit: this paper contains no numbered computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or a related class. Its central finding is an incentive-compatibility analysis, not a complexity result.

The closest numbered result is Proposition 3, cited in Section 4 from Dagaev and Sonin (2018), not proved here. As the paper describes it, tournament systems with one round-robin tournament followed by multiple knockout tournaments with noncumulative prizes can be strategy-proof only if vacant slots are awarded based on the round-robin results. That is an incentive theorem, not a computational classification, so it cannot honestly serve as one of the required anchors. The paper’s own counterexample and proposed policy change are unnumbered. There are therefore no qualifying anchors to mirror.

As a prospective computational extension, the best case I can make is Mass Seeding-Paradox Search. Consider a large, open-entry qualification system with many clubs under a common seeding code. A type records everything the rule uses: association class, domestic-result class, coefficient tier, titleholder status, feasible result changes, and the club’s utility for each pot. The regime has \(N\) clubs and \(\tau\) such complete types, with \(N\) much larger than \(\tau\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of clubs of type \(t\). Scale the four pot capacities to \(1/4\) of the population each, and apply the paper’s priority rule: first-pot titleholder and champion priorities, vacancy filling by association order, and coefficient order for the remaining placements. At a cutoff, split tied type mass fractionally.

An instance also gives a rational polytope \(\mathcal F(\mu)\) of feasible, league-consistent mass transfers \(x_{tt'}\), where a transfer represents a club cohort moving from one domestic-result type to another after improved results. Write \(\Phi(\nu)_{t,p}\) for the mass of type \(t\) assigned to pot \(p\) under the seeding rule, and \(\mu'_{t'}=\sum_t x_{tt'}\). The question is whether a feasible transfer can leave a positive mass of clubs worse off. More precisely, maximize the mass that moves from a pot \(p\) to a less-preferred pot \(q\), over couplings \(z_{tt'pq}\) of the before-and-after assignments, with their marginals fixed by \(\Phi(\mu)\) and \(\Phi(\mu')\). A positive optimum is a witness; an optimum of zero certifies that no feasible improvement pattern produces this mass-level paradox.

I would expect this specified version to be tractable when the transition polytope and priority tiers are explicit: the pot capacities and type flows suggest an LP or flow formulation. That is a conjecture about the extension, not a result of this paper. It raises related questions about the minimum policy changes needed to eliminate all such mass-level harms, and about the discrete-to-continuous relationship for rational populations.

The scenario is plausible for a large feeder system with many clubs sharing a finite set of complete regulatory and coefficient types; it is a weaker fit for the Champions League as studied. The soft spot is also the main one: the paper’s claim concerns an individual team being punished for better results, while this formulation asks whether a positive mass of clubs can be harmed. It preserves the seeding paradox and makes it a computational search problem, but it does not supply a continuous mirror of a named computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying computational result in this paper to anchor a mirror to. Its main contribution is an incentive-compatibility counterexample and a proposed policy change. Proposition 3 is a result from Dagaev and Sonin, cited in the paper; as described here, it characterizes when a tournament system is strategy-proof, not the complexity of a computational problem. The paper itself proves no complexity theorem. The proponent’s “Mass Seeding-Paradox Search” is therefore a proposed extension, not a mirror of a named result.

That extension has a clear population mismatch with the paper’s mechanism. The UCL example turns on a small number of exceptional, named positions: titleholders, association champions, and vacancies among 32 group-stage places. In a growing population, any one such club has vanishing mass, so a positive-mass harm objective can miss the individual punishment the paper identifies. Scaling the pots is a reasonable co-modification, but scaling the number of titleholders or association vacancies to preserve the same effect changes the mechanism into a different quota-allocation system. Keeping a named club to preserve the individual claim instead gives a hybrid model with a focal agent, not a mass-level version of the paper’s question.

The proposed LP also pushes the key modeling difficulty into its inputs. If the feasible transition polytope and the pot assignments are supplied, maximizing harmed mass may be a straightforward flow or LP exercise. But the paper’s counterfactuals arise from league results among specific clubs, where match outcomes, standings, and tiebreaks link their changes. A type-flow model does not capture those links unless it carries enough identity and competition structure to lose the hoped-for compression. Resolving that tradeoff could motivate a separate tournament-design project, but it does not yet make this paper a strong continuization case.

The negative case is not airtight: one could study many repeated, comparable tournaments and ask what fraction of clubs are exposed to analogous seeding harms. That would provide a plausible aggregate setting. But it shifts the object toward a population of tournament instances and leaves the paper’s own result without a computational anchor. I would not count the proposed search as a surviving mirror of this paper.
