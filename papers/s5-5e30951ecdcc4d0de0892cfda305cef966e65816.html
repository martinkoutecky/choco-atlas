The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(A\) and \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and strict rankings of opposite-side types, each type is a process in the paper's complete bipartite message-passing model. Let \(\delta=\max_{a\in A}(\max_{b\in B} r_b(a)-\min_{b\in B} r_b(a))\), and let \(\tau=\max(|A|,|B|)\). Can every process output its incident entries of a rational coupling \(x\) satisfying \(\sum_b x_{ab}=\mu_a\), \(\sum_a x_{ab}=\nu_b\), and stability— for each \((a,b)\), either the mass of type \(a\) matched to types it prefers to \(b\) is zero or the mass of type \(b\) matched to types it prefers to \(a\) is zero—using \(O(\tau(\delta+1))\) received messages per process, each of \(O(L+\log\tau)\) bits, where \(L\) is the input bit length?

The model it lives in

A complete bipartite network has one process per preference type, with binary-rational mass at each type and rankings over opposite-side types. The output is a stable mass coupling; the computational measure is received messages per type process and message bit length.

The objection that survived

The paper measures communication among individually identified agents, while the mirror uses one process per type to report mass flows; the paper provides no bridge showing that this communication architecture preserves the original problem's constraints.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 16's distributed algorithmic result and the zero-similarity lower-bound question from Lemma 2. It leaves open the paper's bounds for general \(\Delta\), their optimality, and the effect of arbitrary rational masses.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does an \(O(\tau(\delta+1))\)-message algorithm exist for arbitrary rational masses?
  • Does the \(\Omega(\tau/\log\tau)\) lower bound persist in the equal-mass, zero-similarity type-process model?
  • Under what conditions can a stable mass coupling be disaggregated into a stable matching of individuals?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is a mirror of the paper’s distributed computation of a stable matching. The continuous object would be the population, and the result would be a matching of mass between types. Theorem 16 is my lead anchor; Lemma 2 supplies a lower-bound boundary. The paper proves no NP-hardness result, so I would not present either anchor as one.

Lead mirror — Similar-Type Stable Mass Matching. The anchor is Theorem 16, proved in this paper: in its complete bipartite, synchronous message-passing model, Algorithms 2 and 3 compute a stable matching while each node receives \(O(n(\Delta+1))\) messages of \(O(\log n)\) bits. Here, \(\Delta\) bounds how much a man’s rank varies across women’s preference lists. This is the paper’s central algorithmic result.

Here is a continuous analogue that preserves the matching question and the similarity condition. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be finite sets of types on the two sides, with support size \(\tau=\max(|A|,|B|)\). Type \(a\in A\) has rational mass \(\mu_a\), and type \(b\in B\) has rational mass \(\nu_b\), with \(\sum_a\mu_a=\sum_b\nu_b=1\). A type completely specifies an agent’s preferences and other matching-relevant properties. Each type has a strict ranking of the types on the opposite side. Let \(r_b(a)\) be the rank type \(b\) gives to type \(a\), and define the similarity parameter
\[ \delta=\max_{a\in A}\left(\max_{b\in B}r_b(a)-\min_{b\in B}r_b(a)\right). \]

A solution is a rational coupling \(x=(x_{ab})\), where \(x_{ab}\) is the mass matched between types \(a\) and \(b\). It must satisfy \(\sum_b x_{ab}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}=\nu_b\). Define \(P_{ab}=\sum_{b':\,a\text{ prefers }b\text{ to }b'}x_{ab'}\) and \(Q_{ab}=\sum_{a':\,b\text{ prefers }a\text{ to }a'}x_{a'b}\). The coupling is stable if \(P_{ab}=0\) or \(Q_{ab}=0\) for every pair \((a,b)\): there cannot be positive mass on both sides who would prefer to match with each other. There is no welfare objective; the task is to compute a stable coupling.

For the distributed version, each type is a process that knows its own mass and ranking. Processes communicate across the complete bipartite graph under the paper’s rule: in each round, a process may send at most one message or receive at most one, and simultaneous messages to the same recipient are lost. A message can carry an exact rational mass. Given total input bit length \(L\), the question is whether every type process can output its incident matching masses using \(O(\tau(\delta+1))\) received messages, each of \(O(L+\log\tau)\) bits.

I expect this problem to be tractable, with a proof adapting the paper’s similarity-based ordering and deletion structure to mass proposals and fractional acceptance or displacement. Theorem 16 does not establish that bound for this model; it gives a concrete algorithmic result to mirror and a plausible structural route to investigate. The coupling is the natural type-level counterpart of “each node computes its partner”: an individual has one partner, while a type’s mass may be split among several counterpart types.

Lower-bound mirror — Zero-Similarity Stable Mass Matching. The second anchor is Lemma 2, proved here using the set-equality communication lower bound cited from [10]. When \(W_{ij}=m_j\) for all \(i,j\), so every woman has the same ranking and \(\Delta=0\), the lemma says that computing a stable matching requires \(\Omega(n/\log n)\) rounds. Its proof uses the fact that a man’s partner depends on which higher-ranked women are already matched.

The continuous question is whether the same lower bound holds in the type-process model restricted to \(|A|=|B|=\tau\), \(\mu_a=\nu_b=1/\tau\), and \(\delta=0\), with arbitrary strict rankings on the \(A\)-side and a common ranking of \(A\)-types on the \(B\)-side. Each process must output its part of a stable coupling. Stability in this equal-mass subcase forces the highest-ranked \(A\)-type to take its preferred \(B\)-type, then forces the same choice for each successive type among those remaining. Thus the coupling is the discrete matching from the corresponding \(\tau\)-by-\(\tau\) instance. I expect the \(\Omega(\tau/\log\tau)\) message lower bound to transfer.

That remains a communication lower bound, not an NP-hardness result. It is also a bound in the number of distinct types: if each type represents \(K\) people, the bound is in \(\tau\), not in the total headcount \(K\tau\). That is the right scale for the continuous instance, whose input is the type support and its masses.

A plausible regime is a large apprenticeship or residency matching market. Many applicants share complete preference lists over employer categories, and many positions share rankings over applicant categories; the matching-relevant details of a person are included in their type. There may be millions of people but far fewer types. The low-\(\delta\) setting describes employer types whose rankings of applicant types stay within a narrow range. The continuous description then reports fractions of each applicant type matched to each employer type. It is natural when identities within a category do not affect preferences; it does not claim that named individuals can be ignored in markets where they do.

The strongest point is that the model retains the paper’s actual computational task—finding a stable matching—and gives its population-level output a precise form. It covers Theorem 16 and Lemma 2, not every result or implementation detail in the paper. It also produces useful follow-up questions: does the upper bound survive arbitrary rational masses; how does the optimal message complexity depend jointly on \(\tau\), \(\delta\), and \(L\); and when can a stable coupling be disaggregated into a discrete stable matching?

My weakest point is the communication model. Replacing individual processors with one process per type, and allowing a message to carry mass, is a substantial co-modification. It is motivated by the continuous input, but it changes the paper’s distributed architecture; the original proposal-by-proposal proof does not automatically handle fractional flows. The authors may accept the stable-matching problem as their analogue while rejecting this particular communication model. That is the main issue a prover—and a referee—would need to settle.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is weakest at the point where the paper is strongest: it has a named computational result. Theorem 16 gives a distributed message bound, and Lemma 2 gives a communication lower bound. So the objection cannot be that there is nothing computational to mirror.

The more serious mismatch is what the input and output mean. In the paper, preference lists rank *named people*. Even at \(\Delta=0\), when every woman has the same ranking of men, men may rank women arbitrarily. Those rankings distinguish the women for the matching problem. To preserve that information in types, a type must capture the relevant pattern of how the opposite side ranks its members; with strict rankings of individuals, that can leave essentially one type per person. A distribution over types then hides the headcount without reducing the support or the identity-sensitive structure that drives the algorithm.

The strongest continuous proposal avoids that by giving each type a ranking over counterpart *types* and returning a coupling of masses. That is a coherent matching problem: the flow can be disaggregated into matches between individuals, so no person needs to receive a fractional partner. But copies within a type are now interchangeable, and stability is assessed between types. This is a natural mass-market variant, but it changes the preference granularity and the object every participant computes.

That distinction matters especially for Theorem 16. Its resource bound is tied to one process per individual, each with its own message slot and exact partner to report. A process per type instead acts for an entire cohort and reports a mass allocation. The number of processes, who controls them, and what a message represents all need new choices. Keeping individual processes leaves the original identity-sensitive problem; using type processes yields a distributed algorithm for a type-level market. The theorem does not identify a canonical communication model connecting those two.

Lemma 2 has the same difficulty. Its set-equality argument relies on distinct women being separate recipients, even though they share the same ranking of men. If those women are grouped into one type, the recipient side and its communication bottleneck change. If they remain separate types to preserve the rankings men assign to them, the support retains the individual distinctions the population quotient was meant to remove. The proponent’s equal-mass construction may still motivate a lower bound in a chosen type-process model, but that would be a result about that model, not an automatic continuation of the paper’s bound.

A large cohort matching market is a plausible setting for the mass version, and the type-level question could be worthwhile in its own right. That is also why this is not an airtight case against every mirror. The strongest objection is narrower: the paper’s defining contribution measures communication among individually identified agents, while the continuous proposal measures communication among aggregate types. The connection is substantial, but the paper does not supply the bridge.
