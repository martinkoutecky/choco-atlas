The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest anchor-based case for a continuous mirror here: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result such as membership in P, NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. Its results concern which priority profiles make deferred acceptance obviously strategyproof (OSP).

The closest numbered result is Theorem 2.9, cited from Ashlagi and Gonczarowski [AG18], not proved in this paper. It says that acyclic priorities make deferred acceptance OSP; gives a specific cyclic OSP profile,
\[ 1:a\succ b\succ c,\quad 2:a\succ c\succ b,\quad 3:b\succ a\succ c; \]
and says that DA is not OSP for either of two displayed priority profiles:
\[ (1:a\succ b\succ c,\ 2:b\succ c\succ a,\ 3:c\succ a\succ b) \]
or
\[ (1:a\succ b\succ c,\ 2:a\succ b\succ c,\ 3:c\succ a\succ b). \]
That is a structural existence result, not a complexity classification. The paper’s own Theorem 3.2 proves that limited cyclic priorities make DA OSP; Theorem 4.1 rules out each of the priority patterns in Figure 1; and Theorem 4.2 proves that avoiding those patterns implies limited cyclicity. Together with the unnumbered Main Theorem, these characterize OSP implementability, but they do not assert the running time or hardness of a computational problem.

There is also a serious obstacle to turning even that characterization into a population-continuous mirror. In the paper’s strict-priority model, an applicant’s complete type must include their priority position. Since every applicant has a distinct rank in each strict priority list, applicants do not repeat complete types: the support size would grow with the population. Replacing strict priorities with tied priority classes might create repeated types, but it changes the model whose OSP properties are characterized.

So there is no qualifying anchor for which I can state the requested continuous problem. A mass-based OSP classification for matching markets could be a prospective extension, but presenting it as a mirror of this paper’s computational result would overstate what the paper establishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational-complexity result in the paper to mirror. Theorem 2.9 is a result from Ashlagi and Gonczarowski; Theorem 3.2 constructs OSP mechanisms for limited cyclic priorities; Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 rule out priority patterns and complete the characterization. The Main Theorem says when an OSP implementation exists. None studies an algorithmic problem such as bribery, control, or optimization over a society. One could define a recognition problem—given priority lists, decide whether DA is OSP—but that would be a computational recasting of the paper’s structural predicate, not a computational result the paper establishes. Its forbidden-subpattern characterization even suggests a direct finite scan. A continuous version would first need to explain what it means for a distribution of applicants to be OSP.

The best population scenario is a large school-choice or hiring market with many applicants. But the paper’s fixed priorities are strict rankings of named applicants. An applicant’s complete priority signature across positions is therefore unique: even one strict ranking assigns each applicant a different rank. If that signature is part of the type, the support grows with the number of applicants, so aggregation does not produce a high-multiplicity regime. If applicants are grouped by a coarser attribute, their ordering within each group still affects DA’s assignments and stability. Treating them as tied instead changes the strict-priority model being characterized. Here, identity is not an incidental feature such as an idiosyncratic price that can be included in a type; the exact rank is the distinguishing feature.

The strongest proposed repair is a continuum of applicants with priority ranks and preferences, matched to capacities by a mass-based DA rule. That may be a worthwhile new model, but it does not preserve the paper’s central distinctions automatically. The characterization turns on exact patterns among three or four applicants, and its cyclic OSP examples differ from a common priority order by finitely many adjacent swaps. In a nonatomic limit, those swaps and the forbidden finite patterns have zero mass. An almost-everywhere classification can therefore erase distinctions the paper treats as decisive. Requiring OSP for each individual would retain those distinctions, but then the priority order and deviations must be specified pointwise; the population distribution alone is insufficient. The alternative—weak or tied priorities and fractional type-level matches—changes the environment and asks for a new OSP theory.

So the strongest negative case is that this paper offers a valuable characterization of a finite, identity-sensitive mechanism property, but no named computational problem whose population axis can be continuized. A continuum matching model with type-based priorities could be interesting, yet it would be a prospective extension rather than a mirror of one of this paper’s computational results. The case is strongest against treating this paper as a ChoCo mirror; it cannot rule out authors finding a new continuum mechanism-design question worthwhile.
