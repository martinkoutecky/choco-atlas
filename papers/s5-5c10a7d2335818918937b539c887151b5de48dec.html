The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its named results are economic characterizations: for example, Theorem 10 and Theorem 14 (proved here) equate preference and resource cores under additional assumptions. The closest bridge is Theorem 18 (also proved here): under the paper’s basic assumptions and social group monotonicity, an allocation is in the \(\gamma\)-core exactly when its social-loss mapping is zero, for any admissible nonzero reference bundle. That is a characterization, not a result that the underlying problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The paper gives no algorithm or computational formulation it works out; its conclusion leaves conditions for existence of zeros to future research.

A plausible follow-on is \(\gamma\)-Core Loss Optimization for a finite-type continuum exchange economy. Consider a large regional exchange economy with an atomless population and finitely many types \(T\), where \(\tau=|T|\) is small relative to the population. A type specifies an agent’s endowment, coalition-dependent consumption rule, and other-regarding preferences. For instance, many households may share an endowment profile and care about the distribution of consumption across the region. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s population mass and \(x_t\) its status-quo bundle. Population mass, rather than headcount, is the object being continuized; the commodity allocation remains the exchange allocation in the paper.

A coalition can select mass \(a_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) from each type and assign its members a distribution \(\eta_t\) of bundles, with total mass \(a_t\). Outsiders keep their status-quo bundles. Thus the post-deviation distribution is
\[ \nu'=\sum_{t\in T}\bigl((\mu_t-a_t)\delta_{(t,x_t)}+\delta_t\otimes\eta_t\bigr). \]
The coalition must respect its type-dependent consumption rules and its initial resources:
\[ \sum_t\int q\,d\eta_t(q)\leq \sum_t a_t e_t. \]
Each participating type-\(t\) agent must weakly prefer their assigned bundle given \(\nu'\) to the status quo given \(\nu_x=\sum_t\mu_t\delta_{(t,x_t)}\). For a reference bundle \(g\), the optimization asks for the largest \(\lambda\geq0\) such that the coalition can still meet those preference conditions while saving at least \(\lambda g\) in resources. Its output is the maximum loss and a coalition and bundle distribution attaining it, or a certificate that the loss is zero. With a finite rational description of preferences, consumption rules, and any distributional statistics they use, the complexity question can be stated in \(\ell,\tau\), and the input bit length.

This mirrors the paper’s \(\gamma\)-core and Theorem 18’s loss characterization, rather than merely making commodities continuous. For this broad version, I would put the problem on the Class C watchlist: fractional coalition masses and bundle choices interact through the externality distribution and may produce nonconvex constraints. That is a conjectural forecast, not a result of the paper; a tractable Class A case may emerge under stronger structure. Further questions include when the measure-valued decisions admit finite-support certificates, what changes for the \(\alpha\)-core’s universal outsider reactions, and how the problem relates to discrete high-multiplicity economies.

The weakest point is the symmetry assumption needed for this mirror: the proposed types share preferences over a distribution of consumption, whereas the paper allows preferences over the full, potentially non-anonymous allocation of named agents. So this is a plausible economic extension of the paper’s core problem, but it is not an anchored computational mirror of a result the authors prove.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor is Theorem 18, and it does not clear the programme’s computational-result gate. It says that an allocation belongs to the \(\gamma\)-core exactly when its social-loss mapping is zero. That is an economic characterization, not a result about the complexity of computing the mapping, testing core membership, or finding an allocation. The paper gives no finite encoding of preferences: continuity and completeness alone do not make a preference relation a computable input. Asking for an algorithm therefore requires choosing a representation and restrictions that the theorem neither states nor studies.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror would ask whether a finite-type society has a \(\gamma\)-blocking coalition, or would compute its maximum social loss. That can be a coherent research problem. But to keep the paper’s full model, a type must capture how an agent evaluates the consumption of every other agent and which consumption sets apply for every coalition containing them. Those descriptions can distinguish every individual, so the number of types need not stay small. To obtain a useful finite-type continuum, one must instead impose anonymity or summarize externalities by type-level statistics and make feasibility depend on coalition composition. Those may be reasonable economic assumptions, but they add a new model and a new computational input language; Theorem 18 supplies no complexity result for either.

That distinction defeats the anchor, though it does not prove the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could ever be built from this paper. An anonymous, finite-type exchange economy might be worth studying in its own right, and the paper’s authors might recognize it as a natural extension. The honest negative case is narrower: the proposed construction is not a computational mirror of a result in this paper, and the paper gives too little computational structure to establish that such a mirror is worth the programme’s time.
