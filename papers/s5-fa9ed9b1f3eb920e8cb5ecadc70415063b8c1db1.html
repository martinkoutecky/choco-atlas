What the mirror covers

The proposed follow-on concerns the implementation-existence result in Theorem 1; it does not computationalize the paper's other implementation characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that the paper studies an economy where a continuous population is a natural model. In a large market, let a type \(t\) be a household’s full utility and endowment \((u_t,\omega_t)\), and let \(\mu_t\) be its share of the population. An allocation assigns a per-capita bundle \(x_t\) to each type, with feasibility \(\sum_t \mu_t x_t=\sum_t\mu_t\omega_t\). This captures the paper’s exchange-economy subject and makes type-level resource clearing part of the mirror.

The nearest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for \(n\ge3\), if the designer knows there is at least one partially honest agent but does not know their identity or number, then every efficient social choice correspondence is partially honestly implementable in Nash equilibrium by a natural price-quantity-\((n-1)\) mechanism. This is a substantial named implementation result. But it is not a computational result: it establishes the existence of a mechanism, not a complexity bound or an algorithm for solving a computational problem. The paper contains no named result asserting that a problem is in \(P\), hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. So Theorem 1 cannot honestly serve as the required computational anchor.

A concrete research follow-on would be Continuous Partial-Honesty Implementation. An instance would specify a finite set of types with rational masses, their utility and endowment data, and an explicitly represented efficient correspondence \(F\). A solution would be a specified mechanism mapping agents’ reports to a measurable allocation, with aggregate feasibility, forthrightness, and best responses, such that its Nash-equilibrium allocations equal \(F\) for every admissible distribution of partially honest agents. The intended question is whether such a mechanism exists for \(F\) in the continuous population model. This is a plausible extension of the paper’s concern with implementing efficient allocations when the designer cannot identify honest agents; it does not yet yield a complexity question anchored in the paper.

The main obstacle is the equilibrium concept. In an atomless population, one person’s deviation has zero mass, so the finite-agent Nash logic of Theorem 1 may not carry over. Treating each type as a single strategic bloc would restore consequential deviations, but would make the bloc coordinate its members’ actions—an assumption the paper does not make. Requiring a positive mass of honest agents also changes the paper’s condition that there merely be at least one. These changes may be defensible in a follow-on model, but they are substantial.

Thus the positive case is that large, repeated exchange economies make a continuous-population *implementation* question worth asking. The soft spot is decisive for this assignment: the proposed question is a new implementation-theory problem, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper. It has no honest tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific complexity prediction on the evidence given here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is the proponent’s own admission: Theorem 1 is not a computational result. It says that, for \(n\ge3\), an efficient social choice correspondence has a natural mechanism whose Nash outcomes implement it. It gives no algorithm or complexity bound for an encoded input. The other named results characterize which correspondences mechanisms can implement; they also do not classify a computational problem. So the paper offers no computational task for ChoCo to continuize.

The best proposed population model is sensible on its own terms: let a type include a household’s utility, endowment, and honesty status, give each type a rational mass, and impose feasibility through per-capita bundles. But that yields a new implementation-existence question, not a computational mirror. For a fixed known economy, asking for a mechanism tailored to that economy drops the paper’s information problem. To retain implementation, the mechanism must work across a domain of economies; yet the paper permits arbitrary utility functions and arbitrary correspondences \(F\), with no finite representation for either or for the mechanism to be output. Choosing parametric utilities, a representable class of \(F\), and a restricted mechanism language could make a complexity problem—but those choices would define a new project, and would determine its complexity.

There is also a real issue in lifting the paper’s honesty assumption to an atomless population. Its result uses a named individual’s unilateral deviation, and the designer knows at least one such individual is honest. In an anonymous, aggregate-based continuum model, one individual has zero mass, so their report cannot move the aggregate allocation. A positive mass of honest agents does not by itself restore that pivotal role: treating them as a coordinated bloc, or making outcomes depend on a person’s identity, changes the strategic model. Those could be defensible choices, but neither follows from the paper’s high-multiplicity interpretation.

I would not claim the broader universal negative is airtight. Repeated households with shared utilities and endowments are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a carefully specified continuum implementation theory might be worthwhile. The stronger conclusion is narrower: that possibility does not supply this paper with a computational mirror. On the evidence here, it is a potentially valuable new implementation-theory direction, outside the complexity landscape this programme is meant to chart.
