What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns Theorem 1’s regret guarantee and Theorem 2’s approximation-factor lower bound for shared caching; both fit the paper’s weighted-agent extension.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is the paper’s shared caching problem with a large user population split into a small number of request cohorts. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) types, where a type is a group with the same fairness parameter and the same class-level demand profile. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users in that cohort. A platform chooses one cache \(y_s\in\Delta_k^N=\{y\in[0,1]^N:\sum_j y_j=k\}\) before seeing the current demands. Members of type \(t\) receive reward \(\langle x_t(s),y_s\rangle\); their cumulative reward is \(R_t^\pi(T)=\sum_{s=1}^T\langle x_t(s),y_s\rangle\). The objective is the population-average \(\sum_t\mu_t\phi_\alpha(R_t^\pi(T))\), compared with the best single cache \(y^*\) fixed in hindsight. This keeps the shared cache and cumulative \(\alpha\)-fair objective; the society, rather than the cache, is what becomes continuous.

That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a platform may serve millions of users but have relatively few recurring request cohorts. The cache capacity need not grow with the population, and per-capita rewards remain meaningful. A fluid cohort’s demand vector can be fractional, as the paper’s general formulation allows. With rational masses and \(\tau\ll M\), where \(M\) is the number of users, the policy can aggregate by type rather than maintain one state per named user.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For NOFRA, OPF achieves \(c_\alpha\)-regret with \(c_\alpha=(1-\alpha)^{-(1-\alpha)}\le e^{1/e}<1.445\). Its regret rates are \(O(T^{1/2-\alpha})\) for \(0<\alpha<1/2\), \(O(\sqrt{\log T})\) at \(\alpha=1/2\), and \(O(1)\) for \(1/2<\alpha<1\); the \(\alpha=0\) case gives the usual \(O(\sqrt T)\) rate. The continuous question is: Continuous Population Fair Caching. Given \(N,k,\alpha\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), and adversarial type demands revealed after each cache choice, construct a causal policy whose weighted regret against the best fixed cache satisfies those bounds. A solution is a policy and a proof of its guarantee. The natural candidate aggregates the current gradients as \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t(s)/R_t(s)^\alpha\) and projects onto the cache polytope. The paper’s proof appears to extend by replacing its sums over users with mass-weighted sums over types; each round then has work polynomial in \(N,\tau\), and the input bit length. I expect this mirror to be tractable.

A second, useful anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. For \(\alpha\)-fair online shared caching with two users, any policy meeting the paper’s sublinear \(c_\alpha\)-regret criterion must have
\[ c_\alpha\ge\max_{0\le\eta\le1/2} \frac{\eta^{1-\alpha}+(1-\eta)^{1-\alpha}} {(1-\eta/2)^{1-\alpha}+(\eta/2)^{1-\alpha}}>1, \qquad 0<\alpha<1. \]
Its continuous counterpart is the Two-Cohort Caching Barrier: take two cohorts of mass \(1/2\), a unit-capacity cache, and the two alternative future-demand sequences from the theorem—after a common first phase, one cohort’s later demand favors file 2 while the other has uniform demand, or the roles reverse. Ask for the smallest multiplicative factor \(c_\alpha\) achievable by a causal policy against those alternatives. The theorem’s bound carries over because the population-average objective is the two-user objective scaled by \(1/2\), which cancels in the ratio. I expect this performance barrier to transfer; it is not an NP-hardness result. A natural follow-up is to determine the exact minimax factor and close the gap to OPF’s upper bound.

The paper has no named NP-hardness, \(P\), or parameterized-complexity classification to anchor on. These two theorems are its relevant named online-algorithm results: an achievable guarantee and a lower bound on the guarantee.

The weakest point is that OPF’s published theorem is stated for named agents, not a mass distribution. Its weighted finite-support version still needs to be written and proved, and the barrier theorem’s randomized individual requests are represented here by fluid, type-level demand profiles. The strongest claim is therefore the direct finite-type extension of Theorem 1; the second anchor is persuasive for the same fluid model, but its connection to stochastic individual request histories would need extra care.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these two anchors is that the finite-support mirror is already inside the paper’s weighted model. For homogeneous cohorts, its population-average objective is
\[ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\phi_\alpha(R_t(T)). \]
Set the paper’s agent weights to \(w_t=\mu_t\), and this is exactly its weighted fairness objective. The paper explicitly says its algorithms and theoretical results extend to that objective. In shared caching, the type-level cumulative rewards and cache decisions match directly, so Theorem 1’s proposed continuous question is already represented by the paper’s model. That is a model collision, rather than an objection that the result would be easy or unsurprising.

The same applies to Theorem 2’s two-cohort barrier: give each cohort weight \(1/2\). This scales both the online and offline welfare by the same factor, leaving the approximation ratio unchanged. The continuous wording adds no new barrier beyond the weighted version of the paper’s construction.

A stronger mirror would preserve variation among individuals within a cohort. A cohort’s mean demand vector generally cannot do that: with half its members requesting file 1 throughout and half requesting file 2, a cache holding file 1 throughout gives population welfare \(\tfrac12\phi_\alpha(T)\). Replacing those people with their average demand gives \(\phi_\alpha(T/2)\), which differs. Splitting the cohort into request-history types fixes the discrepancy, but for finite support it returns to the paper’s weighted-agent formulation; with many distinct histories, the claimed small-support aggregation can disappear.

This weakens the proponent’s average-demand interpretation, but it does not establish that every continuous-population version is unworthy. Shared caching is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and richer models of within-cohort stochastic demand could pose new questions if their information structure and fairness objective were specified. So the negative case defeats these anchors as distinct contributions; it does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
