The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results concern equilibrium welfare and price of anarchy; they do not classify the complexity of computing an outcome or solving an underlying problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time, FPT, or similar. So I can’t honestly state a ChoCo computational mirror for an anchor.

The closest result is Theorem 5.1, proved here. It says that there is an instance with \(n\) unit-demand agents and \(n^2\) items, with \(\gamma=c=1\) and \(\widetilde{\opt}\approx\opt\), in which, under no-overbidding, every item-bidding mechanism and every equilibrium \(\sigma\) have
\[ \opt/\eq(\sigma)=\Omega\!\left(\frac{\ln n\,\ln\ln\ln n}{\ln\ln^2 n}\right). \]
That is a strong strategic inefficiency result, but it is not a computational hardness result.

A nearby population model is imaginable: agents have signal-vector types \(x\in\{0,1\}^{m}\), mass \(\mu_x\), and strategies map types to bids and participation decisions; one could ask for the worst equilibrium welfare ratio under no-overbidding. But this is not yet a credible mirror of Theorem 5.1. In the continuum, an individual winner has zero population mass, so preserving indivisible item awards requires extra structure or a change to the allocation model. And the theorem’s lower bound relies on fluctuations in finitely many sampled signals across items; replacing the sampled population by deterministic type masses can remove those fluctuations. The paper’s \(n^2\)-item construction also gives no evident high-multiplicity compression: its signal-vector type space is huge, and realized bidder types are likely mostly distinct.

The weakest point is therefore decisive for this task: even a well-defined continuum auction model would ask about equilibrium inefficiency, an analytic question, rather than the computational landscape the programme targets. Theorem 5.1 is worth noting as a possible neighboring question, but treating it as a ChoCo anchor would overstate what the paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives no computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Its theorems bound price of anarchy: they compare optimal welfare with welfare at equilibria. They do not ask for an algorithm, classify the complexity of a computational problem, or give an input and output for one. Theorem 5.1, the proponent’s closest candidate, quantifies over every auction and every equilibrium; it proves an asymptotic welfare gap. It does not show that finding an equilibrium, a bad equilibrium, or a mechanism is computationally hard.

The strongest version of their proposed mirror is coherent: represent agents by masses of complete signal-vector types \(x\in\{0,1\}^{m}\), and scale item supply so that allocations to a continuum remain meaningful. Then ask whether the paper’s welfare-ratio guarantee persists for equilibria of the corresponding population auction. But that is still an equilibrium-efficiency question, not a computational one. To make it computational, one would need to add a task—such as computing a worst equilibrium or designing a mechanism. Neither is a result in this paper, and adding one would make it a new research direction rather than a mirror.

The finite-sample fluctuations in Theorem 5.1 may disappear under a deterministic mass model, but that would affect the answer, not whether the question is worth asking, so it is not a sound objection. The stronger point is that even the best population model leaves the paper’s central result outside ChoCo’s computational scope. This is a strong case against a *paper-anchored ChoCo mirror*; it cannot establish that no adjacent computational problem would be worthwhile.
