What the mirror covers

The proposed weighted GLOC check extends the paper’s protocol to rational masses; the paper’s axiomatic classification and implication have no computational results for this mirror to cover.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a weighted GLOC consistency problem. In a large ranked election, let a voter type be a complete ranking of the candidates, and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of voters with ranking \(t\). The regime is one with many voters but relatively few distinct rankings: a national election, for example, may have millions of voters and a much smaller support of ballot types.

An instance consists of the candidate set \(C\), a distribution \(\mu\) over its supported rankings with binary rational masses, a clone partition \(\mathcal K\) valid for those rankings, and one of the paper’s voting rules \(f\), with its tie-breaking convention fixed. Extend \(f\) to weighted profiles by applying its usual tally to the masses. Compute \(f_\infty(\mu)\), then compute the weighted version of \(GLOC_f(\mu,\mathcal K)\): run \(f\) on the collapsed clone sets, then run it within each winning clone set. The solution is both winner sets and a yes/no answer to whether they are equal. This has no optimization objective; it asks whether the two procedures agree on this society.

That is a natural population continuization of the paper’s formal protocol: the ballots remain types, while their aggregate mass replaces the list of individual voters. Authors could plausibly recognize it as the high-multiplicity version of evaluating \(GLOC\). For a fixed rule with a polynomial-time weighted tally, I would expect this instance-level problem to be tractable: it requires one global call and at most \(|\mathcal K|\) local calls. That is an inference about the proposed problem, not a result established by this paper. Further questions include whether the classification changes for arbitrary real masses rather than rational input masses, and how small a support is needed to witness a failure of consistency.

The obstacle is the requested anchor. The supplied text’s Theorem is unnumbered, and says that STV, Schulze, and SAV are not even weakly OIoC; Schwartz is wOIoC; and Ranked Pairs is OIoC. That is an axiomatic classification, not a claim that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. The Proposition that OIoC implies IoC is also a logical implication, not a computational result. Neither qualifies as the named computational anchor the prompt requires, so the proposed consistency problem cannot honestly be presented as mirroring a qualifying anchor.

The weakest point is therefore substantive: the paper’s main question is axiomatic, while the proposed instance-level computation is just weighted evaluation of that axiom’s protocol. Its high-multiplicity interpretation is plausible, but the paper supplies no computational result from which to build the requested complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is decisive for the proposed mirror: the paper contains no computational result to continuize. Its main theorem classifies five rules by whether they satisfy OIoC or wOIoC; the proposition says OIoC implies IoC. Neither states a complexity bound, gives an algorithm, or studies an optimization problem. The positive case identifies this gap, but then proposes a different problem.

That proposed problem is sensible. Rankings make natural voter types, and comparing weighted \(f(\mu)\) with weighted \(GLOC_f(\mu,\mathcal K)\) is a well-defined high-multiplicity evaluation. But it asks whether the axiom holds on one supplied instance. For the fixed rules in the paper, it amounts to running \(f\) on the collapsed profile and on the relevant local profiles, then comparing the results. The theorem instead classifies each rule over all profiles and clone partitions. The paper gives no computational claim about that global classification for the weighted model—or even for the discrete model.

One could formulate a new computational project around finding a violating distribution, minimizing its support, or measuring how robust GLOC agreement is to changes in the masses. Those may be worthwhile questions. They would need their own formulation and motivation, though; none is a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The continuous population regime itself is not the problem: weighted rankings are a credible model. The case against is that this paper offers axiomatic results to mirror, not computational ones.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed *mirror*, but it cannot honestly establish that no nearby continuous computational question could be worthwhile. The supplied text gives no computational anchor for one.
