The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its results are empirical findings about which proposals people and LLMs prefer. So I cannot honestly give it a qualifying anchor or a continuous problem *per anchor*.

There is a natural population model behind its Nash Product. In a large constituency, let each type \(t\) be a group whose members share the same utilities over proposals, and let \(\mu_t\) be that group’s fraction of the population. For an explicitly listed set of proposals \(C\), the Continuous Nash-Product Proposal Selection problem would take rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), positive rational utilities \(u_t(c)\geq 1\), and return a proposal maximizing \(\prod_t u_t(c)^{\mu_t}\), with any maximizer accepted. This preserves the paper’s weighted Nash Product and fits a plausible regime of many constituents divided among relatively few groups. The paper’s Equation 2 already uses group weights, however, and it neither studies this as a computational problem nor establishes a complexity result for it. With an explicitly listed proposal set, the problem is simply to compare the proposals; making the proposal space combinatorial would add a new ingredient the paper does not analyze.

The strongest positive case is therefore a plausible high-multiplicity interpretation of the paper’s formalism, especially its Nash Product. Its weakness is decisive for this assignment: that interpretation is not anchored in a named computational result. The paper’s human and LLM findings, and its analysis of where the mechanisms disagree, do not supply one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies no named computational result to mirror. The paper’s results are empirical claims about which proposals people and LLMs prefer; its equations define aggregation rules, but it gives no complexity theorem, algorithmic problem, or computational analysis of choosing among proposals. Raising no anchor is decisive under the brief.

The proposed population model is coherent: for type masses \(\mu_t\), it selects \(c\in C\) to maximize \(\prod_t u_t(c)^{\mu_t}\), equivalently \(\sum_t \mu_t\log u_t(c)\). A large constituency divided into types is plausible, and that model preserves the paper’s weighted Nash Product. But with the paper’s explicit list of proposals and utility table, selection is just evaluating the listed alternatives. The paper studies neither a costly search problem nor how to compute an intervention or feasible policy.

A stronger computational version could optimize that objective over a compactly described set of feasible proposals—for example, grant portfolios subject to a budget. That could be worthwhile research, but the paper supplies no such feasible-set representation, constraints, or utility model. Adding them creates a new optimization problem; it does not turn this paper’s empirical findings into computational results. The hard part would come from the added proposal space, not from a computational question the paper posed.

So the negative case is strong for the requested *mirror*, but it cannot establish that no useful follow-on problem exists. A combinatorial extension may be worth studying on its own. The paper, as given, does not provide the computational result that would anchor that work as a continuous mirror.
