The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many contract classes and a support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where each type specifies its bounded fully substitutable valuation, contract roles, and compatible local network position, define feasible aggregate allocations \(x_{t,\Psi}\geq 0\) over integral bundles \(\Psi\), with \(\sum_\Psi x_{t,\Psi}=\mu_t\) and contract balance respecting the stated compatibility structure. Let \(P^*_\mu\) be the prices supporting a competitive equilibrium of this aggregate market. Does the process that samples type \(t\) with probability \(\mu_t\), selects a demanded bundle, and updates prices on its un demanded incident contracts produce average prices \(\bar p\) with \(\mathbb{E}[d_\infty(\bar p,P^*_\mu)]\leq\varepsilon\); what is its running-time dependence on \(m,\tau,L,R,\Delta,\) and \(\varepsilon\), especially when masses are small?

The model it lives in

A market with finite support \(T\) of complete agent types and rational masses \(\mu_t\). Each type keeps its integral bundle choices and local trading role; aggregate variables \(x_{t,\Psi}\) split its mass across bundles, subject to compatibility-aware contract balance. The price objective is \(L_\mu(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\max_{\Psi}(v_t(\Psi)-\sum_e\chi^t_e p_e)\), and the clock process samples a type in proportion to its mass.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is the paper’s Proposition 4, proved here in Appendix A.4. For a market with maximum degree \(\Delta\), \(m\) trades, \(n\) agents, and valuations bounded in absolute value by \(R\), it considers the stochastic price process with step size \(R\sqrt{2m/(T\Delta)}\). For \(T\ge 2n^2R^2m\Delta\), the proposition says the average price \(\bar p\) has expected \(L_\infty\) distance \(O(1/\sqrt T)\) from the set of competitive-equilibrium prices. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result; the paper gives no comparable named NP-hardness result.

A natural mirror is Type-Clock Equilibrium Pricing. Let \(E\) be a finite set of standardized bilateral contract classes and \(T\) a support of agent types. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with rational \(\mu_t\ge 0\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type specifies an agent’s full valuation, buyer or seller role on each available contract class, and local network position. Its valuation \(v_t\) is integer-valued, bounded by \(R\), and fully substitutable; each type is incident to at most \(\Delta\) classes. Thus agents of one type share every feature used by the market, while the population itself is described by masses.

An allocation assigns masses \(x_{t,\Psi}\ge 0\) of type \(t\) to integral bundles \(\Psi\subseteq E_t\), with \(\sum_\Psi x_{t,\Psi}=\mu_t\). For each contract class \(e\), buyer and seller mass must balance:
\[ \sum_{t,\Psi} \chi^t_e\,x_{t,\Psi}=0. \]
The allocation is a competitive equilibrium at prices \(p\) if every bundle receiving positive mass is demanded by its type at \(p\). Fractional quantities arise from splitting a type’s mass across bundles, while each individual still receives an integral bundle.

The problem is: given this market, find prices close to the equilibrium price set \(P^*_\mu\). Its natural potential is
\[ L_\mu(p)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \max_{\Psi\subseteq E_t} \left(v_t(\Psi)-\sum_{e\in\Psi}\chi^t_e p_e\right). \]
The mirror of the paper’s clock process samples \(t\) with probability \(\mu_t\), chooses a demanded bundle, and updates prices of incident classes the type does not demand by \(p_e\leftarrow p_e-\eta\chi^t_e\). The question for a prover is whether, and with what dependence on \(m,\tau,L,R,\Delta\), and an accuracy target \(\varepsilon\), the average prices achieve
\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[d_\infty(\bar p,P^*_\mu)\right]\le\varepsilon, \]
where \(\tau=|T|\) and \(L\) is the bit length of the rational masses and valuations. Under full substitutability, equilibrium prices minimize the aggregate indirect-utility potential, so this is directly the paper’s equilibrium-price computation question in a population represented by types.

The regime I believe in is a large market with recurring local roles: for example, many suppliers, intermediaries, and buyers in standardized supply chains, with finitely many valuation and network-neighborhood types. Millions of agents can then be represented by a much smaller support. The mirror is plausible because the paper already studies a stochastic clock with centralized prices, and its convergence proof identifies price adjustment with stochastic subgradient descent. Sampling a type according to its mass gives the corresponding population-weighted update. The per-capita allocation remains integral; it is the population mass that becomes continuous.

I expect this pricing problem to be tractable, at least for the potential-gap objective: \(L_\mu\) is convex and piecewise linear, and each demand calculation can be performed from the type’s bundle description. The more delicate question is the proposition’s *price-distance* guarantee. After normalizing masses, the potential’s slope away from equilibrium can depend on the smallest nonzero mass combination. That quantity may be small relative to the bit length \(L\). So I would not claim the paper’s stated rate transfers with constants polynomial in the continuous input size without proving an appropriate error bound. Further questions include whether exact prices can be found in polynomial time for bounded \(\Delta\), and how the price-distance rate depends on rational masses.

The weakest point is the network abstraction: a continuum of agents cannot be represented by type masses alone if named counterparties or individualized contract links matter. I would restrict the mirror to markets with finitely many repeated contract classes and type-complete local neighborhoods. Also, this is a mirror of the paper’s price-finding algorithm, not a claim that an atomless agent has the same strategic influence as a finite agent. That limitation narrows the regime, but leaves a credible population-continuous computational question anchored in the paper’s main convergence result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 4 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives a named convergence guarantee for finding competitive-equilibrium prices. So the decisive objection that the paper has no computational result is unavailable, and “the continuous version might be easy” would not defeat it.

The best objection is that the proposed type model may discard the network the result is about. In the paper, a trade is between particular agents, and each agent’s valuation is over bundles of their particular incident trades. Balancing buyer and seller mass for each contract class does not, by itself, ensure that those trades can be assembled in the original network. Preserve the counterpart links in the types, and the support may grow with the population; collapse them into interchangeable contract classes, and the model permits rewiring. A more faithful limit might need to represent the network and its edge incidence as well as the distribution of agent types. That is a substantial modeling choice, not just a change from counts to proportions.

There is also a mismatch in the clock dynamic. In the finite market, one sampled agent changes prices by a full step, regardless of how many agents share that type. In an atomless population, a sampled individual has zero mass and cannot move aggregate prices. Keeping the full step gives a stochastic optimization routine over types, rather than a literal limit of the decentralized market; scaling the step by type mass instead gives a different process. The aggregate potential
\[ L_\mu(p)=\sum_t\mu_t\max_{\Psi}\left(v_t(\Psi)-\sum_e\chi^t_e p_e\right) \]
does yield a plausible price-finding question, but the paper’s price-distance guarantee does not transfer automatically: converting potential error to distance can depend on small rational masses, potentially exponentially small in their bit length.

Those objections narrow the mirror; they do not eliminate its best version. Standardized markets with interchangeable counterparties and finitely many recurring agent and contract types form a plausible, well-defined regime, and the authors could recognize weighted equilibrium-price computation there as their problem. For that reason, I cannot make a strong universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. My negative case rests on the gap between the paper’s network structure and the compressed model, plus the change in meaning of the clock process. A network-aware continuum model could answer both objections, and I do not have grounds to dismiss it.
