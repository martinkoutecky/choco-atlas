The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under the anchor requirement. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result—such as a complexity classification or an algorithmic bound.

Its numbered “Resultado 1–4” are empirical findings. They report, respectively, that proposals vary with institution and strategic incentives; individual acceptance is higher under democracy than dictatorship; collective rejection is lower under democracy; and earnings are higher under democracy, with indirect democracy performing at least as well as direct democracy. Those findings come from repeated three-person laboratory games, not from a computational problem the paper analyzes.

A continuous population model of citizens’ proposal and acceptance behavior could be a plausible *extension*: types might encode those behaviors, mass their population shares, and the institution determine how proposals are selected and accepted. But the paper gives no computational question or named computational result for that model to mirror. Presenting it as a mirror of this paper’s results would go beyond the evidence and miss the required computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case for raises no computational anchor, so there is no named result here for a continuous mirror to preserve. That is the strongest objection: Results 1–4 are empirical findings from repeated three-person bargaining games. They report estimated differences in proposals, acceptance, rejection, and earnings across treatments. The paper gives no algorithm, complexity claim, or computational problem.

The strongest candidate mirror would put a distribution over complete behavioral types and model how groups of three are formed, then compare expected outcomes under dictatorship and the two democratic institutions. That could be a sensible population model. But its questions would be about predicting or explaining behavior across institutions, not about the computational task the paper studies: there isn’t one. To bring in optimization or complexity, one would have to add a task—such as finding a welfare-maximizing proposal or institution—that the paper does not formulate.

So the negative case is strong within ChoCo’s scope, though limited: this paper offers no computational result to continuize. That does not establish that a population model of its bargaining experiments would be uninteresting; it establishes that such a model would be a new research question, not a continuous mirror of the paper’s results.
