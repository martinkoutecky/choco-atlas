The continuous mirror question

Given a rational metric \(d\), a common destination \(D\), \(\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}>0\), and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over rider types \(t=(p_t,\alpha_t)\), find an ordering \(\pi\) of the support minimizing its route length to \(D\), subject at every cohort pickup \(j\ge2\) to \(\delta_j\sum_{k<j}\mu_{\pi_k}\alpha_{\pi_k}\le\mu_{\pi_j}\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}d(p_{\pi_j},D)\), where \(\delta_j=d(p_{\pi_{j-1}},p_{\pi_j})+d(p_{\pi_j},D)-d(p_{\pi_{j-1}},D)\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity cohort-shuttle model: each type specifies a pickup point and detour sensitivity, its mass is its population share, and each cohort boards at one pickup stop. Normalize aggregate costs per capita; ask for a minimum-distance ordering of support pickups with budget-balanced charges that keep existing cohorts’ disutility nonincreasing at each cohort arrival.

The objection that survived

The continuous constraint models a whole cohort arriving at once; under individual sequential arrivals, the first rider at a hub faces a detour bound that shrinks with the number of earlier riders. The proponent identifies cohort arrivals as a deliberate service scenario, though whether the authors would accept that SIR variant remains the main semantic risk.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of the paper’s route optimization problem, in a commute service where many riders share a pickup hub, destination, and detour sensitivity. A type is the complete request description \(t=(p_t,D,\alpha_t)\): pickup location, common destination, and inconvenience rate. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of riders with that type. A type is a cohort, picked up together; the number of riders can be much larger than the number of distinct pickup types.

The anchor is Theorem 8, proved in this paper in Appendix B.2. It says that Opt-SIR-Route is NP-hard: given pickup points, a common destination in a metric space, and positive operating and passenger detour-cost coefficients, find an SIR-feasible route of minimum total distance. The proof reduces Metric-Path-TSP to this problem: it sets all passenger sensitivities equal and places the destination far enough away that every pickup ordering is SIR-feasible. The theorem and its reduction are the result I consider strongest for a continuous mirror.

I would call the mirror Mass-SIR-Route. Its input is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over pickup types \(t=(p_t,D,\alpha_t)\), a rational metric \(d\), and \(\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}>0\). A feasible route orders the support types, visiting each pickup location once and collecting that type’s mass at that stop. For a route \(\pi\), let \(\ell(\pi)\) be its total distance, including the final leg to \(D\). The question is to find an SIR-feasible ordering minimizing \(\ell(\pi)\).

Here is a precise cohort-level SIR condition for this mirror. When type \(\pi_j\) joins after \(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_{j-1}\), it adds detour

\[ \delta_j = d(p_{\pi_{j-1}},p_{\pi_j}) +d(p_{\pi_j},D) -d(p_{\pi_{j-1}},D). \]

In the high-multiplicity limit, the cohort pickup is feasible when

\[ \delta_j \sum_{k<j}\mu_{\pi_k}\alpha_{\pi_k} \le \mu_{\pi_j}\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}d(p_{\pi_j},D), \qquad 2\le j\le \tau. \]

The left side is the previous riders’ mass-weighted increase in inconvenience; the right side is the incoming cohort’s mass-weighted room below its private-ride cost. This is the limit of the paper’s aggregate SIR budget-balance condition as population size grows. A solution is an ordering together with per-type charges or rebates at each prefix that respect this condition and keep existing riders’ disutility from increasing. The route objective remains vehicle miles; the population has been continuized, while the route order over its finite support remains a decision.

The regime is a high-volume shuttle corridor: many riders share each pickup hub and destination, while the distinct hubs are the support types. The model assumes pickup cohorts arrive as batches and that the service can scale with demand. That co-modification is part of the mirror: mass is what lets us express the cumulative inconvenience borne by a cohort without treating each rider as a separate type.

This mirror is hard for its own support-ordering reason, so I expect Class B, hardness transfers. Given a Metric-Path-TSP instance on \(\tau\) locations, assign each location mass \(1/\tau\), set all sensitivities to \(\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}\), and put \(D\) at equal distance \(L\) from every location. If \(B\) is the largest distance between two locations, choose \(L>\tau B\). Then every ordering satisfies the cohort SIR condition, while its route length is \(L\) plus the path length through the locations. Minimizing the continuous route therefore solves Metric-Path-TSP. Moreover, each location can represent \(K\) identical riders for arbitrarily large \(K\): population size grows while the support ordering still carries the hard combinatorics.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors Theorem 8’s single-destination metric route optimization. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s fairness characterization or its ordered-vehicle allocation algorithm. It also keeps the society continuous, rather than continuizing the outcome space or the time process.

The weak point is the batch-arrival interpretation. The paper states SIR for individual sequential arrivals; grouping identical riders into a cohort changes that transition, and the limiting mass condition is a proposed analogue rather than a theorem proved in the paper. The case survives if authors accept a pickup hub releasing a cohort as a natural state change in their shared-service model. The next questions would be whether this condition has a finite-population approximation with explicit error bounds, and whether approximation or parameterized algorithms become possible in \(\tau\) or in geometric parameters of the pickup metric.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 8 is a real computational result, so the negative case has to turn on whether the proposed population is genuinely high multiplicity for the paper’s problem. I think that is the weak point. SIR protects customers after each arrival, and a customer’s place in that arrival sequence affects the constraint. Two riders with the same pickup, destination, and detour sensitivity but different arrival positions are therefore not indistinguishable in the sense high multiplicity requires. If arrival order is part of a complete type, most types are singletons.

The discrepancy changes the feasible routes. Suppose there are \(K\) riders at each of several hubs, all with \(\alpha_i=\alpha_{\mathrm{op}}\), and riders arrive sequentially as in the paper. The first rider at hub \(j\) changes the route by detour \(\delta_j\) after \(K(j-1)\) earlier riders. The paper’s SIR condition requires

\[ \delta_j \le \frac{d(p_j,D)}{1+K(j-1)}. \]

As \(K\) grows, that permits only vanishing detours. The proposed mass condition instead gives, for equal masses, \(\delta_j \le d(p_j,D)/(j-1)\). That is a nonzero bound independent of \(K\). It comes from treating the \(K\) riders as one simultaneous cohort, so their combined private-ride slack can compensate existing riders. That is a coherent batch-arrival variant, but it is not the limit of the paper’s individual-arrival SIR condition. It changes the granularity at which sequential rationality is enforced, which is central to the paper’s contribution.

There is a plausible rescue: scheduled shuttle cohorts at pickup hubs could arrive as batches, and a route could collect each cohort together. But the physical scaling then matters. If one vehicle serves all riders as \(K\) grows, its capacity must grow with the population and its operating cost per rider tends to zero. If capacity stays fixed, the population requires many vehicles or repeated trips; the decision becomes an allocation across trips, not Theorem 8’s single route through all pickup points. Either model may be worth studying, but each adds a substantive service-system assumption to make the mass model work.

So the strongest case against this anchor is that its proposed continuous mirror obtains useful mass constraints only by replacing individual sequential arrivals with cohort arrivals, while retaining a single route whose capacity scales with the whole population. The proponent’s Metric-Path-TSP reduction is valid for that proposed model, but it does not settle whether the model is a mirror the authors would recognize as their SIR problem. I cannot make the universal negative airtight: a scheduled, high-capacity cohort service is plausible, and the authors might accept batch SIR as a natural extension. If they do, Theorem 8 survives as an anchor; the negative case is weak exactly at that boundary.
