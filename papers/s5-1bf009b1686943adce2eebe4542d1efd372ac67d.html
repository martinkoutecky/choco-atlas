The continuous mirror question

Fix two values \(l<h\) with constant ratio \(h/l\), a finite set \(\Theta\) of public classes, and a rational distribution \(\nu\) over \(\Theta\times\{l,h\}\) of binary encoding length \(L\). Feasibility is a downward-closed set \(\mathcal F\subseteq[0,1]^{\Theta}\), given by a membership oracle, of feasible aggregate service masses. A black-box allocation rule \(\mathcal A\) maps each such distribution to service rates \(x_{\theta,r}(\nu)\in[0,1]\), with \(y_\theta=\sum_{r\in\{l,h\}}\nu_{\theta,r}x_{\theta,r}(\nu)\in\mathcal F\). Does there exist a uniform wrapper running in time polynomial in the support size \(\tau\) and \(L\), with polynomially many adaptive queries to \(\mathcal A\) and the feasibility oracle, that for every \(\mathcal A,\mathcal F,\nu\) returns feasible rates monotone in reported value, \(x_{\theta,h}(\nu)\ge x_{\theta,l}(\nu)\), and welfare at least \(c\sum_{\theta,r}r\nu_{\theta,r}x_{\theta,r}(\nu)\) for some fixed \(c>0\)?

The model it lives in

A population distributed over a fixed set of public classes and two reported values, with mass \(\nu_{\theta,r}\) in each class-value type. Each individual receives an indivisible service; \(x_{\theta,r}\) is the cohort's service probability, and aggregate served mass \(y_\theta\) must lie in downward-closed \(\mathcal F\). The wrapper has black-box access to the allocation rule and feasibility oracle, and must preserve a constant share of welfare while making service rates monotone in reported value.

The objection that survived

The paper's finite-agent monotonicity compares outcomes after a one-agent report change, while the mirror's atomless conditional-rate condition is not shown to arise as a limit without regularity assumptions on the black-box rule.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's constant-fraction welfare-preservation impossibility for two values with a constant ratio. It leaves the other results, including the full-welfare, approximation-ratio, and positive transformation theorems, unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a polynomial-time monotone wrapper with a constant welfare factor exist in this anonymous cohort model?
  • Which regularity conditions, if any, make the cohort monotonicity condition a limit of finite-population monotonicity?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is the paper’s impossibility result for preserving a constant fraction of welfare pointwise. It keeps the central mechanism-design question intact: can a black-box wrapper turn any allocation rule into a truthful one while retaining a fixed share of its welfare?

Consider a service market with a very large population split among \(\tau\) public classes—say, classes defined by eligibility and resource requirements. Members of each class are identical in the respects the feasibility constraints use. At a truthful input, each member’s value is \(l\) or \(h\), with \(h/l\) fixed. A type is a public class together with its value; its mass is the fraction of the population in that class. A cohort can contain millions of people, while \(\tau\) remains small. Service to an individual is still all-or-nothing; a mass allocation records what fraction of a cohort receives service.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover: Two-Value Cohort Black-Box Welfare Preservation. An instance has a finite public-type set \(\Theta\), a rational distribution \(\nu\) over \(\Theta\times\{l,h\}\), and a downward-closed feasible region \(\mathcal F\) for aggregate service masses, given by membership oracle. A black-box allocation rule \(\mathcal A\) maps each such population to service rates \(x_{\theta,r}\in[0,1]\). The mass served from public class \(\theta\) is \(y_\theta=\sum_{r\in\{l,h\}}\nu_{\theta,r}x_{\theta,r}\), which must lie in \(\mathcal F\); welfare is
\[ W_{\mathcal A}(\nu)=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{r\in\{l,h\}} r\,\nu_{\theta,r}x_{\theta,r}. \]
The question is whether there is a uniform polynomial-time wrapper \(\mathcal T\), with polynomially many adaptive queries to \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal F\), whose output is feasible, monotone in each participant’s reported value, and satisfies \(W_{\mathcal T(\mathcal A)}(\nu)\ge cW_{\mathcal A}(\nu)\) for every population and every \(\mathcal A,\mathcal F\), for some fixed \(c>0\). Monotonicity means that for a tagged participant, holding the rest of the population fixed, reporting \(h\) cannot lower their service probability compared with reporting \(l\). The runtime is measured in \(\tau\), the binary encoding length of the masses, and the oracle query costs, rather than the headcount.

My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that when \(l<h\) and \(h/l\) is constant, no polynomial-time transformation exists that is simultaneously monotone and constant-fraction welfare-preserving—even if the transformation is randomized and adaptive and may make polynomially many queries to the feasibility set. The continuous question above asks whether that barrier survives when the society is represented by cohort masses, or whether the discrete obstruction depends on distinctions among named agents that disappear in the high-multiplicity regime. The theorem itself does not answer this new question.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem: it retains the black-box transformation, downward-closed feasibility, welfare guarantee, and monotonicity needed for truthfulness. The fractional \(x_{\theta,r}\) is a population description of which members get an indivisible service, rather than a change to divisible outcomes. This mirror covers Theorem 2’s two-value impossibility result only; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s other results.

My tentative prediction is Class C, continuum-specifically hard, in the unrestricted oracle model. Even with few cohorts, an arbitrary black-box rule could depend on finely encoded rational masses in ways a polynomial number of queries cannot uncover. That is a reason to investigate a new lower bound, not a consequence of Theorem 2. The question also leads to useful boundaries: does the answer change for an explicitly represented polytope \(\mathcal F\), for rules with regular dependence on masses, or when \(h/l\) grows with a population-resolution parameter?

The soft spot is the restriction to anonymous, cohort-based environments. The paper’s lower bound uses arbitrary feasibility sets and constructions over individually labeled agents; collapsing agents into a few public classes may erase the very combinatorics that makes Theorem 2 work. An opposing analyst could reasonably argue that the proposed oracle model needs more structure before it is a convincing market model. I still think the mirror is worth asking: the answer would show whether the paper’s black-box impossibility is fundamentally about population size or about identity-level structure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a genuine computational result, so I can’t dismiss the anchor on the ground that the paper has no result to mirror. The problem is that its obstruction is built around named coordinates. The proof hides a special allocation among subsets of agents, permutes their positions, and uses monotonicity along one-agent-at-a-time changes to force the transformation to find it. The feasibility set can also distinguish those labeled subsets. In that general model, people with different feasibility roles are different types; keeping all such roles can make the support size grow with \(n\), eliminating the proposed high-multiplicity regime.

The proponent’s best repair is a coherent new model: anonymous cohorts, per-capita feasibility that scales with population, and service rates for each cohort and reported value. But its incentive condition needs care. In a continuum, a tagged person has zero mass, so changing their report leaves \(\nu\) unchanged. One can impose \(x_{\theta,h}(\nu)\ge x_{\theta,l}(\nu)\) on the conditional service rates, but that defines an atomless incentive condition. It does not follow as a limit of the paper’s finite-population comparisons without assumptions on how rates behave as masses change. Finite comparisons move mass \(1/N\) between value classes; arbitrary black-box rules need not have a well-behaved limit.

That leaves an awkward choice. Retain the paper’s unrestricted, identity-sensitive algorithms and feasibility sets, and the number of types may scale with the population. Restrict to anonymous cohort rules and mass-level feasibility, and the question may be worthwhile, but it is a different black-box transformation problem whose relation to Theorem 2 is unclear. Arbitrary dependence on binary-encoded masses could itself make that problem hard, but the paper gives no reason to treat that as an economically meaningful continuation of its lower bound.

So the negative case is not airtight: large anonymous service populations are plausible, and the repaired question could merit study on its own. But Theorem 2 does not establish that its central impossibility survives continuization. I would resist treating this anchor as a strong mirror of the paper’s result.
