The case FOR (proponent)

I can sketch a plausible population version of the paper’s question, but I cannot make it a qualifying ChoCo mirror: the paper contains no named computational result to anchor it.

The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(n\ge 3\), there is no random assignment rule that agrees with probabilistic serial (PS) on every profile of strict preferences and is both ex post efficient and weakly SD-strategyproof on the full domain that allows indifferences. The impossibility does not require anonymity, neutrality, or equal treatment of equals. That is a mechanism-existence impossibility, not a classification of a computational problem as hard, tractable, FPT, or otherwise. Lemma 2, also proved here, says that for \(n=3\) with indifferences, the ex post efficient assignments are exactly the SD-efficient assignments. It is a structural equivalence, not a computational result. Lemma 1 gives a size-\(k\le n\) trading-cycle characterization with distinct agents and objects; it too is structural.

The most sympathetic extension would use a large, balanced allocation market. A type would be a complete weak ranking over a fixed set of \(M\) object categories; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of the population with type \(t\). With total population mass one, give each object category supply \(1/M\), preserving the paper’s equal agent–object ratio. Let \(x_{t,o}\) be the per-capita probability that type \(t\) receives category \(o\), with \(\sum_o x_{t,o}=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{t,o}=1/M\). A natural question is whether a rule on these societies can agree with fluid PS on strict-ranking societies while remaining ex post efficient and weakly SD-strategyproof.

That is a plausible population-level mechanism-design question, but it is unanchored here and not a computational problem. It also changes the incentive test: an individual in a nonatomic population has zero mass, so their report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. Requiring that they not benefit from switching to another type’s allocation is different from the paper’s finite-agent deviation, which changes the reported profile. Requiring deviations by positive-mass groups gives a different model again. The authors might recognize this as a natural extension of their incompatibility question, but the paper does not establish which continuum incentive notion is the right analogue, or what computational task follows from it.

So I would treat this as a possible new mechanism-design lead, not as a mirror of a result in the paper. The weakest point is the central one: the paper proves an axiomatic impossibility, while ChoCo’s mirror needs a computational question about a continuous population. Its fractional random assignments do not fill that gap; they continuize outcomes, not the society.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is the proponent’s central diagnosis: Theorem 1 is an axiomatic mechanism-existence result, not a computational result. It quantifies over rules defined across preference profiles; it gives no algorithmic task with a society as input and a solution as output. A continuum version asking whether a rule extends PS while meeting the two axioms would preserve the paper’s subject, but it would still be a mechanism-design impossibility question. Making the population continuous does not turn it into a complexity question.

The balanced market the proponent sketches is plausible. A type can be a complete weak ranking, and \(\mu_t\) can be its population share. I would not reject that mirror on multiplicity grounds, or because assignments are fractional. The difficulty is that the axioms are global: weak strategyproofness compares a rule’s outcomes across reports. Given just one distribution \(\mu\), asking whether an assignment is ex post efficient does not retain that incentive condition; specifying the rule and its behavior across reports brings us back to an axiomatic question. Defining a computational task such as finding the smallest mass of agents to manipulate would add a new objective and deviation model that this paper does not study.

The better computational-looking route is to build on Lemma 2: given \(\mu\) and a type-average assignment, decide whether it is ex post efficient or SD-efficient. But that is a new assignment-certification problem, not a computational mirror of the lemma’s claim that the two notions coincide for exactly three agents. Three types with positive mass are not three agents: each type can contain a continuum of people, and its average allocation can split that mass across objects. The finite-agent structure behind the lemma does not carry over just by renaming agents as types. Lemma 1 offers a related cycle-detection angle, but it is also a structural characterization, not a complexity result in the paper.

I can therefore make a strong case that this paper is a poor ChoCo candidate: every direct mirror remains axiomatic, while the plausible computational variants require adding a new problem. I cannot honestly defend the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous follow-up exists. An atomless allocation mechanism may be worth studying in its own right; the negative case is weakest precisely there, because that would be a separate mechanism-design project rather than the computational mirror the programme seeks.
