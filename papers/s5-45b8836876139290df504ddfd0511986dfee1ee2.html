The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I can defend is that this paper has a natural continuous-population problem in it, but it has no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your rules.

The closest named results are Lemma 1 and Lemma 2, both proved here. Lemma 1 says that, when every major \(d\) has \(\min_P \pi_d(P)>4\lambda_d\), any Bayesian Nash equilibrium fills every seat at both stages: \(\pi_d(P)P_1^d=4\lambda_d\) and \(\pi_d(P)P^d=\lambda_d\). Lemma 2 says, under that same condition, if \(P\) and \(\widetilde P\) have positive coordinates and \(z(P)=z(\widetilde P)\), where \(z_d(P)=\pi_d(P)P^d\), then \(P=\widetilde P\). Together these support uniqueness in the paper’s restricted homogeneous setting. They are equilibrium results, not results that an underlying computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. The paper gives no numbered algorithmic or complexity result to anchor a mirror on.

The closest candidate mirror would be an equilibrium allocation problem for large admissions cohorts. A type would specify an applicant’s relevant characteristics, preferences, and grade-shock distribution. Mass would be the fraction of applicants of each type. In a regime with many applicants but a moderate number \(\tau\) of such complete types, capacities would be specified as per-applicant seat shares, including the first-stage quotas that scale with final seats. The decision variable would be each type’s mass choosing a major (or an ordered list of majors). A solution would give those choices, the exam cutoffs, and the resulting success probabilities, all mutually consistent: each type chooses to maximize expected utility, while the cutoffs admit the required mass at each stage. This mirrors the paper’s endogenous choices, thresholds, and equilibrium success probabilities; scaling seats with the population is a natural co-modification of the model.

The scenario is plausible: large recurring admissions cohorts can contain far more applicants than distinct applicant profiles. The paper itself discusses national-scale competition and models applicants’ strategic choices before exams. Its own sample, however, is a single university’s 2004 cohort, narrowed to 2,867 applicants for two medical schools. The paper does not establish that this sample has a small number of complete types, or analyze the resulting high-multiplicity computational problem.

I would not assign the candidate a tractability class on this evidence. The paper’s uniqueness result applies only under homogeneity; it does not settle existence, uniqueness, or computation in a heterogeneous continuum. The main weakness is therefore decisive for this task: although the population mirror is recognizable and concrete, there is no eligible named computational result for it to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The positive case has identified a plausible continuous-population admissions model, but Lemmas 1 and 2 do not anchor it as a computational mirror. Lemma 1 characterizes when seats fill at both stages; Lemma 2 proves injectivity, hence uniqueness of success probabilities, in a restricted homogeneous setting. Neither states an algorithmic or complexity result: there is no encoded input, computational task, running-time bound, or hardness claim. The counterfactual section’s iteration does not fill that gap; the paper says “if this algorithm converges,” without establishing convergence generally.

The strongest proposed mirror is to compute a heterogeneous cohort’s equilibrium choices, cutoffs, and success probabilities from a finite list of applicant types with rational masses and per-capita capacities. That is a coherent question. But it requires choosing a type representation, specifying how grade and preference shocks are encoded, and defining what counts as an equilibrium output. The paper supplies no heterogeneous-continuum result for this task: its uniqueness claims are homogeneous, and its empirical model uses individual covariates and simulated shocks. Those choices might be made in a useful new project, but they are additional modelling and computational work, not a continuous counterpart of either lemma.

The most faithful mirror would retain the homogeneous setting and pass to population shares. That preserves the seat-filling and uniqueness statements, but leaves an equilibrium characterization rather than a computational result to mirror. One could ask how to compute such an equilibrium from a specified distribution of shocks; the paper does not pose or answer that problem.

So the negative case is strong under the required result-anchor standard: this paper offers no qualifying computational result. The broader claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror is much weaker. Large admissions cohorts make the high-multiplicity regime plausible, and a finite-type equilibrium-computation problem could be worth studying. I would classify that as a promising extension inspired by the paper, not an anchored mirror of its stated results.
