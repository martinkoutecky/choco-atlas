The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\) and a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) strict rankings with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute candidate points \(x_c\) and type points \(y_t\) in \(\mathbb{Q}^{\tau}\), with polynomial bit length, such that \(c \succ_t c'\) exactly when \(\|y_t-x_c\|_2 < \|y_t-x_{c'}\|_2\), for every \(t \in T\) and \(c,c' \in C\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types \(T\). The decision variables are locations \(x_c\) for candidates and \(y_t\) for supported types; the output must realize each type’s ranking under Euclidean distance. The masses identify the population distribution but do not affect the embedding constraints.

The objection that survived

The construction is invariant to the mass values, so its computational content comes from compressing repeated rankings; this limits its population-level payoff but does not defeat exact embedding of all supported types.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the preference-embedding results in Theorems 7 and 8, with the support-dimension version anchored on Theorem 8; it leaves the Condorcet-dimension bounds in Theorems 3 and 5 and the lower bound in Lemma 6 untouched.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is the paper’s preference-embedding results. It contains no named hardness result, and its main Condorcet-dimension bounds are structural rather than complexity classifications. But Theorem 8 explicitly gives a polynomial-time embedding algorithm, so it is a clean computational anchor.

Theorem 8 (proved here, using the construction of Bogomolnaia and Laslier) says: given \(m\) candidates and \(n\) voters with strict preference lists, one can compute in polynomial time a proximity embedding in dimension \(D=n\), for any \(p\)-norm with \(p>1\). My lead mirror is Continuous Type-Coordinate Embedding. Its input is a candidate set \(C\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) strict rankings of \(C\), and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). For a fixed Euclidean norm, the task is to output candidate points \(x_c\) and one point \(y_t\) for each supported type, in \(\mathbb{Q}^{\tau}\), such that for every \(t\) and every pair \(c,c'\),
\[ c\succ_t c' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \|y_t-x_c\|_2<\|y_t-x_{c'}\|_2. \]
The coordinates must have polynomial bit length; a solution is the point assignment itself.

This has a direct construction. Write \(r_t(c)\) for candidate \(c\)’s position in ranking \(t\), set \(y_t=B e_t\), and set \(x_c=(-r_u(c))_{u\in T}\), with, for example, \(B=\tau m^2+m\). For a type \(t\), the coordinate \(t\) makes the distance difference between any two candidates with different ranks dominate the contribution from the other coordinates. Thus the construction realizes every supported ranking in dimension \(\tau\), in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the mass encoding length. The masses specify the population distribution over these points; they do not need to be expanded into individual voters.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 7, proved here by a candidate-simplex construction. It says that for \(m\) candidates and \(n\) voters with strict preference lists, a dimension-\(m\) proximity embedding can be computed in polynomial time for any \(p\)-norm with \(p\ge0\), as stated in the paper. Its mirror is Continuous Candidate-Coordinate Embedding: on the same distributional input, output points in \(\mathbb{Q}^{m}\) realizing every supported ranking. For the Euclidean case, set \(x_{c_i}=2m e_i\) and \(y_t=(m-r_t(c_1),\ldots,m-r_t(c_m))\). If \(c_i\) ranks above \(c_k\) for type \(t\), the squared-distance difference is \(4m(r_t(c_i)-r_t(c_k))<0\), so the ranking is preserved. This takes polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\). Both mirrors are Class A: they have explicit polynomial-time constructions.

The high-multiplicity regime is a large electorate whose ballots come from relatively few complete rankings: for example, millions of voters represented by a few dozen or hundred ballot types, with \(\mu_t\) recording each type’s share. A ranking is a complete type for this task because the embedding must reproduce exactly the preferences the paper uses. Authors should recognize both questions as their embedding problem with repeated identical inputs grouped together. Existing high-multiplicity election work supports that regime; it is not a novelty collision.

The scope is deliberately limited to Section 5’s embedding results, Theorems 7 and 8. I am not claiming a mirror of the paper’s two-dimensional Condorcet-dimension bounds, or of the lower-bound lemma. A natural further question is whether the support-dimension result can be obtained under the Manhattan norm, which Theorem 8 does not cover. One could also ask for the minimum dimension that realizes a given supported profile.

The weakest point is that the masses do no computational work here: the task depends on which rankings occur, not on their proportions. So this is a narrow, support-sensitive high-multiplicity version of the paper’s embedding problem, rather than a new complexity landscape driven by fractional population mass. That limits the programme’s payoff, but does not make the mirror artificial: the population is represented by its type distribution, and the question being computed is exactly whether those preference types admit the proximity representation the paper studies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against rests on a limitation shared by both anchors: their embedding constraints depend on which rankings occur, not on how much of the population holds each ranking.

For Theorem 7, the dimension-\(m\) construction uses each ranking to assign coordinates directly. Given a distribution \(\mu\), replacing the voter list with its support gives the same construction with one point per supported ranking. The masses do no work: changing them while keeping the support fixed changes neither the embedding nor the question being answered. This is a valid compression of repeated ballots, but it leaves no population computation for continuization to expose.

Theorem 8 offers a stronger version because its dimension can fall from \(n\) to the number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings: use one coordinate for each supported type rather than each voter. But that is still grouping duplicate constraints. Every positive-mass type must be embedded, however small its mass; letting its mass approach zero does not relax the requirement, while setting it to zero removes the type altogether. The mirror therefore turns on support membership, not on the distribution over a continuous population.

A more population-sensitive proposal would ask for the lowest-dimensional embedding that represents at least \(1-\varepsilon\) of the mass, or the greatest mass representable in two dimensions. That could be a worthwhile spatial-fitting problem. But it adds an approximation objective absent from Theorems 7 and 8; the paper supplies no result about that tradeoff to continuize. It is a promising new question, not a stronger mirror of these anchors.

I would not call this a novelty collision, and the large-electorate, few-ballot-types scenario is sensible. The negative case is narrower: the proposed mirrors are exact support-compression results whose answers ignore population shares. That makes their payoff to a programme about computation over distributions limited. It does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; a mass-sensitive embedding question could survive that objection.
