The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) product classes, finitely many additive types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), values \(v_{tj}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), compute an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal ordered mass single-price policy and determine whether the worst-case ratio \(\mathrm{OPT}/B_{\mathrm{SP}}\) can remain \(\Omega(\log m/\log\log m)\) when each type’s mass may split across prices and positions.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic market has type masses \(\mu_t\) and per-capita supplies \(q_j\). A policy divides each type’s mass into ordered blocks \((t,\lambda,p)\), with each block offered every remaining product at price \(p\) per unit; its welfare is maximized over these policies and compared with the unrestricted allocation optimum. Individuals receive integral bundles, while type-level average allocations may be fractional.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s single-price welfare limitation. It leaves aside the communication bounds for other mechanism classes, the remaining approximation results, and the broader equilibrium analysis.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the theorem’s \(\Omega(\log m/\log\log m)\) welfare gap persist when type mass can split across price and order blocks?
  • Can an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal mass single-price policy be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), the support size \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\varepsilon\)?
  • How does the worst-case gap depend on \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: the paper’s single-price welfare limitation has a natural high-multiplicity market mirror. The relevant anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It shows that there are additive-buyer profiles for which the *best possible* single-price mechanism has approximation ratio \(\Omega(\log m/\log\log m)\). Consequently, for every \(C>0\), no single-price interpolation mechanism with \(C\) bits of first-round communication per bidder gets an \(o(\log m/\log\log m)\)-approximation on every additive profile. The result still holds when every item value is an integer between \(1\) and \(m\). I rely on the first claim: it is the mechanism class itself, not a shortage of communication, that creates the welfare barrier.

A plausible regime is a large marketplace with \(m\) product classes and many buyers drawn from a small catalogue of additive value profiles. A type \(t\) is the buyer’s complete vector \(v_t\in\{1,\ldots,m\}^m\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of buyers with that vector. The total population is normalized to mass \(1\), and product supplies \(q_j\) are measured per unit of population. This scaling has a direct replication interpretation: repeat each buyer cohort and each product supply proportionally, then take the population limit. In the theorem’s construction, the number of distinct profiles can be \(n=\Theta(\log m/\log\log m)\), while the number of actual buyers is \(N\gg n\). Thus the society has many agents over relatively few types.

The mirror I would hand to a prover is Mass Single-Price Welfare Optimization. Its input is \(m\) product classes, finitely many additive types \(t\in T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\), integer values \(v_{tj}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), rational supplies \(q_j\), and an accuracy parameter \(\varepsilon>0\). A policy is a finite ordered list of population blocks \((t,\lambda,p)\): block mass \(\lambda\) has type \(t\), faces the same posted price \(p\) per unit of every product, and appears at that point in the sequence. Blocks may split a type’s mass across prices and positions, with their masses summing to \(\mu_t\). For each product \(j\), the policy allocates its supply to the earliest mass that wants it, meaning \(v_{tj}\ge p\); at a supply boundary, mass may be split. Each individual receives an integral bundle, although a type’s average allocation may be fractional.

The task is to return a policy whose welfare is within \(\varepsilon\) of the best such policy’s welfare,
\[ B_{\mathrm{SP}}=\sup \sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_0^1 v_{z(u),j}a_j(u)\,du, \]
where \(z(u)\) and \(a_j(u)\) give the type and allocation at population position \(u\). The unconstrained welfare benchmark is
\[ \mathrm{OPT}=\max \sum_{t,j}v_{tj}x_{tj} \quad\text{such that}\quad 0\le x_{tj}\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{tj}\le q_j. \]
The main follow-up question is whether the theorem’s bad family still forces
\[ \mathrm{OPT}/B_{\mathrm{SP}}=\Omega(\log m/\log\log m) \]
in this population model. This is the theorem’s analogue because both the welfare objective and the single-price choice survive; the mirror does not replace pricing with a different allocation problem.

My prediction is a Class B-style transfer of the approximation obstruction: the construction’s trade-off across item-value buckets appears to survive when buyer counts become masses. The finite proof’s counting step has a plausible mass analogue: price positions across the relevant value intervals must share a fixed total population mass. That is a reason to expect the lower bound, not a proof of it. A separate question is whether \(B_{\mathrm{SP}}\) itself can be computed efficiently; Theorem 3.1 does not establish hardness for that optimization problem. Useful next questions are whether the same gap holds with type mass splittable across prices, how the gap depends on support size \(\tau\), and whether the best policy has a polynomial-size optimizing description.

This mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s single-price limitation, not the paper’s other mechanism classes or its full communication results. The authors should recognize the policy as the population limit of their second-phase mechanism: an ordered stream of buyers, each offered every remaining item at one per-item price.

The weak point is exactly the extra flexibility in the limit. A type cohort can be divided among many prices and order positions, whereas the theorem’s construction has finitely many named buyers. That flexibility could erase the gap. I would make the case only because the bucket argument suggests a way to control it in mass terms; until that extension is proved, the theorem is an anchor for a precise research question, not a result already transferred.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror drops the paper’s central computational question. Theorem 3.1 first shows a welfare gap for the best single-price mechanism; its communication lower bound then says a first phase cannot overcome that gap. Mass Single-Price Welfare Optimization models the first claim, but removes the phase-one communication, strategic behavior, and equilibrium guarantees that connect the paper’s results. It asks a planner to optimize over price policies, which is a different task from studying how agents’ messages select a truthful mechanism.

A stronger mirror would retain the interpolation framework: each unit of population sends a bounded message, the distribution of messages selects a truthful continuation, and the goal is a welfare or price-of-anarchy guarantee. That is conceivable, but the population distribution alone does not settle how identical types can use different messages, or how the mechanism assigns them different prices and positions. Requiring one message per type would restrict the paper’s bidder-specific choices; allowing a type’s mass to split across messages would preserve those choices but define a different, richer model. Those choices affect what the continuous result would say, so the authors’ recognition of it as their problem is less clear than for the proposed policy optimization.

The bucket argument also does not transfer automatically. In the finite proof, each named bidder has one price, and the price intervals share a bound on the number of bidder-bucket pairs. In a mass model, a cohort can be divided across price intervals. The analogous mass bound might still support a lower bound, but establishing that would be new work. It is a reason not to present the proponent’s predicted transfer as established; it is not, by itself, a reason the question is unworthy.

I cannot make a strong universal negative from this paper. Large cohorts with repeated additive value profiles are plausible, welfare is an aggregate objective, and the continuous limit need not degenerate. The best case against is therefore a scope objection: the proposed mirror may study a neighboring market-optimization problem rather than the paper’s communication-and-incentives question. That could make this anchor a weaker fit for ChoCo, but it does not defeat the narrower mass analogue, and I see no honest basis here for claiming that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
