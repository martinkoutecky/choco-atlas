The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is a narrow mirror of Probabilistic Serial’s equilibrium-welfare problem. One qualification up front: the paper has named algorithmic performance theorems, but no theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar. The closest anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: with \(n\) agents and \(n\) items, unit-sum valuations, and pure Nash equilibrium play, Probabilistic Serial has pure Price of Anarchy \(O(\sqrt n)\). In full, the worst ratio—over valuation profiles—of the maximum welfare attainable by a matching to the welfare at the least-welfare pure equilibrium is \(O(\sqrt n)\).

A plausible population regime is a large placement or appointment market: \(N\) people compete for \(N\) distinct slots, and many people share the same complete utility vector over those slots. The type is that full vector; its mass is the fraction of the population with that vector. As \(N\) grows, the support size \(\tau\) can remain much smaller than \(N\). Each slot’s supply scales with the population, so with total mass normalized to \(1\), slot \(j\) has rational supply \(q_j\), where \(\sum_j q_j=1\). This makes population mass and slot supply co-scale. Probabilistic Serial already produces lotteries in the finite model; the mirror’s new continuous object is the population distribution, not the lottery.

Here is a concrete problem for a prover. Call it Continuous-PS Worst-Equilibrium Welfare. An instance consists of \(m\) slots with rational supplies \(q_j\); a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) true utility types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); unit-sum rational utility vectors \(u_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^m\); and a rational threshold \(B\). Reports are weak rankings of the slots. Extend PS by letting the mass reporting each ranking consume its most preferred available slot at a rate proportional to that mass; a member of that report group receives its per-capita consumption shares. A continuum reporting equilibrium may split a true type across reports: every report used by positive mass must maximize that type’s expected utility against the aggregate report distribution, where a single agent’s deviation has zero effect on that distribution.

The question is whether there is such an equilibrium whose welfare is at most \(B\), and a yes-solution is the equilibrium’s type-by-report masses. Welfare is \(\sum_{t,r,j}x_{tr}p_j(r;\rho)u_{tj}\), where \(x_{tr}\) is the mass of type \(t\) reporting \(r\), \(\rho_r=\sum_t x_{tr}\), and \(p_j(r;\rho)\) is the per-capita PS assignment to slot \(j\). The comparison optimum is the transportation problem
\[ \max_y \sum_{t,j}y_{tj}u_{tj} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_j y_{tj}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t y_{tj}=q_j,\qquad y_{tj}\ge0. \]
The corresponding optimization problem is to find the least-welfare equilibrium and its ratio to this optimum. This is recognizably the paper’s underlying question—how poor equilibrium welfare can be under PS—while making population mass, type support, and slot supply explicit.

My low-confidence prediction is Class C for exact worst-equilibrium computation when \(m\) is part of the input: the equilibrium condition couples best responses to a PS outcome whose event order changes with the report masses. Fixed \(m\) may be tractable. Theorem 2 motivates asking whether every equilibrium in this continuous model still has welfare within \(O(\sqrt m)\) of the optimum, but it does not prove that bound or classify the new computation problem. Natural follow-ups are whether equilibria always exist, whether the bound is tight, and how the continuous answer relates to finite PS instances as \(N\) grows.

The soft spot is substantial: in the continuum, an individual deviation does not change the aggregate PS process, whereas in the paper each finite agent’s deviation can change it. Thus the equilibrium concept changes in the limit, and Theorem 2 does not automatically carry over. That is also why this is an honest but limited ChoCo case: it offers a plausible computational question about a continuously distributed population, while the paper’s anchor is a welfare guarantee rather than a complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper does not give ChoCo a computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 bounds Probabilistic Serial’s Price of Anarchy; Theorems 6 and 7 extend that welfare guarantee to other equilibrium concepts. None classifies the complexity of an input problem or gives an algorithm whose continuous counterpart can be compared with the finite one. That leaves a gap between the paper’s result and the proposed decision problem.

The proposed market is a sensible high-multiplicity setting: repeated course seats or appointment slots, with types given by complete utility vectors and supplies scaled with population. I would not reject it merely because it changes the capacities. But it changes the strategic limit in a consequential way. In the nonatomic version, one person’s report does not change aggregate consumption or item-exhaustion times. Given those fixed times, reporting items in true utility order picks the most valuable available item at every moment. Truthful reporting is therefore weakly dominant. With strict values, any equilibrium report that changes an agent’s allocation over a positive interval would be worse than truthful reporting. The finite game’s strategic effects largely disappear; ties can still permit different equilibria.

That does not mean the welfare ratio becomes uninteresting. Truthful PS could still perform poorly relative to the cardinally optimal transportation allocation, because PS uses rankings while the benchmark uses cardinal values. But studying that ratio would be a welfare analysis of a nonatomic fractional assignment rule, not a computational-complexity result of the kind the programme is meant to chart. Conversely, the proposed question—whether a particular finite-support society has a low-welfare equilibrium—is a legitimate new problem, but it is not the universal worst-case guarantee established by Theorem 2. Turning the theorem into a computational task would require a separate formulation for optimizing over societies and equilibria; that formulation would be new work, not a computational mirror supplied by this paper.

So the best negative case is a mismatch of research target, not a claim that no plausible population regime exists. A continuous PS welfare study could be worthwhile on its own, and the supplied text does not rule out a useful result. The case against its value *for this programme* is substantial but not airtight.
