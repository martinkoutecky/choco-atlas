The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 15

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 15 — survived: The opponent engaged this anchor and accepted that the continuous type–bundle model is a legitimate high-multiplicity question.

The continuous mirror question

Given goods with per-capita supplies \(q_j\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and each type's cap and explicit nonincreasing marginal utilities, let \(z\) range over integral bundles and let \(U_t(z)=\min(c_t,\sum_j\sum_{\ell=1}^{z_j}u_{t,j,\ell})\). Choose masses \(x_{t,z}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_j\le q_j\); can one compute an allocation whose \(\exp(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}\log U_t(z))\) is within factor \(e^{1/e}+\varepsilon\) of the maximum, in time polynomial in the encoded instance and the support rather than an expanded population?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population with rational mass \(\mu_t\) for each standardized type \(t\), per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and integral bundles for every individual. The decision variables \(x_{t,z}\) give the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(z\); maximize mass-weighted log capped utility subject to type-mass and supply constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns the approximate NSW computation and its running-time question anchored by Theorem 15; it does not mirror the paper's separate envy, certification, large-market, or CG–BMV-bound results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the configuration problem be approximated in time polynomial in the support size and encoded input, without expanding the population?
  • Can the type-level bundle pricing problem be solved efficiently, or does its item-selection structure make the continuous problem hard?
  • How does the continuous optimum relate to finite replicated populations and to rounding a mass allocation back to a finite allocation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead is ChoCo-NSW\(_\infty\): approximate Nash social welfare for a nonatomic population whose individuals still receive indivisible bundles.

A plausible regime is a large public distribution programme with millions of recipients but relatively few standardized need profiles. Everyone in a profile has the same diminishing marginal values for each good and the same utility cap. The programme’s supplies scale with the eligible population, so \(q_j\) denotes the number of copies of good \(j\) available per unit of population. This is a claim about that regime, not about fair division generally: a small committee with individually negotiated preferences would not fit. The paper’s own discussion of large markets, where each good is small relative to an agent’s total value, supports the plausibility of the regime.

My anchor is Theorem 15, proved in the paper. With \(n\) agents, \(M=\sum_j k_j\) total items, \(U\) the ratio of the largest to smallest positive utility, and \(r=1+\varepsilon\), it bounds the number of iterations of the paper’s algorithm by \(n^3M^2\log_r(MU)\). This is the runtime result for the algorithm computing an approximate NSW allocation; the theorem itself asserts the iteration bound, not the approximation ratio. [Theorem 15 in the published paper](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol122-fsttcs2018/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2018.25/LIPIcs.FSTTCS.2018.25.pdf). The paper’s introduction also mentions NP-completeness and APX-hardness, but cites those results from other work; they are not numbered results proved here, so I do not use them as anchors.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. An instance gives \(m\) goods with per-capita supplies \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\); a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) recipient types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); and, for each type \(t\), a cap \(c_t\) and nonincreasing marginal utilities \(u_{t,j,\ell}\). Let \(K_j\) be the largest relevant number of copies of good \(j\) in an individual bundle, with marginal schedules given explicitly up to that bound. For an integral bundle \(z\in\prod_j\{0,\ldots,K_j\}\), define
\[ U_t(z)=\min\!\left(c_t,\sum_j\sum_{\ell=1}^{z_j}u_{t,j,\ell}\right). \]
The decision variable \(x_{t,z}\ge 0\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(z\). Require
\[ \sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\quad\text{for each }t, \qquad \sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_j\le q_j\quad\text{for each }j. \]
Maximize
\[ \operatorname{NSW}_\infty(x) =\exp\!\left(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}\log U_t(z)\right), \]
with value zero if positive mass receives zero utility. A solution is a feasible, preferably sparse list of the \(x_{t,z}\) values whose NSW is within factor \(e^{1/e}+\varepsilon\) of optimum. The individual bundles remain integral; only the share of a type receiving each bundle is fractional. If finite agents of type \(t\) number \(n_t\) among \(n\), then \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and supplies \(k_j\) correspond to \(q_j=k_j/n\). This makes the model a genuine high-multiplicity relaxation, rather than a divisible-goods version of each person’s problem.

I would provisionally expect Class A for this approximation question. With a fixed number of goods and type profiles, the formulation is a configuration LP: its variables are type–bundle pairs, and its objective is linear in those masses after taking logs. An extreme point can use only a small number of bundle configurations. The paper’s price-based algorithm and its explicit dependence on \(n\) make a natural starting point for asking whether the computation can instead run on masses and type support, in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,K,L,1/\varepsilon\), without expanding a huge population into named agents.

The weak point is the pricing step. A column-generation proof needs an efficient way to find, for each type, a bundle balancing \(\log U_t(z)\) against the goods’ prices. That subproblem may retain hard item-selection structure, and Theorem 15’s \(n^3\) iteration bound does not itself compress repeated agents. If pricing proves hard, the mirror may be continuum-specifically hard despite the sensible model. That is exactly the boundary question the programme should ask. Further questions include whether the continuous optimum is the limit of finite replicated populations, what rounding loss restores a finite allocation, and whether the paper’s envy-up-to-one-copy guarantee has a useful type-distribution analogue. My case covers the NSW computation behind Theorem 15; it does not claim a continuous mirror for every fairness or certification result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The one anchor is Theorem 15, and it is a genuine computational result: the paper bounds the iterations of its approximate NSW algorithm by \(n^3M^2\log_r(MU)\). The strongest objection is that this bound is tied to the paper’s individual-agent algorithm. If \(n\) and the supply \(M\) grow with the population, it gives no algorithm polynomial in the support size \(\tau\) and the bit length of the type masses. Running it on an expanded population could take time on the order of the fifth power of population size.

That weakens the connection between Theorem 15 and the proposed mirror, but it does not defeat the better version of the question. The type–bundle formulation is a legitimate high-multiplicity relaxation: each person still gets an integral bundle, while mass records how many people of a type get each bundle. A standardized public programme is a plausible setting, and scaling supplies per capita is an admissible co-modification. The mass-weighted log objective is also the natural continuous counterpart of finite NSW. Nothing essential vanishes in that model.

Nor can the divisible-goods Fisher-market results cited in the paper dispose of it: those make goods divisible, while this model keeps individual bundles integral and makes the population continuous. The configuration LP is easy to state, but its pricing problem may be hard. That uncertainty is a reason to investigate the mirror, not evidence that it is unworthy.

So the negative case is weak. Theorem 15 does not itself promise compression by type, and the proponent’s configuration LP is a new algorithmic question rather than a direct translation of the theorem’s iteration bound. But I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in the standardized-profile regime. The single anchor survives the stronger formulation.
