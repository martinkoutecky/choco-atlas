The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow computational one: approximate the long-run social cost of anchored deliberation. It matches what the paper studies, and its convergence theorem gives an efficient randomized algorithm for that task.

Take a large online community deliberating over a one-dimensional policy. Suppose there are \(N\) members but only \(\tau\) distinct ideal points, with \(N\gg\tau\). A type is an ideal point \(u_i\); its mass \(p_i\) is the fraction of the community with that ideal point. Assume members share the paper’s anchoring strength \(\lambda\) and absolute-distance cost. With \(G=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i\delta_{u_i}\), a deliberation round draws \(U,V\) independently from \(G\) and updates the current outcome \(x\) to \(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)\operatorname{med}(x,U,V)\). The current outcome is the state; the quantity to estimate is its expected social cost at stationarity.

This gives a precise problem, Anchored Stationary Social-Cost Estimation. The input is rational support points \(u_i\), rational masses \(p_i\), rational \(\lambda\in[0,1)\), additive error \(\varepsilon>0\), and failure probability \(\delta>0\). Output a rational estimate \(\widehat J\) such that, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), it is within \(\varepsilon\) of \(\mathbb E_{X\sim\pi_{\lambda,G}}[\mathsf{SC}(X)]\). Here \(\mathsf{SC}(x)=\sum_i p_i|x-u_i|\), and \(\pi_{\lambda,G}\) is the stationary outcome distribution.

The anchor is Theorem 2.1, proved in this paper. It says that for every population distribution \(G\) and \(\lambda<1\), the chain has a unique stationary distribution and its \(1\)-Wasserstein mixing time is at most \(\left\lceil\log(1/\eta)/\log(2/(1+\lambda))\right\rceil\) at accuracy \(\eta\). For \(0<\lambda<1\) and \(0<\eta<1/2\), it also gives the lower bound \(\left\lceil\log(1/(2\eta))/\log(1/\lambda)\right\rceil\). The paper proves this using a coupling argument; it is not a cited complexity classification.

For rational finite-support \(G\), the theorem yields an additive approximation algorithm. Run independent chains from a fixed starting outcome long enough to make their laws \(\varepsilon/2\)-close to stationarity in \(W_1\), then average their social costs. Since \(\mathsf{SC}\) is \(1\)-Lipschitz and bounded on \([0,1]\), mixing controls the bias and sampling controls the estimation error. The resulting running time is polynomial in the support and input bit lengths, \(1/\varepsilon\), \(1/(1-\lambda)\), and \(\log(1/\delta)\). This is a tractable, Class A computational question under that encoding. Related questions include estimating stationary distortion when the optimal social cost is bounded away from zero, and extending the guarantee to heterogeneous anchoring strengths.

The authors would recognize this as computing a quantity central to their paper: the quality of the mechanism’s stationary outcomes. The scope is only the convergence and stationary-cost analysis, not the paper’s full set of results.

The major weakness is also decisive for the programme’s novelty test: the paper already defines society by a distribution \(G\), draws deliberating agents independently from it, and proves results for arbitrary \(G\). Finite support makes its existing continuous model suitable for computation; it does not turn a previously discrete population into a continuous one. And although Theorem 2.1 gives an algorithmic consequence, the paper contains no named result classifying an encoded problem as in P, NP-hard, or similar. So this is a plausible computational follow-up to the paper, but a weak case for a *new continuous mirror*: the population is already continuous in the paper itself.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2.1 is the strongest anchor, but it does not yield a continuous *mirror*: the paper already takes the population to be a distribution \(G\), draws each deliberating pair from \(G\), and defines social cost by expectation over \(G\). For finite rational support, \(G=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i\delta_{u_i}\) is exactly the proposed input. The transition and objective already depend on the population through that distribution; there is no discrete electorate left to continuize. In ChoCo’s terms, this is a novelty collision. A finite population with repeated bliss points is represented by its empirical distribution in the paper’s model already.

The proposed estimation task is well-posed, and Theorem 2.1 does give it an algorithm: run independent chains until their laws are within \(\varepsilon/2\) in \(W_1\), then average the bounded, \(1\)-Lipschitz social costs. But that is a computational consequence of an existing continuous-population model, not a complexity question created by making the population continuous. Its straightforward runtime scales as \(O((1-\lambda)^{-1}\log(1/\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta))\) transition steps. In particular, the theorem alone does not give polynomial time in the binary input length when \(\lambda\) can be exponentially close to \(1\). One could study sharper algorithms or exact stationary costs, but those would be computational follow-ups to the paper’s existing model.

The strongest escape is to start from a finite-community version and study finite-size corrections, or to let agents have different anchoring strengths and make types pairs \((u,\lambda)\). The first approaches the paper’s with-replacement model as population size grows; the second adds heterogeneity that the paper identifies as future work. Either could be worthwhile, but neither supplies a continuous mirror of a named result here: the first approximates a model already stated over \(G\), and the second changes the behavioral model as well as the population representation.

So the negative case is strong against calling this a new continuization programme instance: the paper already works in the programme’s population-distribution regime, and its main theorem is about Markov-chain convergence rather than computational complexity. It is weaker as a claim that no valuable computational project exists; stationary-cost algorithms could be useful. The point is that their value would come from computationalizing an already continuous model, not from continuizing this paper.
