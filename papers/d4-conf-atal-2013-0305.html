The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-based case for a continuous mirror from this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result such as hardness or tractability. Its numbered equations define the reputation model, and its findings are experimental comparisons; neither supplies an eligible anchor.

A continuous population of witnesses might be a plausible setting for studying the model, but any complexity question built from it would be a new question, not a mirror anchored in a named result of this paper. I won’t present one as though the paper established such a result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the paper’s main weakness: it states no formal complexity theorem. But that does not establish that there is no worthwhile continuous mirror. Sections 4.2 and 4.3 report computational findings about robustness to attacks: in the single-profile experiments, every tested attack kept the proposed model’s \(D_{\max}\) below \(0.4\), and 13,065 of 13,084 profiles gave \(D_{\mathrm{mean}}<0.1\); the multiple-profile experiments compare how those errors change as attacks accumulate. These are empirical results, not complexity classifications, so a mirror should not be presented as extending a theorem.

A direct population version would treat witnesses as a distribution over the features the model actually uses. For a fixed truster and trustee, a type can specify the rating, its age, its similarity to the truster, and the witness’s rating count—the inputs that determine the weight in Equation (5). This is a plausible high-volume setting: a platform may have many witnesses with the same effective profile, and the paper itself studies Sybil accounts and aggregate reputation errors. The population is what becomes continuous; the rating scale, time windows, and reputation formula can remain as they are.

That gives a computational robustness question tied to the paper’s results: how much attacker-controlled mass is needed to move a trustee’s reputation past a threshold, or to make \(D_{\max}\) exceed a specified error? For a fixed truster, let type \(t\) have weight \(w_t\), rating \(r_t\), and mass \(\mu_t\). After adding attacker mass \(y_t\), the score is
\[ R(y)=\frac{\sum_t(\mu_t+y_t)w_t r_t}{\sum_t(\mu_t+y_t)w_t}. \]
For a threshold \(q\), the condition \(R(y)\ge q\) becomes the linear constraint
\[ \sum_t(\mu_t+y_t)w_t(r_t-q)\ge 0, \]
assuming a positive denominator. With explicit attack types, minimum-cost manipulation is therefore an LP. If attack profiles are given compactly rather than listed, the number of possible types can be enormous, and the central algorithmic question becomes whether the best profile can be found efficiently—the kind of pricing question the programme is designed to study. Extending this to the paper’s worst-case error metric would preserve its focus on resistance to strategic attacks.

So the proposed mirror does not recover a formal result the authors proved; it turns their central experimental question—how much do attacks distort reputation?—into a worst-case computational problem over witness mass. That is the honest qualification, and also the limit of this countercase: its anchor is the paper’s empirical robustness work, not a theorem about complexity.
