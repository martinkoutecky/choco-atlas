The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Proposition 2, proved here, says that with at least three agents, a shared continuous and strictly increasing instantaneous utility \(u\), and majority voting, the strict social preference is intransitive whenever fewer than half the agents share any one discount factor. Theorem 3, also proved here, establishes cycles for a broader class of locally non-dictatorial voting rules, within arbitrarily small neighborhoods of certain consumption streams. Both are impossibility results about collective preferences; neither classifies an algorithmic problem by complexity. The paper’s other named results are likewise about preference aggregation, not computational complexity.

The strongest positive case I can make is therefore a candidate mirror, not one anchored to a qualifying result. Take a large electorate choosing a common public consumption stream, with agents grouped into a small number \(\tau\) of shared discount-factor types. Let type \(t\) have mass \(\mu_t\), with rational masses summing to one. For a shared \(u(c)=c\), and fixed \(x,\gamma>0\), ask for three streams in \(C(x,\gamma)\) such that each stream defeats the next under mass majority: more than half the population prefers the first to the second, the second to the third, and the third to the first. A solution is the three streams themselves; there is no optimization objective. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime—many citizens, few shared preference types—and it keeps the paper’s central question about voting over common consumption streams.

Proposition 2 supports that mirror’s plausibility, but does not meet the required anchor rule: it proves existence of cycles for the finite electorate, not a computational classification or an algorithm with a stated running time. For rational finite-support inputs, its proof’s linear-algebra construction suggests that finding a cycle may be tractable; that is an extrapolation, not a result claimed by the authors. The mirror covers the paper’s majority-voting intransitivity result only. Its weakest point is precisely this gap between an axiomatic existence theorem and a computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 and Theorems 2–4 characterize preference aggregation; Proposition 2 establishes intransitivity under stated conditions. None poses an algorithmic problem or classifies its complexity. They have no computational input model, objective, or running-time parameters. Continuizing the electorate may yield a sensible economic theorem, but that alone does not make it the computational complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed majority-cycle search is a new computational task built around Proposition 2, not a continuous version of a computational result in the paper. Its particular combination of linear utility and the restricted set \(C(x,\gamma)\) also does not match the paper’s cycle results: Proposition 2 concerns a broader choice domain, while Theorem 3’s local result relies on strict concavity. Recasting the proposal to use a concave utility and a finite-support distribution of discount-factor types would fit Theorem 3 more closely, and the population regime is plausible. But it still leaves the paper’s axiomatic existence claim behind: a computational study would have to choose how utility functions, alternatives, and voting rules are represented and what problem an algorithm is to solve. Those choices could define a worthwhile new project; the paper does not supply a canonical one.

I would not claim the universal negative is airtight. Large electorates with repeated preference types are plausible, and an algorithmic study of cycle construction could be interesting. The negative case is strongest on the narrower point that such a study would be an adjacent research direction, not a computational mirror anchored in this paper’s results.
