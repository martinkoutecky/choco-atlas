The continuous mirror question

Input a finite candidate set \(M\), a finite set of location types \(T\), a rational metric \(d\) on \(T\cup M\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), a target \(k\), and a proposed selection \(X\subseteq M\) with \(|X|=k\). For \(S\subseteq T\), let \(\mu(S)=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t\), \(A_r(t)=\{c\in M:d(t,c)\le r\}\), and \(A_r(S)=\bigcup_{t\in S}A_r(t)\). Does every \(\ell\in[k]\), radius \(r\), and type coalition \(S\) with \(\mu(S)\ge \ell/k\) satisfy \(|\bigcap_{t\in S}A_r(t)|\ge\ell\ \Longrightarrow\ |X\cap A_r(S)|\ge\ell\)?

The model it lives in

An audit of a fixed center selection for a society represented by rational mass over exact-location types. A coalition is a subset of types whose total mass meets the proportional quota; its support determines the same cohesiveness and coverage tests as in mPJR.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the verification hardness in Theorem 6 and, as a related fixed-level question, Proposition 11. It leaves the paper’s mPJR+ and DC-mPJR+ verification algorithms, approximation guarantee, and empirical study outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of the mass audit when parameterized by the number of types \(\tau\), or on tree and Euclidean metrics?
  • Which additional structural restrictions on type locations or masses make the audit tractable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a continuous mass audit of mPJR, anchored by Theorem 6. The paper proves Theorem 6: “Checking whether a given center selection satisfies mPJR is coNP-complete.” Its proof uses the approval-to-metric embedding in Lemma 5 and the coNP-completeness of approval-based PJR verification from Aziz et al. (2018). This is the paper’s central hardness result, and the mirror keeps its underlying question: does a proposed set of centers give sufficiently large, cohesive groups enough representation?

For the mirror, let \(T\) be a finite set of agent-location types, \(M\) a finite set of candidate centers, and \(d\) a rational metric on \(T\cup M\). The input also gives a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), a target number \(k\), and a proposed selection \(X\subseteq M\) with \(|X|=k\). A type records the location—and thus the distances to every candidate—that matters to the paper’s fairness checks. A coalition is any submass \(\nu\) with \(0\leq\nu_t\leq\mu_t\); its mass is \(\sum_t\nu_t\), and its support is \(S_\nu=\{t:\nu_t>0\}\). For a radius \(r\), write \(A_r(t)=\{c\in M:d(t,c)\leq r\}\), and \(A_r(S_\nu)=\bigcup_{t\in S_\nu}A_r(t)\).

The Continuous Mass mPJR Audit asks whether, for every \(\ell\in[k]\), radius \(r\), and coalition \(\nu\) of mass at least \(\ell/k\), the following implication holds: if \(\left|\bigcap_{t\in S_\nu}A_r(t)\right|\geq\ell\), then \(\left|X\cap A_r(S_\nu)\right|\geq\ell\). The output is yes or no; a no-instance has a violating level, radius, and coalition support. This replaces the paper’s quota \(q=n/k\) with its population share \(1/k\), while leaving the representation condition and discrete choice of centers intact.

This is not a weakened proxy for mPJR. The fairness conditions depend on a coalition’s type support and total mass. If a fractional coalition on support \(S\) reaches the quota, then the entire mass of the types in \(S\) reaches it too, with exactly the same approval geometry. So a witness can always be taken to include whole types. In a discrete instance, set \(\mu_t\) to the fraction of agents of type \(t\): discrete coalitions and mass coalitions then give the same violations. Conversely, any rational \(\mu\) can be realized by scaling its denominators to obtain an integer population. This is a direct high-multiplicity version of the paper’s audit problem, not continuity in the center or outcome space.

The intended regime is a large population with repeated exact locations or distance profiles: for example, a city’s residents treated as demand at a moderate number of sites, with a finite set of eligible service-center locations. There may be millions of residents but far fewer than a million types, and the natural entitlement is a fraction of the population. The authors should recognize this as their problem: their criterion already treats agents through distances, coalition sizes, and a quota proportional to \(n\). Replacing counts by masses preserves those ingredients.

I expect this mirror to remain coNP-complete, so it is a hardness-transfers case. Theorem 6’s discrete instances become atomic mass instances with \(\mu_t=n_t/n\). A violating discrete coalition gives a mass witness, and any mass witness can be expanded to all types in its support to give a discrete one. With an explicit rational metric input, non-satisfaction also has a polynomial certificate: a level, a type subset, and a radius drawn from the finitely many relevant distances.

A second, narrower anchor is Proposition 11. The paper proves that, given a metric clustering instance, a size-\(k\) center selection \(X\), and a level \(\ell\in[k]\), deciding whether \(X\) satisfies fixed-\(\ell\) mPJR+ is coNP-complete. Here mPJR+ requires that every coalition of at least \(\ell q\) agents around each unselected center \(c\) be covered by at least \(\ell\) selected centers at radius \(r(c,S)=\max_{i\in S}d(i,c)\). The proof is given in this paper: it establishes fixed-\(\ell\) hardness for approval-based PJR+ in Proposition 20 by reduction from Balanced Biclique, then transfers that result through Lemma 5.

Its Continuous Fixed-Level mPJR+ Audit has the same mass instance as above, plus \(\ell\). For each unselected \(c\in M\setminus X\) and each coalition \(\nu\) of mass at least \(\ell/k\), set \(r(c,\nu)=\max_{t\in S_\nu}d(t,c)\). The question is whether \(\left|X\cap A_{r(c,\nu)}(S_\nu)\right|\geq\ell\) always holds. Again the answer is yes or no, with a violating center and type support witnessing no. The same whole-type argument makes this exactly the normalized mass version of fixed-\(\ell\) mPJR+. I expect coNP-completeness here too: the paper’s Balanced Biclique instances embed with equal type masses, so the continuum does not remove their combinatorics. Proposition 11 is a useful second anchor because it shows the barrier persists for a fixed representation level within mPJR+, rather than only for the full mPJR audit.

The main soft spot is that exact shared distance profiles may be common in repeated-site or aggregated-demand data but uncommon in an unprocessed Euclidean point cloud; in the latter, \(\tau\) may be nearly \(n\). The reductions also establish hardness, not an algorithmic payoff from small \(\tau\). That limits how broadly useful this scenario is, but it does not make the mirror artificial: population mass is the natural unit in the stated regime, and the questions remain about auditing the paper’s actual representation guarantees. Further questions include whether these audits become tractable on Euclidean or tree metrics, and what their parameterized complexity is in \(k\), \(\ell\), and support size \(\tau\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is about the regime, not the mathematics. In the paper’s own synthetic setup, \(M=N\) and points are sampled from a continuous distribution. For distinct points, the distance to the candidate set identifies the point itself, so each has its own type and \(\tau=n\). Aggregating nearby points into types could change distance thresholds and therefore change whether a coalition qualifies. Large datasets alone do not create the high multiplicity this mirror needs.

But that objection does not defeat the better version of the mirror. With a fixed set of facility sites and residents aggregated by exact location, a location is a complete type for these fairness checks, and population mass is a natural input. The proposed mass audit is well-defined there: if a fractional coalition has enough mass, taking the full mass of every type in its support preserves its geometry and still meets the quota. Theorem 6 therefore has a direct high-multiplicity counterpart. Its hardness can persist even when each type represents many agents: cloning every type scales the quota with the population while preserving the violating supports.

Proposition 11 survives the same way. Its fixed-level mass audit is also well-defined, and replicating each approval type scales the quota without changing which type supports witness a violation. It is a restricted version of the first audit, so it adds little to the case for a mirror, but I cannot use that overlap to defeat either one.

I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The paper has named computational results, and fixed-site service demand supplies a plausible high-multiplicity regime for auditing them. The negative case is weak: the paper’s particular experiments do not show that regime, and exact aggregation may be uncommon in generic point clouds, but neither point rules out the stronger, sensible mirror.
