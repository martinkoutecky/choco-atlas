The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), finite item species \(J\), per-capita supplies \(0\le q_j\le1\), and additive values \(v_{tj}\ge0\), choose masses \(y_{t,S}\ge0\) of type-\(t\) agents receiving integral bundles \(S\subseteq J\), satisfying \(\sum_S y_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}y_{t,S}=q_j\), to maximize \(\exp\!\left(\sum_{t,S}y_{t,S}\log\!\left(\sum_{j\in S}v_{tj}\right)\right)\), with \(\log 0=-\infty\), or compute an \(\alpha\)-approximation.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity indivisible-item Nash-social-welfare allocation: \(\mu_t\) is divisible population mass, each individual receives an integral bundle, \(y_{t,S}\) is the mass assigned to that bundle, and welfare is optimized under per-capita item supplies. The natural configuration-LP pricing term is \(\max_{S\subseteq J}\{\log(\sum_{j\in S}v_{tj})-\sum_{j\in S}\lambda_j\}\).

The objection that survived

The finite reduction's binary switch gadgets need not survive when positive-mass types split across integral bundles, so no transfer of Theorem 4.3's hardness should be presumed; this limits the expected result but does not invalidate the continuous question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover Theorem 4.3 and Corollary 3.1; they leave the existence and non-convexity results, exact-equilibrium complexity classifications, constant-dimensional algorithms, rounding analysis, and other Nash-welfare bounds untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the configuration LP admit an exact or \(\varepsilon\)-approximation algorithm polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|J|\), the encoding length, and \(1/\varepsilon\)?
  • Is the pricing problem \(\max_{S\subseteq J}\{\log(\sum_{j\in S}v_{tj})-\sum_{j\in S}\lambda_j\}\) polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or fixed-parameter tractable under useful restrictions?
  • Which finite-instance hardness results survive when positive-mass types may split across integral bundles?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible positive case, strongest around two distinct mirrors. The lead is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s indivisible-item problem.

The paper’s best hardness anchor is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper in Section 4.2. It states that, for every constant \(\delta>0\), there is no \((\sqrt{8/7}-\delta)\)-approximation algorithm for maximizing Nash social welfare with additive valuations, unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). The proof uses the cited hardness theorem for bounded-occurrence MAX-E3-LIN-2 and a construction adapted from [12], but Theorem 4.3 itself is established here.

A precise continuous mirror is:

\(\mathrm{NSW}^{\mathrm{bundle}}_\infty\). An instance consists of finitely many voter/agent types \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), finitely many indivisible item species \(J\), per-capita item supplies \(q_j\), and additive valuations \(v_{tj}\ge0\). Type \(t\) is a complete valuation vector \((v_{tj})_{j\in J}\). Let \(y_{t,S}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq J\). A feasible solution satisfies

\[ \sum_{S}y_{t,S}=\mu_t \]

for every \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t,S:j\in S}y_{t,S}=q_j \]

for every item species \(j\). Each individual therefore receives an integral bundle; only the population of identical agents is split among bundles. The objective is

\[ \max_y \exp\left( \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq J} y_{t,S}\log\left(\sum_{j\in S}v_{tj}\right) \right). \]

This is the continuum geometric mean of utilities. A solution is the mass assignment \(y\), together with its achieved welfare, and an \(\alpha\)-approximation must achieve at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/\alpha\).

The natural regime is a large market with millions of agents but a modest catalogue of valuation profiles: for example, consumer or household cohorts with identical valuations for repeated copies of products, advertising slots, or service packages. A finite realization has \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(Nq_j\) copies of item \(j\). Thus \(N\) can be much larger than \(|T|\) and \(|J|\), while every individual still receives an indivisible bundle.

The authors should recognise this as their problem. It preserves the Nash-product objective, additive valuations, item indivisibility at the individual level, and the assignment decision. It does not replace indivisible allocation by fractional allocation to an individual; \(y_{t,S}\) is a population allocation. The scaling of item supplies is the necessary co-modification when the population itself becomes continuous.

My expectation is that the hardness in Theorem 4.3 will not automatically transfer. The reduction’s switch items encode a binary choice for each finite agent. In \(\mathrm{NSW}^{\mathrm{bundle}}_\infty\), a positive-mass type can split its members between bundles, potentially dissolving that binary gadget. The continuous problem therefore looks like a genuine Class A/Class C boundary question rather than a restatement of Theorem 4.3. Its configuration LP has exponentially many bundle variables, and its pricing problem is essentially

\[ \max_{S\subseteq J} \left\{ \log\left(\sum_{j\in S}v_{tj}\right) -\sum_{j\in S}\lambda_j \right\}. \]

If this pricing problem is tractable, the mirror is a Class A problem; if it is hard, that would be a continuum-specific Class C obstruction. My prior is that the high-multiplicity version may admit substantially better approximation, but the pricing question is itself a worthwhile computational problem.

A second, cleaner anchor is Corollary 3.1, also proved in this paper. It states that Algorithm 1 is an FPTAS for computing an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate thrifty and modest equilibrium in money-clearing Fisher markets with both earning limits and utility limits. The algorithm rounds utilities to powers of \(1+\varepsilon\), computes an exact equilibrium in the perturbed market, and runs in time polynomial in the number of buyers, the numerical input size, and \(1/\varepsilon\). Lemma 3.5 establishes that the perturbed equilibrium is an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate equilibrium for the original market.

The corresponding population mirror is \(\mathrm{FisherEquilibrium}^{\mathrm{sat}}_\infty\). Its input is a finite type set \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\), goods \(G\), per-capita supplies \(q_j\), per-capita seller earning caps \(d_j\), and for each type \(t\), budget \(b_t\), utility cap \(c_t\), and utilities \(u_{tj}\). The instance is required to satisfy the mass-weighted money-clearing condition

\[ \sum_{t\in S}\mu_t b_t \le \sum_{j\in N(S)}d_j \]

for every \(S\subseteq T\), where \(N(S)\) contains the goods valued positively by some type in \(S\).

The output is a price vector \(p\) and per-agent allocations \(z_t\). With

\[ \alpha_t=\max_j \frac{u_{tj}}{p_j}, \qquad u_t^\star=\min\{c_t,b_t\alpha_t\}, \]

each type must receive a modest bundle satisfying

\[ \sum_j u_{tj}z_{tj}\le c_t, \qquad \sum_j p_jz_{tj}\le \min\{b_t,c_t/\alpha_t\}, \]

and utility at least \((1-\varepsilon)u_t^\star\). Aggregate allocations must not exceed supply, and every positively priced good must clear. The question is whether such an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium can be computed in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|G|\), the encoding length of \(\mu\) and the market parameters, and \(1/\varepsilon\).

This mirror is especially plausible. It is exactly the paper’s market model with sums over individual buyers replaced by mass-weighted sums over indistinguishable buyer types. The many-agent regime is natural for large populations of customers with a small number of budget, valuation, and satiation profiles. The MBB graph, surplus flow, price descent, and feasibility LP all have obvious mass-weighted analogues. I would expect this problem to be Class A, although the paper does not prove that extension.

The weakest point is the Nash-welfare mirror: allowing identical agents to receive different bundles may erase precisely the finite binary structure used by Theorem 4.3, and an opponent could claim that the configuration formulation is merely fractional allocation in disguise. The answer is that each member of the continuum still receives an integral bundle; only the aggregate mass is fractional. That is the standard high-multiplicity move, and it is precisely what makes the continuous question worth asking. The equilibrium mirror is less vulnerable, but its FPTAS extension would still require a proof that the finite buyer algorithm survives rational masses and the altered supply normalization.

These mirrors cover only Theorem 4.3 and Corollary 3.1. They do not claim to continuize the whole paper, and neither is a novelty collision: the paper studies finite buyers, while the proposed questions make the population itself the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that neither anchor survives as a faithful high-multiplicity version of the paper’s own combinatorics.

For Theorem 4.3, the hardness construction depends on a binary switch item for every variable. Each switch item is effectively assigned to one of two individually distinct agents, thereby encoding a truth value. In a genuine high-multiplicity population, there are only two sensible choices. If the switch remains a singleton, its per-capita supply tends to zero and it has no effect on the continuum welfare. If its supply is scaled with the population, it becomes a repeated item species that can be divided between the two positive-mass types. The binary assignment disappears. The same applies to equation-item gadgets: type members can be split across bundles, so the reduction’s assignment structure is replaced by a population-level fractional allocation.

One could prohibit such splitting and require every member of a type to receive the same bundle. But then the variables \(y_{t,S}\) are forced to be either \(0\) or \(\mu_t\). The model is merely a weighted finite allocation problem, with type masses acting as multiplicities; the continuum has contributed no genuine mass-transfer freedom. Thus the proposed mirror either ceases to represent the theorem’s problem or ceases to be a genuine continuous-population model.

The equilibrium anchor is even weaker as a research case. Because buyers with the same type are interchangeable and all utilities, caps, budgets, and market-clearing constraints are linear, the weighted version is obtained mechanically. Replace a type \(t\) by aggregate budget \(\mu_t b_t\), aggregate utility capacity \(\mu_t c_t\), and aggregate flow variables \(F_{tj}=\mu_t f_{tj}\). Seller capacities are similarly rescaled. The MBB graph, surplus conditions, feasibility LPs, and price descent are unchanged. Averaging allocations among identical buyers remains valid because their demand sets are convex. Hence the paper’s algorithm already supplies, essentially verbatim, the algorithm for the proposed type-weighted market.

This is not a novelty collision in the strict sense, and neither point proves that the questions are ill-posed. Indeed, that is where the negative case is genuinely weak. The Nash-welfare relaxation is still a legitimate new Class A/Class C question, precisely because the switch gadgets may dissolve; and the weighted Fisher market is a legitimate high-multiplicity formulation even if its answer is a routine extension. The programme explicitly treats such answers as worthwhile.

So I cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that this paper has no worthwhile continuous mirror. The best objections show only that Theorem 4.3’s hardness reduction does not survive unchanged and that Corollary 3.1’s mirror may require little new machinery. Under the programme’s stated standards, both anchors remain defensible, with the Fisher-equilibrium mirror the stronger one.
