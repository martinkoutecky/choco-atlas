Why no mirror

The theorems give explicit tests for whether an individual can manipulate a finite profile. In an atomless society, an individual has zero mass; the proposed LP instead optimizes coordinated cohort mass and permits split reports, changing the strategic unit to a coalition. That is a coherent extension, but it does not preserve the question answered by either anchored result.

What the mirror covers

The proposed extension draws on Theorems 3.1 and 4.1, but asks for a cohort's minimum coordinated reporting mass rather than characterizing individual manipulation; it leaves the stochastic-dominance characterization, two-agent profile counts and optimal-\(k\) results, and lexicographic comparisons aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a mass threshold for strategic manipulation by a homogeneous bloc. Consider a large election with \(N\) voters but only \(\tau\) distinct complete rankings, where \(N\gg\tau\). A type \(t\) is one such ranking, and \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the electorate. For fixed \(k\), let \(W_k(\mu)\) be the set of \(k\)-approval winners.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that under worst-element comparison, \(k\)-approval is non-manipulable at a profile \(p\) if and only if, for every agent \(i\), at least one of these holds:
\[ |\varphi_k(p_{-i})\cap\beta_k(p(i))|=1; \qquad |\varphi_k(p_{-i})|=1; \qquad A\setminus\beta_k(p(i))\subsetneq\varphi_k(p_{-i}). \]
This is an exact characterization of when no single agent can report another ranking and strictly improve the worst alternative in the winner set.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Minimum-Mass Worst-Comparison Manipulation for \(k\)-Approval. Given \(m,k\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, and the \(k\)-approval winners \(W_k(\mu)\), find the least mass of one true type \(t\) that must change its reports to make its members strictly prefer the new winner set under worst comparison. More precisely, choose report masses \(x_r\ge 0\) for rankings \(r\ne t\), with \(\delta=\sum_r x_r\le\mu_t\), and form
\[ \mu'=\mu-\delta e_t+\sum_{r\ne t}x_r e_r. \]
Minimize \(\delta\) subject to
\[ \omega_t(W_k(\mu'))\succ_t\omega_t(W_k(\mu)). \]
Return the infimum and, when attained, a transfer witnessing it; return \(+\infty\) if no such transfer exists. This asks how much of a preference cohort has to coordinate on misreporting before it can improve its members’ outcome.

There is a concrete reason to expect this version to be tractable. For a fixed \(t\), the set of winners must all lie above the old worst winner in \(t\)’s ranking. Choosing which candidate is the highest-scoring candidate among that preferred region gives linear score inequalities. Although there are \(m!\) possible reports, only the reported top-\(k\) set affects scores. If \(\delta\) mass is redistributed, its aggregate approval-score contribution \(y\) ranges over
\[ 0\le y_c\le\delta,\qquad \sum_{c\in C}y_c=k\delta. \]
That is a compact linear description of all mixtures of \(k\)-approval ballots. The resulting threshold problem is a polynomial number of rational LPs, one for each relevant type and candidate. I would expect Class A, with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). The paper itself does not prove this algorithm; it supplies the manipulation concept and the structural anchor.

A second, independent anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved here. Under best-element comparison, it says non-manipulability at \(p\) holds exactly when, for every agent \(i\), at least one of the following holds:
\[ \begin{aligned} &(\beta(p(i)\mid\varphi_k(p_{-i})),x)\in p(i) &&\text{for every }x\in\varphi_k^-(p_{-i});\\ &\varphi_k(p_{-i})\cap\beta_k(p(i))=\varnothing &&\text{and }\varphi_k^-(p_{-i})\cap\beta_k(p(i))\ne\varnothing;\\ &|\varphi_k(p_{-i})\cap\beta_k(p(i))| &>|A\setminus[\beta_k(p(i))\cup\varphi_k(p_{-i})]|. \end{aligned} \]
Here \(\varphi_k^-(p_{-i})\) is the set of alternatives scoring one less than the winners without \(i\). Its mirror is Minimum-Mass Best-Comparison Manipulation for \(k\)-Approval: use the same instance and mass transfer, replacing worst comparison with best comparison. For a proposed improved outcome, some candidate ranked above the old best winner must be a winner. Guessing that candidate and requiring it to attain maximum score again gives linear constraints over the same \(y\)-polytope. I would also expect this problem to be in Class A. Theorem 3.1 remains my lead because its worst-winner condition gives the cleaner cohort-level threshold question.

The interpretation of type is exact in the high-multiplicity sense: voters with the same type share the same complete preference, and the paper assigns them no different prices, costs, or other attributes. A discrete deviation by one voter in an \(N\)-voter election changes mass \(1/N\) from type \(t\) to a reported ranking. Grid-restricting transfers to multiples of \(1/N\) recovers coalition deviations in a compressed profile; a single voter is the one-unit case. The continuous problem then asks what happens when coalition mass can vary continuously. This is a plausible election regime for large electorates with relatively few ballot types, rather than a claim that every committee or election has high multiplicity.

The scope is deliberately limited to the worst- and best-comparison characterizations, Theorems 3.1 and 4.1. I would leave the stochastic-dominance characterization, two-agent profile counts, and optimal-\(k\) results outside this mirror. The paper contains no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification theorem; these anchors are exact characterizations, not complexity-class results. The tractability claim above is a prediction for the newly stated continuous problems.

The weak point is real: the paper studies unilateral manipulation, while a positive-mass bloc is a coalition, and the coalition’s ability to split reports across rankings adds behavior the theorems do not characterize. The authors might regard Minimum-Mass Manipulation as a nearby coalition problem rather than the continuous form of their unilateral one. The single-agent \(1/N\) calibration helps connect the models, but does not erase that distinction. The case still holds as a focused extension: it preserves their \(k\)-approval rule, true rankings, winner-set preferences, and strategic-reporting question, while replacing the scale of a manipulation with the fraction of a homogeneous electorate required to make it work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that they depend on a voter’s unit-sized influence. In Theorem 3.1, the sets of winners and “almost winners” are computed after removing one agent; the almost winners are exactly one score point behind. In Theorem 4.1, the same one-point margin drives the characterization. Those details are not incidental: they capture when one ballot can change the outcome.

In an atomless society, one person has mass zero. If the score maximum is unique, sufficiently small changes to any type’s reports leave the winners unchanged. A unilateral infinitesimal deviation can matter only at a tied maximum. Across the full profile simplex, such ties lie on score-equality hyperplanes; on specially restricted supports they may persist, but the question is still about breaking a tie, not about the paper’s one-vote pivotality. Giving a voter positive mass would preserve influence, but would change the voter into a bloc.

The proposed minimum-mass questions avoid that collapse by letting members of one type coordinate and split their reports. That is a coherent model: the members share preferences, and the score changes can be expressed through a fractional mixture of \(k\)-approval ballots. The resulting LP may be tractable. But the \(1/N\) calibration does not make it a continuous version of the paper’s unilateral question: \(1/N\) tends to zero, whereas a mass threshold can remain positive. Grid-restricting the transfers gives coalition deviations, not single-agent deviations. The proposed model also replaces the paper’s exact one-point conditions with score-margin constraints for coordinated transfers.

This applies to both anchors. For Theorem 3.1, the proposed worst-comparison problem asks how much a cohort must coordinate to move every winner above its members’ current worst winner. For Theorem 4.1, the best-comparison version asks how much must coordinate to make some more-preferred candidate win. Both may be worthwhile coalition problems, but neither carries over the individual-manipulation characterization that anchors the paper’s result.

There is a further gap: the paper proves no numbered polynomial-time or hardness result. Its theorems characterize individual manipulability at a profile, and its two-agent results count profiles. Those claims are not a complexity classification waiting to be continuized. The proposed LP would establish a result about a newly specified optimization problem; it would not transfer or classify a computational result from this paper.

So the negative case is strongest against a *direct* mirror: the natural atomless version erases individual pivotality except at ties, while the positive case restores influence by changing the strategic unit to a coalition. The weakness is that this coalition model is plausible and computationally well-posed. I cannot honestly rule out its value to ChoCo; I can only argue that it is an adjacent extension, not a continuous mirror of either theorem.
