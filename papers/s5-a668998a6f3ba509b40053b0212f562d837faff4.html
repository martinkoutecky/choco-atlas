What the mirror covers

The proposed one-dimensional recovery concerns only Theorem 7's median-equilibrium characterization; it supplies no distinct computational mirror for the other equilibrium results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive point is that this is a highly plausible high-multiplicity regime: many constituents share one of a finite set \(T\) of preference types, and \(\rho_t\) is the fraction of the electorate with type \(t\). The relevant scenario is an electorate much larger than its number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct types. A type is described by its policy utility \(u_t\) and ideal point \(x_t\); a representative chooses a policy, while voters use type-specific cutoffs to decide whether to retain her. Representatives care about discounted utility and the benefit of holding office.

But the paper already makes the population continuous. It takes \(N=[0,1]\) as the electorate and uses a law of large numbers to give each type mass \(\rho_t\). So this model is strong evidence that the population regime is sensible, while a mirror that simply replaces voter counts by masses would restate the paper’s central model.

There is no named computational-complexity result in the paper to anchor a continuous mirror. Its numbered theorems are equilibrium-existence and characterization results. The closest computationally suggestive one is Theorem 7, proved in this paper: when \(d=1\) and \(\delta\beta/(1-\delta)\ge\alpha\), there is a unique simple equilibrium, and every type of representative chooses the weighted-median ideal point \(x_m\) with probability one. That gives a useful structural clue, but the theorem makes no claim about an input encoding, runtime, or complexity class.

An unanchored follow-up could ask for one-dimensional simple-equilibrium recovery: given finitely many types with binary-rational masses and explicitly represented utilities, together with the model parameters satisfying Theorem 7’s condition, compute the equilibrium policies and voting cutoffs. Theorem 7 identifies the policy component as \(x_m\), so in an explicitly encoded finite-input version the median can be found by sorting the types. This is a plausible computational question about the paper’s model, but it is not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in the paper.

The weakest point is therefore decisive for the requested case: the paper already uses a continuous population, and its results do not state computational bounds. The positive case establishes that this kind of population model is natural and points to an algorithmic follow-up; it cannot supply an eligible anchor or a genuine discrete-to-continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 7 is an equilibrium characterization, not a result about the complexity of an encoded computational problem. By the stated standard, that is decisive: a follow-up that asks for an algorithm may be worthwhile, but it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 7 is the closest proposed anchor. Even its stronger algorithmic version is direct: with finitely many types, rational masses and explicitly represented one-dimensional utilities, sort types by their ideal points to find the weighted median \(x_m\). The theorem gives the equilibrium policies; evaluate \(u_t(x_m)\) to recover the voting cutoffs. That is a simple consequence of the theorem, not a substantive complexity landscape.

The more ambitious alternative would be to compute simple equilibria in the multidimensional model. That could become a real research question, but it requires choosing finite representations for the policy space and utilities, and deciding how to represent or approximate mixed strategies over policies. Those choices define a new equilibrium-computation problem; they do not supply the missing computational result to mirror. The paper itself already models a continuous population with finitely many types and masses \(\rho_t\). That makes the high-multiplicity regime especially convincing, but also means a population continuization would restate the model.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed anchor, but weak as a claim that no computational project based on this model could ever be worthwhile. Approximate equilibrium computation under a carefully chosen finite encoding may be worth studying. The narrower conclusion is that this paper has no eligible computational result for a continuous mirror.
