What the mirror covers

No named result in the paper gives an algorithm, complexity bound, or computational hardness result to mirror; the numbered results concern axiom satisfaction and incompatibility.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind ChoCo requires. Its numbered results are axiomatic: Theorem 3.1, proved here, says that no scoring procedure on the universal ranking domain can satisfy both independence of irrelevant matches and self-consistency. Independence requires a remote change to leave the relative order of two untouched objects intact; self-consistency requires an object not to rank below another when, in a decomposition into unweighted comparisons, it does at least as well against opponents who are at least as strong, with a strict ranking when any such comparison or opponent strength is strictly better. The paper’s other numbered results likewise concern axioms and domain restrictions, not complexity or algorithms.

There is a plausible population story: many reviewers make paired comparisons, a type records a reviewer’s complete comparison pattern, and mass records the fraction of reviewers of each type. Their aggregate judgments induce comparison matrices; one could ask whether a scoring procedure over those distributions satisfies the two axioms. But that would be a continuous-population version of an axiomatic compatibility question, not a computational problem anchored by a result in this paper. The theorem’s finite witness would not establish a complexity landscape for such a model.

So I can’t honestly give a qualifying continuous problem or a tractability expectation per anchor. The reviewer-population interpretation is the strongest positive lead, but it does not meet the programme’s computational-anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 3.1 has no computational problem for a continuous population to inherit. Its object is whether any scoring procedure can satisfy IIM and SC across the ranking domain. The other numbered results also concern axiom satisfaction, characterizations, and domain restrictions; none gives an algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or hardness result. The named scoring methods themselves are computed by summing scores or solving linear systems.

A reviewer population is the most plausible repair. Let each reviewer’s type be a complete ranking, and let the population distribution induce pairwise comparison data. One could then ask how much mass must be reweighted to change the ranking under a chosen scoring method. That is a coherent robustness problem, but it adds a baseline society, a reweighting cost, and a specified target. The paper supplies none of those. Its theorem quantifies over scoring procedures; it does not ask how to alter reviewers or data. If the question instead asks whether IIM and SC can both hold on the continuous domain, it remains an axiomatic existence question.

The reviewer model also changes the data domain. Rankings as reviewer types constrain the aggregate comparisons to those obtainable from mixtures of rankings. The paper permits arbitrary incomplete and multiple paired comparisons with intensities. Types rich enough to preserve every such comparison pattern would describe full assessment vectors rather than the ranking types in ChoCo’s central model.

So the proponent’s reviewer example is a plausible adjacent project, but it does not provide a computational mirror of this paper’s results. The negative case is strong under the programme’s stated anchor requirement. Its limit is that ranking robustness under reviewer reweighting could still be worth studying on its own; the text does not establish that no such follow-on project would merit the programme’s time.
