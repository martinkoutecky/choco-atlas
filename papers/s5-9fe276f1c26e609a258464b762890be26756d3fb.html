What the mirror covers

The proposal covers evaluating Theorem 5’s weighted-median rule. It leaves aside that theorem’s strategic guarantee and does not address Theorems 2–4.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest foothold is Theorem 5, proved in the paper. It shows that on the line, with single-peaked preferences and a rooted-tree invitation network, there is a deterministic social choice function satisfying strategy-proofness over networks, Pareto efficiency, anonymity on structure-distance pairs (AN-SD), and voter relevance through distance two (VR-2). Its construction gives a voter at distance one weight \(k+1\), where \(k\) is the number of children they invite; voters at distance two get weight \(1\); deeper voters get weight \(0\). The outcome is the median of voter peaks counted with those weights.

A narrow continuous mirror is Depth-Two Mass Facility Selection. An instance is a finite-support, rational distribution \(\mu\) over voter types \((d,k,p)\), where \(d\) is distance from the moderator, \(k\) is the number of children invited, and \(p\in[0,1]\) is the voter’s peak. Mass \(\mu_{d,k,p}\) is the fraction of participating voters of that type; the reported invitations are already reflected in \(d\) and \(k\). Given this society, return the lower weighted median of the peaks, with weight \(w(1,k)=k+1\), \(w(2,k)=1\), and \(w(d,k)=0\) for \(d\ge3\). Equivalently, return a minimizer of
\[ \sum_{d,k,p}\mu_{d,k,p}\,w(d,k)\,|x-p| \quad\text{over }x\in[0,1], \]
using the lower median to settle ties. Here the decision variable is the facility location \(x\); the objective is aggregate weighted distance. A weighted quantile computation solves it in polynomial time in the support size and input bit length, so I would expect this mirror to be tractable.

The regime could be a large public facility consultation run through a referral tree: many residents, but relatively few peak-location and network-role types, perhaps because residents cluster around common locations and repeated referral structures. The distribution over \((d,k,p)\) then describes the population more naturally than a list of individual votes. The rule also has a recognizable connection to the authors’ problem: it computes the very weighted-median choice they construct, with population mass replacing voter counts.

The weakness is substantial. Theorem 5’s contribution is an axiomatic existence result, not a complexity classification. More seriously, an individual in an atomless population has zero mass, so changing that person’s report or invitations does not change the aggregate distribution. Strategy-proofness then becomes vacuous and individual VR-2 fails. Replacing individual deviations with positive-mass cohort deviations would change the authors’ incentive question. The paper therefore offers a plausible continuous *computation of its constructed rule*, but no strong continuous mirror of its main theorem under the programme’s computational and individual-incentive standards. None of its numbered results asserts a complexity classification such as membership in P or NP-hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but it is an axiomatic existence result. The paper’s other main results are also axiomatic: Theorems 2 and 3 show incompatibilities among strategy-proofness, Pareto efficiency, anonymity and voter relevance; Theorems 4 and 5 give mechanisms satisfying selected combinations. None asserts a complexity bound, hardness result, or algorithmic optimization guarantee. A continuous version of that compatibility frontier could be interesting social-choice theory, but it would not mirror a computational result of this paper.

The proposed mass rule is well-defined, and a large consultation with repeated referral roles is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But its computation is just a weighted quantile: sort the support’s rational peak locations and find where cumulative weight reaches half. That takes \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) comparisons, with polynomial bit complexity under binary rational encoding. Theorem 5 already gives the rule explicitly; its proof does not leave a computational problem of finding the outcome. Writing the same weighted median as an aggregate-distance minimization does not add one—the paper establishes Pareto efficiency, not minimization of total weighted distance.

There is also a gap between computing the rule on a reported society and preserving the theorem’s strategic claim. A distribution over \((d,k,p)\) records a realized profile, but the paper’s strategy-proofness concerns what happens when a voter changes which actual children they invite, thereby changing participation in the tree. In an atomless population, one voter’s report or invitation has zero mass, so individual relevance disappears and strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. Treating a positive-mass cohort as the deviator could restore influence, but it changes the incentive question; retaining individual deviations requires a model of the invitation structure and feasible deviations that the proposed distribution does not supply.

A richer continuous referral model might yield worthwhile new axiomatic questions, and the public-consultation regime is not inherently implausible. The negative case is therefore about fit, not impossibility: the paper offers no computational theorem to anchor such a mirror, its explicit mechanism makes the proposed computation routine, and preserving its central incentive properties in a continuum requires building a new model.
