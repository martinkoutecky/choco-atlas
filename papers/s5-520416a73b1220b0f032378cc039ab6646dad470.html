The continuous mirror question

Fix \(C=\{a_1,p,b_1,b_2,b_3\}\), axis \(a_1\prec p\prec b_1\prec b_2\prec b_3\), scoring vector \(\langle4,3,2,0,0\rangle\), and the average extension. Given a rational distribution \(\nu\) over single-peaked weak-order types of nonmanipulators and rational coalition mass \(\rho\), with \(\sum_t\nu_t+\rho=1\), is there a distribution \(x\) over permitted reports such that \(\sum_qx_q=\rho\) and \(p\) is a winner after the coalition reports are assigned according to \(x\)?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity CWCM instance has nonmanipulator ballot types \(t\) with rational masses \(\nu_t\), coalition mass \(\rho\), and permitted single-peaked weak-order reports \(q\). The decision variables \(x_q\ge0\) assign coalition mass to reports; the constraints require \(\sum_qx_q=\rho\) and \(\sum_t\nu_t s(t,p)+\sum_qx_qs(q,p)\ge\sum_t\nu_t s(t,c)+\sum_qx_qs(q,c)\) for every candidate \(c\).

The objection that survived

With five fixed candidates and a fixed scoring rule, the mass problem is a fixed-dimensional feasibility LP whose tractability does not use Theorem 11’s structural results.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the asymmetric five-candidate CWCM hardness case of Theorem 11 for single-peaked weak orders with the average scoring extension; it does not cover the theorem’s other domains and extensions, its polynomial case, or the paper’s consistency results.

Open questions for a prover

  • How can a feasible mass allocation be rounded to an exact finite electorate when \(p\)'s winning margin is zero?
  • Does tractability persist when the number of candidates is variable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 11, proved in this paper. It says that for the scoring vector \(\langle 4,3,2,0,0\rangle\), weighted CWCM is in P for single-peaked total orders, single-peaked weak orders, and single-plateaued weak orders when the target \(p\) has two candidates on each side of the axis (\(m_1=m_2=2\)); it is NP-complete for all three domains when \(p\) has one candidate on the left and three on the right (\(m_1=1,m_2=3\)). The NP-complete result holds for each of the paper’s scoring extensions: max, min, round down, and average. The paper gives a Partition reduction for the hard case, adapting the total-order construction from Brandt et al.; it then extends the result to votes with ties.

My lead mirror is Asymmetric Single-Peaked Weak-Order Mass-CWCM. Fix \(C=\{a_1,p,b_1,b_2,b_3\}\), the axis \(a_1\prec p\prec b_1\prec b_2\prec b_3\), the scoring vector \(\alpha=\langle4,3,2,0,0\rangle\), and the average extension for tied votes. An instance gives a rational mass distribution \(\nu\) of nonmanipulator voters over single-peaked weak-order types, supported on \(\tau\) types, and a rational manipulator mass \(\rho\), with \(\sum_t\nu_t+\rho=1\). Ask whether the coalition can make \(p\) a winner by assigning its mass across single-peaked weak-order reports. A solution is a rational vector \(x_q\geq0\), one entry for each allowed report \(q\), such that \(\sum_q x_q=\rho\) and, for every \(c\in C\),
\[ \sum_t \nu_t s_\alpha(t,p)+\sum_q x_q s_\alpha(q,p) \;\geq\; \sum_t \nu_t s_\alpha(t,c)+\sum_q x_q s_\alpha(q,c). \]
This is the paper’s nonunique-winner question: ties for first place count as success. Each unit of mass still casts one whole weak-order ballot; only the fraction of the coalition using each ballot is divisible.

The regime is a large electorate choosing among five ordered policy options: many voters share a small set of ballot types, while an organized coalition can coordinate its members’ reports. The number of people can be very large while the number of types stays bounded by the finite set of single-peaked weak orders on these five candidates. For the problem, coalition members have the same role and available reports, so their unreported sincere preferences do not distinguish them. This seems recognizable to the authors as a high-multiplicity relaxation of weighted manipulation: the paper’s weight-\(w\) manipulator represents many voters, and the continuous version lets those voters divide across reports.

The expected result is Class A: tractable. There are only finitely many allowed reports for this fixed five-candidate axis, so the feasibility question is a rational linear program with a constant number of report variables and score constraints. Its exact feasibility can be decided in polynomial time in the binary input length. That gives a concrete explanation for why this hardness anchor may dissolve: in the discrete reduction, manipulator weights \(4k_i\) are indivisible items that must be assigned to reports as wholes. In the mass problem, only their total matters.

For example, the average-extension construction has nonmanipulator weights \(20K\) and \(8K\), and total manipulator weight \(8K\). Normalize by \(36K\). Regardless of whether the \(k_i\) admit a partition, assign coalition mass \(1/9\) to each of \(p>a_1>b_1>b_2>b_3\) and \(p>b_1>b_2>b_3>a_1\). Then \(p,a_1,b_1\) all score \(23/9\), and \(p\) wins. The fractional allocation erases the Partition constraint while preserving the underlying question of whether a manipulator coalition can make \(p\) win.

This is deliberately a narrow mirror: it covers Theorem 11’s asymmetric, five-candidate CWCM case for single-peaked weak orders with the average extension. It does not claim a result for variable \(m\), the paper’s consistency problem, or its other election systems. Further questions include how to round a mass solution to an exact finite electorate when the winning margin is zero, and whether the same tractability extends to variable numbers of candidates.

The weak point is that the discrete anchor’s weighted manipulators each cast one ballot for their whole weight, while the mirror lets that mass divide among ballots. That is the source of the tractability, so it must be presented as the intended high-multiplicity relaxation rather than as an equivalent weighted problem. The case survives if that relaxation is accepted: the electorate, axis, ballot domain, scoring rule, coalition action, and winner condition all remain the paper’s; the indivisible Partition items are precisely what population continuization removes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against the proposed mirror is about its payoff. For this anchor, \(m=5\), the scoring vector is fixed, and there are only constantly many allowed single-peaked weak-order reports. The continuous question is therefore a fixed-dimensional rational LP: choose mass for each report and impose the score inequalities. That procedure works for any finite voting game of this size; it does not use the paper’s structural results about axes or ties.

The Partition reduction makes the effect especially plain. In the average-extension construction, splitting manipulator mass equally between the two reports gives \(p,a_1,b_1\) score \(23/9\), whatever the Partition instance. The mirror exposes the role of indivisible weight blocks, but its algorithm is just finite-action convexification. For a programme seeking questions that need its continuous-optimization machinery, this particular mirror looks like a low-yield use of time.

That objection does not establish that the mirror is ill-posed. Theorem 11 is a named computational result, and a large election over five ordered policy options, with a coordinated coalition whose members can divide among reports, is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Nor does making the number of candidates variable rescue this specific anchor: it changes the fixed five-candidate theorem into a broader problem. Such a broader problem may be worthwhile, but it cannot be counted as a reason this mirror of Theorem 11 fails.

So the negative case is weak on the programme’s central gate. The proposed mirror survives as a sensible continuous question; the strongest argument against it is that its answer may be too generic and elementary to merit priority. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no scenario or reformulation here is worth the programme’s time.
