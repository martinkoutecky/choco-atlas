The proposed menu question is statable, but replicating customers leaves the answer dependent only on the support of \(\mu\), not the population proportions.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Lemma 3.1. Theorems 1.1–1.3 are structural results; the paper's concluding question concerns continuous type domains, not a continuous population.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: it rests on Lemma 3.1, proved in this paper, rather than on the paper’s main two-player theorem. Lemma 3.1 says that for a finite type space with valuation matrix \(T\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}\) and a prescribed outcome rule \(g\in[m]^r\), \(g\) is incentive compatible exactly when there is a payment vector \(x\in\mathbb{R}^m\) satisfying
\[ T_{i,g(i)}-x_{g(i)}\ge T_{i,k}-x_k \quad\text{for every }i\in[r],\ k\in[m]\setminus\{g(i)\}. \]
That is a polynomial-size linear feasibility formulation. The paper does not state a running-time bound, but with rational input it gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m\), \(r\), and the input bit length.

There is a likely typo in the lemma’s printed “in particular” clause: it says the system is equivalent to keeping only constraints with \(k\notin\operatorname{im}(g)\). The proof instead explains that constraints against outcomes outside \(\operatorname{im}(g)\) can be deleted by setting their payments sufficiently high. The displayed full system above is the sound characterization; I would not rely on the printed reduction.

A plausible population mirror is Population Menu-IC Feasibility. A large marketplace or public service provider serves many customers, each choosing one of \(m\) service plans. A type is a customer’s complete valuation vector over those plans; customers with the same vector are indistinguishable for the mechanism. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) valuation types in the population, with binary rational masses summing to one. The input also specifies a desired plan \(g(t)\) for each type. The question is whether one common price vector \(x\) makes each type’s assigned plan utility-maximizing:
\[ t_{g(t)}-x_{g(t)}\ge t_k-x_k \quad\text{for every supported type }t\text{ and every plan }k. \]
A solution is such a price vector, or a certificate that none exists. The resulting LP has \(m\) variables and at most \(m\tau\) constraints, so this mirror is tractable in \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\). Its expected class is A.

This scenario has many actual customers and relatively few distinct valuation types. It is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s single-player IC check: the common menu is used independently by every customer, and customers of one type impose the same incentive constraints. The authors could recognize the underlying mechanism-design question, though the population interpretation is an application of their single-player result, not a mirror of their main theorem.

The mirror covers Lemma 3.1 only. Theorem 1.1, which says that for a finite two-player type space and any IC outcome function \(g\), one can keep the first player’s type space fixed and choose a second type space on which \(g\) is an affine maximizer, is a structural existence result rather than a computational result. The paper’s counterexamples in Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 are structural too.

The weak point is substantial: the population masses do not affect menu implementability once the positive-mass support is known. This is a credible replicated-customer setting, but it does not turn the paper’s central two-player perturbation problem into a population-complexity result. It supports one modest continuous mirror, not a case for continuing the paper’s main results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed menu interpretation makes Lemma 3.1 look like a population result, but the population does no work in that problem. For a society with masses \(\mu_t>0\) on valuation types \(t\), the common price vector must satisfy
\[ t_{g(t)}-x_{g(t)}\ge t_k-x_k \]
for each supported type and alternative. The values of the masses never enter. Any two societies with the same support have the same feasible price vectors, even if their proportions differ completely.

That reflects what the lemma’s \(r\) counts: possible reports for one player, not people in a population. Its LP already has one constraint set per distinct type. Repeating each type a million times neither adds constraints nor compresses the input; the algorithm is polynomial in \(m\) and \(r\), exactly the number of alternatives and types the lemma already lists. The marketplace story is plausible, but it is just many independent copies of the same one-person menu problem. There is no aggregate allocation or interaction for the society distribution to affect.

A stronger candidate might add capacity constraints or ask for the largest share of customers whose assigned plans can be supported by a common menu. Those could make \(\mu\) matter. But capacities introduce a coupled allocation problem, while a share-based objective replaces exact dominant-strategy implementability with a population-weighted relaxation. Either could be worth studying, but neither mirrors Lemma 3.1’s question of whether a prescribed outcome function is IC.

One could also put a continuum of valuation vectors into the type domain and ask for prices that implement an allocation rule across that domain. That may be a worthwhile continuous-type-space question, and the paper itself identifies such questions as open. Its difficulty would come from representing the continuum of possible types and the allocation rule, however, not from high multiplicity in a society over a fixed type set.

So the negative case is strongest against the proponent’s actual anchor: its proposed finite-support population mirror contains no population-dependent question. I would not claim that no related mechanism-design problem could be worthwhile. I would claim that making mass affect the answer requires adding a different problem, while the paper’s other highlighted results are structural rather than computational.
