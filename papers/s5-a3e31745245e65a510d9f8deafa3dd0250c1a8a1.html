What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror: Theorem 1 and Corollary 2 are analytical impossibility results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest possible positive lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for a tail-regular signal distribution with \(F(q)=\Theta(q^\alpha)\) and \(\alpha\ge1\), every disclosure policy and transfer scheme either yields inefficient learning—\(\sum_t\Pr[a_t\ne\theta]=\infty\)—or has infinite expected total absolute transfers, \(\sum_t\mathbb E[|\tau_t(\nu_t)|]=\infty\). Corollary 2, also proved here, says that when \(\alpha\ge1\) and transfers are forbidden, every disclosure policy yields inefficient learning.

But neither is a computational result of the kind this programme requires: neither classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. The paper contains no named computational result, so neither can serve as a valid anchor. There is therefore no per-anchor continuous computational problem I can responsibly hand to a prover.

A large population of consumers learning about a technology or treatment might motivate a cohort version of the paper’s model. But its private-belief distribution \(F\) describes signal uncertainty, not the population’s mass over complete agent types. And the paper’s infinite sequence is indexed by arrival time: each agent acts once after a different history. Replacing that sequence with a continuum of agents or cohorts would change what actions are observed and how they inform later decisions. That could be an interesting model, but Theorem 1 does not establish a complexity claim about it. This is the positive case’s limit: the application suggests a plausible population-scale extension, while the paper’s actual result cannot anchor a continuous computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case for does not leave a computational anchor standing. It names Theorem 1 and Corollary 2, then correctly observes that both are analytical learning results, not complexity results. Theorem 1 rules out efficient learning with finite expected transfers under its assumptions; Corollary 2 rules it out with disclosure alone. Neither asks for an algorithm, classifies computational difficulty, or supplies an optimization problem whose complexity the paper studies.

The strongest attempt to build a mirror would be to turn Theorem 1 into a finite-horizon policy-design problem: given a rational signal distribution, a horizon, and a transfer budget, choose disclosure and transfers to minimize expected mistakes. But making that a continuous-population problem changes the mechanism the theorem analyzes. In a continuum cohort with common social belief \(\nu\), transfer \(\tau\), and resulting private-signal cutoff \(c\), the high-action mass conditional on state \(\theta\) is exactly \(1-F_\theta(c)\). The planner observes the action mass. For an informative signal structure, a cutoff can distinguish the conditional distributions, so an aggregate action share can reveal the state without the sampling uncertainty that drives sequential social learning. The coarse, noisy feedback at the heart of Theorem 1 disappears. A finite cohort preserves that uncertainty, but then it is a finite-agent model; retaining it in a limit requires an additional noise or scaling model, rather than the programme’s population-as-distribution continuization.

Corollary 2 fares no better as an anchor. Its best version would ask whether disclosure alone can achieve some finite-horizon mistake target in a mass cohort. It inherits the same problem: aggregate action frequencies replace the paper’s individual random actions with exact population rates. If instead agents remain sequential individuals, their arrival positions and evolving information contexts matter to the process; compressing them into masses erases the order that creates the learning problem.

A consumer or treatment-adoption story does make large populations plausible, but it does not fix this mismatch. Customers who arrive at different times face different social histories; putting them into synchronized cohorts changes what is observed and how it informs later choices. The paper’s conclusion does mention an open policy-optimization question, but that is likewise an analytical design problem. Encoding a finite approximation of it could be worthwhile computational work; the encoding and discretization would define a new problem, rather than expose a computational question already studied in this paper.

So the negative case is strong for a *mirror of this paper*: it has no named computational result to mirror, and the most natural mass-based reconstruction removes the stochastic feedback that makes its results interesting. It is not an airtight claim that no adjacent computational project could be worthwhile. Finite-cohort policy design might be one; the case against is that it would be a new project, not this paper’s continuous computational mirror.
