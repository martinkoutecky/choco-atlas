The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(I,J\), rational type masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\), rankings with ties, acceptable pairs, and a designated pair \((i^*,j^*)\), choose flows \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq\beta_j\). For each acceptable pair \((i,j)\), let \(S_i(j)\) be the mass of type \(i\) unmatched or strictly preferring \(j\) to its assignment, and \(W_i(j)\) the mass unmatched or weakly preferring \(j\). Require \(S_i(j)W_j(i)=0\) and \(W_i(j)S_j(i)=0\). Find the maximum \(x_{i^*j^*}\) over such strongly stable flows, returning an attaining flow or reporting that none exists.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic two-sided matching market with interchangeable agents within each type, rational type masses, rankings with ties, and aggregate assignment flows \(x_{ij}\). Strong stability rules out positive mass on both sides of any blocking pattern, in either orientation; the objective is the greatest stable mass assigned across a designated type pair.

The objection that survived

The stated stability equations check only one orientation of blocking; a faithful formulation must also forbid positive mass on the reverse pattern, where one side is indifferent and the other strictly prefers the match.

What the mirror covers

This mirrors Theorem 4’s stable-pair existence component through positive mass on a designated type pair, and extends it by maximizing that mass. It does not mirror the man-optimal output or the paper’s full lattice characterization, representation algorithms, or maximal-sequence results.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of maximizing designated-pair mass as a function of the supported types, acceptable pairs, and mass bit length?
  • When masses are rational, how do stable flows relate to stable matchings in finite clone markets at increasing common-denominator multiplicities?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is the paper’s stable-pair computation. Theorem 4 states: for an edge \((m,w)\in E\), there is an \(O(nm)\)-time algorithm to decide whether \(\mathcal M_G(m,w)\)—the strongly stable matchings containing \((m,w)\)—is empty, and, if it is not, to compute a man-optimal matching in that set. This theorem is proved in the paper; its algorithm builds on the cited strongly stable matching algorithm in Theorem 1. The mirror below takes up the stable-pair question and extends it to ask how much mass can be matched across that pair of types.

Consider a two-sided matching market with finite type sets \(I\) and \(J\). Each type is a complete description: its members share the same acceptable counterpart types and the same weak ranking of them, including ties. Type \(i\in I\) has rational mass \(\alpha_i\), and type \(j\in J\) has mass \(\beta_j\); normalize each side’s total mass to \(1\). Let \(E_T\subseteq I\times J\) be the acceptable type pairs. A continuous matching is a flow \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) for \((i,j)\in E_T\), with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\leq\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\leq\beta_j\). Its entries are matched mass: people remain individually matched to at most one partner, while \(x\) records how the population is paired in aggregate.

For each acceptable pair \((i,j)\), define the mass of type-\(i\) agents who are unmatched or strictly prefer \(j\) to their assigned partner as
\[ A_{ij}(x)=\alpha_i-\sum_{k:\,k\succeq_i j}x_{ik}, \]
and the mass of type-\(j\) agents who are unmatched or weakly prefer \(i\) to their assigned partner as
\[ B_{ij}(x)=\beta_j-\sum_{\ell:\,\ell\succ_j i}x_{\ell j}. \]
Call \(x\) strongly stable if \(A_{ij}(x)=0\) or \(B_{ij}(x)=0\) for every acceptable pair. If both quantities are positive, some positive mass on each side can form a blocking pair under the paper’s strict-on-one-side, weak-on-the-other definition.

The continuous problem is: given these type masses and rankings and a designated acceptable pair \((i^\*,j^\*)\), find
\[ \max_x x_{i^\*j^\*} \]
over strongly stable matchings, returning the exact optimum and a matching that attains it; report if no strongly stable matching exists. The stable-pair answer is yes precisely when the optimum is positive. The paper’s edge becomes a type pair, and whether it can occur becomes whether positive population mass can be paired across it. The maximum matched mass is a natural quantitative extension of that yes-or-no question.

A convincing regime is a large, repeated one-to-one placement market—for example, applicants and individual job slots—with hundreds of thousands or millions on each side but far fewer distinct preference-and-eligibility types. Members of a type must really be interchangeable for the market’s purposes; the example is plausible where applicants and slots fall into shared ranking classes. The model’s size is the number of supported types, the acceptable type pairs, and the bit length of the masses, not the headcount. For rational masses, a common-denominator clone market gives a useful high-multiplicity bridge, but whether stable fractional flows correspond to stable clone matchings is a question to establish, not an assumption.

My initial expectation is Class A, as a research hypothesis. The paper’s pair-conditioned result is a tractable anchor, and the continuous instance still has a finite type description. A prover should aim for an exact algorithm polynomial in the number of types, acceptable pairs, and input bit length, perhaps by exploiting rank cutoffs and flow or LP structure. That is not an immediate lift of Theorem 4: the stability condition above is disjunctive, so proving that the type-level choices admit a compact optimization method is the central question. If they do not, the disjunction may instead expose continuum-specific hardness.

This mirror deliberately covers only Theorem 4’s stable-pair component. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s full characterization of all strongly stable matchings or its man-optimality result. The weakest point is the change from a named edge in an indivisible matching to positive mass across a type pair: that is the honest population-scale analogue, but it may not preserve every detail of the edge-conditioned theorem. The useful follow-up is to establish the exact bridge to finite clone markets, including whether a positive continuous optimum can be rounded to stable matchings at increasing multiplicity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror does not preserve all of Theorem 4’s output. The theorem tests whether a particular named edge can occur and, if so, returns a man-optimal matching conditioned on that edge. Asking whether a type pair carries positive mass preserves the yes-or-no part; maximizing its mass adds a different optimization question. A closer mirror would characterize all stable mass assignments compatible with a type pair. That is a substantial change in the target, but it does not make the question uninteresting.

There is also a fixable issue in the displayed stability condition. Under the paper’s verbal definition—neither side worsens and at least one improves—the equations check only one direction of blocking. They miss a pair where the \(I\)-side is indifferent and the \(J\)-side strictly prefers the match. A faithful mass model must rule out blocking in both orientations. That makes the feasible set a union of regions defined by disjunctions, so Theorem 4’s algorithm does not immediately become an LP method. But this is a modeling correction, not a reason to abandon the computational question.

I cannot make the broader case that no worthwhile mirror exists. Large placement markets with repeated applicant and position types are plausible high-multiplicity settings, and rational masses give a direct bridge to typed clone markets. Existing work on groups of similar agents and on fractional matchings makes this area crowded, but it does not answer the continuous-population computational question posed here ([Meeks and Rastegari](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/222769/), [Caragiannis et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S000437022030165X)).

So the honest negative case is weak: the proponent’s exact formulation needs repair, and the maximum-mass objective is not a direct restatement of the theorem. Once corrected, however, the stable-pair mirror survives. I would not argue that it is outside the programme’s scope.
