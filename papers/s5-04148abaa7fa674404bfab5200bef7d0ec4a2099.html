The continuous mirror question

Input: a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_i\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), compatible unordered type pairs, and for each type \(i\) a complete weak ranking \(\succeq_i\) of compatible partner types and unmatched, with compatible partners preferred to unmatched. Decide whether a symmetric assignment \(y_{ij}=y_{ji}\geq 0\) exists, with \(y_{ij}=0\) for incompatible pairs and \(\sum_{j\in T}y_{ij}\leq\mu_i\); \(y_{ii}\) counts endpoints of type \(i\) paired within their type. Let \(u_i=\mu_i-\sum_j y_{ij}\), \(W_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succeq_i k}y_{ik}\), and \(S_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}y_{ik}\). Require that no compatible pair \(\{i,j\}\) satisfies \(W_i(j)>0\), \(W_j(i)>0\), and \(S_i(j)>0\) or \(S_j(i)>0\); for \(i=j\), positive mass represents distinct nonatomic members.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic typed roommate market with rational type masses \(\mu_i\), rankings over compatible partner types, and a symmetric mass assignment \(y_{ij}\) subject to type capacities. The diagonal records endpoint mass for within-type pairs. The objective is a decision: find a feasible assignment with no positive-mass strongly blocking pair.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s existence problem for standardized preference and compatibility types. It leaves aside instances whose preferences distinguish particular individuals.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does this positive-mass strong-stability problem admit a polynomial-time algorithm in the number of types and input bit length, or is it continuum-specifically hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the existence question in Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It establishes that Algorithm 1 correctly decides whether a finite roommates instance has a strongly stable matching; the paper’s runtime argument shows that this decision can be made in polynomial time. The theorem covers a finite simple graph with complete, transitive preferences over incident edges and unmatched, where every incident edge is preferred to being unmatched. A strongly stable matching has no pair that both weakly prefer each other to their current partners and for which at least one strictly prefers the other. The paper’s cited NP-completeness result for *weak* stability is not another anchor: it concerns a different stability notion and is due to Ronn, not proved here.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large roommate or peer-pairing programme whose participants are described by standardized compatibility profiles. Think of millions of people, but far fewer preference types: each type captures everything the model uses, including a participant’s ranking of compatible partner profiles and any eligibility constraints. Members of a type are interchangeable for the problem. That is a demanding assumption for ordinary roommate matching, where people often have individual preferences, but it is credible in a system where preferences are expressed over standardized profiles. The continuous society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\mu_i\) the fraction of participants of type \(i\).

I would call the mirror Strongly Stable Roommates Existence over a Continuum. An instance has a finite type set \(T\), a set of compatible unordered type pairs, rational masses \(\mu_i\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and for each type \(i\), a complete weak ranking \(\succeq_i\) of its compatible partner types and unmatched, with compatible partners ranked above unmatched. A compatible pair \(\{i,i\}\) means two distinct members of type \(i\) may pair.

A solution is a symmetric mass assignment \(y_{ij}=y_{ji}\geq 0\), with incompatible pairs fixed to zero, and
\[ \sum_{j\in T} y_{ij}\leq \mu_i \qquad (i\in T). \]
For \(i\ne j\), \(y_{ij}\) is the mass of type \(i\) paired with type \(j\). On the diagonal, \(y_{ii}\) is the mass of type \(i\) *members* paired within their own type, so it counts endpoints; the corresponding pair mass is \(y_{ii}/2\). Each individual still gets one whole partner: \(y\) records how the population mass is paired. Let \(u_i=\mu_i-\sum_j y_{ij}\) be unmatched mass.

For a compatible pair \(\{i,j\}\), define
\[ W_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succeq_i k}y_{ik}, \qquad S_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}y_{ik}. \]
These are, respectively, the masses of type \(i\) weakly and strictly willing to pair with type \(j\); unmatched participants are included because every compatible partner is preferred to being unmatched. The assignment is strongly stable if there is no compatible pair for which
\[ W_i(j)>0,\quad W_j(i)>0,\quad \bigl(S_i(j)>0\ \text{or}\ S_j(i)>0\bigr). \]
For \(i=j\), this means two distinct members of that type can form the blocking pair. The problem asks whether such a \(y\) exists, and to output one if it does. There is no optimization objective in the mirror, because Theorem 1 is a decision result.

This is a close analogue of the theorem’s *underlying problem*: it asks whether a strongly stable pairing exists, with the same blocking criterion. A finite strongly stable matching in a typed population induces a mass assignment of this form after normalizing by population size. The converse need not hold: a mass assignment can split a type across partner types in ways a small finite electorate cannot. That is the intended high-multiplicity relaxation, not a claim that the paper’s integral matching result carries over unchanged.

My working expectation is tractability (Class A): the input has finitely many types, the decision is expressed in aggregate masses, and the paper gives a polynomial LP-based route for the corresponding finite existence question. But the mass-level blocking conditions involve positive-mass tests, so they do not immediately become the paper’s LP. Establishing a polynomial algorithm—or finding continuum-specific hardness—is the question to hand a prover. This mirror covers Theorem 1’s existence problem only; it does not claim to cover all graphs and individual-specific preferences in the paper’s full generality.

The weakest point is exchangeability. In most real roommate settings, people distinguish between particular individuals, not just partner types. The mirror requires preferences and compatibility to be fully captured by a small number of types. That narrows the theorem’s application, but it is also the precise regime in which the high-multiplicity question makes sense. The paper’s strong-stability criterion remains intact, and the authors should recognize the mass version as their existence problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about model fidelity. The paper’s input allows arbitrary individual compatibility graphs and preferences over particular partners. In a large roommate population, those details often distinguish people, so a type that fully captures them may be nearly unique to each person. That leaves little compression by type.

To keep the number of types small, the proposed mirror instead makes compatibility and preferences depend on partner *types*. That is a coherent high-multiplicity regime, but it restricts the paper’s model to clone-like populations. Its mass-level blocking test also asks whether several preference-defined groups have positive mass; it is not simply the paper’s individual-pair test with counts replaced by fractions. The paper’s LP and odd-set matching constraints therefore do not, by themselves, provide an algorithm for this new input model.

That objection is not enough to rule the mirror out. Large standardized cohorts are plausible, and the mirror still asks whether a strongly stable pairing exists. The gap between the paper’s LP and the mass model’s blocking conditions could itself produce a computational question. The continuous roommate-market literature I found studies existence analytically, while the fractional strong-stability work is adjacent rather than an answer to the finite-support rational-mass complexity problem ([continuum roommate markets](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/TE5915), [fractional matching and LP](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/pdf/10.1287/moor.18.4.803)). Under the programme’s criteria, neither establishes a collision.

So I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proposed mirror narrows the paper’s general model and needs its own stability and complexity analysis, but the polynomial-time existence result in Theorem 1 is a credible anchor for a typed, high-multiplicity version of stable roommates. The negative case is weak precisely where it needs to be strongest: it cannot exclude a sensible population regime.
