The continuous mirror question

Let \(\mu\) be a finite-support distribution over complete consumer types, each specifying a utility and wealth or endowment, and let \(d_\mu(p)=\int x_\theta(p,w_\theta(p))\,d\mu(\theta)\). Given aggregate spending-share observations \(g^k=\operatorname{diag}(p^k)d_\mu(p^k)\), a current android pool, and a dual state \(U\) obtained from CRM’s wealth-redistribution problem on those observations, compute a linear-utility android parameter \(y\) maximizing \(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle Ue_k,e_{j_k(y)}\rangle\), where \(j_k(y)\in\arg\max_j\{y_j-\log p_j^k\}\) with the paper’s tie-breaking rule. The continuous-market question is the computational complexity of this pricing task when the aggregate observations and CRM state are generated by a population with many consumers but relatively few types.

The model it lives in

A market whose consumers follow a finite-support measure \(\mu\) over utility and wealth types, with CRM receiving aggregate demand observations rather than individual records. The decision is the coefficient vector \(y\) of the next linear-utility android; its objective is the improvement score across the sampled prices.

The objection that survived

The reduction proves hardness for unrestricted pricing inputs, but does not show that its price and dual data can arise from aggregate observations of a high-multiplicity market and a reachable CRM state.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem D.1’s linear-android pricing oracle only; it leaves the learning guarantees, other separation results, and fair-allocation experiments unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does strong NP-hardness persist when the observations and dual state must be realizable from a high-multiplicity market?
  • How does the pricing problem’s complexity depend on the number of goods, price samples, and support size of \(\mu\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The most defensible mirror is for the paper’s linear-android separation problem, not for CRM as a whole.

Take a large market such as residential electricity or mobile-data customers. A consumer’s complete type records their utility and wealth function (or, in an Arrow–Debreu market, their utility and endowment). Consumers with the same type are interchangeable. If the type distribution is \(\mu\), then \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of the population of type \(\theta\), and aggregate demand is
\[ d(p)=\int x_\theta(p,w_\theta(p))\,d\mu(\theta). \]
For a normalized market, assume total wealth is one, so its aggregate spending share is \(g(p)=\operatorname{diag}(p)d(p)\). The regime is \(m\gg\tau\): many customers, but relatively few distinct combinations of preferences and wealth. The paper observes only aggregate demand at sampled prices, so \(\mu\) can remain latent, as the individual consumers do in its data model.

My anchor is Theorem D.1, proved in this paper. It states that the separation problem for linear utility is polynomial-time solvable when \(K=1\), and strongly NP-hard when \(K\ge2\). The hardness proof reduces Maximum Acyclic Subgraph to the separation problem.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: Continuous-Market Linear-Android Pricing. The market is represented by a type distribution \(\mu\), and its observed aggregate spending shares at \(K\) price contexts are the data being fitted by CRM. Given those price contexts \(p^1,\ldots,p^K\) and dual weight vectors \(u^1,\ldots,u^K\) from a CRM pricing state, find a linear-utility android that most improves the fit. Its type is a coefficient vector \(c\ge0\), identified up to positive scaling; write \(y=\log c\). At price \(p^k\), it spends its budget on a good
\[ j_k(y)\in\arg\max_j\{y_j-\log p^k_j\}, \]
with the paper’s deterministic tie-breaking rule. The objective is
\[ \max_y\ \sum_{k=1}^K \langle u^k,e_{j_k(y)}\rangle. \]
A solution is a parameter \(y\), its induced good choices at every sampled price, and the attained objective value. This is the underlying task of choosing the next android, not a question about encoding it as a mixed-integer program. The price contexts \(K\) are samples, not population types.

I expect hardness to survive. The paper’s reduction places its combinatorics in the goods and the consistency of the android’s choices across price contexts. Representing the customers who generated the aggregate data by a distribution over types does not remove those constraints. In the programme’s trichotomy, this is closest to hardness transferring: the population size is not what makes this oracle hard. The follow-up questions are whether the hardness remains when the dual weights must arise from a reachable CRM state on realizable market data, and whether useful approximation or parameterized algorithms exist under restrictions on goods or price contexts. The mirror covers this android-pricing result only; it does not claim to continuize all of the paper’s learning or allocation results.

The weak point is that the paper’s theorem is about selecting an artificial consumer, and its complexity depends on goods and price samples, not on the number of real consumers. The authors may regard the continuous market as a natural interpretation of the aggregate demand being fitted, but the paper does not itself require or identify a population distribution \(\mu\). The positive case is therefore that their computational subproblem remains the same when its underlying market is a sensible high-multiplicity population—not that Theorem D.1 already studies population mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor is the right one to examine: Theorem D.1 is a genuine computational result. But the proposed mirror puts the continuous population outside the problem the theorem solves.

In the separation problem, the input is a set of \(K\) price contexts and dual vectors, and the output is one artificial consumer’s parameter \(y\). Neither the real consumers nor their types, masses, or support size appear in the optimization. Saying the observations came from a market with distribution \(\mu\) gives the task an economic setting, but does not make \(\mu\) part of the computational question. Here, \(K\) counts price samples; it is not the number of population types.

The strongest repair would restrict the oracle to price-demand data and dual states that can arise from a market with many consumers and relatively few complete types. That is a sensible question, but the paper’s reduction does not establish hardness under those restrictions: it constructs price and weight data for the oracle, not a high-multiplicity market and a reachable CRM state that produce them. Proving such a connection would be new work. If the restrictions change the feasible instances, the result would concern a constrained oracle problem; if they do not, the continuous population remains contextual rather than computationally operative.

That makes this a weak case against a mirror, not a decisive one. Large markets with repeated preference and endowment types are plausible, and the paper’s aggregate-demand setting gives the authors a reason to recognize that regime. I can argue that this particular theorem has not yet been tied to it. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
