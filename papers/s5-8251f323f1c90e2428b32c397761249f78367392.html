The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of structural roles \(R\), opinions \(I\), initial masses \(\mu_{r,i}\) with preferred mass \(\sum_{r\in R}\mu_{r,0}\ge\eta>0\), and adoption probabilities \(\alpha_0=1\) and \(\alpha_i\le 1-\epsilon\) for \(i\ne0\), consider synchronous biased-voter updates on symmetric, regular type-level interaction kernels chosen adaptively subject to conductance lower bounds \(\phi_t\). What cumulative conductance \(\sum_{t\le T}\phi_t\) suffices in the worst case for the induced mass dynamics to reach preferred-opinion mass at least \(1-\delta\)?

The model it lives in

A society is a mass distribution over types \((r,i)\), where \(r\) is a structural role and \(i\) is an opinion. Symmetric regular interaction kernels specify the edge flow between types; each update applies the paper's biased adoption rule to those masses. The objective is the worst-case number of rounds or conductance budget to reach ε-consensus.

The objection that survived

The adaptive adversary may exploit opinion–graph correlations that a finite role-level interaction kernel does not preserve, and the proposal does not specify a network family that resolves this.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the positive-initial-mass, ε-consensus analogue of Theorem 1.3; it leaves the rare-seed threshold and exact finite-population extinction behavior unresolved, along with the standard-model bounds and lower bounds.

The case FOR (proponent)

I find no eligible computational anchor. The named results are proved in the paper, but they analyze how a stochastic process evolves; they do not classify a computational problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest results are Theorems 1.1–1.3. Theorem 1.1 bounds the number of rounds for standard voter-model consensus: with probability at least \(1/2\), consensus occurs once either the cumulative conductance reaches \(b m/d_{\min}\) or the cumulative squared conductance reaches \(b n\log n\). Theorem 1.2 gives a lower bound: with probability at least \(1/2\), disagreement remains until the cumulative upper conductance reaches \(bn\). Theorem 1.3 shows that, in the biased model on dynamic regular graphs, if the preferred opinion starts on at least \(c\log n\) nodes and the other opinions have popularity at most \(1-\epsilon\), the preferred opinion prevails with high probability once cumulative conductance reaches \(b\log n\); for static graphs, the bound is \(O(\log n/\phi)\). These are consensus-time bounds for the dynamics, not computational complexity results. Calling the round bounds “polynomial time” would conflate process time with the running time of an algorithm solving an input problem.

The most plausible positive direction is a role-based continuous consensus model. In a large regular interaction network, let a type be a structural role together with an opinion and, in the biased model, its popularity. Mass is the fraction of the population of each type; the regime would have \(n\gg\tau\), with a fixed or modest number of roles and opinions. An interaction kernel records how mass in each role is connected to other roles, including the adversary’s allowed rewiring. A possible question is to find the worst-case conductance budget needed to reduce nonpreferred mass below a threshold \(\varepsilon\). This gives the model, state variables, and objective, but it is a new adversarial control question, not one computed or classified in this paper.

That direction has a substantial weakness: exact consensus in the paper depends on finite-population extinction events. An atomless mass model’s deterministic update can approach consensus without ever reaching it. Using an \(\varepsilon\)-consensus target changes the paper’s endpoint, while preserving exact absorption requires retaining population granularity or adding stochastic limiting dynamics. And arbitrary graph rewiring may depend on network structure that a small set of roles does not capture.

So the strongest honest case is a plausible extension of the paper’s dynamics, with Theorem 1.3 as its closest supporting result. There are zero anchors under the stated rule, however, so I cannot give a compliant per-anchor continuous computational problem or predict a Class A/B/C outcome. The continuous-time coalescing-random-walk work mentioned in the paper also does not supply an anchor: it makes time continuous on a finite graph, not the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper states no computational complexity result to mirror. Theorem 1.1 bounds the number of rounds until standard voter-model consensus; Theorem 1.2 bounds how long disagreement can persist; and Theorem 1.3 bounds the rounds until a preferred opinion takes over. These are properties of a stochastic process, not algorithmic running times. Recasting any of them as a computation would require adding a new input and output problem.

The proponent names no anchors, and those three theorems do not supply them. Their most plausible proposed question—minimize the conductance budget needed to reduce nonpreferred mass below \(\varepsilon\)—is a new adversarial control problem. The paper proves bounds for a process under conductance constraints; it does not ask an algorithm to choose a graph sequence or optimize that budget. It could be a worthwhile project in its own right, but its value would come from added choices about the model and objective, not from continuizing a computational result in this paper.

There is also a modelling cost to that proposal. A small set of roles and an interaction kernel could describe a restricted family of large networks, but the paper allows adaptive rewiring of arbitrary graphs. To predict opinion changes, a role model must retain how opinions sit across edges; conductance alone does not determine that information. Keeping enough network detail may leave few agents per type, while compressing it to a handful of roles imposes a new mixing assumption. And exact consensus is a finite-population extinction event: a deterministic mass process may approach consensus without reaching it. Replacing it with an \(\varepsilon\)-threshold changes the endpoint; retaining a threshold of one person brings the population scale \(n\) back into the model.

The biased result sharpens this issue. Its \(c\log n\) initial seeds are a vanishing fraction, \(c\log n/n\), as \(n\) grows. A positive-mass initial condition is a different regime; preserving the rare-seed behavior requires finite-size stochastic effects, which is exactly what a plain continuum mass model removes. Continuous-time coalescing walks cited in the paper change the clock on a finite graph, not the population, so they do not resolve this gap.

This is a strong case against treating the paper as a source of a ChoCo mirror: there is no computational anchor, and the natural continuous reinterpretation becomes either analytic population dynamics or a newly specified control problem. It does not prove the literal universal claim that no carefully designed population-level optimization inspired by the paper could be worth studying. The honest negative is narrower: the paper itself gives no worthwhile continuous *computational mirror* to pursue.
