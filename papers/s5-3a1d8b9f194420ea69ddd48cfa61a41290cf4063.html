The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchored positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. Its numbered formal claims are Property 1 and Property 2, which require permutation-invariant aggregation and a fixed-length output. The experimental findings—including that DeepGroup outperforms baselines and that plurality gives stronger reverse-preference predictions than Borda—are not named computational results. So there is no qualifying anchor to restate, and no anchor for which I can honestly classify a continuous problem as tractable or hard.

There is a plausible, but unanchored, population version of the paper’s task: on a large platform, let a type capture a user’s preference ranking and other stable information relevant to which groups they join. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of users of type \(t\), represent each observed group by its type-mass vector, and predict the decision of a new group—or infer preferences from the decisions of observed groups. That is recognizably about a continuous population. The main weakness is that DeepGroup relies on links between named users across overlapping groups; aggregating users into types could lose that signal, and the paper offers no computational theorem showing what survives. I would not present this candidate as a mirror of a named result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Its formal claims are two properties of aggregators; its contributions are a neural model and empirical accuracy findings. There is no theorem about the complexity of group prediction or reverse social choice, and no algorithmic classification for a continuous version to sharpen or overturn. That leaves no anchored mirror of the kind ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed population model also loses the paper’s main signal if types record only preferences. DeepGroup uses which named users recur together across observed groups; equal preference rankings do not make two users interchangeable when their group histories differ. A more faithful type would include participation history as well as preference. But then the support can grow with the number of distinct histories, often approaching one type per user, rather than staying at moderate \(\tau\). In reverse social choice, a distribution over types could estimate prevalence, but it cannot identify the preference of a particular user: that user has zero mass in the continuum. Keeping a marked individual and their links to observed groups preserves the target by retaining the identity structure the population representation was meant to compress.

There is a plausible broader project here: infer a population’s preference distribution from outcomes of large committees whose members fall into recurring, behaviorally identical types. That would be a continuous inverse problem, but it changes the target from predicting a specified group’s decision or recovering a specified participant’s preference. It is a new problem suggested by the paper, not a continuous mirror of a result it establishes.

So the negative case is strong against an anchored, paper-faithful mirror, and weaker against a newly formulated population-level inference problem. The latter may be worth studying on its own; this paper gives no computational result that makes it a ChoCo mirror.
