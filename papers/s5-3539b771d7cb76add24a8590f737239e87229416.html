The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a positive case for a continuous version of the paper’s optimization problem, but there is an important limit: the paper has no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its named computational findings are about optimal voting behavior and ordering. So none is a qualifying complexity anchor under your rule.

The closest anchor is Proposition 5, established here by exhaustive exact calculations rather than a deductive proof. It says that, for the paper’s three-juror model and any prior probability \(\theta\), optimal sequential majority voting reaches its highest correctness probability when a median-ability juror votes first. When \(\theta=1/2\), starting with a non-median juror is strictly suboptimal; the order of the final two jurors does not affect the optimum. This is a substantive result about the underlying optimization problem, but it is not a complexity classification.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large panel of assessors judging one shared binary state, with many assessors in each of a few standardized ability grades. There are far more assessors than grades, and ballots are shown in an open roll call. Let the ability grades be \(a\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}\), with rational mass \(\nu_a\). An assessor’s problem-relevant type is their ability and signal channel; conditional on state \(X\), the mass of ability-signal types \((a,s)\) is \(\mu^X_{a,s}=\nu_a p^X_a(s)\), where \(p^A_a=f_a\) and \(p^B_a=g_a\) are the paper’s signal distributions.

Call the resulting problem Continuous Sequential Jury Ordering. Its instance is a rational prior \(\theta\) and ability distribution \(\nu\). Choose an ordering schedule \(\pi:[0,1]\to\{0,1,2,3,4\}\) whose masses match \(\nu\), and a threshold policy \(\tau(a,q,H)\) for each ability, position \(q\), and preceding ballot history \(H\). An assessor with signal \(s\) votes A exactly when \(s>\tau(a,q,H)\); voting stops when a side has half the total mass. The objective is to maximize \(Q=\theta\mathbf 1[V_A=A]+(1-\theta)\mathbf 1[V_B=B]\), where \(V_X\) is the verdict induced by the policy when the state is \(X\). A solution is an ordering schedule and threshold policy attaining the maximum. The decision version asks, given rational \(\rho\), whether some schedule and policy achieve \(Q\ge\rho\).

For this exact atomless model, I expect Class A, but the reason exposes the mirror’s main weakness. If any positive mass has ability \(a>0\), a positive prefix voting at threshold \(0\) has different deterministic A-vote rates in the two states: \(1-F_a(0)\) under A and \(1-G_a(0)\) under B. Later assessors can distinguish the state from the observed prefix and vote to make the verdict correct, giving \(Q=1\). If all mass has ability \(0\), signals carry no information and the best value is \(\max(\theta,1-\theta)\). Thus this version has a simple solution—but the exact continuum removes the signal uncertainty that makes the paper’s ordering question interesting. The median-first result need not survive.

This mirror covers only Proposition 5’s objective of optimizing correctness through sequential majority voting and voter ordering. It does not cover the paper’s results on honest voting, wrong convictions, or simulations for larger juries. The natural follow-up questions concern how the ordering result returns when finite-population signal noise is retained, or when the signal masses are uncertain rather than exact.

The weakest point is therefore substantial: the scenario can have many assessors over few types, but the atomless signal limit may no longer be the problem these authors mean to study. A more faithful stochastic limit might help, but it would need a careful definition of the population’s signal uncertainty.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered result classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 5 is the strongest possible anchor, but it establishes an optimal ordering for three jurors from exhaustive calculations; it does not give an algorithmic or complexity result. Still, take that optimization question seriously and give it the best high-multiplicity setting: many assessors judging one shared state, with a fixed distribution over a few ability grades and ballots shown in an open roll call. Repeated ability grades make this a plausible population regime, and the common objective does not depend on anyone’s identity.

The problem is that the exact continuum removes the uncertainty that makes Proposition 5 interesting. For any positive mass of assessors with ability \(a>0\), let a small initial block vote at threshold \(0\). Its exact fraction of A votes is \(1-F_a(0)\) if the state is A and \(1-G_a(0)\) if it is B; these differ. Choose the block to have less than half the total mass, so voting continues. Its public vote rate then identifies the state, and the remaining mass can vote for the correct verdict. The median-first ordering question has collapsed: the optimal correctness probability is \(1\), regardless of the subtle finite-jury trade-offs.

That collapse is the fundamental objection, not merely a dull answer. Proposition 5 depends on random signal realizations and on how each individual ballot changes the information available to later voters. With a fixed positive mass of independent assessors, the continuum makes each block’s signal frequencies exact. No individual has positive mass, and the public history reveals the state. Including realized signals in the population types does not rescue the question: their exact state-dependent masses reveal it even more directly.

A more faithful finite-\(n\) version could retain sampling noise and study how the best ordering behaves as juries grow. But that keeps a finite stochastic electorate rather than making the society itself continuous. With fixed signal quality, the large-population limit again washes out the ordering effect as correctness approaches certainty. Preserving nontrivial uncertainty would require an additional scaling assumption—such as weakening signal quality with \(n\)—or a common shock that correlates signals. Either changes the model’s information structure; neither is a continuous-population mirror of the paper’s fixed signal channels. Such limits may be worthwhile probability or control questions, but they do not supply the computational complexity landscape ChoCo is seeking.

The negative case is not airtight: a very large panel of assessors is conceivable, and a second-order study of finite-jury reliability could be interesting. But the best direct mirror of Proposition 5 loses the phenomenon as soon as the population is made continuous. The paper’s own examples remain small, and its named results do not identify a computational problem whose complexity a continuous population could clarify.
