The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of workload types with positive rational workloads \(w_t\) and population masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), choose assignment masses \(x_{ti}\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{ti}=\mu_t\), and minimize \(\max_i L_i/\min_i L_i\), where \(L_i=\sum_{t\in T}w_t x_{ti}\).

The model it lives in

A population of standardized service requests, represented by workload types \(t\), masses \(\mu_t\), and workloads \(w_t\), assigned across \(k\) identical processors. Each request receives one processor; \(x_{ti}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to processor \(i\), and the objective is to minimize the ratio of the largest processor load to the smallest.

What the mirror covers

This mirror covers Theorem 27 for \(k\)-PART; it leaves the \(k\)-SSR results, including Theorems 15 and 32, unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • After assigning masses equally, what is the tightest finite-cohort rounding guarantee in terms of \(\tau\), workload values, and cohort counts?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the continuous \(k\)-way number-partitioning ratio problem for a large population of standardized service requests assigned to \(k\) interchangeable teams or processors. That scenario fits the paper’s own scheduling interpretation: there are many requests, relatively few processing-time types, and each request is assigned wholly to one team.

My anchor is Theorem 27, proved in this paper. For fixed \(k\), given a multiset \(A\) of \(n\) positive integers and \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), it gives an FPTAS for \(k\)-PART: the algorithm partitions all input elements into \(k\) disjoint sets and returns a partition whose largest-to-smallest sum ratio is at most \(1+\varepsilon\) times optimal, in \(O(n^{2k}/\varepsilon^{k-1})\) time. The introduction calls the problems NP-hard, but the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing that hardness; Theorem 27 is therefore the appropriate named anchor.

Here is the mirror, Continuous \(k\)-PART Ratio. An instance has a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) request types, a positive rational workload \(w_t\) for each type \(t\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population that has workload \(w_t\). Choose assignment masses \(x_{ti}\geq 0\), where \(x_{ti}\) is the measure of type-\(t\) requests assigned to team \(i\), subject to \(\sum_{i=1}^k x_{ti}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\). Each individual request goes to one team; \(x_{ti}\) records how much of a large, indistinguishable type cohort goes there. Team \(i\)’s load is \(L_i=\sum_t w_t x_{ti}\). Return a feasible assignment minimizing \(\max_i L_i/\min_i L_i\).

This problem has an exact optimum of \(1\): assign \(x_{ti}=\mu_t/k\) for every type and team. Every team then has load \(\frac{1}{k}\sum_t\mu_t w_t\), and no ratio can be below \(1\). So I expect this mirror to be tractable—indeed, solvable directly, without an optimization algorithm. The discrete-to-continuous change is the relaxation of integer cohort counts to masses. It keeps the partitioning decision and load-ratio objective intact, while removing finite-population granularity.

The regime matters: think of many recurring requests drawn from a small catalogue of exact service-time classes, with fixed \(k\) and \(N\gg\tau\). If a finite population has \(n_t\) requests of each type, its continuous mass vector is \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). Distributing each type’s requests as evenly as possible across teams gives each team load within \(\sum_t w_t\) of the average load \(W/k\), where \(W=\sum_t n_t w_t\). Thus, when \(W/k>\sum_t w_t\), this simple assignment has ratio at most
\[ \frac{W/k+\sum_t w_t}{W/k-\sum_t w_t}. \]
With fixed types and growing cohort counts, that bound tends to \(1\). This makes the continuous model a meaningful high-multiplicity limit of the finite assignment problem.

The mirror covers Theorem 27 only. Its weakest point is also clear: identical teams and unrestricted assignment make the continuous optimum trivial, so a referee might see little computational substance and question whether a population of jobs is the right “society.” I think the authors could still recognize it as their \(k\)-PART objective with high-multiplicity job cohorts: the requests remain indivisible individually, but a continuum of identical requests can be divided by measure. The useful next question is how the finite-population error depends on the growth of \(\tau\) and the workload types, and when the ratio-one continuous allocation can be rounded to a near-optimal discrete partition.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the fair-division reading of Theorem 27. There, \(k\) is the fixed number of recipients and the input population is the set of goods. A continuous population of recipients would change the theorem’s fixed-\(k\) regime; with a fixed supply of goods, adding recipients can also leave some with zero-valued bundles. Scaling the goods supply to avoid that problem shifts the continuum onto the resources, which is outside ChoCo’s population-first scope.

That objection does not defeat the proponent’s scheduling mirror. Treat each request as an agent whose type is its workload. Requests with the same workload are indistinguishable for the objective, and a nonatomic cohort can be divided among processors while each individual request still receives one processor. The finite-cohort rounding argument also gives this model a clear high-multiplicity interpretation. The paper itself cites scheduling as an application, so it is plausible that its authors would recognize the mirror.

The mirror’s exact continuous optimum is \(1\), since every workload type can be split equally among the processors. That makes it a weak source of new computational questions, but under the programme’s rules, a simple answer is not a reason to reject a well-posed mirror. I therefore can’t honestly defeat this anchor: the case against works only if ChoCo excludes job populations as societies. If it accepts them, Theorem 27 survives, and the negative case is weak.
