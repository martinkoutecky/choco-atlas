The case FOR (proponent)

There is a natural population lift. On the unit circle \(\mathbb T=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z\), let each type \(x_i\) be a voter’s ideal point, with rational mass \(\mu_i>0\) and \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). A choice is a point \(p\in\mathbb T\). It is a \(\beta\)-plurality point when, for every alternative \(q\in\mathbb T\),
\[ \sum_{i:\,\beta d(p,x_i)\le d(q,x_i)}\mu_i\ge \frac12. \]
This fits a high-multiplicity regime: many voters share one of relatively few ideal points, and mass is their population share. The paper’s distance-based preferences and its plurality condition carry over directly.

The closest named result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: there is a metric space—specifically, the unit circle—for which \(\beta^*=\frac12\). The three equally spaced voters give the upper bound, and the proof establishes the matching lower bound for every finite voter multiset on the circle. For rational masses, the same statement follows by multiplying through by a common denominator and treating each mass as repeated voters.

That is good evidence that the population model is sensible, but Theorem 2 is not a qualifying computational anchor under the stated criteria: it proves a geometric bound, not a complexity classification or an algorithmic result. Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 are also bounds, not complexity results; the proof’s algorithmic remark gives no runtime classification. The EPTAS and NP-hardness mentioned in related work are cited from elsewhere. So I cannot honestly claim this paper has a named computational result to anchor a ChoCo case on.

A precise follow-on problem would be Weighted \(\beta\)-Plurality Point: given rationally specified positions \(x_1,\ldots,x_\tau\in\mathbb T\), rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\), and rational \(\beta\), decide whether there exists \(p\in\mathbb T\) satisfying the inequality above for every \(q\in\mathbb T\); output such a \(p\) when one exists. Theorem 2 implies every instance has a solution at \(\beta=\frac12\), while its three-type example rules out a universal guarantee above that threshold. I would tentatively expect the fixed-circle decision problem to be tractable, but this paper does not establish that.

The soft spot is that the proposed model is very close to weighted voter counts, and rational masses can be expanded into an explicit multiset. The mirror is recognizable and mathematically faithful; what is missing is the computational result needed to make it a strong positive case for this programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the only named result the proponent anchors on, and it is a geometric extremal bound: for the circle, the worst-case value of \(\beta\) is \(1/2\). It gives no complexity classification or algorithm for an input society. Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 are bounds of the same kind; the algorithmic remark supplies no running-time result. The EPTAS mentioned in the introduction belongs to prior work. So the paper has no named computational result to continuize.

Theorem 2 does make the population interpretation plausible. For rational masses on the circle, its majority comparisons are exactly those of a finite multiset after clearing denominators, and its three-point witness remains a three-type society. But that carries over a geometric statement, not a computational one. Clearing denominators can also produce a multiset exponentially larger than the binary encoding of the masses, so it does not by itself give an efficient reduction for the proposed weighted input.

The proposed Weighted \(\beta\)-Plurality Point problem changes the question from a universal bound over societies to deciding whether a particular weighted society admits a point. Theorem 2 only settles the universal guarantee at \(\beta=1/2\), and gives one witness against a higher universal guarantee. It does not classify that input-specific decision problem. That is a reasonable follow-on research question, but it is not a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

Even that follow-on has a direct geometric route. For fixed \(p\) and \(\beta\), each voter type \(x_i\) contributes an arc of alternatives \(q\) satisfying \(d(q,x_i)<\beta d(p,x_i)\). A point \(p\) is valid exactly when no such \(q\) lies under arcs whose total weight exceeds \(1/2\). On a circle, fixed-\(p\) feasibility is a weighted arc-depth sweep; as \(p\) varies, the arc endpoints are piecewise linear, with finitely many ordering changes. This suggests an arrangement-based algorithm in the support size \(\tau\), without the continuous optimization machinery that motivates ChoCo. It does not prove the task is uninteresting, but it weakens the claim that this paper opens a substantial new computational landscape.

The negative case is strongest against a mirror of this paper: it contains no computational result to mirror, and its central bound lifts to rational populations without needing a new algorithmic idea. It is weaker against the broader claim that spatial voting admits no worthwhile continuous computational problem. A society with many voters sharing a few ideal points is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the weighted optimization problem could still be useful. The paper alone does not support ruling out every such scenario.
