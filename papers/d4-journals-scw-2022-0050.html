The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 4.1 and 4.2 give sufficient conditions for efficiency when \(j\) or \(i\) proposes first; Proposition 4.3 describes the role of strict convexity when \(k\) proposes first. They are equilibrium claims for a three-player bargaining game, with no complexity classification or algorithmic problem stated. There are therefore no eligible anchors among the results the other analyst identified.

The best candidate extension is a society with finitely many bargaining types and a value function \(V(q)\) over coalition masses \(q\). But the paper’s protocol makes an individual proposer and an individual rejecter strategically decisive: a rejection can burn the proposed coalition and make the rejecter the next proposer. In an atomless population, one person has zero mass, while the protocol gives that person pivotal power. A mass vector cannot record which person rejected or which exact coalition was burned. Treating each type as a collective proposer and rejecter resolves that mismatch by making each type a bloc player; the strategic game then has a finite set of blocs, rather than a high-multiplicity population of bargaining agents. Threshold acceptance or other aggregate rules could define a continuum game, but the paper gives no basis for choosing those rules as the authors’ intended version of BCBM.

The resulting computational questions—such as finding equilibrium payoffs or deciding whether the grand coalition forms—would thus need a new player model, coalition-value representation, and bargaining protocol. The paper’s three-player propositions do not anchor those choices. Its remarks about larger games point to the many possible coalitions, but specify no computational problem over them.

This is a strong case against a continuous *mirror of this paper*. It is weaker as a universal claim: a separately motivated continuum theory of coalition bargaining could be worthwhile. The paper itself does not make that theory a continuation of its results.
