The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your criteria. Theorem 1 proves that every bounded-step GCC protocol has an \(\epsilon\)-SPNE for every \(\epsilon>0\). Theorem 2 proves that an informed tie-breaking version can have an SPNE. Theorem 3 characterizes the NE outcomes of a constructed protocol. None classifies a computational problem by tractability or hardness.

Theorem 1 is the closest near-miss: its proof discretizes the cake and uses backward induction. But the theorem asserts equilibrium existence, not an algorithm with a stated complexity bound. Treating it as an algorithmic anchor would overstate the paper’s result.

Nor does the paper’s divisible cake supply the missing continuous-population mirror: its agents remain a finite, ordered set of named players, and its GCC protocols assign actions to those players. The cake is continuous; the society is not. I can’t give a qualifying continuous problem per anchor without changing the result or the problem’s scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that Theorems 1–3 do not classify a computational problem by complexity. But “no named computational result to mirror” is too strong: Theorem 1 is a named, constructive existence result. Its proof discretizes the cake and uses backward induction. That gives a concrete computational question for a high-multiplicity version: how hard is it to find an \(\epsilon\)-SPNE when the population is given by masses of agents sharing the same valuation type, and can the discretization depend on the number of types rather than the number of people? The paper supplies no answer to that question, but an unanswered algorithmic question is not the same as no mirror.

There is a plausible setting for asking it: a large public allocation in which many residents share valuation functions and participate through common cut or choice actions. Represent the society by a distribution over complete valuation types, and extend a symmetric GCC protocol so each type’s mass receives a per-capita allocation. Scaling the cake with population size keeps proportional shares meaningful; using type-level average allocations accommodates fractional averages. The resulting question concerns the continuous population, not the cake’s already-divisible outcome.

The hard objection is that GCC protocols assign actions to particular players at particular points in a sequential game. Grouping people by valuation does not automatically preserve those roles or the paper’s individual-deviation notion of equilibrium. The mass-action extension would need to be defined carefully, and the authors might reject it as a different strategic model. So the proponent has not established that no worthwhile mirror exists, but the case against that claim is narrow: a plausible extension of Theorem 1 yields a computational question, while preserving the original game’s incentives at high multiplicity remains uncertain.
