The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-mass weight densities \(\alpha_t>0\), a feasible current outcome represented by per-capita payoff densities \(b_t\), and prizes \(p_1\geq\cdots\geq p_k>0\), does there exist a mass vector \(z\) with \(0\leq z_t\leq\mu_t\) and an index \(j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\) such that the deviating mass has current payoff \(B(z)=\sum_t b_tz_t<p_j\) and residual weight \(R(z)=\sum_t\alpha_t(\mu_t-z_t)<jW(z)\), where \(W(z)=\sum_t\alpha_tz_t\)? Ties between residual coalitions and the deviating coalition are resolved against the deviator, so \(R(z)<jW(z)\) means the residual mass cannot form \(j\) coalitions of at least the deviator’s weight.

The model it lives in

Types carry mass \(\mu_t\), weight density \(\alpha_t\), and current payoff density \(b_t\). The decision variables are selected masses \(z_t\); for each prize index \(j\), maximize \(\epsilon\) subject to \(0\leq z_t\leq\mu_t\), \(\epsilon\leq jW(z)-R(z)\), and \(\epsilon\leq p_j-B(z)\). A positive optimum for any \(j\) certifies a beneficial deviation, giving an LP with size polynomial in \(k\), \(|T|\), and the rational input length.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6.5’s beneficial SCD decision problem. It leaves the other deviation concepts, core-existence results, and efficiency results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Formalize the type-mass SCD guarantee for all measurable partitions of the residual population and establish its precise relationship to finite high-multiplicity instances.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a mirror of Theorem 6.5, proved in this paper: \(\exists\)-SCD is NP-hard. The full problem is: given a \(k\)-WVG \(G=(N,\mathbf w,\mathbf p,\succ)\) and an outcome \(\omega=(\pi,\mathbf x)\), decide whether some coalition \(C\subseteq N\) is a beneficial single-coalition deviation—meaning that for every partition of the residual players, \(C\) wins a prize worth more than its current total payoff \(x(C)\). The paper proves this by reducing from Partition. I would mirror this result alone; it is a substantial hardness anchor, and its continuous version can be stated precisely.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large federation whose members form blocs to compete for \(k\) ranked grants, offices, or budget awards, shared among the members of each winning bloc. There may be millions of members but only a few weight classes—for example, standardized voting weights for different accreditation levels. For the stability question, types also distinguish members with different current per-capita payoffs. Thus a type describes everything this problem uses, and its mass is the fraction of the federation in that class. This gives the authors’ coalition-ranking and prize-sharing problem a natural large-population interpretation.

I would call the mirror Continuous Beneficial Single-Coalition Deviation. An instance has a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), a positive weight density \(\alpha_t\) for each type, prizes \(p_1\geq\cdots\geq p_k>0\), and a feasible current outcome with per-capita payoff \(b_t\) for each type. A feasible outcome can be represented by a measurable partition of the population into coalitions and prize shares; as in the paper’s Remark 1, the deviation test uses only the current payoffs.

The question is whether some mass vector \(z\), with \(0\leq z_t\leq\mu_t\), forms a beneficial deviating coalition. Its weight is \(W(z)=\sum_t\alpha_tz_t\), its current total payoff is \(B(z)=\sum_tb_tz_t\), and the residual population has weight \(R(z)=\sum_t\alpha_t(\mu_t-z_t)\). I use a tie-robust convention: an equal-weight residual coalition counts ahead of the deviator. Then the residual population can put at most \(\lfloor R(z)/W(z)\rfloor\) coalitions ahead of it, since mass can be divided freely. So the deviator is guaranteed at least prize \(p_j\) exactly when \(R(z)<jW(z)\). A solution is a rational mass vector \(z\) and an index \(j\) such that \(B(z)<p_j\) and \(R(z)<jW(z)\).

This mirror is tractable. For each \(j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\), test whether there are \(z_t\) and \(\epsilon>0\) satisfying \(0\leq z_t\leq\mu_t\), \(\epsilon\leq jW(z)-R(z)\), and \(\epsilon\leq p_j-B(z)\). These are linear constraints; maximizing \(\epsilon\) decides whether that prize threshold admits a deviation. The resulting algorithm is polynomial in \(k\), the support size \(\tau=|T|\), and the rational input length \(L\). The expected classification is therefore Class A: the discrete hardness can disappear when agents of the same type are divisible mass.

The connection to high multiplicity is concrete. For a discrete game with \(n_t\) members of type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), \(\alpha_t=nw_t\), and \(b_t=nx_t\), where \(w_t\) and \(x_t\) are that type’s individual weight and payoff. Choosing \(z_t=q_t/n\) recovers the weight and payoff of a discrete coalition containing \(q_t\) members of each type. The continuous problem allows any \(z_t\), which relaxes the indivisibility that makes the paper’s Partition reduction work.

I believe the authors would recognize this as their SCD question: it retains coalition weight, ranked prizes, shared payoffs, and the pessimistic response of residual players. The scope is just the beneficial-deviation problem in Theorem 6.5; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s other deviation concepts or its core-existence results.

The weakest point is that Theorem 6.5’s reduction uses individually specified weights and a carefully chosen tie-breaking order. It does not establish hardness in a regime with many members but few types. The mirror also adopts a tie-robust convention because a continuum permits equal-mass coalitions and has no natural ordering of named subsets. That is a substantive modeling choice, though it fits the paper’s pessimistic treatment of deviations. So the case is that the underlying SCD problem has a credible continuous analogue whose fractional mass structure invites an LP—not that the paper’s particular hardness proof carries over or that every tie-breaking variant becomes tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about the fit between the paper’s model and a continuous population. Its players are strategic coalition-forming units, not voters whose preferences are aggregated. In the paper’s political example, the players are parties competing for offices; replacing parties with masses of citizens changes who can form coalitions. A large federation of interchangeable members could supply a better scenario, but it needs an extra assumption: members can affiliate independently, so any fraction of a type can join a coalition. If they instead act as a bloc, the proposed mass splitting is unavailable.

The sole anchor raised is Theorem 6.5, which proves that \(\exists\)-SCD is NP-hard by reducing from Partition. That proof uses individually specified weights and payoffs, plus a tie-break rule that favors coalitions containing a particular player. Its hardness instance is therefore far from a low-support, type-symmetric population. To preserve that instance’s exact tie behavior, a continuous model would need to retain identity-sensitive information that can make the type support as large as the population.

The proposed LP avoids this obstacle by choosing a different tie convention: every residual coalition tied in weight counts ahead of the deviator. With divisible mass, the residual can form \(j\) coalitions at least as heavy as a deviator of weight \(W\) exactly when its remaining weight \(R\) satisfies \(R\geq jW\). Thus a deviation guaranteed the \(j\)-th prize must satisfy \(R<jW\), alongside \(B<p_j\), where \(B\) is the deviators’ current total payoff. These are linear conditions. That is a coherent continuous question, but its answer depends on a substantive tie rule absent from the reduction, and the tractability comes from allowing type mass to split freely.

Those points support a modest objection: the paper itself does not establish that low-support continuous instances are the natural regime for its coalition-forming players, and the exact hardness construction does not supply that motivation. They do not defeat the better mirror. A federation with many independently acting members in a few weight and payoff classes is plausible, and the LP answers its SCD question directly. The reduction’s failure to transfer is also precisely the kind of change a high-multiplicity relaxation is meant to reveal. I cannot make a strong case that this anchor has no worthwhile continuous mirror; the negative case is weak.
