The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make a compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or another complexity classification. So there is no eligible anchor, and no continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here: if \(v\) has no positive value-externalities, then for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there is a deadline \(t\) such that for every game length \(\theta\ge t\), every SPNE gives every player expected utility within \(\varepsilon\) of their Shapley value. This is an analytical convergence result as the bargaining horizon grows; it says nothing about the computational complexity of a problem. Lemma 1 and the numbered remarks are likewise analytical, not complexity results.

The most plausible population regime would be many small, interchangeable firms bargaining over mergers: a type would capture each firm’s complete bargaining-relevant characteristics, and mass would represent its share of firms. But turning the paper’s finite-player game into a continuum would require defining coalition values on type-mass vectors and specifying how pairwise bargaining works among massless agents. A computational question built on those choices could be interesting, but it would be a new model without a qualifying result in this paper to anchor it. The paper’s \(\theta\to\infty\) limit also continuizes time, which is outside the programme’s population-continuization scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

This paper has no numbered result about computational complexity. Theorem 1 is a convergence theorem: for a finite player set and a fixed cooperative game, deadline bargaining payoffs approach the Shapley value as the horizon \(\theta\) grows. Remark 1 gives an analytical counterexample, and Remarks 2 and 3 extend the convergence result. None supplies a computational problem or a complexity result to mirror. The other analyst is right to disqualify Theorem 1 as an anchor; in fact, they raised no eligible anchor.

The strongest replacement I can see is the proposed population of interchangeable firms. That is a sensible high multiplicity scenario, so I would not reject it merely because firms have identities. But making it a continuum changes the paper’s central process. In a game with \(n\) players, each period can produce at most one merger. For fixed \(\theta\), at most \(2\theta\) players are directly involved, so the affected population share vanishes as \(n\) grows. A nontrivial continuum limit would need a new scaling of the horizon or a different, mass-level bargaining process. The paper gives no result showing its convergence in \(\theta\) is uniform in \(n\), so its theorem does not settle that limit.

There is a further modeling choice: the paper allows an arbitrary value \(v(I)\) for every coalition. Replacing coalitions with type-mass vectors requires restricting value to depend on those masses, or otherwise retaining distinctions that may make the number of types grow with the population. One could study that restricted model, but then a computational question—such as checking the externality condition or computing a limiting allocation—would be newly added. It would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong for the paper-anchored task: there is no computational result here to continue, and the best firm scenario requires a new population bargaining model before a computational problem even emerges. I would not claim this proves that no worthwhile continuous-player bargaining project exists. It supports the narrower verdict that this paper offers no worthwhile *computational continuous mirror* as the programme defines one.
