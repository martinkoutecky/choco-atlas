The continuous mirror question

Given rational role masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\), direct-choice probabilities \(q_{r\ell}\), role-to-role adoption probabilities \(\rho_{rs}\), a target \(j\), a budget \(\kappa\), and a threshold \(z\), decide whether \(\max F(x)\ge z\), where \(0\le x_r\le\mu_r\), \(\sum_r x_r\le\kappa\), \(p_{rj}=q_{rj}+\sum_s\rho_{rs}\left[x_s/\mu_s+(1-x_s/\mu_s)p_{sj}\right]\), and \(F(x)=\sum_r[x_r+(\mu_r-x_r)p_{rj}]\).

The model it lives in

A society consists of roles \(r\) with masses \(\mu_r\); each role has common choice probabilities \(q_{r\ell}\) and adoption probabilities \(\rho_{rs}\). Choose ambassador masses \(x_r\) within the role masses and total budget, and maximize the target's aggregate share \(F(x)\), with ambassadors choosing \(j\) exclusively.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3's exact ambassador optimization and also poses a mass analogue of Theorem 2's approximation question; it leaves the paper's decision-share, pricing, equilibrium, and herding results alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the bit complexity of exact optimization, or of deciding whether the optimum reaches a rational threshold, as a function of the number of roles and input length?
  • Does \(F\) retain sufficient diminishing-returns structure for a polynomial-time \(1-1/e-\varepsilon\) approximation under the mass budget?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It says that choosing at most \(K\) agents as brand ambassadors to maximize a target choice’s share is NP-hard. The intervention is specific: selected agents choose the target exclusively and stop consulting the network; the other agents keep their original behavior. The paper proves hardness by reducing Vertex Cover: vertices become agents, edges determine who can influence whom, and a set of \(K\) ambassadors reaches the maximum target share exactly when it corresponds to a vertex cover. That is a good anchor because it asks whether a recognizable discrete hardness result survives when the intervention is measured in mass.

The regime I would use is a large recommendation market with a small number \(\tau\) of repeated consumer roles: for example, cohorts in an organized referral program whose members have the same baseline choice probabilities and the same probabilities of consulting each cohort. There may be millions of consumers but only dozens or hundreds of roles. A role is a complete description for this model: its members have the same direct-choice probabilities, adoption probabilities across roles, endowment, and eligibility for ambassador selection. Each member who consults a role samples a member of that role uniformly. This is a genuine high-multiplicity assumption; a market with distinctive, individually influential users may instead have \(\tau\) close to the population size.

Here is the common continuous model for the two questions I would hand to a prover. Let \(R=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\) be the roles, with positive rational masses \(\mu_r\) summing to \(1\), and let \(C\) be the finite choice set. A type-\(r\) consumer directly chooses \(\ell\in C\) with probability \(q_{r\ell}\), or adopts the choice of a uniformly sampled member of role \(s\) with probability \(\rho_{rs}\). Require
\(\sum_{\ell\in C}q_{r\ell}+\sum_{s\in R}\rho_{rs}=1\), with nonnegative probabilities and a collectively decisive baseline network. Fix target \(j\in C\). The decision variable \(x_r\in[0,\mu_r]\) is the mass of role \(r\) made into ambassadors, with \(\sum_r x_r\le\kappa\), where \(\kappa\) is the available ambassador mass. Ambassadors choose \(j\) exclusively and do not consult the network, just as in the paper.

For an ordinary, unselected member of role \(r\), the choice probabilities \(\pi_{r\ell}(x)\) satisfy
\( \pi_{r\ell}(x)=q_{r\ell}+\sum_{s\in R}\rho_{rs}\left[\frac{x_s}{\mu_s}\mathbf{1}_{\{\ell=j\}}+\left(1-\frac{x_s}{\mu_s}\right)\pi_{s\ell}(x)\right]. \)
The first term inside the sum accounts for consulting an ambassador; the second accounts for consulting an ordinary member. The target choice share to maximize is
\( F(x)=\sum_{r\in R}\left[x_r+(\mu_r-x_r)\pi_{rj}(x)\right]. \)
Thus the intervention, objective, and network feedback all remain those of the paper’s problem. Only the population and intervention budget have become continuous.

The exact continuous counterpart of Proposition 3 is Continuous Brand Ambassador Mass Optimization: given the rational type masses, probabilities, target \(j\), and budget \(\kappa\), compute a feasible \(x\) maximizing \(F(x)\) and the optimum value. Its threshold version asks, for rational \(z\), whether some feasible \(x\) has \(F(x)\ge z\). The instance and solution are specified without reference to a particular finite population size. The objective is well-defined: selecting ambassadors only reduces continuation through ordinary agents, so the decisive baseline condition continues to ensure that the relevant linear systems have solutions.

I would also anchor Theorem 2, proved in the paper. It says that the same finite-agent optimization problem admits a greedy approximation with value at least \(1-1/e\) times optimum: starting from the empty set, add on each of \(K\) rounds the agent whose addition gives the largest target choice share. The paper obtains this from monotonicity and submodularity, using the Nemhauser et al. result cited from 1978. The continuous mirror is Continuous Brand Ambassador Mass Approximation: on the same inputs, and given rational \(\varepsilon>0\), return feasible \(x\) with \(F(x)\ge(1-1/e-\varepsilon)\max_y F(y)\).

I expect the approximation question to be Class A. There is a concrete route: replicate each role into many identical consumers in a uniform block network, and independently select each consumer in role \(r\) with probability \(x_r/\mu_r\). The finite objective is monotone submodular by the paper’s Proposition 4; its randomized extension has diminishing returns, and in the large-cohort limit its value converges to \(F(x)\). That suggests continuous-greedy methods for the mass-budget version. It does not say the paper’s discrete greedy algorithm applies unchanged: the continuous mirror needs a fractional optimization method and a proof that this limiting objective retains the required structure.

For exact optimization, my provisional expectation is Class C if hardness survives: a fractional mass decision no longer directly carries the paper’s Vertex Cover reduction, so any remaining hardness would need to come from the continuous objective or the role-interaction structure. I would not claim such a reduction exists. The exact question is whether \(F\) has a tractable concave or linear reformulation, whether exact optimization is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), or whether maximizing this rational function is hard even after individuals within each role are interchangeable. The approximation question may be tractable even if exact optimization is hard.

The authors could plausibly recognize both questions as mirrors of their brand-ambassador problem. The target choice, network adoption, forced recommendation, and aggregate choice share are unchanged; the paper’s \(K\)-agent budget becomes a fraction of the population. Its choice probabilities are uncertainty about individual decisions, not a continuous society, and its discussion of continuous choice sets in §3.3 continuizes the outcome space rather than the population. Neither is a collision with this mirror.

The weak point is the role assumption. Proposition 3’s reduction uses an arbitrary graph, where vertices can have distinct neighborhoods and therefore distinct network roles. My mirror replaces that structure with cohort-to-cohort adoption probabilities and uniform sampling within cohorts. It captures large markets with repeated network positions, but it does not preserve arbitrary individualized influence or the problem of identifying exceptional hubs. That limitation is also exactly why the continuous question is worth asking: does the hardness disappear in a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and what structural complexity remains in the number of roles?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Proposition 3 and Theorem 2 are both about choosing *named vertices* in a particular network. In Proposition 3, the Vertex Cover reduction stores its information in the graph’s individual neighborhoods. To preserve an arbitrary instance in a type model, types would need to retain enough information about each agent’s interactions that the support could grow with the population. If \(\tau\) stays small, the natural replacement is a block network: agents with the same role consult each role with the same probabilities, and the decision variable selects mass from each role. That preserves aggregate targeting, but it removes the problem of finding exceptional individuals whose distinct positions make them valuable ambassadors.

That weakens the connection to the paper’s most characteristic application, but it does not defeat the mirror under the programme’s stated rules. A large market with repeated consumer roles and role-level referral patterns is plausible. The authors could recognize optimizing ambassador mass in such a market as their problem. The individual identities themselves are not an objection once agents of a type are interchangeable for everything the model uses. A fixed ambassador count could make the intervention vanish as the population grows, but replacing it with a population-scaled budget is a natural co-modification.

Theorem 2 offers little independent support for a separate mirror: it gives an approximation guarantee for the same ambassador-selection objective as Proposition 3. Its proof concerns diminishing returns from adding individual agents, so a mass version would need its own justification. Still, the proponent’s limiting argument gives a credible route: randomized selections within large roles may converge to a type-mass objective, to which continuous submodular methods could apply. If that route works, the resulting theorem may be a routine consequence of known machinery; that affects its likely novelty, but does not make the computational question unworthy.

Neither of the other strong grounds applies. The paper contains named computational results, including its NP-hardness result and approximation theorem. Its discussion of continuous choice sets does not already answer a continuous-population optimization problem.

So the negative case remains weak. The discrete hardness proof depends on individualized network structure, and the approximation theorem does not automatically survive the limit. But a role-based mass model is a sensible instance regime, and neither limitation rules out that mirror. I cannot honestly defeat Proposition 3’s proposed continuous question; the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists is not supported by this paper.
