The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s reserve-matching model. The continuous object is the population of patients or applicants, not the medical resource or the ethical principles. I would use three named structural anchors, all proved in this paper. A caveat is important: the paper contains no named \(P\), \(NP\)-hardness, W[1], or FPT theorem. Thus this is an algorithmic/formulation mirror, not yet a test of whether discrete computational hardness dissolves.

Take a finite set of complete agent types \(T\). A type records every feature used by the mechanism: eligibility for each reserve category, beneficiary status, clinical or application priority class, and tie-breaking class. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). If \(N\) is a finite population, this is the high-multiplicity normalization \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). Let \(\rho_c\) be the number of units in category \(c\) per capita, so \(N\rho_c\) is the finite capacity and \(\sum_c\rho_c=q/N\).

The decision variable is an aggregate assignment \(y_{t,c}\): the mass of type \(t\) receiving a unit from category \(c\). It satisfies \(\sum_c y_{t,c}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t y_{t,c}\le\rho_c\). This does not give an individual a fractional ventilator. Every infinitesimal agent receives either one unit or none; \(y\) records the aggregate mass receiving each outcome. Ties in a priority class can be implemented by a lottery whose aggregate result is exactly such a mass split.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1. The paper proves it here. In the paper’s finite model, a matching assigns each patient to at most one category or to \(\emptyset\). It is individually rational if nobody receives a category for which they are ineligible; non-wasteful if an unmatched eligible patient implies that the corresponding category is already full; and priority-respecting if an assigned patient has higher priority in that category than every unmatched patient. Theorem 1 states that a matching has all three properties if and only if it is induced by individual-proposing deferred acceptance for some preference profile over categories and \(\emptyset\), where a patient ranks a category above \(\emptyset\) exactly when she is eligible for it.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuum DA Reserve Characterization. Given \(T\), \(\mu\), category capacities \(\rho\), eligibility relations, category priority orders, and a proposed aggregate assignment \(\widehat y\), decide whether \(\widehat y\) is individually rational, non-wasteful, and priority-respecting. If it is, construct type-level preferences over categories and \(\emptyset\) such that aggregate deferred acceptance produces \(\widehat y\); otherwise certify that no such preference profile exists.

This is the underlying reserve-rationing problem, not a question about a DA encoding. It is a genuine population continuization of the paper’s question: the same categories, capacities, priorities, and eligibility conditions remain, while repeated agents are represented by mass. I expect this to be Class A. With finitely many types, aggregate DA can scan the priority classes and split only the class at which a capacity is reached. The paper itself does not state a polynomial-time bound, so I would not attribute one to it, but the continuous problem is plainly a finite sorting/feasibility problem when \(|T|\) is moderate.

The second anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. It assumes that every patient belongs to at most one preferential treatment category, that the problem has hard reserves, and that \(c'\) immediately precedes \(c\) in one order of precedence \(\triangleright\), while \(c\) immediately precedes \(c'\) in \(\triangleright'\), with all other relative positions unchanged. If \(I_c\) is the beneficiary group of \(c\), the theorem states that \(\varphi_{\triangleright'}(I_c)\subseteq\varphi_{\triangleright}(I_c)\) and \(\varphi_{\triangleright'}(I\setminus I_c)\supseteq\varphi_{\triangleright}(I\setminus I_c)\). Thus, moving \(c\) later weakly helps every beneficiary of \(c\), while weakly hurting everyone who is not a beneficiary.

Its continuous mirror is:

Continuum Adjacent-Precedence Comparative Statics. Given a finite type distribution \(\mu\), hard-reserve eligibility, capacities \(\rho_c\), baseline priority order, disjoint beneficiary groups, and two precedence orders differing only by swapping adjacent categories \(c'\) and \(c\), compute the two aggregate sequential-reserve assignments \(\Phi_{\triangleright}^{\infty}\) and \(\Phi_{\triangleright'}^{\infty}\). Return the beneficiary and non-beneficiary mass changes, and decide whether \(\Phi_{\triangleright'}^{\infty}\) weakly improves the matched mass of \(T_c\) and weakly reduces the matched mass of \(T\setminus T_c\).

The predicted answer is the exact mass analogue of Theorem 2: \(\Phi_{\triangleright'}^{\infty}(T_c)\le\Phi_{\triangleright}^{\infty}(T_c)\) and \(\Phi_{\triangleright'}^{\infty}(T\setminus T_c)\ge\Phi_{\triangleright}^{\infty}(T\setminus T_c)\), with the orientation understood as “\(c\) later is better for its beneficiaries.” This should again be Class A: process the categories in order and take the highest-priority remaining eligible mass. It is especially plausible for high-multiplicity visa allocation, affirmative-action admissions, or public-service hiring, where millions of applicants fall into relatively few reserve and priority types. The paper explicitly presents these as applications of the same model, so this is not an invented simplification.

The third anchor is Theorem 3, proved here. Under the same disjoint-beneficiary and hard-reserve assumptions, let \(I_g\) be the general-community patients who benefit only from the unreserved category \(u\). The theorem considers any matching satisfying individual rationality, non-wastefulness, and respect for priorities. It states that the sequential rule with the unreserved category processed first gives the worst possible outcome for \(I_g\), while a rule with the unreserved category processed last gives the best possible outcome, in the strong set-inclusion sense. In notation, writing \(\Phi^{uf}\) and \(\Phi^{ul}\) for unreserved-first and unreserved-last outcomes, the content is \(\Phi^{uf}(I_g)\subseteq \nu(I_g)\subseteq\Phi^{ul}(I_g)\) for every admissible matching \(\nu\).

The continuous problem is:

Continuum Extreme-Outcome Triage. Given \(T\), \(\mu\), hard-reserve categories, capacities \(\rho\), baseline priorities, and general-community types \(T_g\), optimize the matched general-community mass over every aggregate assignment satisfying individual rationality, non-wastefulness, and category-wise priority respect. Output the minimum and maximum possible values, together with witnessing allocation rules.

Theorem 3 predicts that the minimum is attained by unreserved-first processing and the maximum by unreserved-last processing. In the continuum, literal patient-set inclusion becomes mass dominance; if a nonatomic tie-breaking coordinate is retained, it can be represented as inclusion of measurable subsets. This is a particularly good computational mirror because it turns the paper’s qualitative distributional statement into a precise robust-optimization question: how much access can the general community guarantee, and how much can it possibly receive, across all admissible implementations? I expect this also to be Class A for fixed category orders, with further reserve-design variants potentially becoming genuinely combinatorial.

The regime is credible outside a single hospital. A regional or national pandemic clearinghouse might process hundreds of thousands of patients against a resource stock that scales with the population. Types could be combinations of clinical priority band, essential-worker status, disability status, and other policy-relevant attributes. Likewise, the paper’s hard-reserve applications—immigration visas, school admissions, or public appointments—naturally have very large applicant populations and few policy-relevant types. The authors would recognize the mirror because it preserves their mechanism exactly: reserve categories, per-capita capacities, category-specific priorities, precedence order, deferred acceptance, and the three axioms. Only the repeated population and correspondingly scaled supply are changed.

The weakest point is that the paper’s most important priority orders may be genuinely individual-specific. If every applicant has a unique arrival time, score, history, or location that affects priority, then the number of complete types can be comparable to the number of agents, and the high-multiplicity gain disappears. The medical application also uses soft reserves, whereas Theorems 2 and 3 require hard reserves; those anchors therefore rely on the paper’s equally legitimate visa, school-choice, or hiring interpretations. Finally, because the paper has no named complexity result, these mirrors establish meaningful Class A population-continuum questions but do not yet deliver the programme’s desired hardness-dissolution comparison.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection comes before the choice of type space: this paper has no qualifying computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 is a structural characterization, and Theorems 2–4 are comparative-statics and extremal-outcome statements. None asserts a complexity classification, an algorithmic running time, an approximation guarantee, or a parameterized result. The deferred-acceptance procedure is used as a proof device for characterization, not analysed as a computational problem. The proponent’s caveat effectively concedes this. Under ChoCo’s anchor rule, that is already fatal: a continuous matching model may be sensible, but it is not a continuous computational mirror of a result proved in this paper.

Theorem 1 does not provide a rescue. Its continuous version merely replaces a finite matching by aggregate variables \(y_{t,c}\), with constraints such as \(\sum_c y_{t,c}\le \mu_t\) and \(\sum_t y_{t,c}\le \rho_c\). Eligibility and priority become support and threshold conditions, and ties can be split by lottery. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity formulation, but the proposed “decision problem” is invented around the theorem: the paper does not ask one to verify an allocation or recover a witnessing preference profile. The theorem itself says exactly when such a witness exists.

A stronger version—optimizing welfare over all aggregate assignments satisfying the three axioms, or computing all DA-induced aggregate outcomes—still does not repair the problem. With finitely many complete types and categories, it is an ordinary finite-capacity matching or transportation formulation. If different members of one type are allowed different artificial category preferences, the witness is simply an additional within-type splitting device; if they are not, the proponent has imposed a new homogeneity restriction. Either way, the resulting optimization is new work, not a computational consequence of Theorem 1. If exact priority scores, arrival times, or other individual distinctions are retained, they must be included in the complete type, and the alleged multiplicity compression disappears.

Theorem 2 is no better. Its content is the set inclusion

\[ \varphi_{\triangleright'}(I_c)\subseteq \varphi_{\triangleright}(I_c), \]

together with the reverse inclusion for non-beneficiaries. The proposed continuous statement replaces sets by their masses and computes two sequential allocations. That is a measure-theoretic restatement of a comparative-static theorem, not a computational question supplied by the paper. In the finite-type version, one processes the categories in order and truncates each priority class when capacity is reached. The same proof and the same calculation survive after replacing counts by masses.

One could propose a more ambitious problem: choose the precedence order maximizing the mass assigned to a beneficiary group, or design reserve sizes robustly across several populations. Such problems might be worthwhile research. But they are reserve-design problems absent from this paper, not mirrors of Theorem 2. Adding an objective to an axiomatic comparative static does not turn it into a continuization of a computational result. If the model is enlarged with overlapping beneficiaries, dynamic arrivals, or individualized scores, it becomes a new matching-design problem whose substantive difficulty comes from those additions rather than from continuizing the population in the theorem.

Theorem 3 has the same defect in an especially transparent form. The paper already identifies the extremal rules: unreserved-first is worst for general-community patients and unreserved-last is best. The proposed continuous “optimization” over admissible aggregate assignments simply asks for the minimum and maximum of a quantity whose finite theorem already supplies both witnesses. Replacing set inclusion by mass dominance, or retaining a nonatomic tie-breaking coordinate to preserve measurable inclusion, changes the representation but creates no computational anchor. The interesting computational problem would instead be to design capacities, precedence orders, or policies under an objective; again, that is a new problem not established by Theorem 3.

The high-multiplicity regime itself is not implausible. Large visa pools, school-admission markets, and public-service hiring can have many applicants sharing policy-relevant characteristics. Existing high-multiplicity and matching work should be treated as evidence that such a regime is sensible, not as a novelty objection. But that only establishes that one could study a continuous reserve system. It does not make this paper’s structural theorems computational results, nor does it create a population-level optimization problem that the paper actually formulates.

So the negative case is not that continuous reserve matching is impossible or uninteresting. A separate paper could certainly study high-multiplicity reserve design. The point is narrower and stronger: all three proposed anchors are non-computational theorems, and their continuous versions are either direct aggregate restatements or newly invented optimization problems. No choice among medical, visa, school, or hiring scenarios supplies the missing computational contribution. Against ChoCo’s standard, there is therefore no worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
