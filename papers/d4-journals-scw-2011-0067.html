The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest anchor based case for this paper: it has no named computational result. Theorem 1, proved here as an immediate consequence of Proposition 2, says that an EF game satisfying its condition has a TF representation with any chosen status quo outcome. Propositions 1 and 2 concern when representations coincide and how to construct one. These are results about representation, not about a computational problem’s complexity or algorithms.

The appendix gives a finite algorithm for deriving an EF representation from a TF game, but it is unnumbered and makes no complexity claim. So there is no qualifying anchor, and no continuous problem or tractability forecast I can tie to one. A large population model of rights might be imaginable, but it would be a new question rather than a mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no anchor, so there is no named computational result for a continuous mirror to preserve. Theorem 1 and Propositions 1 and 2 concern representations of games and how to construct them; they do not state the complexity of a computational problem. The appendix’s finite algorithm for deriving an EF representation has no numbered complexity result either. That leaves no algorithmic question from this paper to mirror.

The strongest candidate for a new one might take the mineowner example and ask whether freedom of worship survives when workers are represented by population shares. But the paper’s central distinction is about a particular person retaining power after acting in a coalition. In a continuous population, an individual has zero mass; tracking that person requires retaining identity, which defeats the high-multiplicity aggregation. Treating a whole type as the acting unit instead changes the object from individual rights to rights of blocs. And because powers are specified for overlapping coalitions, type masses alone do not say which members can act together across successive transitions.

One could build a computational theory of rights for mass populations, with new rules for coalitions and individual protections. That might be worthwhile as a separate project. It would supply its own computational problems, though; it would not mirror a computational result in this paper. The negative case is strongest on that distinction: the paper offers a rich formal framework for rights, but no computational anchor for ChoCo’s continuous-population programme.
