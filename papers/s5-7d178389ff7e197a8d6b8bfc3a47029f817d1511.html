What the mirror covers

The proposal targets Proposition 2's implementability criterion, already present in the paper; it does not cover the other theoretical results or the empirical estimation and state-level analysis.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the implementability decision for the optimal seat–vote curve. The named anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper’s Appendix. It says that, in the authors’ model, the socially optimal seat–vote curve is implementable by a feasible districting if and only if
\[ \pi_I\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2\tau}+\epsilon-(\tau+\epsilon)\ln\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\tau}\right)\right)\leq \min\{\pi_D,\pi_R\}. \]
Here \(\pi_D,\pi_R,\pi_I\) are the population shares of Democrats, Republicans, and Independents; \(\tau\) describes the spread of Independent ideologies; and \(\epsilon\) describes aggregate uncertainty across elections. The target curve comes from Proposition 1: \(S^o(V)=\frac12+(\pi_D-\pi_R)(\frac12-\tau)+2\tau(V-\frac12)\). Proposition 2 is an exact yes-or-no criterion, though it does not itself claim membership in a standard complexity class.

A precise mirror is Continuous Optimal Seat–Vote Implementability. An instance gives binary-rational \(\pi_D,\pi_R,\pi_I,\epsilon,\tau\), with the paper’s parameter restrictions. The society has unit mass: Democratic and Republican masses have fixed ideologies \(0\) and \(1\); the Independent cohort has the paper’s uniform ideological distribution, shifted between elections by the uncertain median \(m\). A decision variable is a measurable assignment of these masses across a continuum of equal-sized districts: functions \(d(z),i(z),r(z)\geq 0\) for \(z\in[0,1]\), summing to one in each district and integrating to \(\pi_D,\pi_I,\pi_R\) statewide. The question is whether some such assignment induces the optimal curve \(S^o(V)\) for every feasible statewide vote share \(V\). A yes-solution is a districting assignment witnessing that equality; a no-solution reports that none exists. Proposition 2 supplies the exact criterion for deciding yes or no.

The scenario is a large electorate, such as state or national legislative elections: millions of voters, with a small number of broad electoral cohorts and many voters in each cohort. The paper itself treats D, R, and I as its three voter categories, so it offers unusually direct evidence that the authors would recognize this population-level question. There is a qualification under ChoCo’s strict type definition: Independents have heterogeneous realized ideologies. To fit a finite-type model, one must treat “Independent” as a shared stochastic response profile, or retain ideology as a continuum of subtypes with a compact parametric density. That is a real mismatch, not just terminology.

The likely classification is Class A for this parametric problem: the paper reduces feasibility to evaluating an explicit expression. But it does not establish a polynomial-time bound in the binary input length, especially for exact comparisons near the boundary. A formal bit-complexity analysis would be part of the computational mirror. Follow-up questions include constructing a districting witness efficiently, bounding the error when approximating it with finitely many districts, and determining how geographic constraints change feasibility.

The scope is narrow: implementability of the optimal curve, not the paper’s empirical estimation pipeline or every version of constrained districting. The soft spot is also clear: the paper already makes the population and district allocation continuous, and Proposition 2 already answers the underlying feasibility question mathematically. So the case for ChoCo is that its computational formulation and complexity analysis would be new; the case is not that this paper has yet to continuize districting. There are no named results here asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, or parameterized complexity. Proposition 2 is the best anchor because it gives an exact computational criterion, but the authors do not present it as a complexity-theoretic result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a genuine collision: Proposition 2 already asks and answers the continuous-population feasibility question proposed as the mirror. The paper contains no result about worst-case complexity on encoded instances. Proposition 1 characterizes an optimal seat–vote curve; Proposition 2 characterizes when a districting can implement it.

The population is already represented by aggregate masses. Section II.A describes each district by fractions of Democrats, Republicans, and Independents, with statewide averages fixed at the population shares. The appendix makes this explicit: a districting is a function on \([0,1]\), and feasibility is expressed through integrals. Proposition 2 asks whether such an allocation of population mass induces the optimal curve. Rewriting that question using a distribution \(\mu\) and mass-assignment functions gives the same model and the same feasibility question. The proponent’s own concession—that the paper already makes both the population and its allocation continuous—identifies the collision.

A finite-electorate version could be made by specifying integer voter counts and integer assignments to a finite number of districts. Studying that version’s computational complexity might be useful, but it would run from a discrete model toward a continuous one that this paper already supplies. It does not uncover a missing continuous formulation of the paper’s result.

The type issue reinforces the mismatch. Democrats and Republicans have fixed ideologies, but Independents range over a continuous ideology distribution and respond to an uncertain shift. Treating all Independents as one complete type loses distinctions the model uses; representing their ideologies as subtypes requires a continuous type distribution. That is a reasonable extension of ChoCo’s finite-support template, but it leaves the proposed mirror essentially identical to the paper’s existing population model.

A geographic version could pose a worthwhile new question by adding spatial voter distributions and contiguity constraints. The paper explicitly abstracts from those constraints, so that would change the feasible set and require a new model. It would be a research extension motivated by this paper, not a continuous mirror of Proposition 2.

So the negative case is strong for the anchor the proponent raised: its best version is already the paper’s central continuous feasibility problem. I would not extend that conclusion to every adjacent districting project, especially geographic ones; those may be worthwhile, but they are not missing continuizations of this result.
