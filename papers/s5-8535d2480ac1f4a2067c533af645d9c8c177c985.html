The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a credible population version of the paper’s remaining-admission reallocation. The setting already has roughly 10,000 applicants, four schools, and score-based quotas; the paper says about 59% of the 2021 admission plan was remaining admission. A natural type is \(t=(g,r,\succ)\): exact exam score \(g\), the school \(r\) assigned by the initial lottery, and the student’s strict ranking \(\succ\) of the four schools. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the share of applicants with that complete profile. In a district with many applicants per score-and-preference profile, the support can be much smaller than the population. That is a plausible regime for this problem, and the paper’s references to controlled school choice and type quotas support the model’s relevance.

A concrete candidate is Quota-Preserving Continuum DA. An instance gives the rational masses \(\mu_t\) and the score-by-school quotas \(q_{g,s}=\sum_{t:g(t)=g,\ r(t)=s}\mu_t\) induced by the initial lottery. The decision variables are assigned masses \(x_{t,s}\). Require \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\), \(x_{t,s}=0\) unless \(s=r\) or \(t\) ranks \(s\) above \(r\), and \(\sum_{t:g(t)=g}x_{t,s}=q_{g,s}\). The question is to compute the allocation produced by student-proposing DA on these masses, with each school applying the paper’s score-specific quotas and earlier proposal rounds taking priority. At a tied cutoff, the instance must specify how an equal-priority mass is split. The solution is the resulting allocation vector \(x\); there is no separate numerical objective because the paper proposes a mechanism, not an optimization criterion. I would expect this finite-type, four-school version to be tractable by aggregating proposals and rejections across at most four rounds. Further questions include whether the aggregate outcome agrees with discrete DA on replicated populations, and whether strategy-proofness survives the chosen rule for splitting tied mass.

There are zero qualifying named anchors in the paper. Sections 4.2–5 make unnumbered claims that the proposed mechanism is feasible, preserves each score group’s count at each school, and has DA properties including individual rationality, non-wastefulness, elimination of justified envy, and strategy-proofness. The paper attributes some DA properties to references [1]–[4]. It gives no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result; the bracketed numbers are bibliography citations. So Quota-Preserving Continuum DA is a prospective mirror of the paper’s mechanism, not a continuous problem anchored to a named result in it.

The mirror’s scope is just the reallocation of remaining-admission students. Its weakest point is the priority rule: the paper’s “apply earlier” priority and its handling of simultaneous applications are underspecified. If real submission times distinguish individual students, they may need to enter the type, swelling the support and weakening the high-multiplicity case. The aggregate version is persuasive when application rounds and tie handling are part of a shared, finite procedure; the paper itself does not establish that this version preserves its claimed properties.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no paper results for a continuous mirror to anchor to: the proponent also counts zero. The paper proposes using deferred acceptance and states its feasibility and familiar matching properties, but it does not formulate a computational problem or give a named result about its complexity. Its stated obstacle is students finding exchange partners and the cost of running a platform. Those are implementation concerns, not a complexity question the paper investigates.

The proposed Quota-Preserving Continuum DA is the strongest candidate, and a population with many students sharing a score, initial school, and preference ranking is plausible. That makes the candidate a coherent *new* computational question, but it does not supply the missing paper-result anchor. There is also a fidelity tradeoff: the paper gives priority to students who apply earlier. Modeling those individual submission times preserves that rule but can make timing part of each student’s type; splitting equal-priority mass instead defines a different priority procedure. In a nonatomic model, an individual’s deviation has zero mass, so the paper’s strategy-proofness claim cannot simply be carried over as an individual guarantee.

Continuum school-choice models already provide an analytic framework for stable matching between finitely many schools and a continuum of students. Azevedo and Leshno, for example, characterize stable matchings through supply-and-demand equations. That is not a collision under ChoCo’s standard—this literature does not thereby answer a computational-complexity question—but it reinforces that the proposed mirror would be a new complexity study of a known matching model, not a computational result drawn from this paper. ([Azevedo and Leshno, “A Supply and Demand Framework for Two-Sided Matching Markets”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdf/10.1086/687476?download=true))

This is the strongest negative case, not proof that no worthwhile version exists. A mass-based admission problem is plausible, and the paper’s cited controlled-choice literature supports that regime. The weakness is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror; its best candidate must be added from outside its stated contribution.
