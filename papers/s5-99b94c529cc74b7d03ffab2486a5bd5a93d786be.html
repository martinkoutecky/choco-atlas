The continuous mirror question

Given rational cohort-location masses μ_{ij} ≥ 0 for three cohorts and q ordered cells, with ∑_j μ_{ij} = 1/3 for each cohort i, reveal the cells from left to right. Require each normalized cell mass 3μ_{ij} ≤ ε^2/(100 log 3); a deterministic online policy may cut only between cells and must assign each completed consecutive block immediately to one of two districts. What is the minimum worst-case number of cuts needed to ensure that, for every cohort i and district d, the normalized share 3∑_{j assigned to d} μ_{ij} lies in [1/2-ε/2, 1/2+ε/2]?

The model it lives in

The types are cohort-location pairs {i,j} with population masses μ_{ij}, each cohort totaling 1/3. A deterministic online policy chooses cut boundaries and district labels for consecutive blocks; each cell's mass is assigned together. Minimize the worst-case cut count subject to every district receiving between 1/2-ε/2 and 1/2+ε/2 of each cohort's population.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 5.3's three-measure online lower bound and Theorem 1.2's online upper bound, giving Θ(1/ε^2) cuts for three cohorts; it leaves offline results, necklace bounds, and variants with more cohorts aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can randomized online policies achieve o(1/ε^2) worst-case cuts for three cohorts?
  • What are the optimal offline cut bounds and their dependence on the number of cohorts?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is online balancing of demographic populations across two districts. Take three equal-sized voter cohorts spread along an ordered region. A type is a cohort and a location cell; its mass is the fraction of the whole population in that cohort and cell. If cohort \(i\) has fraction \(p_{ij}\) of its members in cell \(j\), the society has type masses \(p_{ij}/3\). The number of residents \(N\) can be much larger than the number of types \(\tau\le 3q\), with many interchangeable residents in each type. Give the cells in order, revealing their cohort masses as they arrive. A deterministic rule may cut only at legal cell boundaries and must assign each completed interval to a district immediately.

The task is to minimize cuts while ensuring that each district receives between \(1/2-\epsilon/2\) and \(1/2+\epsilon/2\) of every cohort’s mass. Equivalently, the difference between the districts’ shares of each cohort is at most \(\epsilon\). Use rational cell masses and an \(\epsilon\)-appropriate fine grid as in the paper’s online model. A solution is the online policy’s finite cut list and district assignments; the central question is the minimum worst-case number of cuts a deterministic policy needs. This is high multiplicity: a finite count-based electorate with many residents per cohort-location type becomes a rational distribution over those types. The quantities being balanced are residents, not divisible election outcomes.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5.3, proved in this paper. It says that for Online \(\epsilon\)-Consensus Halving with three measures, an adversarial input forces every deterministic algorithm to make \(\Omega(1/\epsilon^2)\) cuts to obtain a proper solution. In the districting interpretation, the measures are the three cohorts’ location distributions, and “proper” means the required cohort balance. This is a lower bound on online cut complexity, not a claim of NP-hardness or a running-time lower bound.

The matching algorithmic anchor is Theorem 1.2, also proved here. It gives an efficient deterministic online algorithm for \(\epsilon\)-Consensus Halving that produces a proper solution using \(O(n\log n/\epsilon^2)\) cuts. With three cohorts, this is \(O(1/\epsilon^2)\). Together, the anchors make the expected answer for this mirror quite sharp: the search task is tractable, and its deterministic online cut complexity is \(\Theta(1/\epsilon^2)\) for three cohorts. In the paper’s oracle model the algorithm is efficient; with the finite rational cell table, the corresponding question is whether it runs in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\epsilon\).

This is a credible analogue of the authors’ problem because it preserves their actual allocation question: an ordered mass distribution must be split among recipients while balancing each measure, with irrevocable online decisions. The scenario changes what the measures represent—from valuations to population cohorts—but not the mathematical task. The paper’s discrete Necklace Halving results also make the ordered, typed mass interpretation natural.

The weak point is that the paper already formulates the problem over non-atomic measures. If its measures are read as population distributions, this is a direct application of its continuous model, not a new mathematical continuization. The contribution of the mirror is the high-multiplicity population interpretation and its finite-type dictionary; the named results already settle the online cut question in the paper’s oracle setting. Also, the lower bound concerns the number of cuts under online information. It does not show that the continuous population version is computationally hard in the usual complexity-class sense. Further questions include whether randomized policies can beat the deterministic cut bound, how the result transfers to finite rational type tables with explicit running-time bounds, and how the bounds change offline or with more cohorts.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest proposed mirror is already the problem the paper studies. The “three demographic cohorts along an ordered region” reading gives each cohort a measure on the interval, then asks for cuts and assignments that balance those measures. That is \(\epsilon\)-Consensus Halving in the paper’s own model, with the measures renamed as cohorts. The demographic story may make the population interpretation vivid, but it does not create a new continuous computational question.

That disposes of both anchors together. Theorem 5.3’s \(\Omega(1/\epsilon^2)\) lower bound and Theorem 1.2’s \(O(n\log n/\epsilon^2)\) algorithm apply to the proposed online balancing task as stated. With three cohorts, they give the claimed matching order of cut complexity. A finite rational table of cohort masses over ordered cells would make the oracle explicit: interval masses are prefix sums, and the paper’s permitted online grid already specifies where cuts may occur. That looks like an input representation for the existing problem, not a new mirror awaiting a complexity landscape.

The best attempt to make the mirror distinct is to insist on ChoCo’s finite-support, high-multiplicity formulation and ask for running time in support size and bit length. But for an explicit table of rational cell masses, the paper’s oracle operations are directly implementable from that table. The lower bound is about the online number of cuts, not conventional running-time hardness; it does not become a new complexity result just by expressing its measures as finite rational data. Adding voter preferences, strategic actions, or election outcomes might create a different ChoCo problem, but then these two theorems no longer answer it.

There is a real boundary here: consensus splitting is already a continuous computational treatment, while Necklace Splitting is its discrete counterpart. The paper itself studies the relation between them. So a demographic high-multiplicity interpretation is plausible, but it mostly renames an existing continuous model and its results. The negative case is strongest for these anchors; it cannot rule out every new population problem one might build from the paper’s ideas, but the proposed mirror does not justify spending the programme’s effort.
