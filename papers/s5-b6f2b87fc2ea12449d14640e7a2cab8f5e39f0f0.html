The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a natural high-volume school-placement extension, but this paper has no named computational result to anchor it. Its named results concern mechanism properties; none establishes a complexity bound, algorithm, or hardness result. So I can describe a plausible follow-up, but I cannot honestly present it as a qualifying ChoCo mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The closest footholds are:

  • Theorem 2, proved here: group strategy-proofness implies group non-bossiness. Group strategy-proofness means that no coalition can jointly misreport so that every member weakly prefers the resulting assignment and at least one member strictly prefers it.
  • Corollary 2, derived here using Theorem 2 and Ergin (2002): the agent-proposing deferred acceptance mechanism is group non-bossy if and only if the objects’ priority structure is acyclic. Ergin’s cited result supplies the relevant characterization of group strategy-proofness and non-bossiness; the paper uses it to obtain the group non-bossiness conclusion.
  • Corollary 1, derived here from known group strategy-proofness of TTC and Theorem 2: TTC is group non-bossy.
  • Theorem 1, proved here: group non-bossiness is strictly stronger than non-bossiness. The paper demonstrates this with a mechanism for three agents and three objects that is non-bossy but not group non-bossy.

A candidate follow-up, Nonatomic DA Group-Non-Bossiness, would model a district with many applicants but relatively few complete types. A type would include a strict ranking of schools and a priority category at each school. The instance would give rational type masses \(\mu_t\), normalized school capacities \(q_a\), and an explicitly specified measurable deferred-acceptance rule, including how priority ties within a type are handled. Every individual still receives one indivisible school place; the aggregate assignment to a type may split its mass across schools.

The question would be whether there is a true profile, a measurable coalition \(S\), and a joint report by \(S\) such that deferred acceptance leaves almost every coalition member with the same school while changing the assignment of positive mass outside \(S\). A witness would consist of the coalition’s type masses, its redistribution among reports, and the before-and-after allocations. A plausible regime is a large centralized placement market where many applicants share rankings and standardized priority categories, so the population is large relative to the number \(\tau\) of types.

The natural conjecture, suggested by Corollary 2, is that this nonatomic version is group non-bossy exactly when the priority structure is acyclic; that would make the global characterization tractable by checking acyclicity. This is the best lead, but it remains a conjecture about a new model, not a result of the paper. It covers DA’s group non-bossiness characterization only; it does not mirror the whole paper.

The weak point is fundamental: the paper studies an axiomatic property of mechanisms, not the computational complexity of deciding that property. To turn the follow-up into a computational problem, one must specify a finite representation for the mechanism and settle how ties among a continuum of same-type applicants are resolved. Those choices could change the question. Thus the population regime is credible, but the paper itself does not provide the computational anchor ChoCo asks for.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 gives a counterexample about two axioms; Theorem 2 proves an implication between axioms; Corollary 1 applies it to TTC; and Corollary 2 characterizes DA through an existing priority condition. None studies the complexity of a problem whose input is a society. That is the gap a mirror would need to fill.

The school-placement setting is plausible for high multiplicity: many applicants may share rankings and priority categories. The difficulty is that group non-bossiness is a property of a mechanism across *all* preference profiles and coalitions. A distribution \(\mu\) describes one profile. Asking whether a coalition at that profile can leave its own assignment unchanged while changing positive mass outside the coalition is a sensible new question, but it is profile-specific; it does not mirror the paper’s mechanism-wide claim. Keep the universal quantifiers, and \(\mu\) is no longer the input being analyzed. That mismatch survives even in the best population regime.

For Theorem 2, a natural mass version may be easy to formulate: count a change only if it affects positive mass, and count a coalition deviation only if a positive mass of its members strictly benefit. With those conventions, the proof appears to carry over: if outsiders gain on positive mass, include some of them in the coalition; if none gain, reverse the report for the whole population. That could be a useful extension of an axiom, but it supplies no complexity question or algorithmic landscape.

Corollary 2 is the strongest candidate, and priority categories make its population setting credible. But the finite result concerns whether DA has a mechanism property across profiles, not how a given society can be manipulated or controlled. A nonatomic version would also need a rule for assigning mass within tied priority categories. Allowing type-level fractional assignments is a reasonable co-modification, but it defines a new DA model; even if the acyclicity characterization carries over, the result would still be an axiomatic characterization, not a computational mirror of a result in this paper.

Corollary 1 has the same problem: the result follows from group strategy-proofness, while a continuum TTC model needs its own account of endowments and mass exchange. Grant a natural model and the group non-bossiness result may follow as an axiom. Theorem 1 is further from a computational anchor: its three-agent construction witnesses a gap between properties. Asking whether that gap persists for nonatomic mechanisms would be an axiomatic existence question, not a complexity result.

So I would argue against a ChoCo mirror of this paper, while conceding the limit of that case: nonatomic school assignment itself is a credible modeling regime. The objection is that the paper’s results concern mechanism properties, whereas making a society continuous does not turn those results into computational questions. A new computational study of profile-specific coalition deviations might be worthwhile, but it would be a new project rather than a continuous mirror of these results.
