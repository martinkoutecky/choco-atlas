The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), positive rational claims \(v_t\), and a rational supply \(\kappa\) of positions per recipient, compute an allocation maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\ge0}x_{t,j}v_t/(2j+1)\), subject to \(0\le x_{t,j}\le\mu_t\), \(x_{t,j+1}\le x_{t,j}\), and \(\sum_{t,j}x_{t,j}=\kappa\). Here \(x_{t,j}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) recipients receiving at least \(j+1\) positions, so each recipient receives an integral bundle; return an optimal allocation compactly, including any mass split at a tied cutoff.

The model it lives in

A distribution over recipient types, each with a claim \(v_t\) and mass \(\mu_t\); cumulative allocation variables \(x_{t,j}\) describe the mass receiving at least \(j+1\) indivisible positions. The objective selects the highest Sainte-Laguë priorities subject to per-capita supply \(\kappa\), allowing a tied cutoff to split type mass.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 3’s arithmetic-sequence selection underlying Sainte-Laguë, with rational type masses replacing unit multiplicities. It leaves the near-arithmetic extension in Theorem 4 and other rule-specific results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can exact weighted selection be solved in time polynomial in the number of types \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\)?
  • How should exact boundary ties be handled, and does the result extend to other arithmetic or near-arithmetic divisor rules?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a high-multiplicity version of highest-averages apportionment. Imagine a large network of local service districts receiving indivisible staff positions according to workload or population claims. The network has \(N\) districts but only \(\tau\) distinct claim levels and rule-relevant attributes, with \(N\gg\tau\). A type records everything the apportionment rule uses for a district; its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of districts of that type. As \(N\) grows, let the number of positions grow proportionally too, so the per-district supply \(\kappa=k/N\) stays fixed. The paper itself names manpower planning as an application of apportionment, which makes this a plausible regime for its problem.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: given \(n\) arithmetic sequences \(A(j)=x_A+y_Aj\), with \(y_A>0\), the value of the \(k\)-th smallest element in their disjoint union can be found in \(O(n)\) time, independently of \(k\). In the paper’s apportionment reduction, each entity’s sequence is \(d_j/v_i\), where \(v_i\) is its claim and \(d_j\) the divisor. When the divisors are arithmetic—as in Sainte-Laguë, \(d_j=2j+1\)—the theorem gives a linear-time way to find the priority cutoff that determines the allocation.

I would ask the authors to consider Continuous Sainte-Laguë Apportionment. Its input is a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), positive rational claims \(v_t\), and a rational supply \(\kappa\) of positions per district. For each type \(t\) and \(j\ge0\), let \(x_{t,j}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) districts receiving their \((j+1)\)-st position. Find a feasible allocation maximizing

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\ge0} x_{t,j}\frac{v_t}{2j+1} \]

subject to \(0\le x_{t,j}\le\mu_t\), \(x_{t,j+1}\le x_{t,j}\), and \(\sum_{t,j}x_{t,j}=\kappa\). The output can be given compactly: for each type, specify the number of positions fully assigned per district and the mass receiving one additional position, with any ties at the cutoff split across the relevant types. Each individual district still receives an integer number of positions; fractional quantities arise only in the aggregate type masses.

I expect this question to be Class A. Its instance is a weighted union of only \(\tau\) arithmetic priority sequences, so a weighted selection and rank-inversion algorithm may find the cutoff without expanding the \(N\) districts or iterating through all \(k\) positions. The paper’s theorem supplies the algorithmic shape, but does not itself settle the weighted, rational-mass version. A prover would need to establish an exact algorithm and its bit complexity, ideally polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). Natural follow-up questions include how to handle exact boundary ties, whether the same result extends to other arithmetic or near-arithmetic divisor rules, and how the continuous allocation relates to finite instances with rational type counts.

This mirrors the paper’s underlying apportionment problem: the same claim scores, divisor priorities, and integral positions per district determine the result. It changes the input regime to a distribution over repeated district types, rather than replacing apportionment with a fractional allocation rule. It covers the arithmetic-divisor algorithmic result, not every highest-averages method in the paper. The limitation is that the paper’s entities are parties or subdivisions, not ordinary voters, and typical legislatures have few such entities. The case is strongest for large-scale manpower or service allocation, where many districts share a small number of claim types. Theorem 3 is the paper’s relevant named result; it offers no comparable hardness anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that the paper’s \(n\) counts the entities receiving seats, not the voters whose totals determine their claims. In the paper’s ordinary settings—parties or named administrative subdivisions—there are often few entities, and their legal roles or eligibility rules can make them genuinely distinct. Making the voters continuous while keeping the recipients finite leaves the algorithm’s main input dimension unchanged. Treating the recipients themselves as the population is a different, less familiar regime.

That objection does not defeat the better version of the mirror, though. Many service districts or comparable units could share the same claim and rule-relevant attributes. Their masses can represent repeated recipients, while the monotonic \(x_{t,j}\) constraints describe distributions over integral seat bundles. Splitting mass at a tied cutoff is coherent for identical units. This is a real high-multiplicity question, and Theorem 3 is a named computational result that gives it a clear anchor. The theorem handles unweighted sequences; it does not settle exact weighted selection from rational masses or its bit complexity.

The mirror’s likely payoff looks narrower than the proponent suggests: it compresses repeated recipients and turns the cutoff into a weighted selection problem. The paper already removes dependence on the number of seats, and the population-based extension does not obviously open a broader optimization landscape. But that is a concern about research leverage, not a fundamental objection. A worthwhile continuous mirror need not uncover a new kind of algorithmic structure, and the programme explicitly values high-multiplicity compression.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The case against is strongest for ordinary party or legislative apportionment, where the recipient population is small or institutionally heterogeneous; it weakens substantially for the paper’s own manpower-planning application. Theorem 3 survives the challenge, and the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror is not supportable here.
