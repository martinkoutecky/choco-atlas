The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor audit is empty: this paper has no numbered result asserting a computational complexity classification such as polynomial time, NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. Its propositions are about the dynamics and their limiting behavior. So I can’t honestly provide a continuous problem *per computational anchor*.

The nearest analytical results are still good evidence that a population mirror is natural. Proposition 2, proved in the paper, says that if some adjacent pair of agents ever has opinion distance greater than \(\sqrt{V/[f(1-f)]}\), every later network is disconnected. Proposition 5, also proved there, says that with uniformly distributed initial opinions and \(n\) sufficiently large, \(V>f(4-3f)/16\) guarantees convergence of all opinions to \(1/2\). Proposition 6 says that under the same uniform-population regime, \(V<f/16\) leads to a disconnected limiting network with at least two components; its proof uses a continuum approximation. These are analytical claims, not complexity results.

The strongest positive case is that the population-continuous object is unusually close to something the authors already study. Their Section 5 takes a uniform distribution of opinions and lets the number of agents tend to infinity to derive long-run predictions from the initial network diameter. A plausible high-multiplicity scenario is a large online deliberation forum: many participants fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of opinion cohorts, share the same flexibility \(f\) and connection benefit \(V\), and each chooses peers from a substantial fraction of the population. The type is a participant’s current opinion; its mass is the fraction of the forum holding it. Opinions and the discrete-time update remain as in the paper—the population is what becomes continuous.

A concrete computational extension would be Mass Opinion Limit Classification. Its input is rational \(f\in(0,1)\), \(V\geq0\), and an initial distribution \(\mu_0=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}p_i\delta_{x_i}\), where the distinct \(x_i\in[0,1]\) and masses \(p_i>0\) are binary rationals summing to one. At each step, every agent in opinion cohort \(i\) chooses the same vector \(a_{ij}\in[0,p_j]\), representing the mass of cohort \(j\) that the agent links to. Writing \(q_i=\sum_j a_{ij}\), \(\bar x_i=\sum_j a_{ij}x_j/q_i\), and \(\sigma_i^2=\sum_j a_{ij}(x_j-\bar x_i)^2/q_i\), the cohort chooses a maximizing vector for
\[ q_i\bigl[V-f(1-f)(\bar x_i-x_i)^2-f\sigma_i^2\bigr]. \]
For \(q_i=0\), set \(\bar x_i=x_i\) and \(\sigma_i^2=0\). This is the paper’s equilibrium payoff normalized by population size, with links represented as mass. Then set \(x_i'=(1-f)x_i+f\bar x_i\), move mass \(p_i\) to \(x_i'\), and merge cohorts with equal opinions. To make the process deterministic, ties between maximizing mass vectors are broken lexicographically by opinion order.

The question is: does the resulting sequence of distributions converge weakly, and, if so, is its limit a point mass (consensus) or non-point-mass (persistent disagreement)? A solution returns one of those three classifications. The complexity question is to determine worst-case running time in \(\tau\) and total input bit length \(L\).

I would investigate Class A first for this restricted model, but only as a conjecture. The paper’s ordered-configuration lemma suggests that optimal peer sets are intervals in opinion order, giving few candidate neighborhoods per cohort in each period; its long-run results also rely on components that split irreversibly. The missing algorithmic result is a polynomial bound for the transient and an exact way to certify the limit. If that fails, continuum-specific hardness is possible; this paper provides no basis for claiming hardness transfers from a discrete problem. Follow-up questions include whether the classification agrees with a large finite cohort model and how long temporary polarization can last.

The soft spot is substantial: this is an algorithmic version of the authors’ analytical question, not a complexity result they prove, and they may regard it as a new agenda rather than the continuous analogue they would prioritize. There is also a modeling choice in making every member of a cohort use the same mass-level linking strategy. The case nevertheless covers a focused part of the paper—long-run consensus and disagreement, especially Propositions 2, 5, and 6—without claiming to mirror its convergence-speed results or its full dynamic analysis.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

All three anchors fail the programme’s computational gate. Proposition 2 gives a sufficient condition for a trajectory to become disconnected. Propositions 5 and 6 give parameter conditions for consensus or persistent disagreement in the uniform-population regime. None states a computational problem or result: there is no algorithm, running-time bound, approximation guarantee, or hardness claim to continuize. These are analytical results about a dynamic system.

The proposed Mass Opinion Limit Classification is the strongest possible rescue, and its population model is plausible. A large forum with many people sharing an opinion and common parameters is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The paper’s own large-\(n\) analysis, including the continuous approximation used for Proposition 6, supports that judgment. I would not count either fact against the mirror.

The difficulty is that the proposed classification remains an analytical question about the limit of a mean-field opinion dynamic. Giving its input rational masses and asking for a running time adds a computational layer, but does not make Propositions 2, 5, or 6 computational results. The exact asymptotic classification also needs a finite decision procedure or certificate to make its runtime concrete: one-period choices optimize over real-valued mass vectors, and the requested output depends on an infinite trajectory. A finite-horizon approximation could be specified, but that would pose a new algorithmic task rather than mirror the stated asymptotic claims.

Other computational formulations are possible. One could ask for the optimal next-period linking choices, or add a planner who seeks the cheapest campaign that prevents disconnection. Those questions use the model’s population structure, but the first introduces an algorithmic task absent from the results, and the second adds an intervention the paper does not study. Either might become worthwhile research; neither rescues these anchors as computational mirrors.

So the strongest negative case is about fit with ChoCo’s stated gap, not about the plausibility of continuous populations or the value of opinion-dynamics research. The paper offers no computational result to mirror, and the proposed classification repackages its central analytical question. The universal claim that no algorithmic follow-up could ever be worthwhile is harder to defend: a carefully specified computational extension may interest the authors. The narrower case against treating this paper as a direct ChoCo mirror is much stronger.
