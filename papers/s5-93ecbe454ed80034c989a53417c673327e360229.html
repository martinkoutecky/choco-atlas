What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror covers nearest-case retrieval from the case base, but neither Proposition 1 nor Proposition 2 is a computational result; the sampling algorithm is attributed to prior work.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a plausible case for the *setting*, but this paper has no numbered computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror.

The nearest named results are Proposition 1, which says the probabilistic distance on weak orders over a finite outcome set is a metric with range \([0,1]\), and Proposition 2, which gives the same metric claim for preference orders over prospects. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper mentions that counting linear extensions is \(\#P\)-complete, but attributes that result to Brightwell and Winkler [1]; its appendix likewise describes the Bubley–Dyer sampling algorithm as prior work [2]. Neither appears as a numbered result of this paper.

There is a natural population scenario behind the paper’s motivation: a large recommender service could have many users but relatively few recurring preference structures. A type would be a user’s complete preference structure, and its mass the fraction of users with that structure. Given a new user’s partial preferences, one could return the supported type closest under one of the paper’s distance measures, using it as a default. That is recognizably related to the authors’ case-based elicitation problem. But the paper gives no named complexity result for this retrieval task, and the mass does not affect which type is closest except by determining which types occur.

So I cannot provide a valid continuous problem *per anchor* under the requested standard. The positive case is plausible as a model extension, but its weakest point is decisive here: it has no eligible named result from the paper whose computational behavior a continuous mirror could test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 establish that the probabilistic distance is a metric. Putting a population distribution over preference structures does not turn either statement into a complexity question. The paper discusses computational techniques, but the \(\#P\)-completeness of counting linear extensions and the Bubley–Dyer sampling algorithm it relies on are prior results, not results proved here.

The recommender-service scenario is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But its direct retrieval task is \(\arg\min_{t:\mu_t>0} d(p,t)\), where \(p\) is the new user’s partial preference structure. The masses do not affect the answer: duplicate cases with the same preference structure collapse to one supported type. The computational work remains the paper’s per-pair distance calculation, whose difficulty depends on the alternatives and partial orders, not on the number of users. The paper gives no complexity result about retrieval as the population grows.

A stronger population-level proposal could choose a representative preference structure by minimizing expected distance, such as \(\sum_t \mu_t d(p,t)\), or select a population medoid. Those are coherent new tasks, but they change the paper’s objective from finding the closest existing case to optimizing a population default. Their computational questions would need to be established independently; they do not mirror one of this paper’s named results. A distribution over supported cases may be useful in a recommender system, but here it does not expose a population-dependent complexity landscape.

I accept that the paper’s case base could contain many users of the same type; high multiplicity is not itself an objection. The negative case is narrower: that population does not enter the paper’s computational claims in a way that yields an anchored continuous mirror. I cannot rule out worthwhile future work on aggregate preference retrieval, but it would be a new problem rather than a continuization of a computational result in this paper.
