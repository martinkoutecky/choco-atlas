The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no eligible computational-complexity anchor under the rule in the prompt. Its numbered results concern equilibrium characterization, convergence rates, graph bounds, and random-graph degree—not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The final discussion of leader selection is a heuristic, not a numbered complexity result.

The closest result is Lemma 3, proved in this paper. It says that with at least one stubborn agent, the dynamics converge to a unique equilibrium. Each agent’s equilibrium opinion is a convex combination of the stubborn agents’ initial opinions, with coefficients given by hitting probabilities in an augmented random walk: partially stubborn agents get auxiliary vertices joined with edge weight equal to their stubbornness; fully stubborn agents remain boundary vertices; the walk stops on first reaching one of those boundary vertices. This is a useful motivation for a mirror, but it does not assert a computational classification, so I cannot count it as an anchor.

A plausible *non-anchored* candidate is Continuous Block-Opinion Equilibrium. Let the population have types \(r\in T\), with mass \(\mu_r\), initial opinion \(a_r\), per-capita stubbornness \(\kappa_r\), and symmetric interaction rates \(w_{rs}\) between types. Types represent agents with the same complete profile, including their interaction rates with every type. A natural regime is a very large dense network with a fixed or moderate number \(\tau=|T|\) of such blocks. Scaling stubbornness with population size preserves its force relative to the growing number of interactions.

Each type chooses an opinion \(z_r\in[0,1]\) to minimize its per-capita cost
\[ J_r(z_r,z_{-r})= \frac12\sum_{s\in T}\mu_s w_{rs}(z_r-z_s)^2+ \frac12\kappa_r(z_r-a_r)^2. \]
Given rational \((\mu,w,\kappa,a)\), with connected interactions and positive stubbornness for at least one type, the problem is to compute the unique equilibrium vector \(z\) satisfying
\[ \left(\sum_s\mu_s w_{rs}+\kappa_r\right)z_r -\sum_s\mu_s w_{rs}z_s =\kappa_r a_r \quad\text{for every }r\in T. \]
The solution is the type-level opinions; it can be computed exactly by solving a rational linear system, in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. This preserves the paper’s local quadratic incentives, stubbornness, and equilibrium question while making the population distribution the continuous object. I would expect this mirror to be tractable.

Its weakest point is that the block interaction matrix supplies a strong structural assumption: the paper’s results depend on the topology and bottlenecks of an individual network, while this model compresses those details into interaction rates. It is plausible for large stochastic block networks, but does not represent arbitrary networks or their topology-sensitive convergence results. And, crucially, even this plausible mirror has no eligible named anchor in the paper. Under the requested anchor standard, the honest positive case therefore has no anchor-defined question to submit.

That candidate is a sensible high-multiplicity model for a large block network, provided each type captures agents with the same interaction pattern. But computing its equilibrium is just solving a rational linear system, as in the finite model. The block assumption compresses the network into \(\tau\) interaction rates; it does not turn one of the paper’s results into a computational question. A more faithful model could retain network structure through a graphon or interaction kernel and ask about equilibrium, convergence, or continuous leader placement. That may be a worthwhile new project. But it introduces a new input representation and, for leader placement, a new optimization problem drawn from the concluding discussion—not a computational result of this paper. With a rich enough kernel, the difficulty may depend chiefly on how that kernel is encoded.
