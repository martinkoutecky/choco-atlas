The continuous mirror question

Given finite student and laboratory types with rational masses, student capacities one, laboratory capacities \(q_\ell\), strict student rankings, weak laboratory rankings with ties, and preference-consistent weights \(w_\ell(s)\), find a mass assignment \(x_{s\ell}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_\ell x_{s\ell}=\mu_s\) and \(\sum_s x_{s\ell}=q_\ell\nu_\ell\), stable in the sense that for every pair \((s,\ell)\), either \(D_{s\ell}(x)=0\) or \(E_{\ell s}(x)=0\), that minimizes \(\max_\ell U_\ell(x)-\min_\ell U_\ell(x)\), where \(U_\ell(x)=\sum_s w_\ell(s)x_{s\ell}/(q_\ell\nu_\ell)\), \(D_{s\ell}(x)=\sum_{\ell':\,\ell\succ_s\ell'}x_{s\ell'}\), and \(E_{\ell s}(x)=\sum_{s':\,s\succ_\ell s'}x_{s'\ell}\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of student and laboratory types carries rational mass. The decision variables \(x_{s\ell}\) assign mass to type pairs while each unit receives one partner and laboratory capacity scales with its type mass; fairness compares each laboratory type's mean utility and minimizes the range of those means over weakly stable assignments.

The objection that survived

The objective measures the range of type-average utilities, not the range across individual agents, so a cohort split among different integral bundles can have equal means while its members receive unequal utilities; the programme permits type averages, but this still bounds how directly the mirror captures the paper's individual-fairness objective.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 19's difference objective in the tied-preference regime; it does not address Theorem 16's strict-preference algorithm or the paper's other results.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of minimizing the utility gap over weakly stable mass assignments in this balanced cohort model?
  • Can the weak-stability complementarity constraints be handled by a tractable formulation for this objective?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for the paper’s tied-preference hardness result, because its reduction exposes exactly where a continuous population can change the answer.

The anchor is Theorem 19, proved in this paper by reduction from Partition. It states that the difference version of Stable Matching with Minimum Utility Gap is NP-hard when ties are allowed. The paper’s construction establishes this even for many-to-one matching with all pairs acceptable, ties only in laboratories’ preference lists, \(A=L\), and average-value utility functions. The hardness comes from choosing, for each \(i\), which of two students goes to the global laboratory: those binary choices encode a subset summing to \(T/2\).

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large placement market with many students in each credential and preference cohort, and many laboratory positions of each laboratory type. Members of a type share their complete preferences, capacity, and value parameters. The item-specific values \(a_i\) can describe how a laboratory values each credential cohort. This is a stylized but recognizable market: the number of agents can be large relative to the number of distinct cohorts, even as the number of cohorts grows.

I would ask about the following continuous analogue, Minimum Utility Gap for Stable Mass Matchings with Tied Preferences. An instance has finite student types \(S\) and laboratory types \(L\), rational masses \(\mu_s\) and \(\nu_\ell\), student capacities one, integer laboratory capacities \(q_\ell\), strict student rankings, weak laboratory rankings that may have ties, and rational weights \(w_\ell(s)\) consistent with laboratory preferences. Assume a balanced, fully matched market:
\[ \sum_{s\in S}\mu_s=\sum_{\ell\in L}q_\ell\nu_\ell. \]
A solution is a nonnegative mass assignment \(x_{s\ell}\) with
\[ \sum_{\ell\in L}x_{s\ell}=\mu_s \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{s\in S}x_{s\ell}=q_\ell\nu_\ell. \]
It represents a measure of individual matches: each student still receives one partner and each laboratory agent receives an integral bundle of \(q_\ell\) partners, though members of a cohort can be assigned differently.

Use weak stability: for every pair \((s,\ell)\), it cannot be the case both that positive mass of type \(s\) is assigned below \(\ell\) and that positive mass of \(\ell\)’s slots is filled by students below \(s\). Formally, require
\[ D_{s\ell}(x)=0\quad\text{or}\quad E_{\ell s}(x)=0, \]
where
\[ D_{s\ell}(x)=\sum_{\ell':\,\ell\succ_s\ell'}x_{s\ell'}, \qquad E_{\ell s}(x)=\sum_{s':\,s\succ_\ell s'}x_{s'\ell}. \]
For each laboratory type, compare its mean per-agent utility,
\[ U_\ell(x)=\frac{\sum_s w_\ell(s)x_{s\ell}}{q_\ell\nu_\ell}. \]
The task is to find a stable \(x\) minimizing
\[ \max_{\ell\in L} U_\ell(x)-\min_{\ell\in L}U_\ell(x). \]
The output is the optimal mass assignment. The means are a deliberate co-modification: individuals retain integral bundles, while fairness is compared across homogeneous cohorts.

The reduction in Theorem 19 gives a concrete check on this mirror. Take \(K\) replicas of each of its types, so the population has \(K(3m+1)\) agents and support size \(\tau=3m+1\). In the continuous market, let \(y_i\in[0,1]\) be the fraction of student type \(s_i\) sent to the global laboratory; send the complementary fraction of \(t_i\) there. The local laboratory type still has utility \(B+T/2\), and the global type has utility \(B+\sum_i a_i y_i\). Choosing \(y_i=1/2\) for every \(i\) makes the gap zero for every Partition instance. The binary subset choice that proves Theorem 19’s hardness has disappeared.

My expectation is therefore Class A for this mirror, at least in the full-capacity cohort setting: assignments are divisible and the gap objective is linear once its upper and lower endpoints are fixed. But the stability condition above contains a complementarity, so establishing an efficient algorithm for the general problem is a real open question; the simple fractional argument settles the hardness construction, not the whole class of markets. Further questions include whether weak stability has a tractable mass formulation, whether an exact discrete-to-continuous rounding or approximation dictionary holds, and how the answer changes if fairness is measured across individual utilities rather than cohort means.

The weakest point is that the objective compares cohort-average utilities, whereas the paper compares utilities of individual agents. The paper’s own average-value functions and the high-multiplicity interpretation make cohort means plausible, and the programme permits this kind of co-modification, but the authors may judge it a material change to their fairness question. This case covers Theorem 19’s difference objective and tied, many-to-one regime; it does not claim a mirror for Theorem 16’s strict-preference algorithm or the paper’s other results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the evidence offered for Theorem 19’s mirror. The paper measures the utility of each individual laboratory and minimizes the range of those utilities. The proposed model assigns one utility to each laboratory *type*: its average across all laboratories in that cohort. The choice \(y_i=1/2\) equalizes those averages. It says nothing about the utility range among the individual laboratories whose bundles make up the average. A positive-mass cohort can contain different integral bundles and different utilities.

A closer mirror would keep a distribution over integral partner bundles and minimize the essential maximum-minus-minimum utility across agents in the population. Under that objective, splitting each student type in half does not establish a zero gap: every positive-mass bundle still contributes to the range. The Partition gadget’s subset-sum structure could reappear at the level of individual bundles. The submitted case therefore shows that a cohort-mean version relaxes away its reduction; it does not settle the continuous version of the paper’s individual fairness question. Proving exactly what happens after replicating the gadget into large cohorts would require new work.

That distinction raises a real authorship question: would the authors recognize fairness across cohort means as their problem? The programme explicitly allows type-level averages as a co-modification, so this is not a scope veto. It does make the proposed mirror’s fit less secure than the case suggests. Its stability condition is a plausible positive-mass notion, and the market scenario is credible. The repaired individual-utility version also looks well-posed and could be worth studying.

So I cannot make a convincing universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The proponent has not established the claimed tractability class for the general mass-matching problem, and their Partition calculation supports only the cohort-mean variant. Those are meaningful limits on the case for this particular mirror; they do not defeat every reasonable mirror of the paper.
