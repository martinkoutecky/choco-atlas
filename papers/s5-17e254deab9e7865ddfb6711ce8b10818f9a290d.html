What the mirror covers

The proposed anchors cover implementability with learning, known-distribution implementation, and delayed payments; no computational result among them yields a population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant case for a computational continuous mirror: the paper has no numbered result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class.

The closest result is Theorem 5, proved here. It gives a necessary and sufficient condition for the first-best dynamic allocation to be implementable under learning: at each stage, the set of current types assigned each object must be an interval. It also gives a sufficient derivative condition. That is an implementability characterization, not a computational result.

Theorem 2 combines Derman, Lieberman, and Ross’s 1972 characterization of the efficient policy with the paper’s result that this policy remains implementable when the type distribution is known. Proposition 6 shows that delaying payments until all arrivals have occurred permits implementation of the first-best policy. Neither is a complexity result either.

The paper already uses a distribution \(F\), but as uncertainty about the value of each of finitely many sequential arrivals—not as a society represented by masses of types. A continuous-population model might be worth formulating, but without a qualifying computational anchor, I can’t supply the requested precise problem-per-anchor case without overstating what this paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper has no computational result for one to mirror. Its nearest results answer mechanism-design questions about implementability and efficient allocation; they give no complexity classification, algorithmic running time, or computational problem with a finite input representation. That is a substantial gap for a programme whose target is computational complexity.

The proponent’s three candidates do not clear that bar. Theorem 5 characterizes when the first-best policy is implementable by checking whether type sets are intervals, and supplies a sufficient derivative condition. Those are mathematical conditions on cutoff functions and beliefs, not an algorithm or complexity result. Theorem 2 establishes implementability when the distribution is known. Proposition 6 establishes implementability when payments can be delayed. Each is an existence or characterization result.

One could pose a stronger new question: given a finite description of a prior and the arrival process, can we compute the efficient policy or decide whether it is implementable? But that requires choosing an encoding and restricting the paper’s general distributions and cutoff functions. It would be a computational extension of the mechanism-design model, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The population reinterpretation also strains the paper’s core information structure. Its \(F\) is a sampling distribution for the values of finitely many agents; it is not the society’s distribution of type masses. The identity of the arriving agent is interchangeable, but the arrival stage is not: the available inventory and future options change over time. Treating stage as part of a complete type leaves essentially one agent per stage. Grouping agents into same-stage cohorts creates multiplicity, but requires changing the one-agent-at-a-time allocation and incentive problem.

A fluid version has a further difficulty. If the designer is given the mass distribution of values, the uncertainty about that distribution that drives the paper’s learning results has disappeared. Under a standard fluid interpretation of many independent observations, the observed mass distribution can also reveal the sampling law at once, removing gradual learning. Preserving the paper’s learning effect therefore calls for retaining finitely informative sequential signals, or adding an observation process that keeps aggregate data noisy. Either choice introduces a new primitive.

A cohort-based dynamic assignment model could still be worth studying. The negative case is weaker if “mirror” includes any such new model. But as a mirror of this paper’s results, the case is strong: the paper establishes no computational result, and the most natural population continuizations alter or eliminate the sequential learning mechanism those results concern.
