The case FOR (proponent)

Under the required anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor: none of its numbered theorems or propositions says that a problem is hard, tractable, fixed parameter tractable, or gives a computational formulation that the paper uses to compute an instance. The introduction’s NP-hardness statements concern results cited from elsewhere; they are not numbered results of this paper.

The closest foothold is Theorem 11, proved here. It says that in a game with a nonempty core, if the grand coalition is vital, the coalitions that are both essential and dually essential form a characterization set for the nucleolus: maximizing the lexicographically ordered satisfactions for just that family gives the same nucleolus as using all nontrivial coalitions. That is a useful structural reduction for computation, but it gives no complexity bound, so I would not present it as a qualifying anchor.

The strongest plausible mirror around that result is a high-multiplicity nucleolus problem. Consider a large cooperative organization with a small number \(\tau\) of complete member types—each type fixes the member’s contribution technology, costs, and outside option. A type has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and the organization’s worth for a coalition depends on its type-mass vector. This is credible for a large cooperative, purchasing consortium, or labor organization with many interchangeable members and relatively few contribution profiles.

A precise candidate is: the input is rational \(\mu\), together with a rational piecewise-linear worth function \(V\) on \([0,\mu]\), where \(V(0)=0\). For each \(q\) that makes every \(q\mu_t\) integral, form a finite game with \(q\mu_t\) members of type \(t\). A coalition \(S\) has mass \(z_t(S)=|S\cap N_{q,t}|/q\) and worth \(qV(z(S))\). Promise that every such game has a nonempty core and a vital grand coalition. Members of the same type then receive the same nucleolus payoff \(x_t^{(q)}\). The continuous question is to decide whether \(x^{(q)}\) converges as \(q\) grows through these multiples and, if it does, compute its limit to any requested rational precision; otherwise, report nonconvergence.

This is a genuine population mirror: \(q\) grows while the type support stays small, and coalition worth and payoffs scale with population size. The authors could plausibly recognize it as their nucleolus problem in a high-multiplicity regime. Theorem 11 applies at each finite \(q\), making \(E\cap DE\) a possible route to computation. The mirror covers only nucleolus computation in balanced games with a vital grand coalition, not the paper’s whole characterization-set theory.

My provisional bet for this *newly defined limit problem* is Class C: convergence and exact computation introduce continuum-specific questions that Theorem 11 does not settle. That is only a hypothesis; I see no basis in the paper for claiming either hardness or tractability. The route toward Class A would be a polynomial separation procedure for the relevant characterization coalitions, together with a convergence result.

The weakest point is the limit definition itself. The paper orders finitely many coalition satisfactions lexicographically; it supplies neither a canonical lexicographic order over continuum many coalitions nor a theorem that the replicated nucleoli converge. Defining the mirror through finite-population limits avoids inventing such an order, but adds a substantive question absent from the paper. The first questions for a prover would be whether the limit always exists under the stated promises, whether Theorem 11’s reductions survive the limit, and whether the needed coalition search has a polynomial-size certificate or separation oracle.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this as a mirror of this paper is that Theorem 11 is not a computational result. It proves that, under the vital-grand-coalition condition, \(E(\Gamma)\cap DE(\Gamma)\) characterizes the nucleolus. It gives no complexity bound, algorithm, or efficient way to find that set. The paper’s computational claims are largely in its introduction and citations to other work. So the proposed limit problem does not continuize a computational answer established here; it uses a structural theorem as a possible tool for a new problem.

There is a more substantive obstacle in the proposed model. A finite game’s nucleolus lexicographically orders the satisfactions of its coalitions, counting every subset. A continuum of type-mass vectors has no corresponding finite list to sort. One could define a new continuum objective using, for example, satisfaction levels or a measure over coalition masses, but those choices are additional modeling decisions. The proposed \(q\)-sequence preserves the finite ordering, but its coalition counts also depend on how many subsets realize each mass vector—\(\prod_t {q\mu_t \choose qz_t}\)—information not contained in the mass vector alone. As \(q\) grows, those counts can affect tie-breaking. Thus the sequence defines a specific asymptotic problem; it does not by itself establish a canonical continuous nucleolus or a continuation of Theorem 11.

That does not defeat the best version of the mirror. Large organizations with many interchangeable members and a small number of complete member types are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and studying whether their replicated-game nucleoli converge could be worthwhile. Theorem 11 could inform that study at each finite \(q\). But convergence, its rate, and the connection to a continuous characterization are new questions, not results of this paper. The honest negative case is therefore narrow: this paper offers a weak computational anchor for the proposed mirror. It does not support the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
