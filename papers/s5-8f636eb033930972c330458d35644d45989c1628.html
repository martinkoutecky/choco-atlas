The continuous mirror question

Given a finite metric type set \(T\), rational mass vector \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^{T}\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), finite candidate set \(C\), distances \(d\), and integer \(k\), compute an integral set \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|\le k\) such that for every \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k\}\), radius \(y\ge 0\), and submass \(0\le\lambda_t\le\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\lambda_t\ge\ell/k\), writing \(S_\lambda=\{t:\lambda_t>0\}\), if \(\left|\{c\in C:d(t,c)\le y\text{ for all }t\in S_\lambda\}\right|\ge\ell\), then \(\left|\{w\in W:\exists t\in S_\lambda,\ d(t,w)\le y\}\right|\ge\ell\).

The model it lives in

A finite-type high-multiplicity metric-clustering model with society mass \(\mu\), integral center set \(W\), and no optimization objective beyond feasibility. Expanding approvals uses aggregate budgets \(b_t=k\mu_t\), with type mass splittable proportionally when a selected candidate consumes one unit of budget.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the finite-candidate \(\mathrm{mJR}\) and \(\mathrm{mPJR}\) selection results and their \((1+\sqrt{2})\)-proportional-fairness, individual-fairness, and transferable-core consequences in Theorem 5. It leaves unbounded candidate geometry, sortition-specific equal-probability sampling, and the appendix's stronger \(q\)-extensions untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does aggregate expanding approvals compute \(\mathrm{mPJR}\) in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|C|\), \(k\), and the encoding length of \(\mu\)?

  • What is the complexity of exact \(\mathrm{mPJR}\) when the candidate space is implicit or unbounded?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a finite-candidate, high-multiplicity version of the paper’s metric proportionality problems. It is a genuine continuization of the population, although the paper has no numbered NP-hardness or explicit “in P” theorem. The closest computational anchors are Propositions 6 and 7, together with the surrounding statements that the corresponding finite-candidate algorithms run in polynomial time. Both propositions themselves are proved in this paper; the algorithms originate in earlier work cited by the authors.

Take a finite metric space containing a finite type set \(T\) and a finite candidate set \(C\). A type \(t\in T\) is a resident location, or more generally a complete distance profile to all candidates and other types. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{T}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The integer \(k\) is the number of centers that may be selected. The decision variable remains an integral set \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|\le k\); population mass is continuous, but centers are not fractional.

This is a plausible regime for, say, a city with \(10^6\) residents but only \(\tau=300\) resident-location or demand-profile types and a few hundred possible clinics. Residents sharing a type are indistinguishable for the clustering problem. More generally, all attributes affecting distances, eligibility, or fairness must be included in the type, exactly as required by the high-multiplicity interpretation.

For rational \(\mu_t=a_t/N\), the finite election or clustering instance with \(a_t\) agents of type \(t\) reproduces the same normalized problem. Conversely, aggregating identical agents gives \(\mu\). Thus the model is not outcome-space fractionalization: it is the intended compression of a large population into mass types.

My lead mirror is Continuous Metric-PJR Selection, anchored in Proposition 7:

“Any outcome returned by expanding approvals satisfies \(\mathrm{mPJR}\).”

This proposition is proved in the paper. The underlying expanding-approvals algorithm is cited from earlier work, but the metric proposition is new to this paper.

For a coalition submass \(\lambda\), where \(0\le\lambda_t\le\mu_t\), let \(S_\lambda=\{t:\lambda_t>0\}\). Define \(I_y(S_\lambda)=\{c\in C:d(t,c)\le y\text{ for every }t\in S_\lambda\}\) and \(U_y(S_\lambda)=\{c\in C:d(t,c)\le y\text{ for some }t\in S_\lambda\}\).

The continuous problem is:

Continuous Metric-PJR Selection\(_\infty\). Given \((T,\mu)\), \((C,d)\), and \(k\), find \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|\le k\) such that, for every \(\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}\), every radius \(y\), and every submass \(\lambda\) with \(\sum_t\lambda_t\ge \ell/k\), if \(|I_y(S_\lambda)|\ge\ell\), then \(|W\cap U_y(S_\lambda)|\ge\ell\).

This is exactly the paper’s \(\mathrm{mPJR}\) condition with cardinalities replaced by population mass. The objective is feasibility: return any committee of at most \(k\) centers satisfying the condition.

The continuous analogue of expanding approvals is especially natural. Give type \(t\) aggregate budget \(b_t=k\mu_t\). Increase \(y\) through the finitely many distances \(d(t,c)\). Whenever an unselected candidate \(c\) has \(\sum_{t:d(t,c)\le y}b_t\ge1\), select \(c\) and subtract exactly one unit of budget from those types, allowing a type’s budget to be split. Since the total budget is \(k\), at most \(k\) candidates are selected. With finite \(T\) and \(C\), this is a polynomial-size mass algorithm: its events are drawn from at most \(|T||C|\) distance thresholds, and all arithmetic is rational.

I would expect this problem to be Class A in the finite-candidate regime. The paper’s budget proof should carry over by replacing sums of individual budgets with sums of type masses. It is not merely a tractable weakening: the selected set remains integral, and the proportionality constraints still quantify over arbitrary coalitions of mass \(\ell/k\).

The second mirror is Continuous Metric-JR Clustering\(_\infty\), anchored in Proposition 6:

“Any outcome returned by greedy capture satisfies \(\mathrm{mJR}\).”

Again, the proposition is proved here, while greedy capture is attributed to earlier work.

The continuous problem asks for \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|\le k\), such that every coalition of mass at least \(1/k\) that jointly approves some candidate at radius \(y\) contains at least one type having a selected center within radius \(y\). Equivalently, for every \(c\in C\) and radius \(y\),

\[ \sum_{t:\,d(t,c)\le y,\ d(t,W)>y}\mu_t<\frac1k. \]

This formulation is particularly clean: no group of at least one \(k\)-th of the population can jointly support an unchosen candidate while being entirely unrepresented at that same radius.

The continuous greedy-capture procedure maintains residual active mass. At radius \(y\), if some candidate has at least \(1/k\) active mass within distance \(y\), it is opened; exactly \(1/k\) active mass is charged to that capture, and all remaining mass within distance \(y\) of the selected center is removed from consideration. A type may be split in the bookkeeping, but the chosen centers remain an ordinary integral set \(W\).

This is also expected to be Class A for explicit finite \(C\). The proof of Proposition 6 becomes almost immediate: if a violating coalition existed at radius \(y\), all of its mass would still be active, so its common candidate would have an active ball of mass at least \(1/k\), contradicting termination at that radius.

The paper’s Theorem 5 explains why these are meaningful computational mirrors rather than merely weighted restatements. It proves that every outcome satisfying \(\mathrm{mJR}\) satisfies \((1+\sqrt2)\)-proportional fairness, \(2\)-individual fairness when the agents are eligible candidates, and belongs to the \((\gamma,2\gamma/(\gamma-1))\)-transferable core for every \(\gamma>1\). The continuous counterpart replaces the discrete coalition sums by integrals against \(\lambda\), and replaces the \(n/k\)-nearest-neighbour radius by the smallest radius containing mass \(1/k\). The same triangle-inequality and mass-counting arguments should survive unchanged.

Thus one continuous run would simultaneously answer several population-level fairness questions: no \(1/k\)-mass coalition can obtain a sufficiently better center, every type receives a center within twice its \(1/k\)-mass neighbourhood radius, and large coalitions cannot improve their average distance beyond the stated transferable-core factor.

The scope is deliberately limited to the paper’s finite-candidate metric-clustering results: Propositions 6 and 7, with Theorem 5 as the principal downstream guarantee. I would not force a mirror for the sortition results, where “each individual is selected with equal probability” becomes awkward under a continuum of candidate individuals. Nor would I claim that the unbounded Euclidean candidate-space discussion is already solved by continuization. The paper itself notes, citing MiSh20’s Theorem 12, that computing greedy capture can become NP-hard when the candidate space is unbounded; its Theorem 11 instead gives constant-factor guarantees by restricting attention to agent locations. That is a useful boundary question for the continuous programme.

My weakest point is the anchor issue. This paper contains no named hardness result and no numbered theorem explicitly classifying one of these problems as polynomial-time. Its strongest computational evidence is the prose statement that finite-candidate greedy capture and expanding approvals are polynomial-time, together with the correctness Propositions 6 and 7. If the programme requires the numbered anchor itself to say “in P,” this paper does not qualify formally.

Even under that strict reading, the mirror is substantively good: it preserves integral centers, metric distances, coalition structure, and proportional quotas while replacing a large population of repeated agents by a mass distribution. The expected result is tractability in the compressed parameters \(\tau\), \(|C|\), \(k\), and the input bit length of \(\mu\). The resulting questions are whether the same constants remain tight for mass societies, whether exact \(\mathrm{mPJR}_\infty\) remains tractable with implicit or unbounded candidates, and whether the finite-population and continuous versions admit quantitative rounding guarantees.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is an anchor problem. Propositions 6 and 7 do not state computational-complexity results. Proposition 6 says that an outcome returned by greedy capture satisfies \(\mathrm{mJR}\); Proposition 7 says the analogous thing for expanding approvals and \(\mathrm{mPJR}\). The polynomial-time claims appear only in surrounding prose, and the algorithms are attributed to earlier work. The paper therefore contains no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary asserting that a computational problem is in \(\mathrm{P}\), is hard, or admits a particular algorithmic complexity. Under a strict programme gate, neither proposition is a qualifying computational anchor.

The best reconstruction of Proposition 6 would ask for a finite-type, finite-candidate mass society and seek \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|\le k\), satisfying

\[ \sum_{t:\,d(t,c)\le y,\ d(t,W)>y}\mu_t<\frac{1}{k} \]

for every candidate \(c\) and radius \(y\). But this is only the weighted implementation of the existing greedy-capture procedure. Sort the finitely many distances, maintain residual type mass, and open a candidate whenever its active mass reaches \(1/k\). Type mass can be split in the bookkeeping, exactly as individual budgets are already fractional in the finite algorithm. The proof of Proposition 6 transfers verbatim.

The same objection is even stronger for Proposition 7. Replacing each individual budget \(k/n\) by a type budget \(k\mu_t\) is an aggregation of the algorithm already described in the paper. With finite \(T\) and \(C\), there are only finitely many distance events, and the mass version is an immediate weighted scan. There is no new optimization problem, separation problem, objective, or complexity boundary. The continuous population contributes compressed input representation, but no computational structure beyond weighted counting.

Allowing an unbounded candidate space does not rescue these anchors. The difficulty identified in the paper comes from searching the candidate geometry, not from the population representation. Replacing unit agents by masses leaves that geometric subproblem intact; restricting attention to agent locations, as in Theorem 11, is likewise a candidate-side workaround. This would be a legitimate result about the interaction between population weights and candidate-space geometry, but it is not a computational consequence of either Proposition 6 or Proposition 7.

That is the entire serious negative case. The usual stronger objections do not work. A city with many residents sharing finitely many distance profiles is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; mass is meaningful, identities are irrelevant to these anonymous axioms, and the continuum does not degenerate. Existing high-multiplicity work would support rather than undermine the model.

Consequently, I cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. If the surrounding polynomial-time statements are accepted as computational anchors, then the finite-type versions of either proposition are genuine, well-posed continuous-population problems, and Proposition 7 in particular survives comfortably. The negative verdict is defensible only under the strict formal rule that this paper supplies no qualifying named computational result; substantively, the proposed mirror is weakly novel but valid.
