  • Theorem 2 — survived: The endpoint lottery and its welfare guarantee extend to rational type masses using the proposed finite-replication exchange model.

The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(d>0\), a finite set \(X\subseteq[0,d]\), and a unit-mass society \(\mu\) over types \((x,t)\in X\times\{L,H\}\) with rational masses and public location marginal \(\nu_x=\mu_{x,L}+\mu_{x,H}\), does there exist a universally truthful mechanism that maps compatible reported type-mass profiles to a distribution over facility locations \(y\in[0,d]\) and guarantees expected post-exchange welfare at least half the maximum over all \(y\) and feasible mass assignments of the houses? Define post-exchange assignment by finite replication of the rational masses, running TTC, and aggregating its outcome; use \(u(y,z,L)=d-|z-y|\) and \(u(y,z,H)=|z-y|\).

The model it lives in

Types are location-preference pairs \((x,t)\) carrying rational mass; the location marginal \(\nu\) is public. The decision is a distribution over facility locations, followed by a TTC exchange of houses defined through finite replication. The objective is expected total mass-weighted welfare relative to the best feasible facility location and mass assignment.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's \(2\)-approximation in individual exchanges with two preference types on a line segment. It leaves the central-exchange result, the nontruthfulness result for the optimal-location mechanism, and settings with other preferences or exchange rules untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a universally truthful mechanism achieve an approximation factor better than \(2\) for every such rational-mass society?
  • Can aggregate TTC outcomes be computed from the support and binary mass encodings without explicitly replicating all agents?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror I see is the paper’s individual-exchanges facility-location result. I would anchor it only on Theorem 2, proved in this paper: on a line segment \([0,d]\), the mechanism that places the facility at \(0\) with probability \(1/2\) and at \(d\) with probability \(1/2\) is universally truthful and achieves a 2-approximation to optimal social welfare when agents exchange locations by TTC. The paper gives no named hardness result, so this is an approximation-mechanism anchor rather than a complexity classification.

Call the continuous version Mass-Exchange Facility Location. An instance has a rational \(d>0\), finitely many public locations \(X\subseteq[0,d]\), and a unit-mass society \(\mu\) over types \((x,t)\in X\times\{L,H\}\). Each \(\mu_{x,t}\) is a binary rational; \(x\) is an agent’s initial location, and \(t\) says whether the agent prefers to be close to or far from the facility. The type is complete for this problem: agents at the same location with the same preference are interchangeable. Let \(\nu_x=\mu_{x,L}+\mu_{x,H}\) be the mass of houses at \(x\).

A mechanism maps reported type-mass profiles, with public location marginal \(\nu\), to a distribution over facility locations \(y\in[0,d]\). After \(y\) is chosen, a mass-TTC exchange assigns each type’s mass to locations while preserving both the type masses and the supply \(\nu\). For rational masses, define this by finite replication: choose a common denominator, clone each type accordingly, run TTC, and aggregate the resulting assignment. Utility is \(u(y,z,L)=d-|z-y|\) and \(u(y,z,H)=|z-y|\), where \(z\) is the assigned location. The benchmark is the maximum total welfare over \(y\) and all feasible mass assignments of houses to agents.

The question is whether there is a universally truthful mechanism whose expected post-exchange welfare is at least half this benchmark for every such society. A solution is a mechanism defined on all compatible report profiles, together with a proof of its incentive and approximation guarantees. The continuous analogue of Theorem 2 answers yes: use the same fair lottery over the two endpoints.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for a large housing or facility-siting programme along a corridor: many residents occupy a relatively small set of public sites, and residents at a site share one of two preferences about proximity to the facility. Then the population size can be much larger than the support size \(\tau\), with \(\tau\leq 2|X|\). The mass assignment is the continuous decision variable, and the objective is average social welfare.

The guarantee carries over directly. At either endpoint, TTC gives each agent a location they weakly prefer to their initial one, so exchange cannot reduce welfare. For every type at location \(x\), the sum of its utilities when the facility is at \(0\) and at \(d\) is \(d\). The endpoint lottery therefore gives expected pre-exchange welfare \(d/2\) per unit mass, while no feasible assignment can yield more than \(d\) per unit mass. The rule ignores reports, so each deterministic endpoint rule is truthful; their lottery is universally truthful.

I expect this problem to be tractable, by the explicit endpoint rule. The follow-up questions are whether any truthful mechanism can beat the factor \(2\) on this continuous class, and how the guarantee compares with finite profiles when masses are represented in binary.

The weak point is that this mirror preserves a guarantee more than it creates a new complexity landscape: the paper’s rule is already simple and report-independent, and the proof survives normalization from \(n\) agents to unit mass. Also, the paper’s separate single-agent manipulation result does not transfer cleanly to a nonatomic population, where one agent has zero mass. My case deliberately covers Theorem 2’s approximation guarantee in the individual-exchange model; it does not claim to continuize that manipulation result or the central-exchange results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is about the programme’s scope, not whether the continuous model is sensible. Theorem 2 is a truthful approximation guarantee, not a complexity result for a computational problem. Its mechanism ignores the reports and chooses between two fixed locations. On a unit-mass society, the proof is the identity
\[ u(0,x,t)+u(d,x,t)=d \]
integrated over the population, plus the bound that welfare is at most \(d\) per unit mass. The input’s support size and bit length play no role. The mechanism outputs a coin flip; it never needs to compute the optimum or simulate TTC. Finite replication may define a mass-exchange outcome, but the theorem’s guarantee follows from voluntary exchanges not reducing welfare.

The proposed corridor scenario is coherent: many residents can share a public location and preference, so the population can have high multiplicity. I would not reject it on modeling grounds. But asking whether the same endpoint rule gets the same ratio over those masses gives ChoCo no computational problem to chart: no pricing problem, optimization over an exponential family, or dependence on \(\tau\) or \(L\). The authors’ result already concerns an input of arbitrary finite size, and the population count is not a bottleneck in its proof.

A stronger follow-up might ask for the best truthful approximation ratio over mass societies, or for a compressed algorithm for mass-TTC. Those are recognizable mathematical questions, but the first is a distortion question and the second is a separate exchange-computation problem. Neither mirrors Theorem 2’s computational complexity. So my negative case is narrow: the mirror is mathematically well-posed and has a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but this anchor offers little for a programme specifically mapping computational complexity. It is weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists; it is stronger as a reason to exclude this particular anchor.
