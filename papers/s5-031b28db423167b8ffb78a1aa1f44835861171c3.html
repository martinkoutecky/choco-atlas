Why no mirror

Propositions 5 and 1 establish analytic claims about learning and imitation, but neither gives a computational result to anchor a mirror. A computational question over continuous cohorts would be a new task, not a continuous analogue of a named computational result in this paper.

What the mirror covers

None: Proposition 5's mislearning result and Proposition 1's anti-imitation characterization are analytic; the paper's other results study belief dynamics and learning behavior.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case has a real foothold: the paper studies how a population learns from others, and its multi-file example already has simultaneous cohorts. One could imagine a continuous mirror with many agents in each cohort, types recording their signal law, observation rule, and learning rule, and mass recording their share of the cohort. Agents choose real-valued belief actions based on their private signals and the observed actions of earlier cohorts. Large medical or financial cohorts with shared information and learning procedures are a plausible regime.

But there is no named computational result in the paper to anchor that mirror. The closest is Proposition 5, proved here: if players use social-learning rules that are strictly and boundedly increasing in private signals, neglect redundancy, and never anti-imitate, then there is positive probability that society converges to certain belief in the wrong state. That is a result about learning dynamics, not the complexity of computing a solution. Proposition 1, also proved here, characterizes when anti-imitation occurs; it too is an economic result, not an algorithmic complexity result.

So under your anchor requirement, there are no eligible anchors and no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover. The cohort model is a possible research extension, but it does not turn Proposition 5 into a computational question. Its weakest point is that Proposition 5 relies on the ordered sequence of individual signals and actions: in an atomless cohort, averaging can erase the early random signal events that drive the positive probability of mislearning. I would present this paper as a promising neighboring model for the programme, not as a case with a valid continuous-computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has not supplied an eligible anchor. Proposition 1 characterizes when rational learners anti-imitate; Proposition 5 gives conditions under which redundancy neglect leads to wrong long-run beliefs. Both are substantive results, but neither asserts the complexity of a computational problem. The cohort example suggests a plausible continuous *model*. It does not provide a computational result for that model to mirror.

The strongest version of the proposal would represent each cohort by a distribution over private-signal types and ask for the complexity of computing equilibrium actions or the probability of eventual mislearning. But those are new questions imposed on the paper. Its results characterize beliefs and learning dynamics; they do not define an algorithmic task with an input family, computational objective, or complexity measure. Making cohorts continuous supplies none of those. One could study such questions, but that would be an adjacent project in computational Bayesian inference or social learning, not a continuous-computational mirror of a result in this paper.

That distinction is the negative case’s strongest ground, and its limit. I cannot rule out a worthwhile continuous-cohort research project: repeated cohorts of doctors or traders are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. If the standard allows inventing a computational question around a paper’s analytic results, that scenario may survive. Under the stated anchor requirement, though, it does not give the reader a named computational result whose continuous mirror can be assessed.
