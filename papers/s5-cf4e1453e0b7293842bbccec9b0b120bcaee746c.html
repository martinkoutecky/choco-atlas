The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete participant types \(T=P\cup A\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\), type-level compatibility edges and reduced costs \(r_{tu}\), and a chain cap \(K\), decide whether there is a feasible positive-mass allocation of chain templates \(Q=(a,t_1,\ldots,t_\ell)\), with \(\ell\le K\), whose total reduced cost is negative. Each template starts at an altruist type and follows compatible edges through pair types; its mass must count against the capacity of every type occurrence.

The model it lives in

Model the registry as a distribution \(\mu\) over complete participant types. Choose nonnegative masses \(x_Q\) for compatible chain templates; for each type \(t\), require \(\sum_Q n_t(Q)x_Q\le\mu_t\), where \(n_t(Q)\) counts its occurrences. Ask whether \(\sum_Q x_Q\sum_{e\in Q}r_e<0\). Repeated types in a template stand for distinct participants of that type.

The objection that survived

The case assumes a registry has many exact exchange-relevant clones, including matching values and type-level reduced costs, but does not establish how common that regime is.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's negative-chain existence question over type masses; it leaves aside the paper's counterexamples to prior algorithms, general cycle-and-chain pricing, and full clearing.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s chain-pricing result. Take a large kidney-exchange registry with \(N\) patient-donor pairs and altruistic donors, where many participants share complete clinical profiles: the profiles determine compatibility and transplant value against every other profile. Let \(T=P\cup A\) be these pair and altruist types, with \(N\gg\tau=|T|\), and let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s fraction of the registry. This is plausible where compatibility and value are computed from recurring clinical categories; the types must include every feature that affects the pricing problem.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: “Deciding whether a negative chain exists is NP-complete.” Its problem is to decide, given a directed weighted compatibility graph \(G=(P\cup A,E)\) and a cap \(K\), whether there is a negative-weight path that starts at an altruist, uses at most \(K\) edges, and visits no vertex more than once. The paper proves the result by reduction from directed Hamiltonian path. In the kidney-exchange pricing formulation, negative reduced weight corresponds to positive price.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover: Continuous Negative-Price Chain. The input is rational type masses \(\mu_t\), a directed compatibility relation between types, rational reduced edge weights \(r_{tu}\), and a chain-length cap \(K\). The weights come from the paper’s reduced-cost formula, with dual values attached to types. A chain template \(Q=(a,t_1,\ldots,t_\ell)\) starts with an altruist type, follows compatible edges through pair types, and has \(\ell\le K\). It may use a type more than once, provided those are different participants.

For each template, let \(x_Q\ge0\) be the mass of registry members assigned to copies of that chain. Count every occurrence of a pair type against its available mass, and count the starting altruists against their type’s mass:
\[ \sum_Q n_t(Q)x_Q\le\mu_t \quad(t\in P),\qquad \sum_{Q:\,a(Q)=a}x_Q\le\mu_a \quad(a\in A). \]
Here \(n_t(Q)\) counts how often \(t\) occurs in \(Q\). Ask whether there is a feasible allocation with
\[ \sum_Q x_Q\sum_{e\in Q}r_e<0. \]
A solution is such a fractional chain allocation. These capacity constraints ensure that no individual is used twice, while allowing distinct members of one type to occupy different positions.

I expect this question to be tractable (Class A). Any negative-weight type sequence can be run at some positive mass: if it uses type \(t\) \(j\) times, choose a chain mass at most \(\mu_t/j\). So the decision reduces to finding a minimum-weight walk of at most \(K\) edges from an altruist type. Repeated types in that walk represent distinct members of the population. A shortest-walk computation handles the resulting problem; the Hamiltonian-path requirement that made Theorem 1 hard has disappeared. This is a concrete way discrete chain-pricing hardness could dissolve when the population becomes a divisible object.

The mirror covers the paper’s negative-chain pricing problem, not its entire clearing problem or every part of branch and price. It keeps the chain, compatibility, reduced prices, and length cap; only the available participants and the no-reuse constraint become type masses and capacity inequalities. The authors could plausibly recognize that as their pricing question in a high-multiplicity registry.

The weak point is that a negative-price chain in the paper is a discrete column, while this formulation detects one that can be executed at *some* positive mass. In a continuum, that mass can be arbitrarily small, and repeated type profiles can make walks available that do not exist as paths through distinct types. That scaling change is exactly what produces the predicted tractability, but it may make the mirror less compelling to authors focused on operationally usable chain sizes. A natural follow-up is to require a negative-price chain allocation of at least a specified mass \(\rho\), or to maximize the mass that can be assigned to negative-price chains; then type capacities matter to the decision itself.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational anchor, so I would not argue that the paper lacks a result to mirror. The strongest objection is that its hardness depends on an input where each vertex is an individually distinct resource: a negative chain must be a vertex-simple path. In a continuous model, a “type” must preserve everything that affects the instance, including compatibility and edge weights. Similar clinical categories are not enough if their members have different compatibility neighborhoods or prices. Exact types may then be nearly singletons, leaving little high-multiplicity regime.

Where exact twins do recur, the proposed mirror changes what counts as a chain. For any negative-weight type walk of finite length, every type it visits has positive mass, so a sufficiently small \(x>0\) satisfies all the capacity constraints. The decision therefore reduces to finding a negative walk of at most \(K\) edges; it no longer tests the simple-path condition that Theorem 1 makes hard. That may be a legitimate high-multiplicity pricing question, but it is a narrow symmetric special case, and its connection to the theorem is weaker than the proponent suggests. In particular, the paper’s general pricing weights include vertex-specific dual values; if those differ between otherwise similar participants, they split into different types.

Requiring a negative-chain allocation of at least \(\rho\) mass would make capacities matter. But that asks how much of the society can be assigned to negative-price chains, not whether the pricing problem has a negative-price column. The paper’s branch-and-price argument needs the latter. A mass-throughput question could be worth studying on its own, but it does not rescue this anchor as a mirror of Theorem 1.

Still, I cannot make the universal negative stick. If a large registry really does contain many exchange-relevant clones, and the authors accept type-level chains that may use different members of one type, then the continuous question is coherent and recognizable. The honest case against is that the paper’s realistic instances may offer few such types, while its clean continuous version sheds the feature responsible for the theorem’s hardness. That makes the mirror’s payoff uncertain; it does not show that no worthwhile mirror exists.
