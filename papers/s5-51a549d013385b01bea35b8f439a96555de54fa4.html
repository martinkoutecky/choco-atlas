The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a common order \(L_0\), a target \(p\), fixed \(r \ge 3\), a listed support of \(\tau\) complete rankings that are \(2\)-peaked with respect to \(L_0\), and rational masses \(\mu_t \ge 0\) summing to \(1\), decide for a rational budget \(\rho\) whether there are deleted masses \(x_t\) with \(0 \le x_t \le \mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t \le \rho\) such that \(p\) is an \(r\)-Approval winner in the remaining society: \(\sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)a_t(p) \ge \sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)a_t(c)\) for every \(c \in C\), where \(a_t(c)\) is \(1\) if \(c\) is in type \(t\)’s top \(r\), and \(0\) otherwise.

The model it lives in

The types are complete rankings that are \(2\)-peaked with respect to \(L_0\); the society is their rational mass distribution \(\mu\). The decision variables \(x_t\) are deleted mass, and the objective is to minimize \(\sum_t x_t\) subject to the mass bounds and the \(r\)-Approval winner inequalities for \(p\).

The objection that survived

The mass-deletion problem is a linear program for arbitrary ranking supports, so its tractability does not use the \(2\)-peaked structure that underlies Theorem 3’s discrete hardness.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s \(r\)-Approval deletion-control result for fixed \(r \ge 3\), in the nonunique-winner model. It leaves the paper’s other vote-control and candidate-control results, parameterized results, and auxiliary graph and packing results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the worst-case additive integrality gap between the minimum deleted mass and the minimum number of voters to delete, normalized by electorate size, when the discrete profile has \(\tau\) ranking types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous version of vote deletion, anchored by Theorem 3. The theorem, proved in this paper, says that for every fixed \(r\ge 3\), \(r\)-Approval Control by Deleting Votes is NP-complete in 2-peaked elections. The paper states that the result holds in both unique-winner and nonunique-winner models. Its proof reduces from Vertex Cover on graphs of maximum degree 3, using the paper’s 2-interval representation lemma.

Here is a direct continuous analogue. Fix \(r\ge3\). An instance of Continuous 2-Peaked \(r\)-Approval Deletion consists of candidates \(C\), a common order \(L_0\), a target \(p\), a rational society \(\mu\) over complete rankings that are 2-peaked with respect to \(L_0\), and a rational deletion budget \(\rho<1\). The support has \(\tau\) ranking types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For each type \(t\), let \(a_t(c)=1\) if \(c\) is in its top \(r\), and \(0\) otherwise. A solution is a deletion vector \(x\) satisfying
\[ 0\le x_t\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_t\le\rho,\qquad \sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)a_t(p)\ge \sum_t(\mu_t-x_t)a_t(c)\quad\text{for every }c\in C. \]
The question is whether such an \(x\) exists; equivalently, whether the minimum mass whose ballots must be deleted is at most \(\rho\). This uses the paper’s nonunique-winner convention, so ties are allowed.

This is recognizably the authors’ deletion-control problem: the rule, target, control action, and restriction on rankings all remain in place. The scenario is a large public vote over ordered options such as dates for an event. Many participants share the same complete ranking, and their preferences have at most two single-peaked blocks along the calendar order. The organizer can exclude a fraction of the counted ballot mass. In the intended high-multiplicity regime, the population \(N\) is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct ranking types.

The continuous question is in Class A. Its variables are the \(\tau\) deleted masses, and its winner conditions are linear. A polynomial-size linear program decides feasibility in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the rational input bit length. A discrete election maps naturally to masses \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and budget \(\rho=R/N\); the continuous problem permits fractional deletion within a type, which is the relaxation being studied.

A second worthwhile anchor is Theorem 5, also proved in the paper. It says that for every fixed \(r\ge4\), \(r\)-Approval Control by Adding Votes is NP-complete in 3-peaked elections, again in both winner models. The proof reduces from Independent Set on graphs of maximum degree 3. This gives a separate, complementary continuous question.

Call it Continuous 3-Peaked \(r\)-Approval Addition. Its instance has the same candidates, order, target, and registered society \(\mu\), plus a rational supply \(\nu_u\) of available voters for each 3-peaked ranking type \(u\), and a budget \(\rho\), measured relative to the registered society’s mass. A solution is an added-mass vector \(y\) with \(0\le y_u\le\nu_u\), \(\sum_u y_u\le\rho\), such that
\[ \sum_t\mu_t a_t(p)+\sum_u y_u a_u(p)\ge \sum_t\mu_t a_t(c)+\sum_u y_u a_u(c) \quad\text{for every }c\in C. \]
The question is whether such a vector exists. This too is an LP, now over the available ranking types, so it is in Class A. The scenario is a large electorate choosing among ordered alternatives, with a large pool of eligible but unregistered participants whose ranking types are shared by many people. The controller’s action remains adding votes; the continuous quantity is how much mass of each available type to register.

Theorem 3 is the stronger lead: deleting a fraction of the electorate is a particularly direct population-level interpretation of the paper’s action. Theorem 5 is also a good mirror, with the extra modeling choice of specifying the pool of available voters. Both LPs raise useful follow-up questions: how their optima compare with integer vote control for finite electorates; whether rounding gives additive guarantees when \(\tau\) is moderate; and how type-dependent prices or eligibility constraints affect tractability.

The main weakness is that the paper’s reductions use individually selectable votes, while these mirrors allow fractional mass within each type. The LP therefore solves a genuinely relaxed problem; it does not settle the discrete NP-complete problem or establish a yes/no identity for each discrete instance. Also, the reductions do not themselves exhibit a large population with few types. The positive case rests on the high-multiplicity regime being sensible for these control actions, not on those reductions having already encoded that regime. The scope here is limited to the paper’s vote-addition and vote-deletion results; its candidate-deletion and parameterized results are not covered by these mirrors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that both continuous problems lose the feature that makes this paper’s results distinctive. In Theorem 3, once voters become divisible mass, deletion is an LP over type masses and approval scores. The 2-peaked restriction and the paper’s graph representation play no role. Theorem 5 has the same issue: addition becomes an LP over available type mass, regardless of whether those types are 3-peaked. These look like generic fractional versions of control under additive scores, rather than mirrors that expose a new algorithmic role for the paper’s peak restrictions. If ChoCo’s scarce effort should go first to mirrors with a real pricing or structural challenge, these are weak candidates.

That objection is about priority, though, not whether the questions are worthwhile. The programme explicitly treats tractable continuous problems as potential Class A results, and a routine LP can still establish a useful point in its complexity landscape. Calling the answer easy would not defeat either mirror.

The stronger possible objection is that deleting mass by preference type assumes the controller can identify and remove an arbitrary fraction of each type, while adding mass assumes a separate eligible pool with known type supplies. Those assumptions can make the operations unlike practical electoral control. But they are compatible with a large ballot population, pooled survey, or organized pool of eligible participants. The type-level grouping is exactly the high-multiplicity premise, and both actions remain recognizable versions of vote deletion and addition. More elaborate eligibility or cost conditions could also be included in the types.

So I cannot honestly defeat either anchor. Theorem 3 has a direct, well-posed mass-deletion mirror, and Theorem 5 has a plausible mass-addition mirror. Their LPs may merit lower priority because they appear generic and do not use the peak structure, but that is not enough to support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
