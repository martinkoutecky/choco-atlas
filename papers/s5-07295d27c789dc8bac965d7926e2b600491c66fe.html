The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is limited: this paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror to. Its theorems concern Wardrop equilibrium and the structure or existence of stable configurations; none establishes that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The supplied text also omits theorem numbers, so I won’t reconstruct them. There are therefore zero eligible anchors, and no anchor-specific problem to hand to a prover.

There is still a plausible, but unanchored, continuous research question. Imagine a large market of small, independently owned edge-service providers, with many providers but only a few capacity and contract types. A type \(t\) records the provider’s capacity contribution \(q_t\) per unit of population mass and any fixed service terms; \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the provider population. For a coalition containing type masses \(x_t\), its capacity is \(K(x)=\sum_t q_t x_t\). Treating capacity as real-valued uses the analytic Erlang–B extension already invoked in the paper’s heavy-traffic analysis.

Call the candidate problem Atomless RB-IA Stable-Duopoly Search. Given rational \((\mu_t,q_t)\), market size \(\Lambda\), and service rate \(\nu\), find type masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) for one coalition, with the remainder in the other. Their Wardrop arrival rates satisfy
\[ B(K(x),\lambda_A/\nu)=B(K(\mu-x),\lambda_B/\nu), \qquad \lambda_A+\lambda_B=\Lambda. \]
For any proposed split \(z\) of either coalition, let \(\underline{\lambda}(z)\) be the minimum Wardrop share that \(z\) can guarantee over partitions of the remaining population. The output must be a duopoly for which no split satisfies
\[ \frac{\underline{\lambda}(z)}{K(z)} > \frac{\lambda_A}{K(x)} \]
for a split from \(A\), or the corresponding inequality for \(B\). This asks for a partition stable against splits under the paper’s RB-IA criterion; the objective is feasibility, since the paper studies stability rather than welfare maximization. The paper’s RB-IA results in Sections 4–5—especially the instability of partitions with at least three coalitions and the heavy- and light-traffic characterizations—support this choice structurally, but they are not computational anchors.

The regime is providers with standardized capacity profiles, where the number of providers is much larger than the number of types. The model keeps the paper’s core interaction: coalitions pool capacity, customers divide according to equal blocking probability, and providers assess merger or split incentives. It co-modifies capacity and market size to work with population mass. The continuous question’s expected class is tentatively tractable (Class A): divisible type masses make every capacity between zero and total capacity attainable, suggesting that split checks may reduce to a one-dimensional optimization after solving the Wardrop equations. That is a conjecture, not a result; exact bit complexity and global optimization for the extended Erlang–B function remain to be worked out. A useful further question is whether this mass model has a finite high-multiplicity correspondence, and how its stable partitions differ outside the traffic extremes.

The weakest point is substantial: provider populations are a less convincing high-multiplicity regime than large electorates, and allowing mass splits changes the paper’s finite-provider game. In light traffic, for example, the paper’s “no subcoalition above half the total capacity” condition would reduce to an exactly balanced split when capacity is divisible. Most of all, even a faithful continuous extension would be a new structural stability problem, not a continuation of a computational-complexity result established by this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no computational anchors in this paper. Its results establish Wardrop equilibrium properties, impossibility under one stability rule, and structural characterizations of stable configurations under others. None asks for the complexity of deciding stability or finding a stable configuration. So there is no computational result here for ChoCo to mirror; a continuous version of the stability theorems would remain an analytic game theory result.

The proponent’s strongest rescue is a plausible setting: many small service providers with repeated capacity profiles, and a search for an RB-IA stable duopoly. That could be a sensible model if providers really are interchangeable within types. But it adds a computational problem the paper does not pose. The authors study which configurations are stable, not the complexity of computing them.

In the proposed model, the type distribution also seems to add little structure. The paper’s blocking and payoff conditions depend on a coalition’s total capacity. If type masses are divisible and capacities are positive, every total capacity from zero to the population’s full capacity is attainable. The search therefore reduces to optimizing a scalar function such as \(\Psi(k;\Lambda)\); the paper already defines the corresponding maximizing capacity for its finite model and analyzes its behavior in traffic limits. A rigorous exact or approximate optimization problem could be formulated, but it would need a precision convention for the real-valued answer and would be a new special-function optimization question, not a complexity mirror of a result in this paper.

Keeping providers indivisible could preserve subset choices, and the proposed provider setting is more credible than treating a small committee as a continuum. I would not use granularity or provider identity as a blanket objection. Even granting that regime, though, it supplies a possible follow-on computational project, not an anchor in this article. My negative case is strong against calling this a worthwhile ChoCo mirror; it is weaker as the universal claim that no related continuous algorithmic question could ever be worthwhile.
