The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), group memberships \(a_{tj}\), a nonnegative rational type-mixing kernel \(K\), a rational seed-mass budget \(\kappa\), and tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\). Define \(q^{(0)}=x\) and \(q_u^{(r+1)}=x_u+(\mu_u-x_u)(1-\exp(-\sum_t K_{tu}q_t^{(r)}))\), and let \(q(x)\) be its limit. For each group \(j\), let \(\mu_j=\sum_t a_{tj}\mu_t>0\) and \(Q_j(x)=\sum_t a_{tj}q_t(x)\). Compute a feasible \(x\) whose value \(\rho(x)=\min_j Q_j(x)/\mu_j\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of the maximum feasible value.

The model it lives in

A society of mass \(1\) is partitioned into finitely many exchangeable types \(t\), with mass \(\mu_t\), overlapping group memberships, and type-level contact intensities \(K\). The decision is a seed-mass allocation \(x_t\); the objective is to maximize the smallest final influenced fraction among groups under the specified mean-field cascade.

The objection that survived

The proposed cascade discards realized within-type neighborhoods, and no limit argument shows that its dynamics or approximation guarantees follow from the paper’s fixed-graph IC model.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.6’s MaxMin, multiobjective first phase. It leaves the full disparity-penalized objective, ABRIS-G, the second greedy phase, and the paper’s sampling and auxiliary bounds outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what scaling of finite IC networks and type-mixing rates does their group influence converge to the stated cascade, including an error bound?
  • Can the MaxMin seed-mass problem be approximated efficiently as the number of types and groups vary, and under what conditions does its influence structure support MWU-style guarantees?
  • Does the population-scaled seed budget describe campaigns where seed selection is planned from a mixing model rather than from realized individual neighborhoods?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s MaxMin phase, not its entire penalized objective. There is no numbered hardness theorem in this paper; my lead anchor is its approximation result for the underlying multiobjective seeding problem.

Consider a large public campaign on a social platform: millions of users, with seed budgets that grow with population size—for example, a fixed fraction of the population. Users fall into a modest number of exchangeable types, such as demographic group crossed with network role. Members of a type have the same group memberships, seed eligibility, and transmission rates to other types. Their contacts are generated by a common type-mixing model. Thus the regime has \(N\) agents and \(\tau\) types, with \(N\) much larger than \(\tau\). This is a stochastic network model, not a claim that a few demographic labels describe every detail of an arbitrary observed graph.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Mass-Fair Cascade Seeding (MFCS). Its input is a finite type set \(T\), type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), overlapping group memberships \(a_{tj}\in\{0,1\}\), a symmetric transmission-rate matrix \(K\), and a seed-mass budget \(\kappa\). Write \(\mu_j=\sum_t a_{tj}\mu_t>0\) for group \(j\)’s population mass. Choose seed masses \(x_t\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\). Define the cascade by \(q_t^{(0)}=x_t\) and, for each type \(u\), \(q_u^{(r+1)}=x_u+(\mu_u-x_u)\bigl(1-\exp(-\sum_t K_{tu}q_t^{(r)})\bigr)\). Let \(q(x)\) be the limit of this iteration and \(Q_j(x)=\sum_t a_{tj}q_t(x)\). The task is to find a feasible \(x\) maximizing \(\rho(x)=\min_j Q_j(x)/\mu_j\), and return both the seed-mass profile and its achieved worst-group influence fraction. This is the type-mass analogue of selecting seeds to maximize the minimum proportion of each group reached.

The anchor is Theorem 4.6, proved in this paper, though it uses the MWU method of Udwani (2021). In the paper’s discrete IC network, group influence functions are estimated from group-specific RR samples. For a threshold \(\alpha\), if each group’s RR sample count meets Equation (6), then, when the number of groups satisfies \(|\Psi|=o(k/\log^3 k)\), MWU at precision \(\lambda\) returns an \((\omega(1-1/e)^2-\lambda-\epsilon)\)-approximate solution to the corresponding multiobjective group-influence constraint problem, with probability at least \(1-1/\min_i n_i\). Here \(\epsilon\) is the influence-estimation error, \(\alpha n_i\) are the group thresholds, \(\omega\) approaches \(1\), and the sampled estimates are \(\hat I_{\psi_i}(S)=n_iF(S,R_{\psi_i})\). The RR-count condition depends on \(\alpha,\epsilon,\mathrm{OPT}_i,n_i\), and the union-bound term involving \(|\Psi|\) and \(\binom{n}{k}\). ABRIS-T uses this threshold procedure in a binary search to obtain its MaxMin solution. The theorem is proved for the paper’s discrete graph, not for MFCS; it is the computational result that makes MFCS a well-motivated continuous question.

I would provisionally expect MFCS to be Class A for a fixed or modest number of types and groups, as an approximation problem. The anchor’s leverage is the groupwise influence structure and the MWU-plus-sampling approach, not a special property of individual names. A continuous version could seek a comparable approximation using mass-based influence estimates and a continuous multiobjective optimization method. The key open work is to establish that the cascade model preserves the needed diminishing-returns structure and that the sampling and approximation guarantees survive the limit. I would not claim an exact polynomial-time optimizer from Theorem 4.6.

This mirror covers the fairness-first MaxMin problem in ABRIS-T. It does not mirror the paper’s complete objective combining total spread with a disparity penalty, nor its second greedy phase. Further questions include whether the limiting group-influence functions admit stronger guarantees than the discrete ones, and when the full total-spread/disparity objective is tractable in this type-mass model.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper optimizes seeds on a fixed, realized graph, while MFCS replaces that graph with an exchangeable type-mixing model. If network position varies sharply within types, a small \(\tau\) may erase exactly the structure that determines influence; encoding enough of that structure could make \(\tau\) large. The mirror is most convincing for campaigns planned against a stable, validated mixing model, with a seed budget that scales with population. In that regime, replacing individual counts by group and role masses is a credible version of the authors’ problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is one real anchor, so the negative case cannot rest on the absence of a computational result. Theorem 4.6 gives an approximation guarantee for the threshold-constrained, multiobjective seeding problem used in ABRIS-T. It does not guarantee the paper’s full disparity-penalized objective, but the MaxMin phase is a substantial enough result to anchor a mirror.

The strongest objection is that the theorem’s computational object is the particular graph’s RR-set coverage structure, not its group proportions. For each seed set \(S\), the functions \(I_{\psi_i}(S)\) depend on which reverse-reachable sets its individual nodes cover, including how those sets overlap. Two networks can have the same group masses and type-level transmission rates but very different bridges, neighborhoods, and best seed locations. If a type records only demographic attributes and average interaction rates, it loses information the paper’s algorithm uses. If it records enough of the realized network to preserve that information, the number of types may grow with the number of nodes. In that fixed-network regime, moderate support \(\tau\) is not assured.

MFCS addresses this by assuming an exchangeable type-mixing network and choosing seed mass by type. That is a coherent model, but it changes both the network information and the seeding decision. The recurrence for \(q(x)\) describes aggregate spread under the mixing assumption; it cannot select unusually central individuals within a type after their realized neighborhoods are known. The paper’s RR-sampling problem is about making that kind of selection on a given graph. Calling the type-mass problem its continuous mirror therefore needs the authors to accept a different information regime, not merely a mass version of their existing input.

A more detailed continuum could retain network position—for example, by representing positions with a graphon-like kernel and choosing a seed allocation over positions. But then the input is a kernel or function, and the complexity question depends on how that object is represented and approximated. A finite step representation returns to the exchangeability assumption; a representation rich enough to preserve arbitrary observed graphs need not have a bounded number of types. This makes the proposed mirror’s computational scope much less clear than the finite-type formulation suggests.

There is also a regime mismatch. A positive seed-mass budget, \(\kappa>0\), makes the type-mass decision meaningful, but it means the number of seeds scales with the population. The paper’s experiments use budgets from 10 to 100 even on graphs with millions of nodes. With a fixed or sublinear budget, the seed mass tends to zero, and individual seed locations and their local reach can remain central. Moving to a population-scale budget is plausible for some campaigns, but it is an additional modeling choice rather than a limit established by the paper.

These points undermine the claim that Theorem 4.6 itself supplies the computational anchor for MFCS: its RR coverage and MWU guarantee do not transfer just by replacing counts with masses. The strongest version of the mirror is a new fair-seeding problem on an assumed exchangeable network, whose relationship to the paper’s fixed-graph problem needs justification.

That is not a decisive universal objection. A campaign planned from a validated block model, with seed budgets that scale with population and no use of realized individual positions, is a plausible setting; its authors could recognize MFCS as their fair influence problem in a high-multiplicity regime. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile question there. The negative case is strongest for observed, heterogeneous networks, and weakens precisely when one accepts the stochastic-block assumptions that make the continuous formulation clean.
