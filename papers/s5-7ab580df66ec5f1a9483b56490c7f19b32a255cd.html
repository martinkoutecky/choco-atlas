The continuous mirror question

Given a finite catalogue \(\mathcal G\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over complete valuation types \(u_t:2^{\mathcal G}\to\mathbb Z\), and a superadditive seller reserve function \(u_0\), compute a continuous NPW equilibrium when catalogue supply scales to one unit of each item per unit of buyer mass. Output anonymous bundle prices \(p(S)\), assignment masses \(x_{t,S}\), unsold-package masses \(r_S\), and a distribution \(\lambda_\gamma\) over catalogue partitions. Require \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,S}+r_S=\sum_{\gamma\ni S}\lambda_\gamma\) for each nonempty \(S\), and \(\sum_\gamma\lambda_\gamma=1\); assignments must use bundles maximizing \(u_t(S)-p(S)\), supported partitions must maximize partition revenue, and \(r_S>0\) only if \(p(S)=u_0(S)\).

The model it lives in

A replicated complementary-goods market with a distribution \(\mu\) over complete valuation types, integral bundles for each individual, and per-capita catalogue supply. The equilibrium variables are prices \(p(S)\), type-to-bundle masses \(x_{t,S}\), unsold masses \(r_S\), and partition weights \(\lambda_\gamma\); a welfare-maximizing LP provides the computation, with catalogue size governing the partition enumeration.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is a mirror of the paper’s equilibrium computation, rather than its full incentive compatible mechanism. Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper, says that under assumptions (A1)–(A3)—integer private values, quasilinear utility, and superadditive valuations for bidders and seller—if bidders bid sincerely, the basic ascending auction stops after finitely many rounds and produces a nonlinear pricing Walrasian (NPW) equilibrium. That equilibrium assigns each bidder an optimal bundle and gives the seller an optimal partition at the prices. By Lemma 2.2, its allocation is efficient. The theorem gives finite termination, not a polynomial runtime bound; the paper states no P-versus-NP or parameterized complexity result.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large procurement market for complementary equipment: many schools, clinics, or local agencies want items from a shared catalogue, such as processors, displays, and keyboards. A buyer’s type is their complete valuation function over every bundle—including any budget or other payoff-relevant parameter if the model uses one. Buyers of the same type are indistinguishable for the market. A large cohort may therefore have \(n\) buyers but only \(\tau\) valuation types, with \(\tau\ll n\). To keep the market nontrivial as the population grows, the seller’s inventory scales with it: normalize to one unit of buyer mass and one unit of each catalogue item per unit of population. Each individual still receives an integral bundle; only the type-level assignment masses are fractional.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover: Continuous NPW Equilibrium Computation (C-NPW). The input is a finite catalogue \(\mathcal G\), a list of \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one and integer-valued superadditive valuations \(u_t:2^{\mathcal G}\to\mathbb Z\), and a superadditive seller reserve function \(u_0\). Find rational bundle prices \(p(S)\), a mass assignment \(x_{t,S}\), a mass \(r_S\) of unsold packages, and a distribution \(\lambda_\gamma\) over partitions \(\gamma\) of the catalogue, satisfying:

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal G}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t}x_{t,S}+r_S =\sum_{\gamma\ni S}\lambda_\gamma,\qquad \sum_{\gamma}\lambda_\gamma=1. \]

All variables are nonnegative, and the package-balance equation applies to nonempty \(S\). The right side is the supply of package \(S\) when a unit mass of catalogue copies is partitioned according to \(\lambda\). Prices must satisfy \(p(\varnothing)=0\) and \(p(S)\ge u_0(S)\). For a valid output, \(x_{t,S}>0\) only when \(S\) maximizes \(u_t(S)-p(S)\); \(\lambda_\gamma>0\) only when \(\gamma\) maximizes \(\sum_{S\in\gamma}p(S)\); and \(r_S>0\) only when \(p(S)=u_0(S)\). Thus each type receives its mass in optimal integral bundles, the seller uses a revenue-maximizing partition, and any unsold package stays at its reserve price. These conditions make the price rule anonymous and nonlinear, as in the paper.

There is a direct LP behind this question: maximize \(\sum_{t,S}u_t(S)x_{t,S}+\sum_Su_0(S)r_S\) subject to the mass and package-balance equations. Its dual prices support the buyers’ bundle choices and the seller’s partition choice. With \(g=|\mathcal G|\), the formulation has \(O(\tau 2^g+\mathrm{Bell}(g))\) variables, where \(\mathrm{Bell}(g)\) is the number of catalogue partitions. So C-NPW is Class A with respect to population multiplicity: it is fixed-parameter tractable in \(g\), and for a fixed catalogue its exact rational LP is polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. This is a derived algorithmic expectation for the mirror, not a complexity bound proved by Theorem 3.1. If \(g\) grows, the seller’s partition choice brings a pricing problem over partitions; whether that admits a substantially smaller algorithm is a further question. Any hardness there would appear to come from the goods catalogue, not from the number of buyers.

This case covers the equilibrium endpoint of Theorem 3.1: compute a mass allocation and supporting NPW prices. It does not claim that the paper’s round-by-round auction carries over unchanged. Nor would I use Theorem 4.3 as an anchor: its ex post incentive compatibility concerns strategic individuals, while an atomless population makes each person’s effect on aggregate prices vanish. A continuous truthful-mechanism analogue would need a separate formulation.

The weakest point is the scaled inventory. The paper has one finite stock of goods; C-NPW gives the seller a population-scaled supply of catalogue copies. That co-modification is what makes a nontrivial population limit possible, but authors might regard the resulting equilibrium computation as a replicated-market extension rather than a mirror of their original auction. The finite-round convergence proof also does not itself establish convergence of a continuous mass-adjustment process. The positive case survives at the level of the problem the auction computes: an NPW equilibrium with endogenous bundling, complementarity, and integral bundles for individuals.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only credible anchor is Theorem 3.1, but its claim is finite termination, not a worst-case complexity bound. The proposed C-NPW problem therefore asks for a new result about equilibrium computation; it does not continue a complexity guarantee made by the paper. And its LP takes every type’s full valuation function as input, while the theorem concerns a demand-report auction designed to avoid eliciting those functions. That makes C-NPW a different computational formulation of the market.

The population limit also forces a choice. Keep the paper’s finite stock while the bidder population grows, and the fraction of people who can receive indivisible goods tends to zero. Scale inventory with population, as C-NPW does, and the model becomes a replicated market with per-capita supplies. That is a real change to the market. It is permitted by the programme’s scope, though, and it can be plausible for procurement of complementary equipment. So it weakens the connection to this particular auction but does not rule out a mirror.

A more faithful mirror could give each valuation type a mass and let the auction query its aggregate demand at each price. That preserves the paper’s demand-report structure and makes population multiplicity computationally visible. I do not see a fundamental obstacle to defining that problem. The paper’s finite-round theorem might even adapt to it; the paper simply provides no such analysis or complexity bound.

So the negative case is weak where it matters most. Theorem 3.1 is not a complexity result, and C-NPW changes the information model and scales supply. But the complementary-equipment setting offers a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and an aggregate-demand version is an obvious stronger mirror. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror survives.
