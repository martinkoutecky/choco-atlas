What the mirror covers

Theorem 2 characterizes the optimal local rule, and Corollary 7 extends its surplus bound to common independently randomized capacity-feasible policies. The proposed mirror covers those local design results; it supplies no population-level result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for a repeated local-market mirror. The paper has no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, or FPT. Its best computational anchor is Theorem 2, which gives an exact optimizer for a continuous design problem. That makes this a tractability-style case, not a complexity-class result.

Consider a platform with \(N\) independent local allocation pools, each offering one expiring slot. Each pool has \(K\ge2\) intended users and a scalable entrant. Intended users share value \(v_C>1\), but have capacities \(\kappa\) drawn independently from an atomless distribution \(F\); the entrant has value \(v_D\in(0,v_C)\). A user’s type is their role and capacity, and \(\mu(d\kappa)=dF(\kappa)\) gives the capacity mass in the population. The operator uses the same rule in every pool, based only on an account’s commitment \(a\) and strongest local rival \(m\). As \(N\) grows, the number of users and slots grows while the local interaction remains fixed. This is plausible for many independent accelerator reservation windows or comparable local allocation markets.

My lead mirror is Continuum Local Holdback Design. Given \(K,v_C,v_D,F\), choose measurable allocation and retention functions \(x(a,m)\) and \(r(a,m)\), with \(0\le x(a,m)\le \mathbf 1\{m<a\}\) and \(0\le r(a,m)\le a\). Under full commitment by intended users and entrant non-entry, define

\[ U_C(a)=\int [v_Cx(a,m)-r(a,m)]\,dF(m)^{K-1}, \qquad U_D(a)=\int [v_Dx(a,m)-r(a,m)]\,dF(m)^K. \]

The question is to find a feasible rule maximizing \(K\int U_C(a)\,dF(a)\), subject to \(U_D(a)\le0\) for every entrant action, and to intended-user individual rationality and incentives: \(U_C(a)\ge0\) and \(U_C\) nondecreasing. A solution is an optimal pair \((x,r)\) and its surplus value. For the continuum of independent pools, aggregate surplus is \(N\) times this per-pool objective.

The anchor is Theorem 2 (Optimal upper-tail holdback), proved in this paper. Under its stated assumptions, it solves that design problem and attains the upper bound obtained by dropping the intended-user incentive and participation constraints. For \(a>0\), set

\[ B_K=\frac{v_C^K}{v_D^{K-1}},\qquad q_R(a)^K=\max\left\{1-\frac{v_D}{a}F(a)^K,\frac{a}{a+B_K}\right\}, \qquad \bar q_R(a)=\min\left\{1,\frac{v_C}{v_D}q_R(a)\right\}. \]

Its optimizer is

\[ r^*(a,m)=a\,\mathbf 1\{F(m)\ge q_R(a)\},\qquad x^*(a,m)=\mathbf 1\{F(m)<\min(F(a),\bar q_R(a))\}, \]

with both functions zero at \(a=0\). The rule makes full commitment the unique best response of each intended-user type with positive capacity. Entrant non-entry is a best response, though not uniquely selected. The paper proves the result here using an actionwise dual certificate.

A second worthwhile anchor is Corollary 7 (Joint-design bound), also proved here. It says that the same maximum surplus is the supremum when the designer may choose both a local rule and a common, independently randomized, capacity-feasible policy for intended users, provided entrant non-entry remains a best response. The user policy need not be an equilibrium. The supremum is attained by the rule from Theorem 2 paired with full commitment. The corresponding mirror, Continuum Joint Rule-and-Policy Design, asks for the maximizing pair \((x,r,\sigma)\) over those same rule and policy classes in the repeated-pool society. This result is valuable because the mirror does not rest on assuming that intended users must fully commit: the paper’s full-commitment reduction covers shading, pooling, and independent mixing for this objective.

I expect both mirror problems to be tractable in the paper’s analytic sense: the first has an explicit optimal rule, and the second reduces to the first. That is not a claim of polynomial runtime. The paper gives no bit-complexity model for representing \(F\), or formal running-time bound for computing the cutoffs. Further questions include the exact finite-type version when capacities have atoms, efficient computation for rational piecewise-specified distributions, and what changes when users can coordinate or entrants can respond after observing congestion.

The weakest point is the regime. The paper has \(K\) users in a pool, not one atomless crowd. The mirror gets high multiplicity by repeating local pools, so each local interaction remains finite; the number of users and slots grows across the platform. That is plausible for separated reservation windows and fits the paper’s local strongest-rival rule, but it is a narrower case than a globally interacting continuum population. There is also a type-space gap: Theorem 2 assumes a continuous capacity distribution, whereas a strict finite-\(\tau\) formulation would use discrete capacity types and introduce ties. The paper does not prove that discrete-type variant. So the positive case is strongest as a continuum over capacity types and a large population of local markets, with a finite-type ChoCo formulation left as a follow-up.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The two anchors reduce to a dilemma: either the proposed population limit preserves the paper’s local model and restates a problem it already solves, or it changes the local model enough that Theorem 2 and Corollary 7 no longer anchor it.

For Theorem 2, the proposed \(N\)-pool construction leaves each pool with exactly \(K\) intended users, one entrant, and one opportunity. Increasing \(N\) adds independent copies; it does not make any pool’s society continuous. Aggregate surplus is just \(N\) times the surplus of one pool. The capacity distribution \(F\) is already in the theorem, which optimizes over measurable rules for any \(F\) satisfying its assumptions. In this construction, the proposed “continuum local holdback design” is the paper’s existing optimization problem, with the same constraints and objective. The theorem gives its exact rule. The continuum of pools adds no new computational question.

The stronger alternative is to make the population within a pool continuous. With one indivisible opportunity and an atomless population of mass one, allocating to a single person contributes zero to an objective integrated over that population. If instead the pool size \(K\) grows while the opportunity remains one, per-capita allocation value vanishes. The rival’s maximum also concentrates near the top of the capacity distribution: the \(K\)-user and \((K-1)\)-user rival distributions become indistinguishable in the limit. That removes the paper’s distinctive congestion comparison, whose likelihood ratio is \(\frac{K}{K-1}F(m)\). Scaling the number of opportunities with the population avoids the vanishing payoff, but then the model is either back to independent finite pools or becomes a bulk allocation problem with a different allocation technology. That might be a worthwhile project; it is not a continuous mirror of this theorem.

Corollary 7 fares no better as an anchor. Its reduction already allows a common, independently randomized, capacity-feasible policy, including pooling and atoms in the action distribution, and shows that the same surplus bound applies. Repeating those markets gives the same bound pool by pool. A continuous society with shared constraints across pools, correlated behavior, or heterogeneous policies might prevent that reduction, but those additions create a new design problem rather than a population-continuous version of the corollary.

So the proponent’s best scenario is either a repeated-market restatement of results already proved for a continuous capacity distribution, or a genuinely atomless single-market model in which the paper’s one-winner objective degenerates or its local allocation structure changes. This is a strong case against these anchors, not a proof that no useful model could be built. Its weakest point is the scope boundary: researchers could find a per-capita supply modification that the authors would recognize as a natural extension. But that would need its own motivating computational question and result; the paper’s existing anchors do not establish one.
