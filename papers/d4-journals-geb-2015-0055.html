The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is limited by the anchor requirement: this paper has no named computational-complexity result. None of its numbered results asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar, or gives an algorithm with a running-time bound. Its propositions characterize the economic model.

The nearest result is Proposition 3, proved in the paper. With two-point uncertainty \(e\in\{-u_e,u_e\}\) and \(m\in\{e-u_m,e+u_m\}\), each point equally likely, simple majority is optimal when \(u_m<u_e/\sqrt{2}\). When \(u_m>u_e/\sqrt{2}\), the optimal voting-rule distance is \(x_\alpha^*=u_m+u_e/2\): it blocks the median legislator closer to the status quo and constrains the farther one to implement the median citizen’s bliss point \(e\). At equality, both rules are optimal. Proposition 4, also proved here, gives a normal-distribution counterpart: a positive supermajority is optimal when \(\sigma_m^2>\sigma_e^2/2\), and then its distance rises with \(\sigma_m^2\) and falls with \(\sigma_e^2\); otherwise simple majority is optimal. These are useful analytical anchors for the model, but neither meets the requested computational-anchor standard.

A plausible *new, but unanchored* computational question would be: given a large electorate with finitely many quadratic-preference types \(d_i\), each with rational population share \(\mu_i\), and finite rational-support distributions for the common shock \(e\), the median legislator’s deviation \(\mu_m\), proposer offset \(\mu_p\), and legislators’ ideological types, compute an optimal vote share \(\alpha\in[1/2,1]\). The legislative proposal, roll call, and status-quo rules would be those in the paper; the objective would be expected aggregate citizen utility, \(\sum_i\mu_i\,\mathbb{E}[-(S_1-(d_i+e))^2]\). A solution is an optimal \(\alpha\) and its induced policy for each supported state. The regime is a large polity with many citizens sharing a moderate number of preference types. For explicitly listed finite supports, I would expect tractability by enumerating the finitely many voting thresholds and induced policy cases. The authors would recognize the objective—choosing the optimal institutional rule—but this is a proposed algorithmic question, not a result the paper proves.

The weak point is substantial: the paper already represents ideological variation with distributions and legislative support with a vote share. So the population-continuum modeling move is already present in its analytic model; the proposed mirror adds a computational-input and complexity question. Without a named computational result to anchor that question, this is a possible research continuation rather than a strong, compliant case for a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Propositions 3 and 4 do not give computational anchors. Proposition 3 gives a closed-form threshold for two-point uncertainty; Proposition 4 characterizes a one-dimensional optimum under normal uncertainty. Neither defines an encoded computational problem or establishes an algorithmic bound. Computing the first is direct arithmetic. Computing the second amounts to finding a root of the paper’s monotone first-order condition. Those are possible computational reformulations, but they are not results whose complexity the paper studies.

The strongest candidate mirror is to ask for an optimal voting threshold given rational mass distributions over citizen and legislator types. Under the paper’s common-shock symmetry, however, citizen masses do not affect which threshold is optimal. If \(d_i\) is a citizen type’s initial bliss point and \(S_1-e\) has mean zero, then
\[ \sum_i \mu_i\,\mathbb E[-(S_1-(d_i+e))^2] = \mathbb E[-(S_1-e)^2]-\sum_i\mu_i d_i^2. \]
The type distribution contributes only a constant. Legislator masses do affect the vote, but the paper already represents them by \(F_l\) and uses its quantiles to identify pivotal legislators. With finitely many explicitly listed legislator types, only finitely many pivotal thresholds need checking; each induces a policy outcome that can be evaluated directly. That is a reasonable implementation question, but it appears to reduce to enumeration, not to a new complexity landscape for continuous societies.

This leaves a real gap between the paper and ChoCo: the paper is analytic, and its distributions do not supply computational results. But turning its optimal-rule analysis into an algorithm is computationalizing a model that already uses distributions, rather than taking a finite population to a continuum. More elaborate versions—heterogeneous shock exposure, multidimensional policy, or extra chambers—might create worthwhile algorithmic problems. Their difficulty would come from those added features, though, and would require new welfare or voting structure beyond the anchors in Propositions 3 and 4.

So the case against these anchors is strong: neither is a computational result, and their most natural finite-support algorithmic versions look tractable by direct enumeration. The universal claim is weaker. An author-recognized computational extension may still be worth pursuing; I cannot honestly rule out every such scenario from this paper alone.
