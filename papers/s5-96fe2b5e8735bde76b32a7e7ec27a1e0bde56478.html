The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational-complexity result in this paper: none of its numbered results says a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved here, but it is an incentive theorem. It says that in the paper’s finite assignment market, *every* rank-minimizing mechanism that gives positive probability to every rank-minimizing feasible allocation is not obviously manipulable. In full, for every agent \(i\), true ranking \(\succ_i\), and misreport \(\succ'_i\), truth weakly improves both comparisons: the worst rank the agent can face over other agents’ reports and mechanism outcomes, and the best rank they can obtain over those reports and outcomes. So the strongest positive case is an incentive-theoretic mirror adjacent to ChoCo’s computational programme, not a direct complexity result.

A plausible regime is a large, centralized course-assignment market: many students rank the same relatively small set of courses, and the number of distinct rankings \(\tau\) is much smaller than the number of students. A type is a complete ranking of the courses; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the student population with ranking \(t\). Course capacities become per-capita supplies \(\kappa_o\). The decisions are assignment flows \(x_{t,o}\): the mass of type \(t\) assigned to course \(o\). They satisfy \(\sum_o x_{t,o}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,o}\le\kappa_o\), and minimize average rank, \(\sum_{t,o}x_{t,o}r_t(o)\). This preserves the paper’s assignment objective and makes the large-population regime explicit.

My proposed question is Infinitesimal Full-Support RM/NOM. Given a finite course set \(O\) and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa\), ask whether every full-support rank-minimizing rule satisfies the continuous analogue of Theorem 1. For a reported population distribution \(\nu\), let \(X^*(\nu)\) be the set of optimal assignment flows. “Full support” means the rule’s lottery over flows has topological support equal to \(X^*(\nu)\); in a continuum there are uncountably many optimal flows, so requiring positive probability on each individual flow would not be the right analogue.

To retain the paper’s individual-deviation question, include a focal student whose mass tends to zero. Let the other students’ distribution be \(\mu\). If the focal student reports \(q\), the population is \(\nu^\epsilon_q=(1-\epsilon)\mu+\epsilon\delta_q\). Conditional on a flow \(x\), treat students reporting \(q\) symmetrically: the focal student’s chance of course \(o\) is \(x_{q,o}/\nu^\epsilon_q\). The possible courses are therefore \(S_q(\nu)=\{o:\exists x\in X^*(\nu),\,x_{q,o}>0\}\). Using the focal student’s true ranking \(t\), compare the worst and best ranks over these courses, then over all other-student distributions \(\mu\), just as Theorem 1 compares outcomes over other agents’ reports. The question is whether, for every true ranking \(t\) and report \(q\), truth weakly improves both comparisons for all sufficiently small positive rational \(\epsilon\). A yes answer is a proof of those inequalities; a no answer is a type, report, and sequence of arbitrarily small \(\epsilon\) values with violating populations and optimal flows.

I expect the incentive inequalities may survive: each fixed-profile support question reduces to an optimal-transport LP, and the paper’s proof uses a capacity-threshold argument that has a natural mass version. If forced into ChoCo’s trichotomy, this points toward a tractable, structural analogue of Class A. But that is a conjectured direction for the incentive theorem, not a claim that the paper establishes a computational class. A useful further question is whether the robust comparisons over all \(\mu\) have a finite critical witness or an efficient separation procedure.

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and its URM consequence. It does not cover Proposition 1’s equal-treatment-of-equals result or Propositions 2–4 about particular serial-dictatorship and tie-breaking rules. There is no canonical limit of the exogenous individual order used by RM-SD, so those results would need separate models.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper’s NOM notion concerns one named agent whose report changes a finite profile. In an atomless population, one student has zero mass, so I have to define their effect through a vanishing-mass limit. That limit and topological full support are genuine co-modifications, and their behavior at ties may differ from the finite theorem. Most of all, the paper does not make the computational question its object. Its footnote 10 even flags the complexity of finding *all* rank-minimizing allocations as unknown, but supplies no named complexity result to anchor that question. So this is a credible continuous incentive extension, and a weak fit for the programme’s computational-complexity target.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 classifies the incentive property of full-support rank-minimizing mechanisms; it gives no algorithm, runtime, hardness result, or parameterized bound. Propositions 1–4 are also incentive or fairness results. The proponent’s “Infinitesimal Full-Support RM/NOM” is a plausible continuous incentive question, but naming it as a mirror does not make it a computational one.

The proposed course-market model is coherent: with type masses \(\mu_t\) and per-capita capacities \(\kappa_o\), rank minimization is the transportation problem
\[ \min \sum_{t,o} x_{t,o}r_t(o) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_o x_{t,o}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{t,o}\le \kappa_o,\qquad x_{t,o}\ge 0. \]
That has a polynomial-size min-cost-flow formulation in the number of types, objects, and input bits. It could be useful high-multiplicity computation, but the paper’s theorem says nothing about its complexity. Asking for the complexity of checking NOM over all populations, or of implementing a full-support rule, would create a new computational problem. The paper gives no finite representation of the arbitrary mechanisms covered by Theorem 1. Its footnote about finding *all* discrete RM allocations is a possible implementation question; a full-support law over aggregate flows would be a different object, with no canonical limit corresponding to the discrete URM.

Preserving the individual manipulation question requires special care. In an atomless society, a single agent has zero mass, so changing that agent’s report leaves the aggregate distribution unchanged. Giving the focal student mass \(\epsilon\) makes their report consequential, but the resulting question concerns incentives under vanishing-mass perturbations. A positive-mass deviating bloc instead raises a coalition-manipulation question. Either may be interesting, and the \(\epsilon\)-version is the stronger candidate for a continuous extension; neither supplies the missing computational result.

I would not dismiss this as an incoherent model. Theorem 1 may well have a natural mass analogue, and a large-market NOM study could interest the paper’s authors. The URM equal-treatment consequence also has a natural symmetric formulation. Those points support a continuous incentive-theory follow-up. They do not establish a worthwhile continuous *computational* mirror for ChoCo. On the programme’s criterion, that is the decisive objection; the broader claim that no useful continuous research could arise would go beyond what the paper supports.
