The case FOR (proponent)

I do not find a qualifying anchor for a continuous mirror under the rules given. The paper’s numbered results concern equilibrium and dynamics: for example, Theorem 2 says that, with initial allocations fixed, each weighted network reaches a distinct limit point in the Pareto set, and every limit point of the fair-trading dynamics can be reached by some network. It is proved in the paper, but it is not a computational complexity result. The other numbered results likewise concern trade properties, ODE solutions, continuity, or welfare.

The closest computational claim is unnumbered prose in §6: it cites Axtell (2005) for a polynomial complexity result on a broader class of decentralized exchange processes, then treats this model as an instance and reports simulations suggesting linear convergence for Cobb–Douglas preferences. That is not a numbered result of this paper establishing a complexity classification for a stated problem.

So, although a continuous population model of exchange might be imaginable, this paper has no named computational result to anchor the requested mirror questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended “case for” does not offer a positive mirror anchor; it argues that none is present. That is the strongest objection here: every numbered result is about the analysis of a trading process—Pareto efficiency, ODE solutions, or how networks map to limit allocations. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The nearest claim is in §6: the authors cite Axtell’s polynomial bound for a broader class of discrete exchange processes, then report convergence simulations for their own model. That is neither a numbered complexity result of this paper nor a stated algorithmic problem whose continuous mirror could be tested.

The strongest mirror one could construct would take a distribution of agents’ utilities and endowments, add a network description, and ask an algorithm to compute or optimize the limiting allocation. But the network is essential to the paper’s results: allocations depend on who trades with whom, and Proposition 7 concerns the utility of a particular agent placed at the core of a star. A distribution over agent types does not preserve those pairwise relationships or identify that agent. A block network or graphon could restore them and allow positive mass in a network role, but then the computational problem is a newly specified network-design or continuum-dynamics problem. It is not a high-multiplicity version of a computational result in the paper.

That is also where the negative case has a limit. Such a block-model or graphon problem could be mathematically sensible, and the paper’s discussion of changing networks to affect inequality gives it some connection to the authors’ interests. I cannot establish that no worthwhile continuous question could be built from it. The narrower, stronger conclusion is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror; reaching a computational question requires adding one, and the most plausible additions shift the focus to continuum exchange dynamics or network design rather than the complexity landscape this programme is meant to chart.
