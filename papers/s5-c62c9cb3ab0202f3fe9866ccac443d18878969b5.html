What the mirror covers

The proposed computation covers Lemma 2’s existence condition and Proposition 2’s interior equilibrium formulas; it does not turn Propositions 1 and 3–5 or their comparative-statics results into new complexity results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that Grillo’s setting is an unusually clean high-multiplicity model: in a large election, citizens fall into two groups with common preferences and mobilization incentives, with masses \(\mu\) and \(1-\mu\). The action is each group’s turnout rate, \(x,y\in[0,1]\); its vote mass is \(\mu x\) or \((1-\mu)y\), and its mobilization cost is quadratic in that mass. That is a sensible continuous society—but it is already the paper’s own model.

One could pose a computational follow-on: given rational \(\mu,\lambda,c\), output the pure-strategy equilibrium turnout rates, or report that no equilibrium exists. The paper gives strong analytic scaffolding for this: Lemma 2, proved here, states the exact condition for existence and that the equilibrium is unique; Proposition 2, also proved here, gives the turnout rates in the interior case. The computation would be tractable from those characterizations. But these are equilibrium-analysis results, not named results asserting a complexity classification. And computing an equilibrium of a model that already assumes a continuum of citizens is not, by itself, a discrete-to-continuous mirror.

So there are no qualifying computational anchors in the paper—no named result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. I can suggest the equilibrium-computation task as a natural computational extension, but cannot honestly present it as the requested anchored mirror. The positive case is therefore limited: this paper supports the plausibility of a continuous-population regime, while offering no qualifying computational result to continuize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor does not survive: Lemma 2 and Proposition 2 characterize an equilibrium; they do not assert the complexity of a computational problem. In particular, Lemma 2 gives an existence condition, and Proposition 2 gives the interior turnout rates. The appendix divides the remaining cases into six equilibrium regimes. Recasting those results as “given rational parameters, compute the equilibrium or report that none exists” adds an algorithmic label, but little computational substance: there are two turnout variables, two types, and a fixed set of cases. A more elaborate task such as choosing parameters to induce a boycott or maximize mandate would still be built from this same low dimensional analysis. An algorithmic problem with many strategic groups could be more substantial, but the paper neither states nor analyzes one.

This is not a case against high multiplicity. The paper’s continuum of citizens and its two group masses make that regime unusually plausible; treating turnout as a fraction rather than a headcount fits the model. The problem for a continuous mirror is that the paper already starts from a continuous population and asks an equilibrium question. There is no discrete computational result here whose complexity could be charted by making the society continuous.

A finite, high multiplicity version—say, restricting each group’s turnout to increments of \(1/n\)—could motivate new work on how finite equilibria relate to the paper’s continuum equilibrium. But that would be a new computational question about a discretized game, not a mirror of a named computational result in this paper. Likewise, adding many groups or heterogeneous preferences could create a richer algorithmic problem, but it would require extending the model rather than continuizing one of its results.

So the negative case is strong against this paper as an anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme: no qualifying computational result is present, and the obvious equilibrium-computation task is already reduced to a fixed, explicitly characterized two-group model. The broader claim that no useful follow-on could arise in any scenario is weaker; a finite approximation or multi-group extension might be worthwhile, but neither is a continuous mirror of a result this paper proves.
