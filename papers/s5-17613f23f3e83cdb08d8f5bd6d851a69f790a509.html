The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make a case that meets the required anchor standard. The only numbered result that comes close is Lemma 2.1, proved in this paper and described as folklore: a full Condorcet domain is maximal exactly when every triple with multiple never conditions has only implied conditions among them. That is a structural characterization useful for reasoning about maximality. It does not establish a complexity result for a problem, so it is not an eligible anchor.

The paper’s main contribution is an orderly algorithm for generating one representative of each isomorphism class of complete sets of never conditions; for the copious classes considered, the generated domains are maximal. But those algorithmic claims are presented in prose, with no numbered complexity result or stated running-time classification to anchor a continuous mirror.

A distribution \(\mu\) over rankings is an imaginable reformulation: one could ask whether its support defines a maximal Condorcet domain. But the domain property depends on which rankings are in the support, not on their masses. The paper generates sets of admissible preference orders over \(n\) alternatives; it does not study a population whose multiplicities drive the problem. So the weights would add no continuous-population substance, and I would not present this as a mirror the authors or a referee should accept.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has not offered a computational anchor that survives. Lemma 2.1 is a structural characterization of maximality, not a complexity result. The paper’s main contribution is an orderly enumeration algorithm, but its input is the number of alternatives and allowed never conditions; it generates isomorphism classes of domains. Voter multiplicities are not an input to that computation.

The strongest population-based reformulation is to take a domain \(D\) and consider every distribution \(p\) over its rankings. Ask whether each distribution’s pairwise-majority relation is transitive. This sounds like a continuous Condorcet-domain test, but it is just another statement of the existing property: any strict cycle under a real distribution persists under a sufficiently close rational distribution with an odd denominator, which is an ordinary odd electorate. Conversely, each odd electorate gives such a distribution. So the continuous condition neither adds a new population regime nor changes what makes a domain maximal. Maximality remains inclusion between sets of rankings.

One could test a proposed support \(D\) by searching for a distribution that produces a cycle, using linear constraints on the pairwise margins. That is a sensible adjacent formulation, but it asks whether a candidate set is a Condorcet domain; it does not mirror this paper’s contribution of enumerating maximal domains up to isomorphism. The paper’s search already constructs Condorcet domains from never conditions, and the weights cannot replace its combinatorial enumeration or its isomorphism checks. For a single supplied distribution, checking its majority relation is simpler still.

Thus the proposed question—whether the support of a distribution defines a maximal domain—fails in its best version: quantify over all distributions and it recovers the original domain property; use one distribution and it becomes a different task. I would argue against a continuous mirror for this manuscript. The limit of that case is worth stating plainly: a continuous optimization treatment of safe ranking supports is imaginable, but the paper gives no reason to think it would advance the generation problem it actually solves.
