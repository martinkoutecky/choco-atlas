The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant and position type sets \(A,B\), rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\) summing to one on each side, mutually acceptable pairs, strict preference orders over acceptable types, and a target matched mass \(\rho\), choose a rational transport matrix \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) with row sums at most \(\mu_a\), column sums at most \(\nu_b\), zero flow on unacceptable pairs, and total flow at least \(\rho\). For each acceptable pair \((a,b)\), let \(W_{ab}(x)\) be the mass of type \(a\) that is unmatched or assigned to a type ranked below \(b\), and define \(W_{ba}(x)\) symmetrically; minimize \(\sum_{(a,b)} W_{ab}(x)W_{ba}(x)\).

The model it lives in

A two-sided placement market with interchangeable applicant and position types, rational mass on each type, and a mass-splitting assignment; the objective is the density of blocking applicant-position pairs, with a minimum matched-mass requirement.

The objection that survived

At the theorem’s gap scale, both \(\beta/N^2\) and \(F(\beta)\beta/N^2\) vanish as the market grows, so the mirror does not preserve the parameterized question about a few exceptional blocking pairs.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s Min-BP-SMI gap-hardness result through a continuous matching-size target and blocking-pair density; it leaves the Min-BP-SRI result and Theorem 2 on Max-SMTI aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact and approximation complexity of minimizing the quadratic blocking-density objective in the number of types and the input bit length?
  • Under what conditions does the continuous optimum correspond to limits of finite cohort instances?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s Min-BP-SMI result, in a two-sided placement market with large interchangeable cohorts. For example, a yearly placement system could have many applicants and positions, but only a moderate number of preference types: applicants who rank the same job classes in the same order, and positions that rank the same applicant classes in the same order. Mass is each cohort’s fraction of its side of the market. The regime is \(N\) agents per side with \(\tau\) positive-mass types and \(\tau\ll N\). Members of a type are identical for the preferences the model uses; partners within a type are interchangeable.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. In full, for every computable function \(F\), it is W[1]-hard, parameterized by \(\beta\), to distinguish Min-BP-SMI instances \((P,k,\beta)\) where some matching of size at least \(k\) has at most \(\beta\) blocking pairs from instances where every matching of size at least \(k\) has at least \(F(\beta)\beta\) blocking pairs. The construction sets \(k=|U|\), so the target matching is perfect. The theorem therefore rules out FPT approximation to any factor depending only on \(\beta\), unless \(\mathrm{FPT}=\mathrm{W[1]}\).

A continuous version I would hand to a prover is Mass-Min-BP-SMI. An instance has finite sets \(A,B\) of applicant and position types, rational masses \(\mu_a,\nu_b\) summing to one on each side, mutually acceptable pairs of types, strict preference orders over acceptable types, and a rational target matched mass \(\rho\). A solution is a nonnegative rational transport matrix \(x=(x_{ab})\), zero on unacceptable pairs, with row sums at most \(\mu_a\), column sums at most \(\nu_b\), and \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\ge\rho\). It represents the fraction of each type assigned to each other type.

For an acceptable pair \((a,b)\), let \(W_{ab}(x)\) be the mass of type \(a\) that is unmatched or matched to a type it ranks below \(b\); define \(W_{ba}(x)\) symmetrically. Set
\[ D(x)=\sum_{(a,b)\text{ acceptable}} W_{ab}(x)W_{ba}(x). \]
The task is to find a feasible \(x\) minimizing \(D(x)\). This is the density of blocking pairs among independently sampled cross-side pairs. On finite cohort instances with masses in units of \(1/N\), it equals the discrete number of blocking pairs divided by \(N^2\). The continuous question thus retains the paper’s tradeoff between required matching size and blocking, while allowing mass to split across partners.

I think the authors would recognize this as a continuous form of their underlying Min-BP-SMI problem: it still asks for a matching meeting a coverage target while minimizing mutual blocking. The preference-over-types model is natural when cohort members are interchangeable. My tentative prediction is Class C: the objective is a nonconvex quadratic function of the mass transfers, and type-level choices remain coupled. That is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 1. It raises useful follow-up questions: can the special quadratic structure be optimized efficiently for small support, can it yield a gap hardness result, and when does the fractional optimum agree with the limit of discrete optima?

The weakest point is substantial: Theorem 1 does not establish hardness for this mirror. Its reduction uses individual agents and many private gadget copies, and it gives no reason that the construction survives with \(\tau\ll N\). Fractional transfers may also defeat the gadget logic. Further, replacing rankings of named partners with rankings of interchangeable partner types changes the preference model. So the mirror is a credible continuous computational question anchored in the paper’s result, but the theorem’s W[1]-hardness does not transfer on the evidence given. My scope here is only Theorem 1’s Min-BP-SMI result, not the roommates result or the Max-SMTI approximation theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational result, so the case against cannot dismiss it for lack of an anchor. The best objection is that its hard part is the number of individually specified agents and the sparse set of blocking pairs, while the proposed mirror changes both.

In Min-BP-SMI, preferences are strict rankings of named partners. A low-support model that ranks partner *types* instead makes agents indifferent among everyone in a type. That can be a sensible market model, but it is a substantive change to the preference structure. The reduction’s selector and gadget agents depend on particular relationships among named agents; the paper gives no reason those relationships survive when agents are interchangeable within types. Retaining all named-partner rankings avoids that change, but then the purported types need not compress the instance.

The blocker-density objective is the strongest repair. For a finite market of size \(N\), its value can represent the number of blocking pairs divided by \(N^2\). But Theorem 1 is about distinguishing a fixed small number \(\beta\) of blockers from \(F(\beta)\beta\). Both counts have density tending to zero as the population grows. The density objective therefore asks how to minimize prevalent blocking, not whether a matching can be made to have only a few exceptional blocking pairs. The reduction also does not establish hardness at bounded or moderate support after agents are grouped and transfers may split fractionally. Its W[1]-hardness is not evidence that the repaired continuous problem inherits the result.

That leaves a coherent new quadratic optimization problem, but the theorem supplies neither a hardness result for it nor an evident route to the programme’s LP and pricing toolbox. The mirror may be worth studying on its own; the paper’s headline theorem is a weak reason to prioritize it as a continuization of Min-BP-SMI.

This is the strongest negative case I can make against the anchor, and it is limited: it undercuts the theorem-to-mirror connection, not the value of the new model. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario. Large placement markets with interchangeable preference cohorts are plausible, and the paper’s Max-SMTI result may admit a cleaner mass-based question. The universal negative case is therefore weak.
