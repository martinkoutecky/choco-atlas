The anchor — hardness

Theorem 5 (Complexity of Nash Stability)

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 5 (Complexity of Nash Stability) — survived: The missing reduction and the possibility that fractional team intensities change the answer weaken hardness transfer, but leave a well-posed mass-stability search.
  • Theorem 1 (Deterministic Existence) — survived: The opponent's gap argument undermines the claimed polynomial path bound, but the potential maximization over team intensities remains a concrete analogue under the alignment promise.

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational population shares \(\mu_t\), a maximum team size \(M\), and type-level capabilities and preferences, find rational nonnegative intensities \(z_q\) over integral team templates \(q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{T}\) with \(1\leq |q|\leq M\), satisfying \(\sum_q q_t z_q=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\), such that no member of any used template \(q\) prefers a used destination template \(r+\mathbf e_t\) or a singleton to \(q\); report if no such intensities exist.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity hedonic coalition model: each type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), teams retain integral compositions \(q\), and \(z_q\) records teams per capita. Type-level utilities use the paper's per-capita coalition values, and stability tests whether a member would benefit from joining a recurring destination team.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Nash-stable partition computation and its potential-aligned regime; it leaves the consistency probability bound, CoalT's empirical results, and core and individual stability aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the claimed LCFG hardness reduction survive when the population can mix team templates with fractional intensities?
  • Can the implicit team-template search or its potential LP be solved efficiently in useful regimes of \(\tau\), \(d\), and \(M\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is for the paper’s computation of Nash-stable partitions. The natural large-population setting is a service that runs many copies of a small catalogue of LLM agents: the same model versions, prompts, capability profiles, and task settings recur across a large fleet. A type records everything relevant to the game, including its capability profile and preference behavior. The number of agents \(M\) can then be much larger than the number of types \(\tau\). The paper itself discusses scaling to hundreds of agents, though its experiments use only six.

I would represent a coalition by an integral team template \(q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\tau}\), where \(q_t\) is the number of type-\(t\) agents on the team. Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s population share, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A continuous coalition structure is a vector of nonnegative intensities \(z_q\): \(z_q\) is the number of teams of template \(q\) per agent in the population. It must satisfy \(\sum_q q_t z_q=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\). Thus cohorts can be split across different teams, while every team still gives its members an integral team composition.

For an LCFG-compatible instance, a team’s value can be written \(v(q)=\phi(\max_{t:q_t>0}\mathbf c_t)-\psi(|q|)\), and a type-\(t\) member’s per-capita value is \(u_t(q)=v(q)/|q|\). Preferences can include the paper’s bounded-rational choices, provided they are part of the type description. Call \(z\) mass Nash-stable if, for every used template \(q\), every type \(t\) with \(q_t>0\), and every used destination template \(r\) with room for another member, a type-\(t\) member weakly prefers \(q\) to \(r+\mathbf e_t\); also require that member not prefer becoming a singleton. These tests treat positive-intensity team patterns as recurring in the high-multiplicity population.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5 (Complexity of Nash Stability). It states that verifying a given Nash-stable partition is in P, using \(O(n^2)\) preference queries under explicit value computation; computing a Nash-stable partition is NP-hard in general hedonic games, and the paper says LCFGs retain worst-case hardness when capability dimension \(d\) is unbounded; and, under capability monotonicity and potential alignment, both verification and computation are polynomial. The general hedonic-game hardness is cited to Ballester et al. (2004). The verification and structured-subclass claims are made by this paper; its text asserts that unbounded \(d\) lets LCFGs encode sufficiently rich preferences, but the supplied proof sketch does not give that reduction.

The continuous question I would hand a prover is Mass Nash-Stable Coalition Search: given \(M\), the type shares \(\mu\), type descriptions, and the LCFG value or preference rule, find a feasible intensity vector \(z\) satisfying the mass Nash-stability tests above, or report that none exists. The template set is all integer compositions \(q\) with \(1\le |q|\le M\); it can be represented implicitly, since listing it may be enormous. A solution is a finite-support rational \(z\) meeting the mass constraints and every stability test. The search has no welfare objective: the paper’s underlying computational question is whether a stable coalition structure can be found.

For the unrestricted, unbounded-\(d\) version, my first expectation is Class B: hardness transfers. The paper locates its hard case in capability dimension, rather than in the number of agents. That makes it plausible that the combinatorics survive when many agents share each type. But this is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence established by Theorem 5: fractional team intensities could create stable mixtures that have no discrete counterpart. Showing that the claimed LCFG hardness reduction survives this relaxation is a central follow-up question. The corresponding questions in restricted regimes are whether fixed or small \(\tau\) or \(d\) helps, and whether the implicit template set admits an efficient pricing algorithm.

A second anchor is Theorem 1 (Deterministic Existence), proved in this paper. It says that if an LCFG satisfies the \(\delta\)-value-gap condition, potential alignment, and capability monotonicity, and every agent has \(\epsilon\)-rational preferences with \(\epsilon<\delta/2\), then a Nash-stable partition exists and can be found in polynomial time. The paper’s argument uses improving deviations to increase a potential until they terminate.

Its continuous counterpart is Potential-Aligned Mass Stability: use the same type and template input, promise the corresponding gap, alignment, monotonicity, and rationality conditions for every allowed type-level deviation, and return a mass Nash-stable \(z\). Under these promises, maximize \(\Phi(z)=\sum_q z_qv(q)\) subject to \(\sum_q q_tz_q=\mu_t\) and \(z_q\ge0\). This is an LP over coalition templates. A profitable deviation would increase the potential, so a maximizer cannot permit one. I expect Class A where the template set is explicit or its pricing problem is tractable. With all templates implicit, the relevant pricing question is whether one can optimize \(v(q)-\sum_t\pi_tq_t\) efficiently over integer team compositions. Theorem 1 motivates this LP route, but does not itself prove an efficient algorithm for the continuous problem.

These are credible mirrors of the paper’s problem because they preserve its object: agents form exclusive coalitions, preferences depend on one’s own coalition, and the target is resistance to unilateral moves. The co-modification is the representation of many identical agents by type shares and repeated team-pattern intensities. This is precisely the kind of high-multiplicity regime the programme is meant to examine. The mirror covers the paper’s Nash-stability computation and its conditionally tractable subclass. It does not mirror the empirical CoalT results, the consistency probability bound, or the paper’s claims about core and individual stability.

My weakest point is the hardness anchor. The paper does not show the reduction from general hedonic games to its capability-based LCFG, and even a valid discrete reduction might fail after coalition-pattern frequencies become fractional. The concrete deployment regime is also narrower than the paper’s six-agent experiments: replicas must genuinely share their relevant capabilities, preferences, and other game attributes. Those gaps limit the hardness case, but they do not erase the mirror. The potential-aligned LP gives an independent, concrete continuous question that the authors could plausibly recognize as their coalition-formation problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchors is that the paper has not securely established either computational claim they are meant to carry into the continuum. Theorem 5’s hardness argument says that general hedonic-game hardness transfers to LCFGs when \(d\) is unbounded, but the supplied proof gives no reduction. Hardness of a larger class does not establish hardness of this restricted one. Its \(O(n^2)\) verification claim also counts LLM preference queries as unit-cost operations, without encoding those preferences as part of a standard computational input.

The polynomial claim in Theorem 1 is shaky too. The \(\delta\)-gap separates agents’ coalition values, but potential alignment only says each improving move increases \(\Phi\); it gives no lower bound on the increase in \(\Phi\). The claimed \(O(n\Delta_v/\delta)\) path bound therefore does not follow from the proof sketch. A finite improvement path establishes termination, while the number of partitions can be exponential. These defects make the paper a weak source for a proved complexity landscape.

That attacks the anchors as evidence, though it does not defeat the best continuous mirror. Repeated copies of a small catalogue of model roles are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the team-template formulation preserves finite team compositions while allowing the population to split across them. In the potential-aligned regime, maximizing \(\sum_q z_qv(q)\) is a coherent configuration LP: a profitable type-level move would give a feasible improving direction. Its implicit-template pricing problem is a concrete computational question of the sort ChoCo is meant to expose. Calling it merely a restatement would be a mistake.

The mirror also has a serious limitation: its central extension is a new configuration problem, not a result the paper has proved. The general hardness claim may fail for LCFGs, and even valid discrete hardness would not by itself survive fractional team intensities. The LP’s tractability likewise depends on the template pricing problem. Those are open obligations, not reasons to dismiss the question.

So the best honest negative case is a case for downgrading the paper’s anchors: Theorem 5’s hardness is unsupported, and Theorem 1’s polynomial bound is not justified as written. I cannot honestly turn that into the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The repeated-role setting and the potential LP leave a credible, paper-recognizable computational question standing.
