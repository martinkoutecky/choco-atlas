The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying anchor-based case from this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Section 3 reports simulation findings about relaxation times—such as the faster relaxation under stronger preference correlation and the reported gap between correlated and random lists—but those are empirical observations, not named complexity results. So there are zero eligible anchors, and no per-anchor continuous problem or A/B/C classification to report.

The strongest positive lead is an unanchored mirror of the paper’s relaxation-time question. Let \(T_M,T_W\) be finite sets of complete preference types, with each type specifying a ranking of opposite-side types. Give each side rational mass distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\). Interpret a type as a large cohort sharing the same preferences, and evolve the mass of each type-pair relationship by the fluid-limit dynamics of the paper’s random-encounter rule, with time measured in Monte Carlo encounters per population member. A precise decision question would be: given these type sets, masses, and rational \(q\), does the limiting trajectory reach a stable matching by time \(q\)? Here stability means that no positive mass of men and women both prefer one another to their current assignments. A solution is a yes-or-no answer, with an algorithm or proof.

There is a plausible regime for this model: a large matching market with many participants but relatively few recurring preference archetypes, so \(N\gg |T_M|+|T_W|\). That keeps the population continuous while retaining the paper’s concern with random encounters, correlated preferences, and time to stability. But the paper’s own continuously sampled attractiveness and taste vectors would almost surely give nearly everyone a distinct type; the finite-archetype regime is an additional modeling choice. Also, a fluid limit could lose the finite-population fluctuations that shape the paper’s relaxation-time distributions. Those are the soft spots.

This would be a reasonable research question to develop, but it is not anchored by a named result in this paper. Its complexity direction is therefore open; treating it as a proved instance of tractability or hardness would overstate the evidence.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to anchor a mirror. The paper reports Monte Carlo relaxation times and their association with preference correlation; it gives no theorem or algorithmic complexity result. That leaves no qualifying anchor to defend. The proponent’s mean-field question is a possible new study inspired by the paper, but it does not mirror a computational result the paper establishes.

The strongest version of that proposal would specify finitely many complete preference types, rational masses, an initial distribution of matches, and random meetings scaled by population size. A cohort model is plausible, so lack of multiplicity is not the objection. The trouble is what happens to the paper’s main observable, time to *exact* stability. In a fluid limit, a positive mass of blocking pairs can shrink toward zero without ever reaching zero at finite time. The finite population, meanwhile, stops only when no individual blocking pair remains; the last few pairs contribute to the reported relaxation time and its distribution even though their mass vanishes as \(N\) grows. The proposed “stable by time \(q\)” question can therefore lose the very tail the paper measures. In the usual random-pair mean-field scaling it may simply answer “not by any finite \(q\).”

One can repair that by asking for the time to \(\varepsilon\)-stability, or for an asymptotic convergence rate. That is a more sensible continuum observable, but it changes the target from the paper’s absorption time to macroscopic convergence. It also leads chiefly to analysis or numerical study of a finite-dimensional nonlinear dynamical system. One could impose a computational-complexity question on that system, but it would be a new question, not a computational result of this paper; it does not fill the programme’s stated gap in the complexity of algorithms for social-choice problems. Keeping the exact hitting-time distribution instead requires finite-population fluctuations, which the deterministic mean-field trajectory discards.

So the negative case is strongest on scope and on the mismatch between exact absorption and continuum convergence. It is not a proof that no worthwhile continuous matching-dynamics study exists: a paper on a carefully defined cohort model and its \(\varepsilon\)-relaxation could be valuable. The case is that this paper supplies an empirical motivation for such a study, not a named computational result whose continuous mirror belongs to ChoCo’s complexity programme.
