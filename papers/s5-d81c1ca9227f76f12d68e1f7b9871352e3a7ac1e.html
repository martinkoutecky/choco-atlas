The case FOR (proponent)

My lead would be Continuous Dummy-Type Recognition. Take finitely many voter types \(T\), with rational population shares \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), per-capita voting weights \(a_t\ge 0\), and a quota \(q\) satisfying \(0<q\le\sum_t a_t\mu_t\). A coalition is a vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\): it includes mass \(x_t\) of each type. It wins when \(\sum_t a_tx_t\ge q\). Given a type \(t^\star\), decide whether it is dummy: for every choice of the other types’ coalition masses and every \(z\in[0,\mu_{t^\star}]\), adding mass \(z\) of \(t^\star\) leaves the outcome unchanged. The answer is a yes/no bit, or the set of all dummy types.

A plausible regime is a corporation with many small shareholders but only a few share classes and voting arrangements: the number of shareholders is large while the number of types \(\tau=|T|\) stays small. Mass is the fraction of shareholders in each class. A coalition can include any fraction of a class, as appropriate to the continuum limit; the class members are not assumed to vote as one bloc.

The hardness anchor is Theorem 4.4 of Chalkiadakis, Elkind, and Wooldridge (2011), which this paper cites in §2.2: given a general integer representation of a weighted game, deciding whether a specified voter is a dummy is coNP-complete. It is cited from elsewhere, not proved in this paper. The paper’s own Lemma 3.6 supplies the connection to its work: it says a dummy-revealing version of a power-index sequence can be computed by removing the game’s dummies, applying the index to the reduced game, and assigning zero to the dummies. That lemma does not give a complexity classification, but it shows why identifying dummies matters to the computation the paper studies.

For the continuous problem, I expect tractability. Because each type’s mass is divisible, the coalition weights obtainable from the other types fill an interval. In a valid nontrivial game, any type with \(a_t\mu_t>0\) can contribute enough mass to cross the quota from some losing coalition; only zero-weight or zero-mass types are dummy. Thus all dummy types can be found by scanning the support. This is a sharp, comprehensible case of discrete hardness disappearing when coalition choices become continuous. It is not a collision with the cited theorem: the models differ, and showing that the continuous answer is easy would itself be the result.

The mirror is plausible as a type-level extension of the paper’s dummy property, but that is also its weakest point. The paper asks whether an *individual voter* is dummy; in an atomless population, an individual has zero mass, so individual dummyhood becomes vacuous. This mirror instead asks whether a positive-mass type can affect outcomes. The authors’ interest in type-revealing indices makes that extension recognizable, but they might reasonably regard it as a group-influence problem rather than their original dummy-recognition problem.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it covers a computational prerequisite for the paper’s dummy-revealing indices, not the paper’s main burden of integrating over representation polytopes. It also raises a useful follow-up: whether the tractable mass-level answer predicts dummy status in large finite societies, especially when coalition weights can sit close to the quota.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor is already displaced from the paper’s own results. The coNP-completeness statement is Theorem 4.4 of Chalkiadakis, Elkind, and Wooldridge, which this paper cites; it is not a theorem proved here. The paper’s Lemma 3.6 says how to compute a dummy-revealing index *given the dummy voters*: remove them, compute on the reduced game, and assign them zero. It gives no complexity classification for that step. The paper’s own computational discussion concerns integrating over representation polytopes, but states no named complexity result for it. So the proposed mirror has no computational result of this paper to mirror.

Even granting the cited dummy-recognition problem as an anchor, the proposed continuum changes its content. For type masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), the attainable coalition weights fill the interval \([0,\sum_t a_t\mu_t]\). If \(0<q\le\sum_t a_t\mu_t\), every type with \(a_t\mu_t>0\) can affect the outcome; a type is dummy exactly when \(a_t\mu_t=0\). The proposed classification is therefore a scan of the types. More fundamentally, individual dummyhood becomes vacuous for atomless agents, while positive-mass type influence is a group property. The paper’s dummy property concerns whether one voter can change any coalition’s status; its type-revealing indices concern equivalence among such voters. Neither supplies a nontrivial type-level dummy-recognition problem.

A closer continuation of the paper’s central object fares little better. Its indices average over many weight representations that induce the same finite game. In a continuous coalition model, a nondegenerate threshold game has a decision boundary inside the box of possible coalition masses. That boundary identifies the weight vector up to scaling; normalization then leaves a single representation. The averaging polytopes collapse to a point, so the paper’s representation-compatibility mechanism has no comparable computation to study. At the unanimity boundary, many representations may remain, but the game makes all participants symmetric, leaving no comparable power-classification problem.

One could retain integer coalitions and study games with many repeated voter types. That is a sensible high-multiplicity problem, and the cited high-multiplicity literature supports the regime rather than counting against it. But retaining the integer grid keeps the population discrete; taking the grid away produces the collapse above. Studying finite-size corrections as the grid is refined might be worthwhile, but it would be a new asymptotic project, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The negative case is strongest on this combination: the sole hardness anchor is external, its proposed continuum version loses the discrete phenomenon that made it hard, and the paper’s own average-representation construction degenerates under a genuine population continuum. An aggregate theory of limiting power could still be interesting, but the supplied paper does not formulate the computational question or provide a named result for it.
