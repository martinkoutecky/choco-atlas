The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a high-multiplicity evaluation problem for the Patient Algorithm. The regime is a large kidney-exchange pool: many patient–donor pairs, but a moderate number \(\tau\) of compatibility-relevant profiles. A type includes the profile, arrival rate, and critical-time hazard; pairwise compatibility is drawn from the type-pair kernel. Mass is the number of waiting pairs of each type divided by the market scale. This lets the paper’s \(A_0,A_1,\ldots,A_p\) model represent a population with many pairs and \(p+1\) types. The random compatibility draws are part of the market model, rather than unrecorded differences between agents of the same type.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved here. It gives the Patient Algorithm’s ODE formulation: for pool counts \(|S_t(A_k)|\), arrivals occur at rates \((1-p\lambda)m\) for \(A_0\) and \(\lambda m\) for each \(A_j\), and critical events have rate one per agent. The equations are
\(\frac{d|S_t(A_0)|}{dt}=(1-p\lambda)m-\sum_{k=0}^{p}|S_t(A_k)|\pi_t^*(A_k,A_0)-|S_t(A_0)|\), and, for \(j=1,\ldots,p\), \(\frac{d|S_t(A_j)|}{dt}=\lambda m-|S_t(A_0)|\pi_t^*(A_0,A_j)-|S_t(A_j)|\pi_t^*(A_j,A_j)-|S_t(A_j)|\). Here \(\pi_t^*(A_i,A_j)\) is the probability that a critical type-\(A_i\) agent is matched to a waiting type-\(A_j\) agent under the paper’s hard-type priority rule. The paper computes with this formulation in its loss analysis and simulations.

The corresponding continuous problem is Patient High-Multiplicity Loss Evaluation. An instance gives \(p\), rational \(\lambda\) with \(p\lambda<1\), a density \(d\), a finite horizon \(T\), and an error tolerance \(\varepsilon\). Take the large-market scaling \(m\to\infty\), \(\alpha=d/m\), and let \(z_k(t)=|S_t(A_k)|/m\). Starting from an empty pool, evolve the limiting ODE, with match flows induced by the Patient rule. Return the total fraction of arrivals that perish by time \(T\), to additive error \(\varepsilon\). In this model it is
\(\ell_T=\frac1T\int_0^T\left[z_0(t)e^{-d\sum_{k=0}^{p}z_k(t)}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}z_j(t)e^{-d(z_0(t)+z_j(t))}\right]dt\).
The terms are the unmatched critical mass of each type, normalized by total arrivals. The type-specific loss vector can also be returned.

I expect the fixed-\(\tau\), finite-horizon version to be Class A: it is a finite-dimensional ODE integration and loss calculation. That is a prediction about the new approximation problem, not a complexity theorem in this paper. Theorem 5, also proved here, supports this mirror: for symmetric hard types it establishes ODE solution properties and, in the large-market, large-\(d\) regime, gives \(A_0\) loss \(e^{[-1/2+o(1)]d}\); each hard type and the total loss are \(e^{[-1/2+o(1)]d}\) when \(\lambda p\le 1/2\), and \(e^{-[1-1/(2p)-(p-1)\lambda+o(1)]d}\) when \(\lambda p>1/2\). Questions the mirror opens include polynomial dependence on \(\tau\), certified complexity for the stationary loss, and optimization over policies once waiting costs are added. This mirrors the paper’s analysis of the Patient policy, not its Greedy results or its policy-design problem.

There is an important limit to the case: the paper contains no named result establishing that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. Theorem 4 is the nearest defensible anchor under your allowance for a formulation that the paper computes with; if that allowance requires a discrete optimization encoding, it would not qualify either. The paper already develops a closely related ODE population model, so the mirror’s contribution is the computational question of certified evaluation, not the ODE itself. This is also why the case remains credible: the paper studies a finite stochastic market and derives the aggregate equations and high-multiplicity behavior as results. The softest point is that its compatibility model is stylized; a real exchange may need many detailed profiles to make types complete. That affects how large \(\tau\) becomes, but it does not undermine the high-multiplicity regime when many pairs share those profiles.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative starts with the anchor. Theorem 4 derives expected-flow ODEs for the Patient Algorithm; Theorem 5 proves existence and asymptotic loss bounds. Neither formulates an input/output computational problem or establishes a complexity result. So neither is a computational result of the kind ChoCo charts.

The proposed loss-evaluation problem exposes a dilemma. If its question is “what is the Patient Algorithm’s loss in the large-market model?”, the paper already supplies the continuous ODE and uses numerical integration to evaluate loss over finite horizons. Theorem 5 also gives stationary asymptotics. If the question is instead “what is the bit complexity of returning a certified \(\varepsilon\)-approximation?”, that guarantee would be new, but it is a numerical-computation question layered onto the paper’s existing model. It does not mirror a complexity result in the paper. The proponent’s formula for \(\ell_T\) is a rescaling of the paper’s own loss calculation, so its computational novelty rests on certification and runtime bounds rather than a newly exposed matching problem.

A stronger alternative would optimize matching policies in the continuous population, perhaps including the waiting cost that the paper itself leaves for future work. Here the paper’s finite-dimensional closure becomes a limit on the mirror: its ODE tracks type counts under two particular local policies and a specific independent compatibility model. For a general policy, equal type counts can conceal different realized compatibility graphs and neighborhood structure, which can change both the available matches and their future value. Capturing that structure may require tracking distributions of residual neighborhoods or graphs. That could lead to worthwhile research, but it is a new stochastic-network control model whose state and complexity need to be established; Theorem 4 does not anchor it.

The case against still has a serious weakness. High multiplicity is unusually plausible here: the paper studies very large kidney-exchange pools, the objective is aggregate loss, and patient–donor profiles can define types with shared arrival and critical-time parameters. The paper’s own ODE analysis supports the usefulness of that regime. I would reject the proposed evaluation task as a strong ChoCo mirror, while conceding that the material does not justify the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
