What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns mass-weighted imputation followed by Borda scoring; it covers no named computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes the procedure, and the runtime and winner-recovery claims are empirical.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which specified learners can weighted training and exact winner determination be done efficiently as functions of \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\)?
  • Under what type-level generative assumptions, if any, does the mass-weighted procedure recover the winner for complete hidden rankings?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational complexity result that qualifies as an anchor under the requested standard. Its only numbered theorem is Theorem 1: for any classification algorithm, using it to impute missing ballot information as in Algorithm 1 yields a social choice function. That is a characterization of the procedure, not a claim that a problem is tractable, hard, or has some other complexity classification. The performance guarantee is unnumbered, and the reported runtime and election results are empirical.

There is a plausible continuous version of the paper’s pipeline. For example, in a large platform election with a fixed candidate set, let each type be an observed partial ballot together with the features and missingness pattern used by the learner. Give each type a rational mass \(\mu_t\). For a fixed classifier and tie-breaking rule, train on the mass-weighted examples available at each rank, impute the missing ranks, and return the candidate with the highest expected Borda score. This is a recognizable high-multiplicity scenario: many voters, relatively few distinct observed ballot types. It mirrors the paper’s imputation-and-Borda procedure, but it is a proposed computational question, not a question established by Theorem 1. Its complexity would depend on the specified learner and, for exact winner determination, potentially on score margins.

So the positive case is plausible at the level of the *model*: the population can sensibly be represented by masses over partial-ballot types. But there is no eligible named result to anchor the required classification or to support a claimed tractability or hardness outcome. Presenting that proposed pipeline as a result-anchored mirror would overstate what the paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is the missing computational anchor. Theorem 1 says that imputation by a classifier defines a social choice function; it gives no complexity classification. The performance bound is unnumbered, the runtime claims are empirical, and the manipulation discussion invokes an axiomatic result. None establishes a tractability, hardness, or approximation result for the paper’s method.

The proposed mass-weighted pipeline is coherent for a large election with repeated partial ballots. Given masses \(\mu_p\) over observed ballot types \(p\), a fixed learner could train on weighted examples, impute each type, and select the winner by scores such as
\[ \mathrm{B}_\mu(c)=\sum_p \mu_p\bigl(m-\operatorname{pos}_{\widehat r(p)}(c)\bigr). \]
That is a sensible high-multiplicity formulation. To make it a computational problem, though, one must choose the classifier, features, training objective, numerical precision, and tie-breaking. Once fixed, the winner tally is a support-weighted sum; the computational work belongs to the chosen learning method. The paper neither states nor analyzes that weighted-learning problem. It would be a new research lead, not a mirror of a result this paper proves.

A stronger attempt to preserve the paper’s winner-recovery claim needs information the observed partial-ballot masses do not contain: the hidden rankings, or a specified model for how they were generated. Adding that information changes the input and turns the question into one about statistical inference under a generative model. Theorem 1’s strategic angle fares worse in an atomless population: an individual’s report has zero mass and cannot change the outcome. Studying a coalition with positive mass would replace individual manipulation with a different question.

So the case against is strong as a reason to reject this paper as a result-anchored ChoCo target. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could arise from the paper: the mass-weighted learner could merit independent study. The proponent’s own caveat is decisive here—the model is plausible, but the paper supplies no named computational result for it to mirror.
