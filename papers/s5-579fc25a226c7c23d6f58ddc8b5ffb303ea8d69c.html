The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow, and it does not meet your anchor rule: the paper has no named result about computational complexity—nothing asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results are economic characterizations and convergence results.

The closest bridge is Proposition 3, proved in the paper. It says that the optimal expected welfare in the finite-sample problem \(P_N\) converges, as \(N\) grows, to the optimal welfare in the informative-voting problem \(P\); when the optimal rule \(Q^{**}\) is nonconstant, welfare under \(P_N\) is strictly lower for every finite \(N\). This is a genuine finite-sample-to-continuum result, but it is not a computational-complexity result.

A plausible computational follow-up is IV Public-Good Provision. Take a society with complete types \(t=(\theta,w_j)\), where \(\theta\in\{\theta_L,\theta_H\}\), there are \(\tau\) skill classes \(w_j\), and \(\alpha_j\) is the fraction in skill class \(j\). At state \(p\), the type masses are \(\mu_{H,j}(p)=p\alpha_j\) and \(\mu_{L,j}(p)=(1-p)\alpha_j\). Given rational parameters, a uniform prior on \(p\), and, for example, \(K(q)=aq^2/2\), choose a nonnegative provision schedule \(Q:[0,1]\to\mathbb R_+\) maximizing
\[ \lambda\int_0^1\bigl[\bar v(p)Q(p)-K(Q(p))\bigr]\,dp, \qquad \lambda=\sum_j\frac{\alpha_j}{w_j}, \qquad \bar v(p)=\frac{p\theta_H+(1-p)\theta_L}{\lambda}, \]
subject, for every \(p\le p'\) and every skill class \(j\), to
\[ \theta_Lw_jQ(p)-K(Q(p))\ge\theta_Lw_jQ(p')-K(Q(p')), \]
\[ \theta_Hw_jQ(p)-K(Q(p))\le\theta_Hw_jQ(p')-K(Q(p')). \]
A solution is an exact finite description of an optimal schedule and its welfare. The regime is a large taxpayer population with relatively few taste-skill types. The paper’s Proposition 2, proved there, says that with skill heterogeneity an optimal rule is either constant or has two, three, or four pooling levels. That structure makes tractability a credible conjecture for this restricted input model, though the paper proves no algorithmic bound. I would expect Class A here, with further questions about general cost functions and representations of skill distributions.

The main weakness is substantial: the paper’s central problem \(P\) already has a continuum population. In Proposition 3, \(N\) counts sampled reporters, not the society. So this is a good candidate for computational analysis of a continuous-population model, and its sampling result gives a real bridge to finite samples; it is not a clean new continuization of a discrete society. The proposed problem is therefore a computational follow-up, not an anchor-backed mirror under your stated criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper contains no computational-complexity result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 characterize admissible and optimal provision rules; Proposition 3 proves welfare convergence. None gives a running time, a finite input model, or a complexity classification. The central problem \(P\) already has a continuum population, and its unknown is a provision schedule \(Q(p)\). Turning that analytic problem into an algorithmic one may be useful, but it starts a new computational project rather than continuizing a discrete problem from the paper.

That distinction defeats Proposition 3 as an anchor. Its \(N\) counts sampled reporters, while the society remains the continuum \(I=[0,1]\). The theorem studies how information and individual influence change with sample size; it does not take the population to a continuum. Treating \(N\) as the number of taxpayers would change the model: residents would have positive influence, the realized composition would be finite, and the incentive and financing conditions would need to be derived anew.

The proposed “IV Public-Good Provision” problem, linked to Proposition 2, is the strongest candidate. Finite skill classes, rational parameters, and a quadratic cost make a coherent computational formulation, and Proposition 2 supplies structure for it. But this computes an optimum for a model that is continuous from the outset. Proposition 2 does not establish an algorithm or complexity bound. The proposed encoding can turn the optimization into a computational question, though that would be an algorithmic study of an existing continuous model, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

A finite taxpayer model with many repeated \((\theta,w)\) types is also plausible; public finance is a natural high-multiplicity setting. Yet the paper’s sampling problem does not supply that model: its reporters are drawn from an already continuous economy. A finite-population version would need its own mechanism and incentive constraints, because the paper’s equal-payment conclusion relies on individuals having no direct influence in the continuum. That could support worthwhile research, but the paper gives no computational result for it to mirror.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s strict criterion: there is no named computational result here, and the nearest finite-\(N\) theorem varies the sample rather than the society. It is weaker as a universal claim. A computational treatment of \(P\) could be well posed and worth pursuing, and the population regime is sensible. I would reject it as a continuous mirror of this paper’s computational results, while acknowledging it as a possible adjacent project under a broader remit.
