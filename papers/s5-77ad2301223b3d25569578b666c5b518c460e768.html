What the mirror covers

No result survives as a continuous strategic mirror. Theorem 8 constructs an SPE and Theorem 10 conditionally characterizes SPE outcomes; the paper’s other competitive-equilibrium results are not covered by these anchors.

The case FOR (proponent)

A large labor market is a plausible setting for a continuous population model: many workers and employers could share a relatively small set of complete valuation and reservation-price types. Mass would be the share of each type, and a matching variable could record the mass assigned between each worker type and employer type.

But the paper supplies no named computational result of the kind this programme needs. Its results do not classify a problem as tractable or hard, or give an algorithm with a running time. So there is no qualifying anchor, and I cannot honestly state a prover-ready continuous computational problem *per anchor*.

The closest results are Theorem 8, proved in the paper, and Theorem 10, also proved there. Theorem 8 constructs a subgame-perfect equilibrium for any competitive-equilibrium rule: sellers report the maximum competitive prices for the true market, and buyers use specified continuation strategies; the resulting payoffs are the buyers’ minimum-equilibrium payoffs and the sellers’ maximum-equilibrium surplus. Theorem 10 says that, for rules in \(C^+\), any SPE satisfying the stated conditions on sellers’ reports yields a matching optimal for the true market and the maximum competitive-equilibrium seller surplus. These are equilibrium existence and characterization results, not computational anchors.

The main obstacle to turning them into a computational mirror is strategic behavior. In an atomless market, an individual’s report changes zero mass, so the price and allocation effects central to these theorems may disappear. Making each whole type mass act as one strategic player would preserve market impact, but would replace the paper’s individual, noncooperative agents with coordinated blocks. That is a substantial change to the problem.

So the positive case is that a high-multiplicity labor-market regime is sensible and suggests a continuous assignment model. Its weakest point is decisive under the requested standard: the paper does not give a named computational result to anchor that model, and the most natural continuum limit changes the strategic question the authors study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its numbered results concern competitive equilibria, strategic incentives, and Nash or subgame-perfect equilibrium outcomes; none gives a complexity classification or an algorithm with a running-time bound. Theorem 8 constructs an SPE, and Theorem 10 characterizes some SPE outcomes, but neither is a computational theorem. Turning either into “compute an SPE” would also require specifying how the paper’s general price and matching rules are represented as inputs. The authors do not pose that problem.

The proposed large labor market is a credible high-multiplicity setting. Workers with the same valuation vectors and employers with the same reservation prices can be represented by masses, with a mass matching between types. That makes sense as a continuous assignment model. It does not, by itself, preserve the paper’s strategic question. Theorem 8 relies on sellers’ first-mover advantage in a game where individual sellers report prices. In an atomless version, one seller’s report does not change the aggregate market or its competitive prices. Buyers’ individual reports likewise do not change the aggregate market, though their effect on their own assignment would depend on how the mechanism treats individuals.

The stronger alternative is to let each whole type cohort choose its report. That preserves market impact, but makes a cohort a coordinated player controlling a positive mass of agents. It changes the paper’s individual Nash and SPE game into a game of collective type-level deviations. Theorem 10 has the same problem: its conditions characterize outcomes of the individual-report game, while the cohort version asks a different strategic question. Either version might be worth studying, but neither is a continuous computational mirror of a named result in this paper.

So the labor-market example does not fail the high-multiplicity test; it is the against-case’s weak point. A continuous assignment or equilibrium-computation problem could be useful in its own right. The negative case is narrower: this paper offers no computational claim for ChoCo to continuize, and its main strategic results do not transfer to an atomless population without changing who the players are and how they can deviate. That defeats Theorems 8 and 10 as anchors, though it does not prove that no useful continuous computation can be built from the assignment model.
