What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its numbered propositions concern logical relationships and properties of the update approaches. The closest computationally relevant result is Proposition 19, proved in this paper: for a graph \(G=(V,E)\) and scenario \(\sigma\), any approach \(\Omega\in\{A,E,F,R\}\) reaches a fixpoint within \(\sum_{v\in V}|\mathrm{Mod}(\sigma(v))|-|V|\) iterations. That bounds the number of iterations, but does not classify the complexity of a problem or analyze an algorithm’s running time in the input size. The Equibel implementation is not a named complexity result either.

So, under your anchor rule, there are no valid anchors and no per-anchor continuous problems I can honestly present as mirrors of this paper’s results. A continuous-population model of its agents and belief updates might be proposed as new work, but this paper supplies no named computational result for that case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor audit is right: Proposition 19 is the only plausible computational anchor, and it gives a bound on the number of iterations, not a complexity classification. The other numbered results concern logical properties and relationships between update rules. Proposition 19’s bound counts how many model possibilities can be deleted at individual vertices before a fixed point; it does not bound the running time of an algorithm for a named computational problem.

The strongest proposed mirror would put a large population of agents with finitely many belief types on a weighted communication network, then ask how efficiently to compute the fixed point or the fraction of agents whose beliefs change. But the paper minimizes disagreement by inclusion of sets of edge-level differences. Under that rule, positive population masses do not supply a graded cost: duplicating an interaction does not make its disagreement count proportionally more. In a finite type quotient, Proposition 19’s analogue would count model deletions per type and remain independent of type masses. Making mass affect the objective would require replacing the paper’s set-inclusion preference with a weighted sum or another aggregate loss. That could be a reasonable new model, but the paper supplies no computational result about it.

There is a second obstacle to a strong high-multiplicity mirror. The update rules use graph distances and neighborhoods. If types retain enough structural information to preserve those relationships, a large network may still require many types. If a finite quotient suffices, the population multiplicities can be recorded, but they do not carry the paper’s iteration bound. A graphon or kernel version is conceivable; it would pose a new problem about continuous network dynamics, rather than extend a computational result established here.

So the negative case is strong for the claim that this paper offers a worthwhile *result-level* mirror for ChoCo: it has no named computational complexity result, and its nearest candidate is tied to discrete vertex-by-vertex model deletion. The universal claim that no scenario could make a continuous treatment worthwhile is weaker. A graphon-based computational study could be independently interesting, though this paper does not make it a mirror of one of its results.
