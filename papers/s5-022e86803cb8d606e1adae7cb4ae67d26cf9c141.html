The continuous mirror question

Given a finite MDP \(M\), its occupancy polytope \(\mathcal O\), a finite support \(T\) of normalized reward types \(R_t\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), find \(x\in\mathcal O\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[\langle x,R_t\rangle=\max_{y\in\mathcal O}\langle y,R_t\rangle]\). The output is a maximizing occupancy measure, representing a policy with maximum individually optimal population mass.

The model it lives in

A shared MDP with a population distribution over complete reward types, where each type consists of a normalized reward vector and its mass \(\mu_t\). The decision variable is an occupancy measure \(x\in\mathcal O\); the objective is the total mass of types for whom \(x\) is individually optimal.

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover Theorem 4's plurality inapproximability and Theorem 5's hardness for high-\(\alpha\) approval. They leave the fairness results, the low-\(\alpha\) approval guarantee, Borda results, and experiments unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the exact and approximation complexities in \(|S|\), \(|A|\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\), including for fixed \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the large-population version of the paper’s AI alignment setting. Keep the shared MDP and its occupancy polytope \(\mathcal O\), but represent people by a distribution over reward types. A type \(t\) is a normalized reward vector \(R_t\) on the MDP’s state-action pairs; reward vectors that differ only by a positive affine transformation can be treated as the same type, since the paper’s rules depend on ordinal preferences over policies. The input is a finite support \(T\) and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society with type \(t\). The answer is an occupancy measure \(x\in\mathcal O\), hence a stochastic policy.

A plausible regime is a public-service AI used by millions of people, with preferences represented by a finite vocabulary of recurring constituencies or reward templates. The population can be much larger than the number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct types. This is particularly credible for the paper’s motivation of aligning AI with large groups and societies. It does require exact or model-level shared types; if every person has a distinct reward vector, then \(\tau\) may be close to the headcount and the high-multiplicity benefit disappears.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper in Appendix A.1 by an approximation-preserving reduction from maximum independent set, using the cited hardness result Theorem 7. Theorem 4 says: for any fixed \(\epsilon\in(0,1)\), unless \(\mathbf P=\mathbf{NP}\), there is no polynomial-time \(1/n^{1/2-\epsilon}\)-approximation algorithm for the maximum plurality score. The paper’s reduction makes each graph vertex an agent whose reward encodes its incident edges; agents who can simultaneously be fully satisfied form an independent set.

The corresponding question is Continuous-Mass Plurality Policy Aggregation. Given a finite MDP \(M\), its occupancy polytope \(\mathcal O\), and a rational distribution \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) over reward types, find \(x\in\mathcal O\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbf 1[x\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal O}\langle y,R_t\rangle]\). A solution is a maximizing occupancy measure and its approved mass. This asks for the policy that maximizes the fraction of the population for whom it is individually optimal.

The reduction gives a strong reason to expect hardness to transfer. For its instances, give each vertex type equal mass. The approved types correspond exactly to an independent set, so the best approved mass is the maximum independent-set size divided by the number of vertex types. Replicating every type \(K\) times makes the population arbitrarily large without changing the mass objective or the optimum. Thus the inapproximability transfers as a bound in the support parameter \(\tau\): the paper writes \(n\) for the number of agents, which in the reduction is the number of graph vertices and hence the number of types. The combinatorics remain in the diversity of types and the MDP, even when every type represents a large cohort. This is Class B hardness, not a claim of continuum-specific hardness.

A useful second anchor is Theorem 5, also proved in the paper, in Appendix A.2. It says that for every fixed \(\alpha\in(7/8,1]\), computing a policy with the highest \(\alpha\)-approval score is NP-hard, even with binary reward vectors and closed-form \(F_i\) for every agent. The paper proves this by reduction from MAX-2SAT. Its construction gives three reward profiles per clause; above the \(7/8\) quantile, an approved profile forces the corresponding policy’s variable probabilities to cross the \(1/2\) threshold, linking approvals to satisfied clauses.

The mirror is Continuous-Mass \(\alpha\)-Approval Policy Aggregation. Given \(M,\mathcal O\), a rational type distribution \((\mu_t)\), and fixed \(\alpha\in(7/8,1]\), find \(x\in\mathcal O\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\mathbf 1[F_t(\langle x,R_t\rangle)\ge\alpha]\), where \(F_t(v)=\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal O\cap\{y:\langle y,R_t\rangle\le v\})/\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal O)\), using relative volume in the affine hull when needed. The solution is a policy attaining maximum approved population mass. The MAX-2SAT construction assigns mass \(1/(3q)\) to each of the three clause profiles, where \(q\) is the number of clauses; its maximum approval mass is the maximum number of satisfiable clauses divided by \(3q\). Replicating each profile preserves that objective while producing a large cohort per type. The exact optimization hardness therefore transfers here too. The clause construction’s closed-form return distributions make this a particularly clean anchor: hardness does not rest on having to compute obscure volumetric distributions.

These are the paper’s finite-agent aggregation questions with the counted agents replaced by population mass. They do not mistake the paper’s existing continuous policy space for population continuization: \(\mathcal O\) remains the same outcome space, while the society becomes a distribution over complete preference types. That extension is plausible for the authors because their stated motivation is alignment with large groups and societies, and their voting objectives already aggregate agents by counts of who approves.

The weakest point is that both reductions need the number of distinct types to grow with the hard instance. They establish hardness when \(\tau\) varies, even if the headcount is arbitrarily larger, but do not show hardness for a fixed or very small type support. The questions they generate are whether the problems become tractable or fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), how the approximation barrier depends on \(\tau\) versus MDP size, and where the \(\alpha\)-approval problem changes from easy to hard between the paper’s low-quantile guarantees and the range above \(7/8\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that neither theorem shows complexity driven by a large population. In Theorem 4’s reduction, each graph vertex is a distinct reward type with mass \(1/n\); the approved mass is exactly the independent-set size divided by \(n\). Replicating each type \(K\) times increases the headcount but leaves the support and objective unchanged. That makes the result a hardness statement in the number of types and the MDP, not evidence that replacing people by mass creates a new computational challenge. But the programme explicitly treats hardness that survives continuization as a valid result, so this limits what the theorem establishes; it does not defeat the mirror.

Theorem 5 survives the same translation. Give each of the three clause types mass \(1/(3q)\). The maximum approved mass is then the maximum number of satisfiable clauses divided by \(3q\), and the return distributions remain the paper’s closed-form distributions. Replicating those types again makes the population large without changing the optimization problem. The reduction’s reward profiles are artificial, and real AI stakeholders may not fall into clause-like categories. Still, a hardness reduction is not meant to supply the application scenario; recurring preference templates in a public-service AI could supply one. I don’t see a principled reason the authors would reject the resulting question as theirs.

There is a real concern about the regime: a reward type here must capture the person’s complete preferences over policies, and in a high-dimensional policy space exact repetitions may be uncommon. With a continuously varying distribution of rewards, plurality’s requirement of being individually optimal can also make every policy’s approved mass zero in generic cases. But that does not rule out the stronger finite-support mirror: large groups sharing normalized reward templates are plausible, and \(\alpha\)-approval for \(\alpha<1\) need not collapse under continuous reward variation. This is a caveat about which regimes are useful, not a universal obstruction.

I therefore cannot honestly defeat either anchor under the stated rules. The paper’s continuous occupancy polytope does not itself settle the population question, and the mirrors are weighted high-multiplicity versions of the paper’s voting problems. Their value may be incremental, and the reductions establish hardness only as the support grows; neither point shows that the mirrors are unworthy. The negative case is weak precisely where it needs to be strongest: a credible population scenario and two named computational results both survive.
