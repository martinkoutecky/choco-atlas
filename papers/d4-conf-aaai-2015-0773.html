The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the narrow fact: the paper has no numbered complexity theorem. That fact does not establish that it has no computational result to mirror. Its proposed method, culminating in formulation (MP4), is a concrete algorithmic contribution: it fits a shared weight vector while allowing evaluator-specific thresholds and selecting basis functions. The paper also reports an empirical result for that method.

A plausible continuous question would take a large pool of reviewers assessing outputs from the same optimizer or simulator. Each evaluator type would encode a shared rubric and response pattern; a rational mass \(\mu_t\) would represent its share of reviewers. One could fit common weights and type-specific thresholds by minimizing mass-weighted slack, retaining (MP4)’s basis-function choices. The question could be parameterized by the number of features, evaluator types, comparisons, candidate basis functions, and input bit length. That is a population-continuous version of the paper’s estimation task, not of its outcome space.

The strongest case against it is about fit. In the paper, each expert supplies an observed table of judgments, and the method estimates one criterion from that finite evidence. To preserve those judgments exactly, an evaluator type may need to include their responses across all queried pairs; typically there may be few repeated types. To group evaluators by rubric instead requires a shared model of how rubrics generate responses, which the paper does not provide. The continuous version also estimates a population consensus criterion, rather than fitting the particular panel the paper studies. And its output is a scoring objective for another system to use, not a collective choice among alternatives. That makes it a plausible inverse-preference-learning extension, but a weak anchor for a continuous computational social choice programme.

I cannot honestly make the universal negative case airtight. The weighted version is mathematically natural, and a large pool of evaluators sharing rubrics is a plausible regime; the paper’s authors could recognize it as an extension of their method. The strongest negative conclusion is that the paper supplies no formal complexity result to mirror and that the promising continuous question needs extra modeling assumptions and a social-choice connection. That falls short of showing that no worthwhile scenario exists.
