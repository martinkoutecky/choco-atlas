The continuous mirror question

Given τ cohorts with rational masses μθ summing to \(1\), per-member quotas \(qθ\), horizon \(H\), and an unknown distribution \(G\) over \([0,̄x]^τ\), each round observe an independent shock vector \(Z_s ∼ G\). Choose allocation masses \(a_{s,θ} ∈ [0,μθ]\) with total supply ρ = ∑θ μθqθ per round and cumulative cohort quotas \(Hμθqθ\). Does there exist an online algorithm whose high probability per-member regret for every cohort, relative to the offline policy maximizing expected welfare subject to the same expected quotas, is sublinear in \(H\)?

The model it lives in

A continuous population split among τ cohorts of masses μθ. Members of a cohort share its value \(Z_θ\) for each arriving batch; the planner observes the vector \(Z\) and allocates mass \(a_θ\). Cohort-level averages may be fractional even though each person's received bundle is integral. The offline benchmark chooses πθ(Z) ∈ [0,μθ] to maximize \(ᵓ_G[∑_θ Z_θπ_θ(Z)]\), subject to ∑_θ π_θ(Z) = ρ and ᵓ_G[π_θ(Z)] = μθqθ.

The objection that survived

The mirror replaces the theorem's independent valuations across agents with a shared value shock within each cohort, so Theorem 2's regret bound does not transfer to this model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror addresses the truthful regret question in Theorem 2. It leaves Theorem 1's strategic incentive guarantee aside and does not claim that either theorem proves a result for the correlated cohort model.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an online algorithm achieve sublinear per-member regret with explicit dependence on τ, \(H\), and the input bit lengths when \(G\) is unknown?
  • Can cohort mass allocations be rounded to integral individual bundles while preserving the quota and regret guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make Theorem 2 (Individual Regret) the sole anchor and lead. It is proved in this paper. Assuming all agents report truthfully, Algorithm 1’s epoch based online allocation mechanism satisfies, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), for every agent \(i\),
\[ \operatorname{Regret}_i(T) \le \frac{4\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}-1} \sqrt{nT\log\!\left(\frac{4n\log_2 T+nT}{\delta}\right)}\,\bar{x} = O\!\left(\sqrt{nT\log(nT/\delta)}\right). \]
Here regret is \(T\) times agent \(i\)’s expected utility under the optimal offline allocation policy minus that agent’s realized utility over the \(T\) rounds. The algorithm estimates the common valuation distribution from reports, periodically updates its greedy allocation policy, and observes the stated regret bound. This is an algorithmic performance result, not a theorem that classifies a problem as being in P or NP-hard; the supplied paper gives no named hardness result.

The mirror I find credible is Cohort Online Allocation Regret. Consider a large population partitioned into \(\tau\) cohorts. A type \(\theta\) is a complete allocation-relevant description of a member: the cohort has mass \(\mu_\theta\), a per-member allocation quota \(q_\theta\), and a common response to the current item batch. Each round, the batch has total allocation mass \(\rho=\sum_\theta\mu_\theta q_\theta\). Its value vector \(Z\) is drawn independently across rounds from an unknown distribution \(G\) on \([0,\bar{x}]^\tau\); \(Z_\theta\) is the value of the batch to members of cohort \(\theta\). The planner observes \(Z\) and assigns mass \(a_\theta\) from each cohort, with \(0\le a_\theta\le\mu_\theta\) and \(\sum_\theta a_\theta=\rho\). A randomized assignment within a cohort gives each actual person an integral bundle; \(a_\theta\) is the cohort average, so it can be fractional.

For a horizon \(H\), the offline benchmark is a policy \(\pi\) maximizing expected welfare subject to the type quotas:
\[ \max_{\pi}\ \mathbb E_G\!\left[\sum_\theta Z_\theta\pi_\theta(Z)\right], \qquad \pi(Z)\in[0,\mu],\quad \sum_\theta\pi_\theta(Z)=\rho,\quad \mathbb E_G[\pi_\theta(Z)]=\mu_\theta q_\theta. \]
The online problem is: given \((\mu,q,\rho,\bar{x},H,\delta)\) and sample access to \(G\), design an allocation policy that respects the total type quotas over the horizon and, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), has sublinear regret for every cohort relative to this offline benchmark. Cohort \(\theta\)’s per-member regret compares \(H\,\mathbb E[Z_\theta\pi_\theta^*(Z)]/\mu_\theta\) with its realized total value \(\sum_{s=1}^H Z_{s,\theta}a_{s,\theta}/\mu_\theta\). The question is about computing the underlying online allocation policy and its guarantee, rather than about an encoding.

The intended regime is a platform allocating repeated batches to many users, with \(N\) much larger than \(\tau\): users in a cohort share a quota and valuation response, while actual assignments remain integral. The batch supply and quotas scale with the population, so mass allocation represents cohort averages. This is a plausible high-multiplicity analogue of the paper’s quota constrained allocation problem. Its optimization structure also looks promising: for a finite empirical sample, the offline policy has a finite dimensional price vector, and finding the best allocation for one observed value vector amounts to filling the cohort capacities in order of value plus price. I expect the mirror to be tractable for fixed \(\tau\), in the spirit of Class A; the open work is to establish the regret guarantee and its dependence on \(\tau\) and the input size.

The weakest point is the change in the stochastic model. The paper gives each individual an independent valuation draw, while this mirror uses a shared cohort value for each batch so that mass allocation and online learning remain meaningful. An exactly observed atomless population with independent draws could reveal its valuation distribution immediately, removing the learning problem. The paper itself identifies cross-agent correlation as a natural direction for future work, which makes the cohort version recognizable as a related problem, but Theorem 2 does not prove its result. This case therefore covers the paper’s truthful regret result at the level of quota constrained allocation and learning; it does not claim a continuous analogue of the paper’s strategic incentive guarantee. The main follow-up is whether a finite population rounds to this cohort model with a controlled regret loss.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the paper’s strongest anchor, but it bounds statistical regret, not the computational complexity of a problem. The policy calculation \(\lambda^*(\widehat F)\) is treated as an efficient black box; the theorem analyzes learning from samples and enforcing allocation caps, not runtime, pricing, or an algorithmic complexity landscape of the kind ChoCo targets.

More fundamentally, the paper’s own assumption \(F^{\otimes n}\) makes the exact population limit erase the learning problem. If each member of a growing cohort has an independent valuation draw, then a continuum cross-section reveals \(F\) exactly in each round. With quotas aggregated by type and supply scaled with population, the planner can use \(\lambda^*(F)\) from the outset; the sampling error behind Theorem 2 disappears, as does the cross-sectional randomness that drives its regret bound. Keeping quotas for named individuals avoids that collapse only by retaining a finite-agent problem. In the continuum, individual quotas have zero mass; aggregate quotas are meaningful, but under the paper’s independence assumption they can be met by the known-distribution policy.

The cohort mirror’s common value \(Z_\theta\) for everyone in a cohort is the strongest way to preserve nontrivial learning: population size no longer supplies more observations, so uncertainty remains across rounds. But it does so by replacing the paper’s independent cross-agent draws with perfectly correlated cohort shocks. That is a plausible related model—the paper itself lists cross-agent correlation as future work—but it is not the population limit of the theorem being mirrored. It creates an online stochastic allocation problem with a new source of uncertainty.

So the negative case is strongest for the paper’s proved regret result under its stated model: its natural continuous limit loses the learning question. I would not claim the broader correlated-cohort problem is worthless; it is a credible extension. That is where the case against *any* worthwhile mirror is weakest.
