The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of sellers, a target seller \(i\), fixed strategies for the other sellers, and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete buyer types encoding ratings, eligibility, and effort costs, compute a mass allocation of bribery actions that maximizes \(i\)'s payoff under the paper's budget and shared-buyer constraints. Is the analogue of Proposition 1's greedy rule—bribing the lowest-rated eligible mass first, allowing a partial mass at the cutoff—always a best response?

The model it lives in

The sellers remain finitely many strategic players. Buyers form a population with rational type masses \(\mu_t\); each complete type records the buyer's relevant rating and eligibility state across sellers and the paper's effort-to-rating costs. The target seller chooses how much eligible mass of each type to bribe, subject to the budget and shared-buyer constraints, to maximize its payoff.

The objection that survived

Shared-buyer constraints may require joint types across sellers, increasing the support and making the proponent's one-seller type description incomplete; the proponent does not explicitly address this, though joint types can preserve those constraints in a plausible repeated-state regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1's greedy best response and can express Lemma 1's no-profit threshold as a sufficient certificate; it leaves the Nash-equilibrium existence claim and Corollary 1's social-welfare comparison aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the greedy best response remain optimal when effort costs vary across buyer types?
  • Can best responses in the mass model be combined into an efficient method for computing an equilibrium of the finite-seller game?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is buyer mass. The many agents are buyers of the same app in a large marketplace; sellers can remain a finite set of strategic players. For a fixed seller, a type records everything the paper’s model uses about a buyer: whether they have already rated the app and, if so, their current rating. Under the paper’s assumptions, those characteristics also determine how effort changes a rating and what it costs. A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with rational masses summing to one. A platform may have hundreds of thousands of buyers but only a handful of relevant states—such as unrated and each of five star ratings—so the number of buyers can greatly exceed the support size \(\tau\).

The population is what becomes continuous: a seller chooses how much mass of each type to bribe, and the resulting rating and remaining potential-buyer pool determine its payoff. The rating scale and the strategic sellers do not need to be continuized. This is a natural high-multiplicity reading of the paper’s market: its buyers are already numerous, and its greedy strategy sorts buyers by their current rating. Fractional mass is particularly apt when a seller can reach a fraction of a large buyer group, rather than target the group at one-person granularity.

My lead anchor is Proposition 1. It says that, holding the other sellers’ strategies fixed, seller \(s_i\)’s greedy bribery strategy dominates every other strategy with the same budget. The paper presents this as its result, drawing on conclusions from Grandi and Turrini [6]; it is not a complexity classification. The matching continuous problem is Continuous Mass Bribery Best Response:

  • Instance: A finite set of buyer types with rational masses, each type’s rated or unrated status and current rating, the seller’s initial rating state and remaining potential-buyer mass, the paper’s linear utility parameter \(k\), a budget \(B\), and the other sellers’ fixed strategies.
  • Question: Which feasible allocation of bribery effort across buyer types maximizes the seller’s payoff, given the paper’s utility \(u=kP\bar r\) and effort costs?
  • Solution: An allocation of mass and effort to each type, together with its resulting payoff.

Here the continuous analogue of greedy can split the mass at the cutoff type: raise the lowest-rated already-rated mass first, then consider unrated mass, as the paper’s strategy does. I expect this restricted problem to be Class A: sort the \(\tau\) types by rating, apply the greedy rule, and evaluate the resulting payoff using rational arithmetic. This is the same bribery decision the authors study, with a large buyer cohort represented by type masses; it is not a simpler problem obtained by removing the seller’s payoff or objective.

A second anchor, Lemma 1, says that seller \(s_i\) has no profitable strategy if \(|B_i|/N\ge k\), where \(|B_i|\) is its mass of already-rated buyers and \(N\) is its potential-buyer count in the lemma’s notation. The paper proves this here using Proposition 1: its argument shows that a profitable strategy requires \(|B_i|/N<k\). The continuous version is Continuous Bribery-Proofness: given the same type-mass market and payoff data, determine whether any feasible bribery allocation yields positive net payoff; return either a profitable allocation or a certificate that none exists. Replacing counts by masses preserves the ratio, so the lemma supplies a no-profit certificate whenever the stated threshold holds. I also expect this problem to be Class A under the paper’s assumptions, by optimizing with the mass version of the greedy rule and checking the resulting rational payoff. The threshold alone is sufficient for a “no” answer; below it, it does not establish that a profitable strategy exists.

These mirrors cover the paper’s greedy bribery and single-seller profitability results, not its full equilibrium analysis. The paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time-complexity, or parameterized-complexity result. Its Theorem 5 says that the bribery game has at least one Nash equilibrium, invoking Nash’s theorem [14]; it provides no method for computing one. I would not use it as a stronger computational anchor. Nor would I rely on Corollary 1, whose stated comparison between equilibrium bribery and the social optimum is less firmly supported than the greedy and threshold results.

Further questions follow naturally: does the greedy rule survive type-dependent bribe prices, utility \(u=kP\bar r^\Omega\) for \(\Omega\ne1\), or shared type-mass constraints across sellers? Can one compute an equilibrium when sellers compete for the same continuous buyer mass? Those extensions would test how far the tractable mirror reaches.

The weakest point is that the anchors are simple, model-specific structural results, not a deep complexity landscape. Also, Proposition 1’s support in this paper is brief, and the continuous split at a type cutoff must be checked against its precise assumptions. The case is therefore a credible, narrow mirror of the bribery allocation problem—not evidence that every part of the paper has a useful continuum analogue.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper is that its main object is a game among a finite set of sellers, while buyers are a pool those sellers can target. Making the buyer pool continuous gives sellers mass-valued actions; it does not make the strategic population continuous. Theorem 5 is an existence claim, not an algorithmic result, and a useful computational question about equilibrium would need additional structure beyond the paper’s buyer-count model. That question would be about the seller game, not a high-multiplicity population.

Proposition 1 is the other analyst’s strongest anchor, but their proposed mass-allocation problem needs care to remain the same problem. In the full market, a buyer’s eligibility can depend on which sellers they have interacted with and which other sellers’ strategies have already claimed them. An exact buyer type may therefore need to record their state across all sellers, as well as their rating and bribability. With arbitrary real-valued ratings, that type space could have support close to the number of buyers. Alternatively, reducing types to “rated at value \(r\)” and “unrated” for one fixed seller loses the shared-buyer constraints of the multi-seller setting. The paper’s simplified strategy of choosing a number of buyers avoids this allocation problem, but then the proposed type-distribution optimization is no longer what Proposition 1 establishes.

Lemma 1 gives a narrower target still: a sufficient no-profit condition in terms of \(|B_i|/N\) and \(k\). Replacing counts by masses preserves that ratio, but it does not produce the proposed decision problem of finding any profitable allocation. That asks for an exact profitability analysis the lemma does not provide. A richer version could be worth studying, but it would be a new problem rather than a mirror of this result.

This is a limited negative case, not a convincing universal one. Buyers of a popular app could plausibly fall into a small number of rating and interaction-history types, and a faithful mass model could preserve the shared constraints by encoding them in joint types. So the paper does admit a credible high-multiplicity regime. The objection is that the case for makes that regime look simpler than the paper’s full market, while the more complete version may lose the small-support advantage. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario.
