The continuous mirror question

Given an obstacle \(o\), traversal factor \(k\in[0,1)\), and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over locations in \([0,o)\cup(o,1]\), determine the smallest \(\rho\) for which a deterministic rule \(F\) maps every such society to a feasible pathway \((a,b)\), is mass-GSP, and satisfies \(\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(F(\mu))\le \rho\min_{(a,b)}\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(a,b)\). Mass-GSP means no positive-mass coalition can change its reports within its members’ regions so that every member strictly lowers its true-location cost.

The model it lives in

Location types are starting coordinates \(x\), with mass \(\mu_x\); the rule chooses pathway endpoints \((a,b)\), and minimizes weighted average travel cost \(\sum_x\mu_x c_{a,b}(x)\) subject to mass-GSP. The TwoExtreme rule uses only the extreme occupied locations.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers deterministic mass-GSP social-cost approximation through TwoExtreme and the question of its best achievable ratio. It leaves maximum-cost, randomized, optimal-pathway characterization, and the real-line model results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For \(0<k<1\), what is the optimal worst-case approximation ratio among deterministic mass-GSP rules, and is the \(1/k\) bound for TwoExtreme tight?
  • At \(k=0\), does the finite-population lower bound rule out every finite uniform ratio over the proposed mass-GSP domain?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror is the paper’s social-cost problem with group strategyproofness. Its natural continuous object is the population distribution over residents’ starting locations; the pathway and each resident’s cost retain the paper’s exact meaning.

Consider a city split by an impassable barrier. A location type is a starting coordinate \(x\), with its side of the barrier determined by \(x\). Residents of a type share the same travel cost, and \(k\) is common to the population, as in the paper. A census gives a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) occupied location types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). The regime is many residents but relatively few distinct origin locations: \(n\gg\tau\). The agency chooses one public pathway \((a,b)\), and its objective is average resident cost,
\[ \mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(a,b)=\sum_t\mu_t\,c_{a,b}(t). \]
Thus the population—not the pathway or the outcome—is what has been continuized.

For the incentive constraint, use a direct rule \(F(\mu)=(a,b)\) and require mass-group-strategyproofness: no positive-mass coalition can change its reports within its members’ respective regions so that every member strictly lowers their cost at their true location. This is a substantive continuum version of the paper’s GSP condition. On a profile of \(n\) equally weighted residents, an individual deviation changes mass \(1/n\), so a rule satisfying this condition also satisfies the paper’s individual SP condition on those profiles.

My lead mirror is Mass-GSP Social Pathway Approximation. Its domain consists of finite-support distributions over rational locations, and its solution is a mass-GSP rule mapping every such distribution to feasible pathway endpoints. The objective is to minimize the worst-case ratio of \(\mathrm{SC}_{\mu}(F(\mu))\) to the unrestricted optimal social cost.

The main anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper: the deterministic TwoExtreme mechanism, which connects the rightmost left-side location \(x_r\) to the leftmost right-side location \(y_l\), is GSP and achieves social-cost approximation ratio \(n/[1+k(n-1)]\) on an \(n\)-agent profile. The paper also gives a matching tight example for this mechanism. This is an algorithmic approximation result, not a cited theorem. For a rational distribution with common denominator \(N\), the finite profile with \(N\mu_t\) residents of each type has exactly the same normalized objective and the same optimum. The bound therefore carries over as \(N/[1+k(N-1)]\), which is at most \(1/k\) for \(k>0\). TwoExtreme itself needs only the support extremes, so it can be computed in \(O(\tau)\) time. Its worst-case ratio over distributions approaches \(1/k\), using the paper’s tight construction with one side’s mass tending to zero. I expect this mirror to be tractable for positive \(k\), with an \(O(1/k)\) approximation. Whether another mass-GSP rule improves that factor remains open.

A second, sharper boundary question is Zero-Toll Mass-SP Social Pathway Approximation: when \(k=0\), can any deterministic mass-GSP rule give a finite approximation ratio uniformly over rational population distributions? The anchor is Lemma 8, also proved in this paper: when \(k=0\), every strategyproof mechanism for \(n\) agents has social-cost approximation ratio at least \(n-1\). It is an approximation impossibility, not a conventional NP-hardness result. If a mass-GSP rule had a finite uniform ratio, restricting it to profiles of \(n\) equally weighted residents would give a strategyproof \(n\)-agent mechanism, contradicting that lower bound as \(n\) grows. So this mirror has no finite uniform ratio: the lower bound transfers from population multiplicity rather than disappearing in the continuous model. At \(k=0\), TwoExtreme’s bound also grows with the common denominator, and the paper’s lower bound shows this dependence cannot be removed by another SP rule.

I think authors and a referee could recognize both questions as theirs: they retain the same pathway, cost functions, social objective, and coalition incentives, and the finite equal-mass profiles embed directly. The scope is specific: these mirrors cover deterministic GSP social-cost approximation and its zero-toll lower bound, not the paper’s maximum-cost or randomized results. The paper states no NP-hardness or standard complexity-class classification; its relevant named anchors are approximation guarantees and impossibility bounds.

The weakest point is the incentive interpretation. In a nonatomic population, an individual has zero mass, so ordinary unilateral SP can become vacuous. Mass-GSP addresses that by testing positive-mass coalitions, and its restriction recovers individual deviations on every finite equal-mass profile. Still, authors might regard coalition deviations by divisible population mass as a changed model. A natural next question is whether the positive-\(k\) factor \(1/k\) is optimal among mass-GSP rules, and how far this formulation extends beyond finite-support location distributions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that both proposed mirrors collapse to weighted versions of finite-profile results, without opening a new algorithmic bottleneck. TwoExtreme only needs the extreme reported locations, and the paper already reduces the social-cost optimum to a finite set of candidate endpoints. For a rational society whose masses have common denominator \(q\), expanding it into \(q\) agents preserves the approximation ratio: dividing total social cost by \(q\) changes both the mechanism’s cost and the optimum by the same factor. Theorem 6 therefore gives the mass version directly, with ratio \(q/[1+k(q-1)]\). The intrinsic bound uses the *reduced* common denominator; an arbitrary choice of \(q\) for the same distribution is not meaningful. As \(q\) grows, the bound approaches \(1/k\) for \(k>0\), and is unbounded at \(k=0\).

That makes Theorem 6 a weak fit for a programme seeking new computational questions: its continuous form needs no continuous-optimization machinery, and its mechanism is computable from the support extremes. But this does not defeat the mirror. An open question about the best mass-GSP approximation ratio could still be worthwhile, and the programme explicitly treats the discrete-to-continuous dictionary as a deliverable.

Lemma 8 transfers even more directly. Restrict a mass-GSP rule to profiles of \(q\) equally weighted agents. A one-agent deviation changes positive mass, so the restriction is strategyproof. The paper’s \(q-1\) lower bound at \(k=0\) then rules out a finite uniform approximation ratio over rational societies as \(q\) grows. This is a genuine continuous-population question, although its answer follows immediately from the paper’s finite result. Calling that transfer “already done” would be a weak objection: the paper does not itself formulate or prove the mass version.

The location-type objection is also not decisive. Exact locations vary continuously, so repeated identical locations may be an artificial model for some cities. But a finite set of origin sites, buildings, or census locations gives a plausible high-multiplicity regime; the prompt explicitly permits finding a better scenario than the paper’s motivating one. And replacing individual SP with mass-GSP is a defensible way to avoid vacuous individual deviations in a continuum. The proposed rule and lower-bound transfer remain meaningful under that choice.

So the honest negative case is weak. The two mirrors may offer little new algorithmic machinery, but I cannot defeat either anchor under ChoCo’s stated criteria. Theorem 6 survives as a tractable mass-GSP approximation question; Lemma 8 survives as a population-multiplicity lower bound with a direct finite-to-continuous transfer.
