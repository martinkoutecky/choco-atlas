The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a compliant anchored case for a continuous computational mirror: this paper contains no named computational result. Its Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes when a symmetric set \(P\) of Pareto-free pairs admits a non-Pareto preference rule that is Pareto optimal, anonymous, independent of irrelevant alternatives, and free of globally incomparable pairs. It says this is possible exactly when there is a nonempty asymmetric, negatively transitive relation \(R_0\) such that pairs outside \(P\) are twinned by \(R_0\), and every pair in \(P\) is ordered one way or the other by \(R_0\). Corollary 1, also proved here, gives a related characterization without the “free of globally incomparable pairs” condition. These are axiomatic characterizations, not results asserting that a computational problem is tractable or hard.

One could define a continuum version by replacing each finite profile with a distribution over rankings and requiring \(\mu\) to leave positive mass on the reverse of each pair in \(P\). But asking whether an anonymous, Pareto-optimal, IIA rule exists on that domain would continuize the paper’s axiomatic question. It supplies no qualifying computational anchor for the mirror this programme asks us to assess.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the strongest negative point: Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 characterize which *preference rules exist*. They do not study the complexity of computing an outcome, or of bribery, control, campaigning, or robustness. The paper’s own national-election motivation makes a continuum of voters plausible, so I would not object that mass is unnatural here. But plausibility of the population model does not supply the missing computational question.

The best version of the proposed mirror is a domain of distributions over rankings, with Pareto-freeness expressed as positive mass preferring each pair’s reverse. One could then ask whether a non-Pareto rule satisfying the paper’s axioms exists. That is a coherent continuous question, and it might yield an interesting axiomatic characterization. It is still an axiomatic question: the object being classified is a rule over a domain of societies, not an algorithm acting on a given society. The fact that the finite paper uses integer vote counts does not change that.

A computational reformulation is possible, but the natural one exposes the mismatch. Given the allowed Pareto-free pairs \(P\), one could decide whether a suitable \(R_0\) exists or construct one. Yet \(P\) determines the theorem’s answer; a particular society’s mass vector does not enter. Making the population continuous therefore adds no computational input to that task. Alternatively, give a mass vector and a specified rule and compute its pairwise output; that is an operational calculation, but it is not a computational result anchored in this paper’s theorems.

So the negative case is strong on scope, not on whether a continuous axiomatic mirror can be formulated. Such a mirror can be formulated, and this paper’s high-multiplicity setting is quite sensible. What fails is the claim that this paper supplies a worthwhile *continuous computational* mirror for the programme: its central results concern axiomatic rule design, and the plausible computational wrappers either leave the population out of the problem or introduce a different problem.
