The continuous mirror question

Given resident types \(T\), hospitals \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), strict type rankings \(\succ_t\) over acceptable hospitals, strict hospital rankings \(\succ_h\) over types, and rational quotas \(\ell_h,u_h\), choose assignment masses \(y_{t,h}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_h y_{t,h}\le\mu_t\) and \(\ell_h\le\sum_t y_{t,h}\le u_h\). Define unmatched mass by \(y_{t,\bot}=\mu_t-\sum_h y_{t,h}\). Require that no positive masses \(y_{t,g},y_{t',h}\), for \(g\in H\cup\{\bot\}\), satisfy \(h\succ_t g\) and \(t\succ_h t'\); maximize \(\sum_{t,h}y_{t,h}\).

The model it lives in

A cohort-based HRLQ placement market: each type represents interchangeable applicants with shared preferences and qualification class, hospitals rank types, assignment variables allocate fractions of each type, and quotas scale with population.

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous MAXEFM: given a finite set of resident types \(T\), hospitals \(H\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational lower and upper quotas \(\ell_h,u_h\), find a feasible envy-free assignment maximizing the mass matched. Each type \(t\) has a preference order \(\succ_t\) over acceptable hospitals; each hospital has a strict ranking \(\succ_h\) of types. Members of one type are interchangeable, so a hospital is indifferent between individuals of the same type.

An assignment is a nonnegative mass \(y_{t,h}\) from each type to each hospital, with \(\sum_h y_{t,h}\le\mu_t\) and \(\ell_h\le\sum_t y_{t,h}\le u_h\). Unmatched mass is allowed. It is envy-free if there are no types \(t,t'\), hospitals \(g,h\) (where \(g\) may be the unmatched option \(\bot\)), with positive mass of \(t\) assigned to \(g\) and positive mass of \(t'\) assigned to \(h\), such that \(h\succ_t g\) and \(t\succ_h t'\). The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{t,h}y_{t,h}\). These constraints keep the paper’s feasibility, envy, and maximum-size questions; mass variables let each individual still receive one integral assignment while a type is split across destinations.

The anchor is Theorem 4(I)(a), proved in this paper. It states that MAXEFM, equivalently MIN-UR-EFM, is NP-hard, and that MIN-UR-EFM has no \(\alpha\)-approximation for any \(\alpha>0\) unless \(P=NP\), even when every resident’s preference list has length at most two. The theorem allows arbitrary hospital upper quotas.

There is a direct mass version of the paper’s Independent Set construction. Given a graph \(G=(V,E)\), let \(N=|V|+|E|\). Give each vertex type and each edge type mass \(1/N\). Add hospital \(x\) and one hospital \(h_i\) per vertex \(v_i\). A vertex type \(v_i\) ranks \(h_i\) above \(x\); an edge type for \(\{v_i,v_j\}\) ranks its two endpoint hospitals. Hospital \(h_i\) ranks \(v_i\) above its incident edge types, and \(x\) ranks the vertex types. Set \(x\)’s lower and upper quotas to \(k/N\), and \(h_i\)’s upper quota to \((1+\deg(v_i))/N\), with lower quota zero.

A complete envy-free mass assignment exists exactly when \(G\) has an independent set of size at least \(k\). For the forward direction, send the types of an independent set of size \(k\) to \(x\), and send each other vertex type to its own hospital. Every edge type has an endpoint outside the independent set; assign it within the remaining market. A stable assignment there matches all remaining mass and is envy-free. For the reverse direction, let \(y_i\) be the mass of \(v_i\) assigned to \(x\), measured in units of \(1/N\). Then \(\sum_i y_i=k\). If \(y_i>0\), no mass of an incident edge type can occupy \(h_i\): it would be envied by the vertex mass at \(x\). Thus the indices with \(y_i>0\) form an independent set if the assignment is complete; since each \(y_i\le1\), there must be at least \(k\) such indices. Consequently, deciding whether Continuous MAXEFM can match all mass is NP-hard. The corresponding minimum-unmatched-mass problem also has a zero-versus-positive gap on these instances.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large centralized placement of healthcare trainees, where applicants share a complete preference list and qualification class, and programs rank those classes. If each type represents \(K\) interchangeable applicants, then the population has \(KN\) applicants but only \(N\) types; \(K\) can be arbitrarily large. Quotas scale with cohort size, so their normalized values are the fractions above. The reduction’s \(\tau=N\) grows with the graph, so it does not establish hardness for fixed \(\tau\). It does show that the hardness survives arbitrarily many clones per type. The resulting problem is still the authors’ maximum feasible envy-free matching question, with typewise priorities and per-capita quotas as the co-modifications required by the population model.

For this anchor I expect hardness transfer: the same independent-set obstruction survives mass splitting because any positive mass of a vertex type at \(x\) blocks all incident edge mass from its vertex hospital. This proves hardness for the stated continuous problem, though it is not a general two-way identity between discrete MAXEFM and its mass version. Further questions include whether the continuous optimum equals the limit of the \(K\)-clone discrete optima for arbitrary instances, whether tractability emerges for bounded \(\tau\), and whether the paper’s stronger approximation bounds also survive fractional assignments.

The weakest point is the type assumption. Hospital priorities in the paper are strict over individuals; this mirror makes members of a type indistinguishable to hospitals, which means equal priority within type. That is natural for genuinely cohort-based placement, but less convincing when programs use idiosyncratic interviews or tie-breaks. The construction also requires a growing number of types, even though it allows arbitrarily many people per type.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 4(I)(a), and the paper plainly has a computational result to mirror. The strongest objection is that the proposed high multiplicity model does not preserve the paper’s strict hospital preferences. In the paper, a hospital ranks individual residents strictly. A type that is complete for envy-freeness must therefore include how each hospital ranks its members; two residents who share an acceptable hospital cannot be indistinguishable in the full problem. Keeping those strict rankings leaves essentially singleton types.

The proposed mirror avoids that by treating hospital priorities as strict over types, which ties everyone within a type. That changes who can envy whom: under the paper’s strict rankings, one member of a cohort can outrank another; in the mirror, they cannot. The Independent Set reduction still proves hardness for this type-priority model, but it does not establish that the hardness theorem survives in a high multiplicity version of the paper’s strict-ranking model. So the case against rests on a relevance question: would the authors regard replacing individual priorities with priority classes as their HRLQ problem, or as a different preference model?

That objection has limits. Cohort placement with priorities based on shared qualifications is plausible, and the programme explicitly allows model changes that authors recognize as part of the mirror. The reduction is also sound for the stated type-priority model: fractional assignments do not evade its obstruction. Thus neither hardness nor the change in formalism alone defeats the anchor.

Proportional quotas are another required assumption: the mirror scales hospital capacities and lower quotas with population. That may fit an expanding placement system, though it is less natural when lower quotas mean fixed staffing minima at existing hospitals. The programme allows this co-modification, so it cannot settle the issue.

My negative case is therefore weak. It identifies a real gap between the paper’s strict individual priorities and the proposed cohort model, but the cohort scenario is credible enough that I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror. The anchor likely survives if the authors accept priority classes and population-scaled quotas as part of their problem.
