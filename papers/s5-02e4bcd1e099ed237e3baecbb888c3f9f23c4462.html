What the mirror covers

The proposed type-mass classification would be a new axiomatic question; it would not mirror a computational result in the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest conclusion is that this paper does not provide a qualifying computational anchor for a continuous mirror.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting nearby: a large population judging a fixed agenda, where a type is a complete rational attitude function and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population holding that type. But the paper studies axiomatic classification of aggregation rules, not computation on a given population distribution. Its aggregators act on profiles indexed by individuals and need not be anonymous; replacing those profiles with type masses can erase the individual identities that matter to the result.

The closest numbered results, both proved in the paper, are Proposition 1 and Proposition 2. Proposition 1 says that every rational, universal, strongly systematic attitude aggregator has a decision criterion that is an \(S\)-algebra homomorphism. Proposition 2 proves the converse: every such homomorphism induces a rational, universal, strongly systematic aggregator. Corollary 1 adds that these aggregators preserve a constant judgment when every individual accepts that constant. These are characterization results, not claims that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The discussion in Section 5.1 connects Boolean homomorphisms to ultrafilters and finite-electorate dictatorships, but it supplies no computational result either.

One could formulate a follow-on question about classifying rational, strongly systematic aggregation rules that depend only on a continuous distribution over attitude types. That would make the population continuous, but it would be an axiomatic classification problem, not the computational question this programme targets. So there is a plausible population-level setting, but no honest way to turn this paper’s results into the required anchored computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror starts with a missing anchor: this paper states no computational result. Propositions 1 and 2 characterize the same class of aggregators in both directions: rational, universal, strongly systematic aggregators correspond to \(S\)-algebra homomorphisms. Corollary 1 derives a preservation property from that characterization. None gives an algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or hardness result to translate.

The proposed type distribution over rational attitude functions does not supply one. The paper’s objects are maps on profiles indexed by individuals, and its criterion is a homomorphism \(f:B^N\to B\). Replacing those profiles with masses would require choosing a new rule that maps a distribution to a collective attitude. Requiring that rule to depend only on masses would add an anonymity or distribution-dependence condition the paper does not study. Classifying such rules could be a worthwhile axiomatic project, but it would not be a computational mirror of these propositions.

The strongest computational salvage would be to fix a finite logic, algebra, agenda, and encoding, then ask whether a proposed aggregator has the paper’s properties or compute its output from a weighted profile. But the paper specifies no such finite input model or computational task. Its characterization quantifies over rational profiles and formulas; turning that into a decision or optimization problem requires adding the question, representation, and size parameters. Any resulting complexity theorem would be new work built on the paper’s framework, rather than a continuization of one of its results.

The paper’s allowance for infinite electorates does not change this: an infinite index set is not a continuous population distribution. So neither the characterization nor its dictatorship discussion provides a surviving anchor. The honest limit of this negative case is that one cannot rule out every valuable computational project inspired by judgment aggregation. What the paper supports is narrower: its contributions are algebraic and axiomatic, and a continuous computational question would have to be supplied from outside its results.
