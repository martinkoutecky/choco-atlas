The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor under your rule. The paper’s numbered Theorems 1–5 are proved here, but none asserts a complexity classification or an algorithmic running-time result. They establish properties of assignments: Theorem 1 says a satisfaction-maximizing fair or ML-fair assignment has no ordinary empty-seat request; Theorem 2 proves existence of a fair, “明白な無駄がない” assignment for LSOS; Theorem 3 says a satisfaction-maximizing fair LSOS assignment has no valid empty-seat request; Theorem 4 proves existence of a fair LSOSTS assignment with no such waste or same-rank seat request; and Theorem 5 says satisfaction maximization eliminates same-rank seat requests. The COP encoding and experiments compute assignments, but they are not numbered complexity results. The NP-hardness mentioned in related work is attributed to Hamada et al. for a different problem, not established by this paper.

The most promising provisional mirror is Theorem 3, proved here: in LSOS, maximizing students’ total satisfaction among fair assignments leaves no student with a valid empty-seat request. I would not count this as an eligible anchor, but it suggests a concrete continuous question.

Call the candidate problem Mass-LSO Welfare Optimization. An instance has a finite lab set \(L\), supported student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, per-capita lower and upper quotas \(q_l^-,q_l^+\), and satisfaction values \(v_t(l)\) consistent with each type’s ranking of labs. A type must capture all relevant student information, including preferences and priority class. Labs rank types, treating members of the same type alike. The decision variable is \(x_{t,l}\ge 0\), the fraction of society of type \(t\) assigned to lab \(l\). Require
\[ \sum_{l\in L}x_{t,l}=\mu_t,\qquad q_l^-\le \sum_{t\in T}x_{t,l}\le q_l^+. \]
Among allocations satisfying the continuous analogue of fairness—no type has positive mass assigned elsewhere that strictly prefers a lab where lower-priority mass is assigned—find one maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{l\in L}x_{t,l}v_t(l). \]
A solution is the allocation \(x\) and its objective value; infeasibility is reported if no fair feasible allocation exists. The continuous analogue of Theorem 3’s seat-request property is that an optimum admits no positive mass transfer from a less-preferred lab into a lab with spare capacity when quotas and fairness remain satisfied.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime if, for example, a large placement programme has many applicants but relatively few recurring preference and priority profiles, and quotas scale with cohort size. It is in scope: the population becomes the mass distribution, and fractional type-level assignments represent the average allocation of many otherwise identical students. The welfare objective is linear, but fairness depends on which types receive positive mass, so the feasible set may have combinatorial support choices; I would treat tractability as open and expect that support structure, rather than the welfare maximization alone, is the main obstacle.

The weakest point is the paper’s strict lab rankings of individual students. If those rankings encode distinct priorities for nearly everyone, then a complete type may be almost unique and \(\tau\) may approach the population size. The mirror is strongest when labs use priority classes, with students within a class treated alike—a plausible co-modification, but one the authors and referee would need to accept.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own caveat is decisive under the stated anchor rule: this paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary about computational complexity. Theorems 1–5 establish properties of assignments. The paper’s computational contribution is a COP encoding and an empirical evaluation, not a complexity or algorithmic guarantee. The NP-hardness result mentioned in related work concerns a different problem. So Theorem 3 is not an eligible computational result to mirror.

The proposed Mass-LSO question does not fix that gap. Theorem 3 says that a satisfaction-maximizing fair assignment has no valid empty-seat request. Its continuous analogue is another property of an optimum. Asking how hard it is to *compute* a maximum-welfare fair mass allocation could be a worthwhile new question, but it is not a complexity result from this paper. The paper supplies no algorithmic bound or classification for its finite version to carry into a continuization.

The strongest objection to that new question is the type structure. Fairness here depends on each lab’s strict ranking of individual students. A complete student type must therefore include their position in every lab’s ranking; with strict rankings, those positions distinguish students, so the support can grow with the population. Repeated preference profiles alone do not solve this: they omit information the fairness test uses. Grouping students into shared priority classes would restore a small type space, but it changes the lab-side preference model that the paper deliberately keeps strict. It may be a plausible extension, though; the paper gives no basis for ruling out a cohort where such classes are natural.

So the negative case defeats the only proposed anchor, but it cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. A type-based placement problem with shared priority classes and scaled quotas could still be worth studying as a new continuous computational problem. Its weakness as a *mirror of this paper* is that the paper proves no computational result for the finite problem that this new question would extend.
