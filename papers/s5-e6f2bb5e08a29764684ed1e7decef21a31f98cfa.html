The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot name an eligible anchor: this paper has no numbered computational result in the sense your brief requires. Proposition 1, proved here, reports properties of \(\alpha^{ceq}\), including that it fails core stability. Proposition 2, also proved here, says \(\beta^{ceq}\) coincides with the Folk solution on \(2\)-mcst problems. Corollary 1 says the paper’s piecewise-linear extension equals the Folk solution for every mcst problem. These are mathematical and axiomatic results, not complexity classifications. The paper mentions Prim’s algorithm and polynomial complexity in its discussion, but neither appears as a numbered computational result of this paper.

The closest positive case is a plausible high-multiplicity *extension*, rather than a mirror anchored in one of those results. Consider a large district served through a fixed set \(T\) of possible locations: many near-identical households live at each location, and the link costs depend on locations. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of \(N\) households at location type \(t\), with \(N\mu_t\) integral. A type is a location, and mass is its share of households. Let \(C_m\) be the cost of the minimum tree connecting the occupied locations to the source, and \(d_t\) the least cost for type \(t\) to connect to the source. The continuous allocation question would be to find a charge density \(y_t\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t(y_t-C_m)^2 \]
subject to
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t y_t=C_m,\qquad 0\le y_t\le N d_t. \]
Here \(y_t/N\) is the charge per household. This is the mass-weighted version of the paper’s capped egalitarian allocation: equal per-household charges, capped by individual rationality. It can be computed by finding the tree and then solving for the cap threshold. I would expect this version to be tractable.

The scenario is credible as a high-multiplicity regime: a large population lives at relatively few service locations, and households sharing a location type have the same relevant costs. The authors could recognize it as scaling up their network and cost-sharing problem. The weak point is decisive for this assignment, though: the proposed tractability claim is not asserted by any named computational result in the paper. It also makes the allocation the computational question, while the paper’s named results chiefly concern its fairness properties. So this is a possible research extension, not an honest anchor-based positive case under the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the anchors: there isn’t a named computational result here to continuize. Proposition 1 characterizes properties of \(\alpha^{ceq}\); Proposition 2 and Corollary 1 identify \(\beta^{ceq}\) with the Folk solution. Those are axiomatic and rule-equivalence results, not complexity theorems. The paper mentions Prim’s algorithm and cites polynomial complexity for piecewise-linear solutions, but it does not present a numbered computational result of its own. A continuous version of one of these propositions could be a new question, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The repeated-location scenario is credible, and it would be a mistake to reject it on multiplicity grounds. Many households at a small set of service locations can have identical cost profiles, with location types and their masses as the input. The proposed continuous \(\alpha^{ceq}\) allocation is also a reasonable formulation. For a population of size \(N\), let \(y_t\) be \(N\) times the per-household charge at type \(t\). Then one can impose \(\sum_t\mu_t y_t=C_m\) and \(0\le y_t\le N d_t\), and minimize \(\sum_t\mu_t(y_t-C_m)^2\). But with fixed link costs, the tree and indirect costs come from the graph on the \(\tau\) occupied types; mass affects only how the fixed total is divided. The allocation is the paper’s constrained equal-awards calculation on those claims. This is a sensible high-multiplicity extension, but it supplies no computational result from the paper to mirror.

The best attempt to add a harder computation is the \(\beta^{ceq}\) core selection. In the fixed-cost model, a coalition’s cost depends on which types it contains, not how much mass it contains. Allowing fractional coalitions therefore adds no new network structure: the core constraints reduce to constraints over subsets of the finite support. That could motivate a separate algorithmic study, but it turns the paper’s rule-equivalence result into a new complexity question. If costs instead depend on the amount of population served, the model may acquire a richer optimization problem, but it is then a demand-dependent network-cost model; the paper’s fixed \(C_m\), indirect costs, and core game no longer specify the problem.

So the negative case is strong under the assignment’s anchor standard, but weaker as a universal claim. The location-type society is a sensible object the authors might recognize, and a continuous core-selection problem could be worth studying. What the paper does not offer is a named computational result whose continuous-population mirror would chart the complexity landscape ChoCo targets.
