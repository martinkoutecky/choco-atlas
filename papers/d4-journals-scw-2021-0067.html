What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered; the proposed mirrors concern Theorem 1's direct-revelation characterization and Theorem 2's implementation construction.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest foothold is Theorem 2 (proved here). For a finite state space satisfying full detection, the paper constructs a dynamic mechanism in which truthful reporting is the unique iteratively undominated strategy profile and induces any chosen social choice function. The mechanism is bounded, has no transfers on the truthful path, and uses only small off-path transfers. Theorem 1 (also proved here) gives a related characterization for direct revelation when players care only about transfers: full detection suffices for the specified mechanism to make truth uniquely iteratively undominated, and it is necessary for any direct-revelation mechanism to do so. These are meaningful structural implementation results.

A charitable research lead would be to study a large population grouped by complete signal, utility, and verification types, with \(\mu_t\) denoting each type’s share, and ask whether a target rule \(F(\mu)\) can be uniquely implemented through reports and verifiable information. But that would require new mechanism theory. The paper’s detection argument concerns deviations by particular finite players and uses other players’ announcements to expose lies. In a nonatomic population, one person’s report has zero mass, so that logic does not carry over directly. And an existence theorem for implementing an SCF is not, by itself, a computational problem with an algorithm or complexity classification to continuize.

So the positive case is only a lead for adjacent work, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results. The paper contains no named computational result: Theorems 1 and 2 establish implementation and characterization claims, not tractability, hardness, or an algorithm for a computational problem. Under the requested anchor rule, neither qualifies, and there is no per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that neither anchor is a computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes when full detection supports truth-telling in a direct revelation mechanism; Theorem 2 proves an implementation result for any social choice function under that condition. Neither studies an algorithm, a complexity bound, or the computational cost of a decision problem. Their being numbered theorems does not make them computational anchors. That leaves no paper result for ChoCo’s complexity landscape to continuize.

The strongest version of a Theorem 1 mirror might group agents by their complete signal, utility, and verification characteristics, represent their shares by \(\mu\), and ask whether full detection still characterizes unique truthful implementation. But full detection is defined through the feasible joint state space: it asks whether other players’ announcements can successively expose a particular player’s lie. A distribution over types alone does not preserve those joint feasibility relations. Encoding enough of them to do so risks making \(\mu\) a relabeling of the state space, rather than a continuous society. And checking or synthesizing such a mechanism would be a newly formulated computational problem, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 2’s best mirror would ask whether a target rule \(F(\mu)\) can be uniquely implemented for a continuum of agents. That changes the strategic object substantially. The paper’s mechanism disciplines each player’s announcements through individual transfers and finite rounds of iterated elimination. In an atomless model, an individual deviation has zero mass; if uniqueness means an exact strategy profile, changing strategies on a null set also complicates what “unique” means. One could define incentives and uniqueness modulo null sets and design transfers to enforce aggregate truthfulness, but that would require new mechanism theory and new computational questions. Those questions may be interesting, but they are not supplied by this theorem.

So the negative case is strong for the programme’s purposes: the paper offers no computational result to mirror, and its central results concern individual strategic implementation rather than computation over a society distribution. The broader claim that no population-based extension could ever be worthwhile is weaker: a carefully specified aggregate implementation problem might merit study. It would be adjacent new work, though, rather than a continuous computational mirror of this paper.
