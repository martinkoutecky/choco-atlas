The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result to anchor a continuous computational mirror. Its numbered theorems concern preference aggregation: Theorem 1 gives a representation of pair preferences, Theorem 3 derives group preferences from \(n-1\) bilateral agreements, and Theorem 7 gives the group utility and its additive multiattribute representation. None classifies an underlying problem as tractable or hard, or gives an algorithmic result of the kind the programme requires. The paper says to choose the alternative with the highest group utility, but does not study the computational complexity of doing so.

A population of many stakeholders sharing complete utility types is conceivable, but it would require a new aggregation model: Theorem 7’s weights come from utility comparison rates along a spanning tree, and the paper does not specify how population masses replace those individuals and agreements. That might motivate a new theoretical extension; it does not supply a named computational anchor or a continuous problem that mirrors one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper proves no computational result. Its numbered theorems characterize pair and group preferences from the Extended Pareto Principle and bilateral agreements. The central construction uses utility comparison rates along a finite spanning tree; Theorem 7 derives the group utility from those rates. The paper gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or formal computational problem. Choosing the alternative with highest group utility is the endpoint of the framework, not a problem whose computation the authors study. So the proponent’s case supplies no computational anchor that survives scrutiny.

The most plausible extension would model many shareholders or employees through a distribution \(\mu\) over complete utility types, with a type-level welfare weight \(\lambda(t)\), and define
\[ U_\mu(x)=\frac{\int \lambda(t)u_t(x)\,d\mu(t)}{\int \lambda(t)\,d\mu(t)}. \]
That population scenario is sensible. But deriving \(\lambda\) from the paper’s bilateral agreements requires a continuum version of its pairwise comparison network and consistency conditions. Supplying \(\lambda\) directly avoids that work and replaces the paper’s main contribution with a new input. Either route could motivate new research; neither mirrors a computational result in this paper. A continuous extension of the aggregation theorem would also be axiomatic, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

This is a strong reason to reject a *paper-level continuous mirror*. It does not show that computation over continuous populations of utility types could never be worthwhile; it shows that such a project would need its own computational question and contribution.
