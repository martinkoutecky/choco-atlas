The continuous mirror question

Given a distribution \(\mu\) with rational masses over a finite support \(T=S\cup R\), where each type records its shared signal model, true state, network role, and type-level influence weights, and a rational block matrix \(A=\begin{bmatrix}T_{SS}&T_{SR}\0&T_{RR}\end{bmatrix}\) with strongly connected sender blocks and \(\rho(T_{RR})<1\), assume the senders learn their true states and receiving types satisfy condition (25). Compute each receiving type's exact limiting belief \(q_r(\theta)\) for every \(\theta\in\Theta\), retaining paths through other receiving types.

The model it lives in

A rational-mass society of behavior and network types, with shared signal models and a type-level influence matrix that preserves the sender and receiver structure. The output is each receiver type's limiting belief vector, given by \(q_R(\theta)=W^\top p_S(\theta)\), where \(W=T_{SR}(I-T_{RR})^{-1}\) and \(p_S(\theta)\) records senders' learned states.

The objection that survived

The proposed LP omits receiver-to-receiver paths represented in \(W\), so it covers only the direct-exposure case; the full mirror must retain those paths.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's receiver-limit formula for sender and receiver cohorts under condition (25), including indirect receiver paths. The proposed LP covers only direct-exposure instances, and Theorem 4's self-awareness bounds and the other learning results remain outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of minimum-cost type-level influence design with rational link costs \(c_{uv}\), target beliefs \(p_r\), and tolerance \(\varepsilon\), when receiver-to-receiver links are allowed and the resulting beliefs satisfy \(q_R=W^\top p_S\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive hook is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that, under condition (25), the limiting beliefs of the receiving agents satisfy
\[ \lim_{i\to\infty}\mu_{R,i}(\theta) = W^{T}\!\left(\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu_{S,i}(\theta)\right), \qquad W=T_{SR}(I-T_{RR})^{-1}. \]
Since the sending sub-networks learn their respective true states, this formula gives each receiver a limiting mixture over those states, with weights determined by the network. That is a concrete aggregate quantity and a plausible basis for a population-level design problem.

A reasonable regime would be a large online community with a few recurring sender and receiver cohorts: many people share the same signal model, role, and influence-design costs, so the number of agents is large while the number \(\tau\) of types stays modest. A type records those shared properties; its mass is the fraction of the community in that cohort.

One possible follow-up is High-Multiplicity Influence Design. Its input is a finite state set \(\Theta\); sending types \(s\), each with a state \(\theta_s\) and assumptions ensuring its members learn that state; receiving types \(r\), with rational masses \(\mu_r\) summing to one; a desired limiting belief \(p_r\in\Delta(\Theta)\) for each receiving type; rational costs \(c_{rs}\); and tolerance \(\varepsilon\). Choose rational exposure shares \(x_{rs}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_s x_{rs}=1\) for each \(r\). These shares determine
\[ q_r(\theta)=\sum_{s:\theta_s=\theta}x_{rs}. \]
The goal is to minimize \(\sum_r\mu_r\sum_s c_{rs}x_{rs}\), subject to
\[ \sum_r\mu_r\lVert q_r-p_r\rVert_1\leq\varepsilon. \]
The output is an exposure allocation. This is an LP, so I would expect it to be tractable. It covers Theorem 2’s limiting-belief structure in a setting where receiver cohorts connect directly to sender cohorts. The paper itself points toward this kind of question: its conclusion proposes designing the network so receiving agents adopt specified limiting beliefs.

This is a credible *follow-up* to the paper, but it does not satisfy the requested anchor criterion. Theorem 2 establishes a limiting-belief formula; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 4, also proved here, bounds self-aware receivers’ limiting beliefs around that formula, while Theorem 1 is a truth-learning result cited from [15]. These are learning and convergence results, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another computational class. The paper contains no eligible named computational result, so there is no formal anchor for the proposed design problem.

The weakest point is the modeling step: the paper studies graph topology and individual belief dynamics, while this design problem assigns influence shares to types. The cohort regime is plausible, especially given the paper’s social-network examples, but preserving detailed network structure may require so many types that \(\tau\) grows with the population. Thus the positive case is a concrete, author-adjacent research direction grounded in Theorem 2—not an anchored computational mirror of a result the paper establishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 2 gives a formula for limiting beliefs; Theorem 4 bounds those beliefs with self-awareness. Neither states a decision problem’s complexity or gives an algorithm for one. Theorem 1 is cited from earlier work, and Theorem 3 is also a learning result. A continuous model of these results might be mathematically interesting, but it would not chart the computational landscape ChoCo is meant to study.

The proposed “High-Multiplicity Influence Design” does not fill that gap. Its inputs include target beliefs, design costs, and an error tolerance that the paper never defines; its output is an exposure allocation the paper never considers. Its LP is easy because it treats each receiver’s shares \(x_{rs}\) as independent choices. But Theorem 2’s weights come from \(W=T_{SR}(I-T_{RR})^{-1}\): they reflect paths through the receiving network, not just direct exposure. The LP therefore models a simplified new design problem, rather than continuizing the theorem.

A more faithful design question could optimize the network while preserving those indirect paths. That would need constraints on the graph and its transition weights, and the limiting beliefs would depend on the chosen matrix through an inverse. Such a project could be worthwhile, but it would introduce a computational problem the paper only gestures toward in its conclusion; it would not mirror an existing complexity result. The same applies to using Theorem 4 to choose self-awareness parameters.

There is a plausible population regime: replicated sender and receiver cohorts with shared signal models and network roles. In a carefully structured graph, those cohorts might admit a compact aggregate description. But an agent’s type would need to include the network information relevant to its belief dynamics. In a general graph, distinct neighborhoods can make the number of types grow with the number of agents. Restricting to a small number of repeated roles may restore a population model, but it does not supply the missing computational question.

So the negative case is strongest on scope, not impossibility: the paper may motivate fresh network-design research, and a special cohort limit may be sensible. Neither makes this paper a source of computational results whose continuous mirrors could advance ChoCo’s stated programme.
