The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result of the kind your brief requires.

The numbered results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2. Theorem 1 says that, in a weighted majority game with at least one winning and one losing coalition, the weight–power correlations are identical for the normalized Banzhaf, Banzhaf, and two Coleman measures; it also says the Holler public good index and its unnormalized measure have identical correlations. Theorem 2 says that for any scale-invariant inequality measure, the corresponding inequality scores are identical within those same two groups. These are mathematical identities between measures, not results about a problem being in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

The paper says it implements known dynamic programming algorithms for computing power profiles and reports experiments, but it does not give a numbered complexity result for those computations. The IMF and EU examples also involve only 24 and 28 institutional players, respectively.

A possible research direction would group many voting bodies by weight type, treat each type’s population share as mass, define a continuous analogue of type-level swing power, and optimize the threshold for correlation or inequality. But the paper does not define that analogue, and its swing counts depend on adding or removing one indivisible player. That proposal would need new modeling choices and would not mirror either theorem as a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The positive case identifies no qualifying anchor. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are identities among power measures: they follow because certain power profiles are scalar multiples of the same swing vector. Neither classifies a computational problem. The paper mentions implementing known dynamic programs and reports finite experiments, but gives no named result on the complexity of computing power profiles or selecting a threshold. On the programme’s terms, there is no computational theorem here to continuize.

The IMF and EU examples do not supply a useful population regime: they have 24 directors and 28 countries, with weights tied to particular institutions. That is evidence against those examples as mirrors, though it cannot settle the question for the research area. A more convincing alternative would be a large shareholder vote with many one-share investors, grouped into weight types. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

The difficulty with that better scenario is the paper’s central statistic. A swing asks whether adding or removing one player changes the outcome. In a population limit, one person has zero mass, so the literal marginal swing disappears. Treat a whole type’s mass as the unit instead, and the type moves as a bloc—changing what power means. Or take a sequence of finite games and study suitably rescaled per-person swings. That is a defensible research question, but it requires choosing an asymptotic normalization and a way to handle pivotality near the quota. It is a new limit analysis of the paper’s measurements, not a continuous version of either theorem’s computational result.

That is the strongest negative case: the paper offers no computational classification to anchor a ChoCo mirror, and its natural high-multiplicity extension runs into a noncanonical limit for individual swing power. But the universal claim is weak. The shareholder scenario is credible, and studying threshold choice or power computation along a high-multiplicity sequence could be worthwhile. I would argue against treating this paper as a strong mirror candidate; I cannot honestly rule out a useful new project built from it.
