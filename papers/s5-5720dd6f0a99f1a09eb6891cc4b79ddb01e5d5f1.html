What the mirror covers

The paper's two numbered theorems are axiomatic impossibilities; neither yields a computational mirror. A distributional restatement of population monotonicity would concern axioms on rules.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is that the paper already treats population as variable and asks what happens when a group leaves. A plausible high-multiplicity setting is siting a public bad in a large municipality: residents with the same complete single-dipped preference relation are one type, and \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of residents. The choice rule selects a location on \([0,T]\). Millions of residents spread across a modest number of preference types is a sensible regime, and fractional departures give population monotonicity a natural mass-based analogue.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 1, proved here, says no choice rule on the paper’s domain can satisfy Pareto efficiency and continuity. Theorem 2, also proved here, says no rule can jointly satisfy Pareto efficiency, population monotonicity, and no-veto power. These are axiomatic impossibility results, not results about complexity or algorithms.

One could ask whether a rule exists for distributions over single-dipped preference types that is Pareto efficient, population monotonic under removal of submass, and satisfies suitable continuum versions of continuity and no-veto power. That would be a plausible axiomatic continuum question, but it is not a computational mirror of either theorem, and translating no-veto power to a continuum population needs care. So this paper offers a natural population-continuum motivation, but no eligible anchor for the requested computational case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The large municipality is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and removing a fraction of residents can give population monotonicity a sensible mass-based version. That makes the paper’s *axiomatic* question a plausible subject for continuization. It does not supply a computational result to mirror.

Theorem 1 rules out a choice rule satisfying Pareto efficiency and continuity; Theorem 2 rules out one satisfying Pareto efficiency, population monotonicity, and no-veto power. Neither defines an algorithmic problem, an encoded input, or a complexity claim. The paper’s outcome space and preferences are already continuous, but the population remains finite. Continuizing the outcome or preference space would miss ChoCo’s target.

The strongest mass-based restatement would give a rule a distribution over complete preference types and ask whether Pareto efficiency, monotonicity under removal of submass, and suitable versions of continuity and no-veto power can coexist. That is coherent, but it remains a question about axioms on rules. No-veto power also needs a deliberate continuum interpretation, since an individual has zero mass; choosing one would refine the axiomatic question, not turn it into an algorithmic one. To get a computational problem, one would need to add an input representation and a task—such as finding an efficient location for a parametric family of preferences. That might be useful work, but it would be a new problem rather than a mirror of either theorem.

So the negative case is strong for the paper-to-ChoCo link: its variable population does not overcome the absence of a computational anchor. The universal claim is weaker. There may be worthwhile computational projects on public-good siting with distributed populations, but this paper does not identify one.
