What the mirror covers

The main characterization and the results on scoring methods concern axiomatic monotonicity. The paper also gives a recognition procedure, but states no computational guarantee for it.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror to. Its main theorem, proved here, characterizes minimally rich domains on which the Borda count is monotonic: exactly the cyclically nested permutation domains. Proposition 1 gives an equivalent nested equal rank difference characterization. Neither states a complexity result for an underlying computational problem.

Section 3 gives a procedure for recognizing those domains, but it is not a numbered result asserting a complexity classification. The paper’s central question is axiomatic—when Borda satisfies Maskin monotonicity on a restricted domain—not computational. A continuous population version would therefore continuize an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s stated scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s strongest result is Theorem A: it characterizes the preference domains on which Borda satisfies Maskin monotonicity. Proposition 1 recasts that characterization, Proposition 2 characterizes which scoring methods are monotonic on those domains, and Corollary 2 draws a further axiomatic consequence. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s Section 3 does give a procedure for recognizing CNP domains, but its input is a set of rankings, not a society described by population masses.

The most faithful continuous version would replace voter counts by a distribution \(\mu\) over a restricted domain \(P\), and ask whether every permitted monotonic improvement preserves a Borda winner. For a candidate \(x\), such an improvement transfers mass only along pairs \((t,t')\) with \(L(x,t)\subseteq L(x,t')\). This is a coherent continuous formulation, but it remains a statement of the same axiom over a domain. Making it a decision problem—whether the axiom holds for \(P\)—does not supply the missing computational result to mirror; it turns the paper’s domain-recognition question into an algorithmic wrapper around its characterization.

A stronger computational proposal would fix \(\mu\), assign costs \(\gamma(t,t')\), and find the cheapest monotonic mass transfer that lets a challenger tie or beat \(x\). That is plausible as a high-multiplicity campaigning or robustness problem, and it can be expressed as an LP over transfers between supported types. But the paper does not study campaigns, prices, feasible interventions, or instance-specific robustness. Adding those ingredients creates a new Borda intervention problem; the axiom supplies a constraint, not an anchor for that optimization question. Calling it a mirror would make “mirror” broad enough to cover almost any new computational problem that uses a rule or concept from an axiomatic paper.

So the negative case is strongest under ChoCo’s stated gate: the population can certainly be made continuous here, but the paper’s results concern which *domains* satisfy an axiom, not how to compute over a society distribution. I would not recommend this paper as a continuous mirror. The honest weakness is that a research programme willing to accept problems inspired by, rather than computationally mirroring, a paper could choose the mass-transfer question. That would be a defensible new project, but its value would rest on the added intervention model, not on a computational result in this paper.
