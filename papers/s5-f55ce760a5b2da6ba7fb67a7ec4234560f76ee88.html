A distribution over metric rankings is an intelligible extension, but its weighted tally is not a computational result proved by this paper, so bit (a) fails.

What the mirror covers

A distribution over metric rankings would extend the questions in Theorems 5.2, 5.4, and 5.6 to weighted majority. It would not cover a computational result from the paper; the empirical checks and the across-dataset representative-ranking analysis remain outside this mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your criteria. Theorem 5.2 proves that single-peaked metric rankings make pairwise majority complete and transitive and satisfy three axioms; Theorems 5.4 and 5.6 give analogous guarantees under group-separability and distance-restrictedness. These are structural social-choice results, not complexity results about an underlying computational problem. Theorem 4.1 is a cited impossibility theorem, also not a computational result.

The closest possible continuous model would treat metrics as a population: a type is a strict ranking of a fixed model set, and its mass is the fraction of criteria inducing that ranking. One could ask whether weighted pairwise majority produces a transitive ranking. But that would extend the paper’s axiomatic question to a continuous electorate; it would not mirror a named computational result from the paper, and I cannot honestly classify it as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard on the basis of these results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case for supplies no named computational anchor. Theorems 5.2, 5.4, and 5.6 establish axiomatic properties of pairwise majority under restricted preference domains; the experiments check whether those domains occur. None states the complexity of a decision or optimization problem. That leaves no computational result here whose continuous complexity the programme can chart.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is still coherent: fix the model set and treat each metric’s ranking as a type, with \(\mu_r\) the fraction of metrics having ranking \(r\). Weighted majority then compares \(A\) and \(B\) using the mass of rankings that prefer each one. A large catalogue of related metrics could make this a sensible regime, so I would not reject it on the grounds that metrics cannot have multiplicity. But the paper’s theorems concern which rankings occur, while the continuous model adds masses to them. The natural computational task—calculate the weighted majority relation and check its properties—is just tallying rational weights. To get a harder algorithmic question, one would need to add an intervention, such as costs for changing metric weights, or a new optimization over candidate rankings. Those may be worthwhile problems, but the paper does not formulate them.

Treating datasets as a population is a stronger alternative, since the paper already uses datasets as observations when it chooses a representative ranking across a suite. But that changes the target: the main theorems concern aggregation of metrics within each dataset, not aggregation of datasets as voters. Optimizing a new continuous version of the across-dataset centrality rule could be interesting, but it would be a new computational problem rather than a mirror of one of the paper’s results.

So I would argue against greenlighting this as a ChoCo mirror: the direct continuation is axiomatic rather than computational, and the more ambitious continuations require adding a problem the paper does not study. The negative case is not airtight, though. A continuous metric electorate or dataset distribution is easy to describe, and a separate project could make either computationally substantive by specifying an intervention or a new optimization target.
