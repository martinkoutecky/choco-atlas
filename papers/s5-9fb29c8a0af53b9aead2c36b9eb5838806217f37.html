What the mirror covers

No computational result is mirrored; Theorems 4 and 5 give extension-existence conditions, while the paper's other results concern fuzzy-relation extensions and their characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make a positive case that meets the anchor requirement: the paper contains no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity.

The closest result is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that, for a fuzzy preorder \(R\) and a fixed 1-Lipschitz generator, a compatible fuzzy order extension with \(\widetilde P(b_i,a_i)=1\) for every specified pair exists if and only if the associated relation \(R^A\) is min-acyclic. That is a structural existence characterization, not a stated complexity result. One could derive a finite-instance decision procedure from it, but presenting that deduction as a named algorithmic anchor would overstate what the paper claims. Theorem 5 is also an existence result, giving sufficient conditions for constrained extensions.

A population interpretation is conceivable: treat types as complete fuzzy preference reports and \(\mu_t\) as their population shares. But the paper’s fuzzy relation values are degrees of connection between alternatives, not population proportions. Making them mass shares would require extra modeling choices about how individual reports aggregate and how extensions correspond to populations. That may inspire a new problem, but it would not be a faithful, anchored mirror of a computational result in this paper. The paper’s continuity is in the preference relation itself; it does not continuize the society.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper proves extension and existence characterizations, not a computational result. Theorem 4 is the best candidate: it characterizes when a fuzzy preorder has a compatible order extension satisfying specified strict comparisons. For a finite input relation, its condition is simply that the graph with an arc \(i\to j\) whenever \(R(a_i,b_j)>0\) is acyclic. Theorem 5 likewise gives conditions for an extension with strict and indifferent comparisons; it does not classify an algorithmic problem. Turning either theorem into a decision problem is possible, but it does not supply the missing population question.

The most charitable population version would take types \(t\) to be individual fuzzy preference relations, aggregate them as \(R_\mu(a,b)=\sum_t\mu_tR_t(a,b)\), then ask whether the aggregate has the constrained extension. That adds a real population distribution, but also adds an aggregation model the paper does not study. In general the weighted average of even crisp rankings need not be a fuzzy preorder, so the theorem may not apply. Restricting to societies whose aggregate is a fuzzy preorder, or repairing the aggregate by taking a transitive closure, adds a substantive modeling choice. Once the resulting relation is formed, the extension question sees only that relation: distinct societies with the same aggregate are indistinguishable, and the mass distribution plays no further role.

This is a stronger objection than saying fuzzy degrees are not population shares: one can give them a population interpretation. The problem is that doing so makes the society an input wrapper around a relation-extension question, rather than making the population the object of the paper’s problem. A compelling application in which the authors endorse that aggregation step could change the assessment, so “no scenario could work” is too absolute to establish. On the paper as given, though, there is no worthwhile, anchored continuous mirror for ChoCo’s computational programme.
