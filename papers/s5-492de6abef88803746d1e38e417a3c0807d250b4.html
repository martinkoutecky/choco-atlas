What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns Proposition 2 and Corollary 6; it leaves the equilibrium characterization, comparative statics, median-voting analysis, and monetary policy results without a computational population-continuum analogue.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. The paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Its closest result is Proposition 2, proved here: under average voting, a finite optimal committee size exists if and only if \(r>\frac15\) and \(\frac{\beta}{\omega}<\frac{5r-1}{(1-r)^2}\). That is an analytic characterization, not a claim about an algorithm or a problem’s complexity. Corollary 6 carries related conditions into the monetary-policy application. So there is no qualifying computational anchor, and I would not present these results as one.

A plausible population regime is a very large public advisory panel drawn from a pool of agents with the same role, information precision, and incentive to conform. It has many members but just one ex-ante type. The closest continuous mirror of Proposition 2 would relax the size of that homogeneous panel to a real mass \(M\ge 1\). Given rational \(r\in(-1,1)\), \(\omega,\beta>0\), each member has the paper’s prior, private signal, and payoff; the panel uses average voting. Define
\[ \gamma(M)=\frac{(1-r)\beta}{(1-r)\beta+\left(1-\frac{2r}{M}+\frac{r}{M^2}\right)\omega} \]
and
\[ U(M)=-\frac{\gamma(M)^2}{\beta M}-\frac{(1-\gamma(M))^2}{\omega}. \]
The named problem would be Continuous-Mass Committee-Size Design: find a real \(M\ge1\) maximizing \(U(M)\), or certify that the supremum is approached only as \(M\to\infty\). A solution can specify the maximizing mass as an algebraic number. Extending the paper’s calculus gives a one-variable rational optimization, so I expect this candidate to be tractable. In the finite-optimum region, the condition is the one in Proposition 2.

This is a credible but limited mirror of the paper’s central committee-size question, not a computational result supplied by the paper. Its weak point is that a true atomless population removes each member’s \(1/N\) influence on the average—the very effect driving the paper’s result. Keeping that effect while replacing \(N\) by \(M\) is a continuous size relaxation; it is not automatically the standard type-distribution model. The candidate therefore supports a narrow population-continuum analogy, but it does not make the paper a computational-complexity contribution to ChoCo.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is a named result, but its task is analytic committee design, not computation over a society. Its inputs are the three scalars \(r,\omega,\beta\); it characterizes when an integer \(N\) maximizing expected performance exists. Corollary 2 reduces the finite optimum to checking the integers around a root of a scalar equation. Corollary 6 and the monetary-policy application carry over the same calculation. They supply no family of society instances, type support, or algorithmic subproblem of the kind ChoCo studies.

The proposed Continuous-Mass Committee-Size Design does not fix that gap. Its \(M\) is a real-valued relaxation of the number of seats. In ChoCo’s normalized distribution over types, total population size disappears. To preserve the paper’s \(1/N\) terms, each member must retain a positive share \(1/M\) of the committee’s vote; that describes finitely many strategic atoms, and \(M\) must be an integer. Letting \(M\) be real smooths the cardinality formula, but does not make the society continuous.

The natural population limit makes the problem’s key mechanism disappear. One can model members’ private signals as a distribution of signal types. In an atomless society, each member has zero influence on the average vote. The equilibrium response coefficient tends to
\[ \gamma_\infty=\frac{(1-r)\beta}{(1-r)\beta+\omega}, \]
and the average private-signal noise vanishes. Accordingly,
\[ \lim_{N\to\infty} E(P)=-\frac{(1-\gamma_\infty)^2}{\omega}. \]
This is a meaningful limiting equilibrium, but it gives a single endpoint rather than a committee-size choice. The finite optimum in Proposition 2 depends on the changing influence of each member and the variance term \(\gamma_N^2/(\beta N)\). Average versus median voting, or the monetary-policy application, does not restore those finite-member effects in an atomless population.

Grouping members into positive-mass signal types does not preserve the mechanism either. If each type acts as a bloc, the strategic units become blocs rather than repeated individual members; if members within each type act individually, each member again has zero mass in the continuum. Restoring finite sampling noise or individual influence brings back a discrete committee. An extension with heterogeneous experts might lead to a new optimization problem, but this paper’s homogeneous model provides no computational result to anchor it, and such an extension would need its own justification.

So the negative case is strong for a ChoCo computational mirror: the real-mass proposal continues cardinality rather than continuizing the society, while the genuine atomless mirror loses the finite-size design question. The universal claim is harder to defend. A large panel drawn from a pool of interchangeable experts is plausible, and its mean-field equilibrium could be worth studying. That would be an analytic limit question of the kind the programme treats as machinery or neighboring work, rather than a computational complexity question supplied by this paper.
