The continuous mirror question

Given rational constituency-role masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_m>0\) summing to \(1\), with independent equiprobable citizen votes within each constituency, choose a monotone simple game \(V\) on the \(m\) delegates minimizing \(\lim_{N\to\infty}\Delta[V[M_{n_1},\ldots,M_{n_m}]]/\sqrt N\) over population sequences with \(n_i/N\to\mu_i\); determine all minimizers.

The model it lives in

The society is a mass distribution \(\mu\) over constituency-role types, whose members follow the paper's within-constituency majority rule and independent equiprobable voting law. The decision variable is the council game \(V\), and the objective is the limiting scaled mean majority deficit \(\widehat{\Delta}(\mu,V)=\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}{2}\left(1-\sum_i\sqrt{\mu_i}\,\beta'_i[V]\right)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's large-constituency optimization of the council rule for mean majority deficit; it does not cover the paper's other identities, its equal-suffrage analysis, or its normative discussion.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give an exact bit-complexity bound for representing and evaluating the optimal square-root weighted council rule from binary-rational \(\mu_i\), including coalitions tied at half the total weight.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s own two-tier model. Take \(m\) constituencies, with \(\mu_i>0\) the fraction of the population in constituency \(i\) and \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). A type is a citizen’s constituency role: citizens of that type face the same internal majority rule and the same voting law. On each yes/no issue, their votes are independent and equiprobable, as in the paper. The regime is many citizens per constituency, with \(m\) fixed or moderate. A federal electorate with millions of citizens in each of a modest number of districts is a plausible example.

Lead problem: Asymptotic Mean Majority Deficit Council Design. Given rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_m\), choose a simple voting game \(V\) for the \(m\) delegates. For a population of size \(N\), let \(n_i/N\to\mu_i\), let each constituency use majority rule \(M_{n_i}\), and write \(W_N=V[M_{n_1},\ldots,M_{n_m}]\). Minimize the limiting, population-scaled mean majority deficit
\[ \widehat{\Delta}(\mu,V) =\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\Delta[W_N]}{\sqrt N}. \]
A solution is a monotone council rule \(V\) attaining this minimum.

This keeps the paper’s underlying decision problem: choose the council’s rule to minimize the expected shortfall from direct majority rule. The society is the continuous object, described by constituency masses; the decision variable remains the council’s rule. The scale \(\sqrt N\) preserves the pivotal-voter effects that would disappear if one replaced each constituency by a perfectly fixed, exactly balanced continuum.

Anchor: Theorem 3 (Morriss). The article states that, when the constituency sizes are sufficiently large, a council rule meeting Lemma 2’s conditions maximizes the composite system’s sensitivity and therefore minimizes its mean majority deficit, with vanishing or negligible error. In full, set \(w_i=\sqrt{n_i}\) and \(w=\sum_i w_i\). Every coalition \(T\) with \(\sum_{i\in T}w_i<w/2\) must lose, and every coalition with \(\sum_{i\in T}w_i>w/2\) must win; coalitions exactly at half are unconstrained. In particular, a weighted voting game with these weights and quota just above \(w/2\) is optimal. The article derives this from Lemma 2 and its asymptotic analysis. It credits the central rule to Morriss, while noting that its proof and the crucial quota proviso were missing from his account.

In the continuous problem, this becomes the same characterization with \(w_i=\sqrt{\mu_i}\). Indeed, the paper’s asymptotics and its identity relating mean majority deficit to sensitivity yield
\[ \widehat{\Delta}(\mu,V) =\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}{2} \left(1-\sum_{i=1}^m\sqrt{\mu_i}\,\beta'_i[V]\right), \]
where \(\beta'_i[V]\) is delegate \(i\)’s absolute Banzhaf power. The optimal council rules are therefore exactly those that lose coalitions below half the total square-root mass and win those above it. This is a structural, Class A-style optimization result: I would expect the mirror to be tractable in an exact algebraic-weight representation. The paper does not give a conventional bit-complexity classification, so exact computational bounds for rational input masses would be a further question.

The authors should recognize this mirror: it preserves their two-tier system, majority rule within constituencies, random-division assumption, and MMD objective. It covers the Theorem 3 result, not every result in the article. Further questions include the finite-population error as \(N\) grows, exact handling of coalitions tied at half, and whether related objectives remain tractable when constituencies use different internal rules.

The weak point is that a deterministic continuum with exactly half the citizens on each side would erase the pivotality effects the paper measures. This mirror must retain the paper’s random voting law through its large-population fluctuation limit. That is a real co-modification, but it is motivated by the paper’s own large-constituency assumption and preserves the population as the object being scaled. There is no named NP-hardness or complexity-class theorem here; the positive case rests on the paper’s named optimization theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed objective survives only as a fluctuation-scale quantity. For every fixed council rule \(V\), the paper’s mean majority deficit is \(O(\sqrt N)\), so the natural per-capita deficit \(\Delta[W_N]/N\) tends to zero for every \(V\). In a deterministic, nonatomic society, an individual has zero probability of being pivotal. The proposal recovers a nonzero comparison by dividing by \(\sqrt N\), which preserves the central-limit fluctuations of the finite Bernoulli electorate. That gives a coherent stochastic limit, but the signal it measures comes from the voting noise, not from the continuous mass distribution itself.

There is also a mismatch with the programme’s computational target. The limit retains \(m\) distinct delegates and asks which finite simple game they should use; the masses \(\mu_i\) supply the delegates’ weights. It does not turn manipulation, control, campaigning, or robustness into a population problem. And Theorem 3 is already a structural optimization result for that council-design problem, rather than a complexity result that opens a new computational landscape. A richer type space would require adding preference or correlation structure absent from the paper, changing the model whose MMD is being minimized.

That is the best negative case, but it does not defeat the anchor. The proponent’s \(\widehat{\Delta}\) is well-defined, the population proportions are genuinely continuous, and retaining the paper’s stochastic fluctuations is a defensible co-modification. Theorem 3’s asymptotics even supply a result about that limit. Under the programme’s stated gate, the authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem. So the case against a worthwhile mirror is weak here; I cannot honestly rule out this scenario.
