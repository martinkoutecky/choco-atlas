Why no mirror

Both theorems are named computational results, but the proposed mirrors preserve the paper's indivisible precincts and its existing weighted audit and plan-generation questions. Normalizing the precinct counts into type masses changes units, and rational masses can be scaled back to counts without changing the constraints. The paper's model is already the proposed population-mass formulation, so there is no distinct continuous-population question to bridge to.

What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation covers the paper's LF Auditing result in Theorem 2 and LFP Generation result in Theorem 3; it leaves the MCMC ranking method and empirical findings outside those anchors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the local-fairness audit, because it preserves the paper’s central question: can a geographically connected, population-balanced group make a justified complaint about its assigned districts?

Take the paper’s precinct graph \(G=(V,E)\), but represent the residents as a normalized measure over types. A type is a precinct and an election-relevant status: red voter, blue voter, or nonvoter. For precinct \(v\), give those types masses
\[ \mu_{v,R}=\frac{\gamma(v)\tau(v)}{\rho(V)},\qquad \mu_{v,B}=\frac{\beta(v)\tau(v)}{\rho(V)},\qquad \mu_{v,A}=\frac{\rho(v)-\tau(v)}{\rho(V)}. \]
The masses sum to \(1\); zero-mass types are omitted. They are rational inputs in binary. People sharing a type have the same location and the same status for this problem, and a district plan assigns everyone at a precinct together. Thus the population is continuous while the paper’s geographic units remain intact. A statewide election is a plausible regime: millions of residents distributed over thousands of precincts, with at most \(3|V|\) relevant types. That makes the number of people large relative to the type support.

Call the resulting problem Continuous-Mass Local-Fairness Auditing. Its instance is \(G\), the type distribution \(\mu\), \(\epsilon,k,c\), and a feasible plan \(\Pi\). The question is whether there is a connected \(W\subseteq V\) with population mass in \([(1-\epsilon)/k,(1+\epsilon)/k]\) that is a deviating group. Write \(R(W)\), \(B(W)\), and \(A(W)\) for its red-voter, blue-voter, and nonvoter masses, and \(q(W)=R(W)+B(W)\). If the plan’s district color at \(v\) is \(\chi_\Pi(v)\), let \(U_R^\Pi(W)\) be the red-voter mass in \(W\) assigned to blue districts, and \(U_B^\Pi(W)\) the blue-voter mass assigned to red districts. A red deviation satisfies \(R(W)\ge B(W)\) and \(U_R^\Pi(W)>c q(W)\); a blue deviation satisfies \(B(W)>R(W)\) and \(U_B^\Pi(W)>c q(W)\), matching the paper’s tie convention. A yes solution is such a set \(W\); a no instance has no such set. Complexity should be stated in graph size, type support, and rational bit length—for example, \(|V|,|E|,\tau,L,k\), with \(\tau\le 3|V|\).

This directly mirrors Theorem 2, proved in the paper: LF Auditing is NP-complete. The paper proves it by reduction from Connected \(k\)-Subgraph on planar graphs with binary weights, whose NP-completeness it cites as [25]. The reduction’s instances use integer precinct populations and voter masses; normalize them by total population and they are instances of the continuous-mass formulation with the same yes/no answer. The hard choice is still which connected part of the map constitutes the complaint. I therefore expect hardness transfer (Class B), with the reduction’s combinatorics living in the geography rather than in a need to distinguish individual voters.

A second, related anchor is Theorem 3, also proved in the paper: LFP Generation is NP-complete. Its continuous mirror, Continuous-Mass Locally-Fair Plan Generation, takes \(G,\mu,\epsilon,k,c\) and asks whether there is a partition of the precincts into \(k\) connected, population-balanced districts such that no connected, balanced \(W\) meets either deviation condition above. A yes solution is such a plan. The paper proves hardness using the same reduction family as Theorem 2; in that family the plan is forced, so this is a useful but less independent anchor. I likewise expect hardness transfer (Class B). Together these mirrors cover the paper’s named hardness results for auditing and generation, not its MCMC ranking method or its empirical compatibility and robustness claims.

The authors should recognize these questions: the paper defines complaints through population balance and voter fractions, and the mirror keeps the same regional complaint, same precinct map, and same district assignment rules. Keeping precincts indivisible while treating their residents as mass is a natural co-modification for a population continuum. It also means the mirror does not smooth away the connected-subgraph problem that drives the hardness.

The weak point is proximity to the paper’s existing model. The paper already aggregates voters by precinct and uses fractional party shares \(\gamma(v)\) and \(\beta(v)\); its audit already operates on sums of those quantities over precinct sets. A referee could reasonably say that replacing counts by normalized rational masses mainly changes the interpretation, since rational instances can be scaled back to integer counts. That limits the claim of novelty. It does not make the mirror incoherent, but it means the honest positive case is a precise high-multiplicity formulation whose hardness carries over—not a claim that the continuous version is a fundamentally different or easier auditing problem.

The natural follow-up questions are whether the hardness persists under further restrictions on the precinct graphs or population masses, and whether parameters such as treewidth or type support permit useful algorithms despite the hardness. The paper’s tree dynamic program makes the latter a particularly concrete direction, though its stated running time is not itself a polynomial-time result in the binary input length.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a novelty collision: the paper already presents the local-fairness audit in mass terms at the precinct level. For each precinct \(v\), its existing data determine red-voter mass \(\gamma(v)\tau(v)\), blue-voter mass \((1-\gamma(v))\tau(v)\), and nonvoter mass \(\rho(v)-\tau(v)\). Normalize those by \(\rho(V)\), and you get exactly the proposed distribution over types \((v,R),(v,B),(v,A)\). The deviation tests and population-balance constraints are already sums of these quantities over a connected set \(W\subseteq V\). No named voter is tracked.

That equivalence is exact for the programme’s binary-rational input model. A common-denominator scaling turns rational type masses into integer weights, while normalization preserves the balance and majority inequalities. So Theorem 2’s LF Auditing problem is already the computational question posed by the proposed continuous-mass mirror, expressed in count units. The objection is not that its hardness survives continuization; hardness would be a useful result. It is that, with precincts indivisible and candidate groups still subsets of precincts, the mirror introduces no distinct problem for the programme to chart.

Theorem 3’s LFP Generation anchor has the same issue. The paper’s feasible plans are partitions of precinct vertices, and its continuous-mass version keeps exactly that plan space and exactly those balance tests. Recasting the precinct weights as normalized masses therefore restates the paper’s plan-generation problem. Theorem 3 uses the same reduction family as Theorem 2, so it does not provide an independent opening once the audit mirror is already a collision.

The stronger alternative would let a district or deviating group split a precinct’s population, or replace precincts with a continuous spatial density and allow arbitrary connected regions. That could define a worthwhile problem, but it changes the feasible complaint or plan space. In a spatial continuum, connected regions can be joined by arbitrarily thin, zero-mass corridors; avoiding that pathology requires further choices about shape, scale, or compactness. The paper supplies no canonical choices, and imposing them would make the contribution a new geometric redistricting model rather than a continuous mirror of its two theorems.

This negative case is strongest for the stated anchors, not for every imaginable spatial model. If the authors want to study sub-precinct geography and can ground its representation and constraints, that may be worthwhile. But the proponent’s mirror preserves the paper’s precinct-level choices, and at that level the computational question is already there.
