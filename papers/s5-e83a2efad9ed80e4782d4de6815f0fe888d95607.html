What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest candidate is an inverse Shapley allocation problem. Let the society be a large electorate with \(k\) party-affiliation types, where \(\mu_i\) is the fraction supporting party \(i\). Choose normalized delegate weights \(x\), with \(\sum_i x_i=1\). These weights define a weighted-majority game \(v_x\): a coalition wins when its total delegate weight reaches the majority quota. The task is to find \(x\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{k}\bigl(\phi_i(v_x)-\mu_i\bigr)^2, \]
where \(\phi_i(v_x)\) is party \(i\)’s Shapley power. For the paper’s all-inclusive scenario, require that no party be a dummy. A solution is a delegate-weight vector attaining the minimum.

The natural regime is a national electorate with many voters but relatively few party affiliations: voters sharing an affiliation are indistinguishable for this calculation, and their mass is their population fraction. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the authors would recognize the allocation question as theirs. But this is also the candidate’s central weakness: the paper already formulates support as proportions \(p_i\), searches over normalized voting stock \(\rho\), and minimizes the squared discrepancy \(F\) between target and computed Shapley values. The proposed mirror largely restates the paper’s own model; it does not uncover a new continuous-population version.

There is no qualifying anchor in the paper. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. The “Search algorithm” is an unnumbered numerical procedure with examples, not a theorem establishing tractability or another complexity classification. So I cannot honestly provide the required numbered anchor or claim that this candidate mirrors a named complexity result.

For the candidate problem, I would expect the coalition combinatorics over the \(k\) parties to remain, so replacing voter counts with masses offers no clear reason for hardness to disappear. Further questions would include whether the target Shapley vector can be attained exactly, how hard it is to find the best no-dummy allocation, and how complexity depends on \(k\) and the input bit length. Those could make a computational programme, but this paper supplies no named result to anchor them to.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only candidate is not anchored to a named computational result in the paper. They acknowledge that the paper has no numbered theorem or corollary about complexity. It does give a search algorithm, but without a complexity or correctness classification. Studying that algorithm more rigorously could be worthwhile; it would not, by itself, supply the discrete computational result whose continuous mirror ChoCo is meant to assess.

The proposed inverse-Shapley problem also already has the continuous population representation. The paper gives party support as proportions \(p_i\), searches over normalized voting stocks \(\rho\), and minimizes the discrepancy between the target and computed Shapley values. Replacing \(p_i\) with masses \(\mu_i\) and writing the objective as \(\sum_i(\phi_i(v_x)-\mu_i)^2\) restates that setup. The paper never needs voter counts or individual supporters: the relevant players are the parties, and the computation depends on the number of parties, not on how many people support them. Treating voters as the players would change the coalition game and the meaning of the Shapley value. Treating delegate weights as continuous makes the outcome continuous, which is also the wrong axis for a population mirror.

One could invent a nearby problem in which support mass moves between parties, or parties are grouped into types. But the former changes the paper’s lever from assigning voting stock to changing voter affiliation; the latter risks erasing party identities that determine which coalitions win. Neither supplies a stronger mirror of a result in this paper. The best case against ChoCo’s investment is therefore a collision: its sole computational candidate already works with aggregate proportions and continuous delegate weights. A complexity analysis of the inverse search may still be interesting, but it would analyze an already continuous problem rather than continuize a discrete-population one.

I would qualify the universal claim: it is hard to rule out every adjacent research question one could build from this paper. The case is strongest for the narrower conclusion that this paper offers no worthwhile *continuous-population mirror* of its computational content.
