The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), quota groups \(T_r\), normalized lower and upper quota counts \(\hat q^-_r,\hat q^+_r\), type dropout estimates \(\tilde d_t\), robustness radius \(\gamma\), and a fixed normalized inclusion intensity \(x_t\), compute a finite-support law \(\rho\) over dropout masses \(Z\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\) maximizing \(\mathbb E_{Z\sim\rho}[\max_{r,\sigma\in\{-,+\}} d_r^\sigma(x,Z)]\). Here \(A_r(x,Z)=\sum_{t\in T_r}x_t(\mu_t-Z_t)\), \(d^-_r=(\hat q^-_r-A_r)/\hat q^+_r\), and \(d^+_r=(A_r-\hat q^+_r)/\hat q^+_r\). Require \(\mathbb E[Z_t]/\mu_t\in[\max(0,\tilde d_t-\gamma),\min(1,\tilde d_t+\gamma)]\) for each type and \(\sum_t\mathbb E[Z_t]=\sum_t\mu_t\tilde d_t\). For a fixed-size panel, \(x_t=p_t/(k/n)\) and \(\hat q_r^\pm=q_r^\pm/k\), where \(p_t\) and \(q_r^\pm\) are the original inclusion probabilities and quota counts.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over types sharing quota memberships and dropout estimates. For a fixed type-level inclusion intensity \(x_t\), the adversary chooses a law over typewise dropout masses \(Z_t\); the objective is expected maximum normalized quota deviation, with the paper’s marginal-rate bounds and expected-total-dropout constraint. In the fixed-size interpretation, normalize inclusion probabilities and quota counts by \(k/n\) and \(k\), respectively.

The objection that survived

For the paper’s fixed-size panels, selected mass per capita vanishes as the pool grows; using normalized inclusion intensities and quotas preserves a nondegenerate objective, but the paper does not establish that scaling as an exact limit of its finite-panel model.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the paper’s worst-case dropout best-response problem. It is narrower than the full sortition algorithm, but it is a named computational result the paper proves, and it survives aggregation because the loss depends on quota-group totals rather than on who, by name, drops out.

Call the mirror Type-Continuum Dropout Best Response. Its instance has a finite set of types \(T\), each defined by all quota-relevant features and a shared estimated dropout rate. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Each quota \(r\) selects a subset \(T_r\subseteq T\), with lower and upper targets \(q^-_r,q^+_r\), expressed as fractions of the pool. The panel policy is a type-level inclusion probability \(p_t\in[\alpha,\beta]\). For example, if \(Z_t\) is the mass of type \(t\) that drops out, the surviving selected mass in quota group \(r\) is
\[ A_r(p,Z)=\sum_{t\in T_r}p_t(\mu_t-Z_t). \]
The loss for a dropout-mass vector \(Z\) is
\[ h(p,Z)=\max_{r}\left\{ \frac{q^-_r-A_r(p,Z)}{q^+_r}, \frac{A_r(p,Z)-q^+_r}{q^+_r} \right\}. \]
Panel size is one of the quotas, with \(T_r=T\) and \(q^-_r=q^+_r\).

The adversary chooses a probability law \(\rho\) over \(Z\in\prod_t[0,\mu_t]\). For each type, its expected dropout rate must lie in the clipped interval \([\tilde d_t-\gamma,\tilde d_t+\gamma]\), and total expected dropout mass must equal \(\sum_t\mu_t\tilde d_t\). The question is: given the type masses, quotas, dropout estimates, robustness radius, and a feasible policy \(p\), compute a law \(\rho^\star\) maximizing \(\mathbb E_{Z\sim\rho}[h(p,Z)]\). A solution is an explicit maximizing law, preferably as a finite list of dropout-mass scenarios and their probabilities.

The regime I find plausible is a large civic-assembly applicant registry: perhaps tens of thousands of volunteers, represented by dozens or hundreds of strata combining quota features such as region and age band with a shared dropout-risk estimate. Then the number of people greatly exceeds the number of types. The rate must actually be shared within a type; if dropout estimates or quota-relevant attributes are person-specific, the type count can approach the pool size and the high-multiplicity case loses its point.

My anchor is Corollary A.1 (Polynomial-time best response), proved in this paper. It states that, given \(p\) and the instance, the adversary’s best-response dropout distribution can be computed in time polynomial in \((n,|FV|,L)\): the authors run Ellipsoid on the dual using their separation oracle, then solve the restricted primal over the polynomially many dropout sets collected. In their model, the objective is expected maximum normalized quota deviation, and the adversary ranges over correlated dropout distributions with bounded marginals and a fixed expected dropout count. This is precisely the underlying best-response problem, not merely an encoding.

I expect the type-continuum version to be tractable. For fixed \(p\), it is a linear optimization problem over probability laws on a box. Its objective is the maximum of finitely many affine functions of \(Z\). In the dual separation step, one can consider each quota and sign in turn, then maximize an affine function over the box by choosing each coordinate’s appropriate endpoint. That gives a polynomial-size separation calculation in the number of types and quota groups. The primal optimum can be represented by a finite-support law. This is my expected continuation of the paper’s argument, not a theorem proved in the paper.

The mapping preserves the substance of the anchor. For a finite pool with identical members within each type, a dropout set maps to the mass dropped from each type. Since all members of a type have the same policy probability and the same quota memberships, their identities do not affect the loss. The continuum law allows fractional aggregate dropout masses, just as the continuous population replaces head counts by mass. The paper’s existing fractional panel vector \(p\in[0,1]^n\) alone does not do this: it still has one coordinate per named person. Here the society itself is the distribution \(\mu\), with complexity measured in \(|T|\).

This covers the adversary’s best-response result, not the paper’s entire sortition procedure. The full minmax problem would be a natural next question: optimize the type-level policy \(p\) against this continuum best response. I would expect the same convex-optimization approach to be promising there, but I would not present that as a result of this single anchor. Nor does this mirror claim to solve the finite-panel rounding problem.

The soft spot is the operating regime. The paper’s observed pools are small, and a citizens’ assembly often selects a small panel; the authors or a referee could reasonably ask whether the proposed large registry is representative of their intended use. Fine-grained attributes or individual dropout forecasts could also make the number of types large. The case survives if there is a real registry with many applicants per relevant feature-and-risk stratum. In that regime, aggregating by type is the high-multiplicity question itself, not a loss of meaningful distinctions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is the mismatch between a continuum of applicants and the paper’s deliberately small, fixed-size panel. If the pool grows while panel size \(k\) stays fixed, the average inclusion probability is \(k/n\), which tends to zero. Quota counts for a citizens’ assembly stay on the scale of people, not population mass. A literal limit in which inclusion probabilities and quota fractions vanish does not preserve the paper’s normalized loss as a nondegenerate mass objective. Keeping quota and panel size as positive fractions of the population avoids that problem, but describes a large, population-proportional institution rather than the small deliberative panels that motivate the paper.

That objection narrows the natural use case, but it does not defeat the strongest version of the mirror. One can rescale inclusion probabilities by \(k/n\), express quota targets relative to panel size, and model selection through type-level sampling intensities. That gives a nonzero limit for the fractional best-response problem while retaining a small panel. It is less direct as a model of the final rounded panel, but Corollary A.1 is about the adversary’s response to a fixed fractional policy, so rounding is not needed for this anchor.

Nor does aggregation itself provide a sound objection. If type captures quota memberships, dropout estimate, and policy probability, the loss depends on a type’s aggregate dropped mass, not on the names of its members. The continuum adversary’s law over those masses is a sensible high-multiplicity relaxation. Its separation problem also appears to retain the paper’s structure: enumerate the quota-and-sign affine pieces, then maximize each over the type-mass box. So the proposed tractability is credible, even though it is an expected extension rather than a theorem in this paper.

The remaining negative case is about priority. This best-response problem is already polynomial-time, and its continuum version looks like a compact, fairly direct extension of the paper’s fractional optimization machinery. That may make it a modest research contribution compared with a mirror that reveals a new tractability boundary. But the programme treats high-multiplicity compression as a substantive bridge, and a large registry with many applicants per quota-and-risk type is plausible. Existing continuous relaxation work does not already answer this correlated-dropout question.

I cannot honestly defeat this anchor in every plausible regime. The fixed-small-panel mismatch limits its reach, and the likely result may be incremental; neither is enough to show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The negative case is weak here, and this anchor likely survives.
