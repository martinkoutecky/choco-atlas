The continuous mirror question

For \(n\) items, \(k\), and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, each pairwise observation is absent with probability \(1-p\); otherwise, a fresh ranking drawn from \(\mu\) reports which item it prefers. The distribution is hidden from the estimator. Characterize the number \(r\) of trials per pair needed to recover, with high probability, the \(k\) items maximizing \(s_i(\mu)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_j \Pr_{t\sim\mu}[i\succ_t j]\), in terms of the gap \(\Delta_k=s_{(k)}-s_{(k+1)}\), including matching upper and lower bounds.

The model it lives in

A continuous society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete voter rankings. It induces pairwise probabilities \(M_{ij}=\sum_{t:i\succ_t j}\mu_t\); each observed comparison samples a fresh representative ranking from that society. The objective is to identify the top \(k\) items by expected pairwise-win score from those observations.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s exact top-\(k\) recovery and Theorem 2’s Hamming-tolerant recovery. It leaves the full-ranking corollary, the general set-system result in Theorem 3, and the experiments outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do the upper and lower sample-complexity bounds remain tight when the hidden population is restricted to a given support size \(\tau\)?
  • Can adaptive choice of which item pairs to compare improve the sample requirement for a distribution over ranking types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a statistical mirror of the paper’s top-\(k\) recovery problem. There is a qualification at the outset: the paper proves named algorithmic and statistical results, but no named theorem classifies a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. So these are anchors for an algorithmic-statistical mirror, not qualifying complexity-class anchors under a strict reading of that requirement.

Take \(n\) items and let a voter type \(t\) be a complete ranking of them. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over its \(\tau\) supported rankings. For each pair, set
\[ M^\mu_{ij}=\sum_{t:\,i\succ_t j}\mu_t,\qquad s_i(\mu)=\frac1n\sum_j M^\mu_{ij}. \]
The paper’s target ordering by pairwise-win scores is then the population’s Borda-style ordering. In a plausible regime, a very large consumer panel or voter population has many members but relatively few recurring preference types. To match the paper’s observation model, each scheduled pairwise comparison is observed with probability \(p\); when observed, a fresh representative voter is sampled from \(\mu\) and reports their preference. Thus each pair has the same pairwise probability as in the paper.

Lead: Continuous Population Exact Top-\(k\) Identification. Given \(n,k,p,r\) and the comparison observations, return a \(k\)-item set \(\widehat S_k\). The hidden population \(\mu\) determines the true top-\(k\) set \(S_k^\star\) by the scores \(s_i(\mu)\). The problem is to characterize the comparison budget needed to recover \(S_k^\star\) with high probability as a function of the gap \(\Delta_k=s_{(k)}-s_{(k+1)}\); the solution is the output set. The population is the continuous object here: the observations are how the unknown population is learned.

Its anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that if \(\Delta_k\ge\alpha\sqrt{\log n/(npr)}\) and \(\alpha\ge8\), the maximum-pairwise-win estimator has error probability at most \(n^{-14}\), uniformly over the paper’s matrix class. Conversely, for \(n\ge7\), \(p\ge\log n/(2nr)\), and \(\alpha\le1/7\), every estimator has worst-case error probability at least \(1/7\). The paper also says the lower bound holds on the BTL and SST subclasses. This gives the mirror a meaningful population regime: BTL pairwise probabilities admit random-utility rankings, so they can be represented by a distribution over voter types. The computational step is counting wins and sorting, hence tractable; the theorem’s deeper contribution is a sharp statistical threshold, not a complexity classification.

Continuous Population Approximate Top-\(k\) Identification uses the same population and observation model, but accepts any \(k\)-set within Hamming distance \(2h\) of \(S_k^\star\). The question is to characterize the sample requirement using the wider separation
\[ \Delta_{k,h}=s_{(k-h)}-s_{(k+h+1)}. \]
Its solution is again an output set, now satisfying \(D_H(\widehat S_k,S_k^\star)\le2h\).

The anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. For \(\Delta_{k,h}\ge\alpha\sqrt{\log n/(npr)}\) and \(\alpha\ge8\), the same maximum-win estimator violates the Hamming guarantee with probability at most \(n^{-14}\). In the converse direction, when \(p\ge\log n/(2nr)\), \(2h\le\frac{1}{1+\nu_2}\min\{n^{1-\nu_1},k,n-k\}\), and \(n\) is sufficiently large, every estimator has error probability at least \(1/7\) if \(\alpha\le\sqrt{\nu_1\nu_2}/14\). The parameters \(\nu_1,\nu_2\) lie in \((0,1)\). This problem is tractable in its computation as well; it asks whether relaxing the recovery target changes the population sample threshold. Natural follow-up questions include how known support size \(\tau\) changes that threshold, and whether adaptive pair selection can improve it.

The authors could plausibly recognize these as population versions of their problem: their score is exactly an aggregate of pairwise preferences, their paper includes voting applications, and their lower bounds already include structured preference models. The case covers top-\(k\) recovery and its Hamming relaxation, not the paper’s entire experimental and statistical agenda.

The weak point is substantial. In ChoCo’s usual formulation, the society is supplied as an explicit support-and-mass list. If \(\mu\) is supplied that way, the scores above can be computed directly, so the paper’s unknown-population estimation problem has disappeared. Preserving the paper’s central uncertainty instead requires a sampling-oracle input, which departs from that explicit-input convention. Combined with the absence of a named complexity-class result, that makes this a credible continuous-population statistical analogue, but a borderline ChoCo computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a mismatch between the paper’s computational object and ChoCo’s population input. The paper gives named theorems, but Theorems 1 and 2 bound statistical error as a function of the separation \(\Delta\) and the number of noisy pairwise observations. The running-time advantage is reported experimentally; no theorem classifies the algorithm’s computational complexity.

The proposed population model is coherent. Given a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, it induces pairwise probabilities \(M^\mu_{ij}=\sum_{t:i\succ_t j}\mu_t\), and hence the paper’s scores. The BTL lower-bound examples can also arise from distributions over rankings. So the objection is not that these rankings make an implausible society. It is that the paper’s theorems concern inference from observations when \(M\) is unknown. ChoCo’s society input supplies the support and masses. With that input, the pairwise probabilities and scores follow from \(\mu\); the theorem’s unknown statistical object and sample-budget question have been replaced by a population Borda-ranking task the paper does not analyze. Keeping the sample-budget question requires hiding \(\mu\) behind a sampling process. That is a coherent statistical problem, but its population is a data-generating distribution rather than the supplied society on which ChoCo’s computational questions operate.

Theorem 1’s exact top-\(k\) anchor therefore supports a plausible continuous-population statistical analogue: estimate the top-\(k\) items from pairwise samples generated by a large voter population. That is the best version of the proponent’s case, and it remains interesting on its own terms. It does not provide a computational-complexity mirror under ChoCo’s stated input model.

Theorem 2 has the same input mismatch. Its Hamming tolerance counts errors among the \(n\) items being ranked; it says nothing about a fraction of voters or mass in the society. The strongest repair keeps a population of ranking types and allows \(h\) mistakes when estimating its top-\(k\) Borda order from samples. That preserves the statistical question and the paper’s result, while leaving the continuous society as the hidden source of observations. Recasting \(h\) as a mass tolerance would change the recovery target and would no longer be this theorem’s question.

My negative case is strongest as a case against these anchors as ChoCo computational mirrors, and weak as a claim that no valuable continuous-population question can be asked here. If ChoCo includes sample-oracle statistical ranking, both anchors survive as credible analogues. Under its supplied-mass and computational-complexity remit, the paper gives no named result for them to mirror.
