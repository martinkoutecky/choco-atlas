The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchor-compliant case from this paper: it contains no named result about computational complexity, such as a problem being in P, NP-hard, or FPT.

The closest result is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that, when all reservation prices are positive, the set of equilibrium price vectors in the finite Gale–Shapley-type market is a disjoint union of axis-aligned boxes; each coordinate interval is either \((r,p]\) with \(0<r<p\), or \([0,p]\) with \(p>0\). That is a structural characterization, not a computational result: the paper gives no algorithm or complexity bound for finding or representing those boxes. Theorem 1, attributed to Gale (1960), characterizes equilibrium allocations as maximum-value assignments. Theorem 2, attributed to Świtalski (2014), characterizes equilibrium allocations as stable matchings under its distinct-reservation-price assumption. Neither is presented as a complexity result. So there are zero eligible anchors, and no per-anchor continuous questions to state under your rules.

There is a plausible *follow-on* question, though it would be unanchored. Consider a large school-admissions market with applicant types \(T\), where a type records a strict ranking of schools and its full vector of reservation thresholds \(r_{td}\). Let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s population fraction, and let \(q_d\) be each school’s capacity as a fraction of the population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=\sum_d q_d=1\). Ask whether there are nonnegative prices \(p_d\) and an allocation \(x_{td}\ge 0\) such that type masses and school capacities clear, and \(x_{td}>0\) only when \(d\) is type \(t\)’s most-preferred school among those with \(r_{td}\ge p_d\). A solution is such a price vector and allocation. This uses population mass, with types defined by every feature the market model uses; the regime could be large applicant cohorts with relatively few shared application and threshold profiles.

That question follows the paper’s school-admissions motivation and its study of equilibrium prices. But the paper does not analyze it computationally. Theorem 3 may suggest useful price-region structure; it does not establish tractability or hardness once type masses and school capacities are introduced. The weakest point is also the main modeling constraint: with strict preferences, identical agents of a type choose the same school, so a type’s mass cannot be split across schools just to fill capacities. That seems faithful to the model, but it can make equilibrium existence brittle. I would leave the complexity class open and present this only as a credible research direction, not as a mirror anchored in a computational result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the lack of a computational result to mirror. Theorem 3 characterizes the geometry of equilibrium price vectors as a union of boxes. Theorem 1 characterizes equilibrium allocations by maximum total value, and Theorem 2 relates them to stable matchings under an extra assumption. None states a computational problem or a complexity result. So the proponent is right that this paper supplies no eligible anchor for the requested kind of mirror.

Their proposed school-admissions question does not fix that gap. As written, it permits a type’s mass to split among schools, but under the paper’s strict rankings every type has a unique best affordable school at given prices. A faithful version would assign each type’s mass to that one school, requiring school capacities to match sums of whole type masses. That is a reasonable model, but it is a new computational question about a many-to-one market; the paper’s price-box theorem does not establish or analyze it.

I can’t honestly defend the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. A large admissions cohort with a modest number of shared rankings and reservation-price profiles is plausible, and studying equilibrium prices for that population could be worthwhile. The negative case is therefore strong against treating this paper as an anchored continuization candidate, but weak against the universal claim: the best proposed mirror needs repair and is unanchored, yet a repaired follow-on problem remains credible.
