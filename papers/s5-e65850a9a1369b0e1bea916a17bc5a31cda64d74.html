The continuous mirror question

Given branches \(B\), service terms \(T\), and a finite support \(R\) of cadet types with rational masses \(\mu_r\), strict preferences over branch-term pairs and the outside option, and base-priority classes \(\rho_b(r)\), compute an exact rational mass allocation \(x_{r,b,t}\). Branch \(b\) has rational per-capita capacity \(q_b\) and uses BfYC priorities: allocate the first \(\lambda q_b\) by base priority, choosing the lowest-cost contract among a type's contracts, then allocate the remaining capacity by descending service term with base priority breaking ties, choosing the highest-cost contract among a type's contracts. Split mass at cutoffs among tied types. Require individual rationality and mass stability, meaning no positive mass of a type prefers a contract that the branch would accept; among stable allocations, return one that is cadet-optimal for every type under its ranking over outcomes.

The model it lives in

A finite-support population distribution \(\mu\) over complete cadet types, with rational masses, preferences, and priority classes; branch capacities scale per capita. The decision variables \(x_{r,b,t}\) give the mass of each type assigned to each branch and service term, with any unassigned mass receiving the outside option. Apply the mass extension of BfYC branch choice, allowing a type's mass to split at a capacity cutoff, and compute a stable allocation that is weakly preferred by each type to its assignment distribution in every other stable allocation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3's stable, cadet-optimal allocation and mass versions of its fairness and improvement properties. It does not preserve individual strategy-proofness in an atomless society or cover the paper's policy analysis and other results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every finite-support rational instance admit a cadet-optimal stable mass allocation under the split-at-cutoff BfYC choice rule, and can it be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(|B|\), \(|R|\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length?
  • Do finite repeated-type cohorts converge to the mass allocation as cohort size grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is narrow: compute the cadet-optimal stable allocation under the paper’s Bid-for-Your-Career (BfYC) priorities in a society with repeated cadet types.

My anchor is Proposition 3, proved in the paper. It states that the outcome of \(\phi_{\mathrm{BfYC}}\) is stable under BfYC priorities and weakly preferred by every cadet to any other stable allocation; moreover, \(\phi_{\mathrm{BfYC}}\) is strategy-proof, fair, and respects improvements. The paper reaches this result using the cumulative offer algorithm and cited results of Hatfield and Kojima. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a complexity classification: the paper makes no NP-hardness or formal “in P” claim.

Call the mirror BfYC Stable Mass Allocation. An instance has finitely many branches \(B\), service terms \(T\), and cadet types \(R\). Each type \(r\) has rational mass \(\mu_r\), a strict ranking over branch-term pairs and the outside option, and a base-priority class \(\rho_b(r)\) for each branch. Branch \(b\) has rational capacity \(q_b\); \(\lambda\) specifies the share allocated first by base priority. A type is complete: it includes every preference and priority attribute used by the allocation rule.

The output is an exact rational mass allocation \(x_{r,b,t}\geq 0\), with at most \(\mu_r\) of type \(r\) assigned and at most \(q_b\) assigned to each branch. Extend BfYC choice to masses: a branch fills the first \(\lambda q_b\) capacity by base priority, taking the lowest-cost contract when choosing among multiple terms for a type; it fills the remainder by descending service term, using base priority to break ties. At a cutoff among indistinguishable types, the rule may split mass.

Require the allocation to be individually rational and stable: there is no type \(r\), preferred contract \((b,t)\), and positive mass \(\varepsilon\) of that type currently assigned to worse outcomes such that branch \(b\) would accept any of that \(\varepsilon\) when it is offered. Among stable allocations, ask for one that is cadet-optimal: for every type, its assignment distribution weakly stochastically dominates that type’s distribution in every other stable allocation, using the type’s ranking over outcomes. Also test the mass versions of fairness and respect for priority improvements. These are questions about the underlying allocation, not an encoding.

The plausible regime is a large pooled ROTC graduating cohort, perhaps across several years, with many cadets sharing branch preferences, service-term rankings, and priority classes. Then the population \(n\) could be in the thousands while the number of recurring types \(\tau\) is much smaller. A type-level cumulative offer procedure looks likely to solve the problem efficiently in \(|B|,\tau,|T|\), and the bit length of the masses: types offer mass down their rankings, branches hold the highest-priority mass, and there are finitely many type-contract pairs to consider. That makes tractability the expected direction. The paper itself does not establish that continuous algorithm or its running time.

The mirror covers the BfYC stable-allocation result, not the paper’s policy analysis or its claims about individual incentives in full. In an atomless society, one cadet’s report has zero effect, so individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. Treating a whole type as the strategic agent would give a different incentive notion. The principal weakness is the type regime: the paper’s OML rankings are strict, so recording each cadet’s exact rank may make \(\tau\) nearly \(n\). To get a genuine high-multiplicity regime, the mirror needs shared priority classes and tie-splitting, a reasonable aggregate model but a change to the paper’s strict-priority specification. Further questions are whether cadet-optimal stable mass allocations always exist under that extension, whether finite repeated cohorts converge to them, and what useful non-vacuous incentive notion survives for positive-mass groups.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 3 is not a computational result in the sense ChoCo is targeting. It proves properties of a mechanism—stability, cadet optimality, strategy-proofness, fairness, and respect for improvements. The paper gives the cumulative offer algorithm for producing its outcome, but does not study the complexity of computing it. The proposed mirror therefore creates a new computational question from the paper’s mechanism rather than continuizing a complexity result.

There is also a concrete high-multiplicity problem with the paper’s exact priority structure. A complete type must include priority, but ROTC uses a strict OML: every cadet has a distinct rank. Even if many cadets share preferences, encoding their exact priority makes the support size \(\tau\) grow with the population. Compressing ranks into bands permits repeated types, but changes the priority model and the individual comparisons underlying fairness and respect for improvements. Keeping rank as a continuous percentile avoids bands, but requires a way to represent and compute from a distribution over priority values, beyond the paper’s finite list of rational-mass types.

The proponent’s repair—priority classes with mass split at cutoffs—is coherent, but it sheds much of Proposition 3’s distinctive incentive content. In an atomless society, one cadet’s report has no effect; a positive-mass group acting together would be a different strategic agent. What remains is the stable mass allocation problem. For finite support and finitely many contracts, that looks like a quantity version of the cumulative offer procedure, with no apparent pricing problem or other new computational bottleneck. Continuum matching work already supplies closely related stability frameworks and deferred-acceptance analogues; that is useful machinery, not a collision with this contract model or its computational question. [Leshno’s supply-and-demand framework](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/a-supply-and-demand-framework-for-two-sided-matching-markets/) and [a continuum model with finite capacities](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881) illustrate the neighboring theory.

That makes the mirror a weaker research target than Proposition 3’s finite-agent incentive result suggests: with exact priorities it does not compress the population, and with priority classes it appears to reduce to a routine mass extension of deferred acceptance. But this does not defeat the stable-allocation question altogether. A continuous BfYC model with repeated preference and priority types is sensible, and computing its stable mass allocation is a real computational question. I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists; the case against is strongest on the loss of the paper’s individual incentive content and weakest on the surviving allocation problem.
