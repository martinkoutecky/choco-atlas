The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) goods, a finite type set \(T\) with \(|T|=\tau\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), positive rational valuations \(v_{tj}\), positive rational per-unit-mass budgets \(\kappa_t\), and an accuracy parameter \(\varepsilon>0\), compute prices \(p_j>0\) and per-unit-mass spending vectors \(z_t\) such that \(p_j=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t z_{tj}\), \(\sum_j z_{tj}\leq\kappa_t\), and each type’s spending utility is within \(\varepsilon\) of its maximum at prices \(p\).

The model it lives in

A quasi-linear Fisher market with a continuum of firms represented by finitely many complete types \(t\), each carrying mass \(\mu_t\), valuation vector \(v_t\), and budget \(\kappa_t\). The decision variables are per-unit-mass spendings \(z_{tj}\), unspent budgets \(w_t\), and prices \(p_j=\sum_t\mu_tz_{tj}\); the weighted convex program minimizes \(-\sum_t\mu_t\sum_j z_{tj}\ln v_{tj}+\sum_t\mu_t w_t+\sum_j p_j\ln p_j\), subject to \(\sum_jz_{tj}+w_t=\kappa_t\) and \(z_{tj},w_t\geq0\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s PR-QLIN convergence result and its market-equilibrium convex program; it leaves Theorem 6’s chaos result, Proposition 15’s best-response stability result, and Proposition 3’s approximate Nash guarantee aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Establish iteration and bit-complexity bounds for computing an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate equilibrium from the binary encoding of \((\mu,v,\kappa)\), in terms of \(m\), \(\tau\), input length, and \(\varepsilon\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is Theorem 4, proved in the paper: given any positive starting portfolio, PR-QLIN converges to the set of market-equilibrium spending vectors of a quasi-linear Fisher market. This is an algorithmic convergence result, not a theorem that the problem is in \(\mathrm P\); the paper gives no named hardness or computational-complexity classification result.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large production marketplace with many small firms and a short catalog of firm profiles. A type \(t\) records a firm’s complete valuation vector \((v_{tj})_{j\in M}\) and its capital parameter \(\kappa_t\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of firms with that profile. For example, many miners or cloud-service providers might share one of a few hardware, contract, and capital profiles. In a finite population of \(n\) firms, the corresponding unit-supply market has \(n_t=n\mu_t\) firms of type \(t\), each with budget \(\kappa_t/n\). That scaling keeps aggregate resources finite as the population grows, and matches the paper’s setting of firms with negligible individual influence.

I would ask a prover to study Continuous-Type Quasi-Linear Fisher Equilibrium. An instance gives \(m\) goods, a finite type support \(T\) of size \(\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational valuations \(v_{tj}>0\), and rational capital parameters \(\kappa_t>0\). A solution is any equilibrium spending profile \(z_{tj}\geq 0\) and price vector \(p\) such that
\[ p_j=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t z_{tj}, \qquad \sum_{j\in M} z_{tj}\leq \kappa_t, \]
and each type’s spending maximizes
\[ \sum_{j\in M} z_{tj}\left(\frac{v_{tj}}{p_j}-1\right) \]
subject to its budget. Here \(z_{tj}\) is spending per unit of type mass; type \(t\) receives \(z_{tj}/p_j\) units per unit mass, so market clearing follows from the price equation.

This question has a concrete optimization form. With \(w_t\) denoting unspent capital, compute a minimizer of
\[ -\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{j\in M}z_{tj}\ln v_{tj} +\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t w_t +\sum_{j\in M}p_j\ln p_j \]
subject to \(p_j=\sum_t\mu_tz_{tj}\), \(\sum_j z_{tj}+w_t=\kappa_t\), and \(z_{tj},w_t\geq0\). This is the type-mass version of the paper’s market-equilibrium program. The corresponding PR update is also explicit: with \(p_j^r=\sum_t\mu_tz^r_{tj}\) and \(S_t^r=\sum_jv_{tj}z^r_{tj}/p_j^r\), set
\[ z^{r+1}_{tj} = \frac{v_{tj}z^r_{tj}/p_j^r}{S_t^r}\min\{\kappa_t,S_t^r\}. \]
The continuous mirror asks whether this type-level protocol converges to the equilibrium set, and how efficiently an equilibrium can be approximated. The expected direction is tractability through the convex program and PR dynamics, with the natural parameters \(m,\tau\), input bit length, and requested accuracy. Theorem 4 supports convergence; it does not by itself establish a polynomial-time bit-complexity bound.

This case mirrors Theorem 4’s market-equilibrium computation. It does not claim a mirror of Theorem 6’s chaos result or the best-response stability result: those concern the behavior of finite-player dynamics, and the paper provides no comparable computational-complexity anchor for them. The main weakness is that the authors’ theorem is about convergence in a finite-agent Fisher market, not a complexity classification for a continuous population. The type-level extension is plausible and precise, but its strongest claim should remain a tractable equilibrium-computation question, not a claim that the paper already proves such a result for continuum societies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about contribution to ChoCo’s complexity agenda, not whether the proposed model is sensible. Theorem 4 proves convergence of a learning protocol; Theorem 8 gives an \(O(1/t)\) objective bound. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. That makes this a weaker anchor for a programme focused on complexity landscapes than a result about bribery, control, or robustness.

The proposed type-mass model is mathematically sound, though. For rational masses, choose a common denominator \(D\), represent type \(t\) by \(D\mu_t\) identical firms, and give each budget \(\kappa_t/D\). If copies of each type start with the same portfolio, the finite PR dynamics preserve that symmetry. Aggregating their spendings gives the proposed price equation and update. So Theorem 4 already supplies convergence for this mirror, and its rate has a direct weighted analogue. The added question of approximate equilibrium computation has no obvious hidden pricing problem: the paper already gives a convex program, and the type version has only \(m\tau\) spending variables.

That direct derivation is the best argument that this mirror may be peripheral: it turns a finite-agent convergence result into a compressed type-level one, rather than opening a new complexity frontier. But the compression can still matter. The denominator \(D\) can be enormous relative to the binary encoding of the masses, while the type-level representation stays small. That is precisely the sort of high-multiplicity gain the programme is meant to notice. The likely mining or cloud-provider scenario also passes the type test if valuations and budgets are included in each type.

The continuous-market literature I found does not dispose of this mirror: the closest infinite-dimensional Fisher-market result has a finite number of buyers and a continuum of goods, the opposite axis of continuity, and it studies equilibrium computation directly. [Gao and Kroer, “Infinite-Dimensional Fisher Markets: Equilibrium, Duality and Optimization”](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/16684)

So the negative case is weak on its central claim. I can argue that this is a straightforward high-multiplicity compression of an existing convergence theorem, with limited novelty for ChoCo’s complexity programme. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: the type model is coherent, the large-population scenario is plausible, and its compressed input can make the algorithmic question meaningful.
