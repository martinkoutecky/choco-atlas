The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a small conceptual case, but not a qualifying ChoCo case: this paper contains no named computational result to anchor one on. Theorem 1, proved here, says that laissez-faire plus weak Pareto forces a social ranking to prefer any state with greater total disposable income, whatever the market incomes. Corollary 1, also proved here, derives strong Pareto. Theorem 2, proved here, says no social ranking satisfies both laissez-faire and Pareto indifference. These are axiomatic results; the paper studies no algorithmic problem, complexity class, or computational method.

The closest continuous analogue would model a large tax population by masses of income types: a type \(t\) represents indistinguishable tax units, \(\mu_t\) is its population share, \(m(t)\) its market income, and \(x(t)\) its disposable income under a policy. Replace income sums by population integrals and ask whether laissez-faire plus pointwise weak Pareto forces social preference whenever \(\int x\,d\mu > \int x'\,d\mu\). Repeated household or earning types could make this a plausible high-multiplicity regime. But the question remains axiomatic: it continuizes the theorem’s social-evaluation framework rather than posing a computational problem. Computing the aggregate for finite-support rational inputs would be easy, but that would be an invented task, not a result the paper establishes.

So there is no honest per-anchor prover question to give here. The nearest analogue is mathematically natural, but it falls outside the programme’s computational scope; that is the decisive weakness in the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest continuous version is easy to state. In a large tax population, let \(t\) describe a complete income or responsibility type, let \(\mu\) give each type’s population share, and let \(m(t)\) and \(x(t)\) be its market and disposable incomes. Theorem 1 then has a natural integral analogue: under continuous versions of laissez-faire and weak Pareto, a greater \(\int x\,d\mu\) must determine the ranking. A continuum of taxpayers with repeated types is a sensible regime, so lack of multiplicity is not a good objection.

That version still mirrors an axiomatic conclusion, not a computational result. Theorem 1 has no algorithmic input, output, feasible set, or complexity question. Corollary 1 derives another axiom from the same conditions; Theorem 2 proves an incompatibility between axioms. Their continuous counterparts would remain axiomatic questions. The paper gives no computational anchor for any of the three.

One could add a task such as optimizing a tax schedule over feasible policies. That would require specifying the policy space, constraints, and input representation, none of which this paper supplies. Any resulting computational question would be a new problem whose content came from those additions. Computing \(\int x\,d\mu\) for finite-support rational input would be ordinary aggregation, not a computational result of the paper.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s stated scope: a continuous population mirror is mathematically natural, yet it stays outside the programme’s computational remit. It could be worthwhile as an axiomatic extension in economics; the paper offers no basis for treating it as a continuous computational social choice contribution.
