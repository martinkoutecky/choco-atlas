The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a case that meets your anchor requirement: the paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational result such as hardness or tractability. Its numbered items are Hypotheses 1.a, 1.b, and 2, which are tested against experimental data. The paper’s main finding is that logit-QRE captures qualitative changes in applications and matchings across payoff treatments in three fixed, three-student problems; that is an empirical result, not a complexity result.

The cited “Theorem 2” is from McKelvey and Palfrey (1995), not this paper. It states that logit-QRE converges to a mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium as \(\lambda\) grows; it does not establish a computational result for the school-choice problem.

A large-population QRE model could be posed, with types defined by preferences, priority classes, and payoffs, and mass application probabilities as the strategy variables. But it would require replacing the paper’s strict individual priorities with priority classes and its finite game with a continuum equilibrium. That is a possible new question, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. There are no qualifying anchors here, so I would not offer it as one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the strongest objection, and I agree: there is no computational result in this paper to anchor a mirror. Hypotheses 1.a, 1.b, and 2 are predictions about behavior and matching frequencies in a fixed three-student game; the paper tests them experimentally. Its numerical QRE calculations and maximum-likelihood estimates are also for that fixed game. None states a complexity result or poses an algorithmic problem.

The best attempt to rescue a mirror would scale school choice to a large district, give students types defined by preferences, priority class, and payoffs, and study type-level logit responses as capacities scale with population. That is a coherent possible research question. But it replaces the paper’s three-player game and its strict individual priority order with an aggregate market model. The paper supplies no computational claim about that model to mirror. It may be a worthwhile new project; calling it a mirror would make “mirror” mean only that both projects concern school choice.

The alternative—many repetitions of the paper’s three-student experiment—preserves its exact QRE predictions, but leaves the game and its computation unchanged. A continuum then just records frequencies across fixed-size groups. And the paper’s stable-matching prevalence result does not transfer literally: in a single nonatomic market, aggregate assignments are mass allocations, not probabilities over the three-person matching outcomes whose prevalence Hypothesis 2 compares.

So the negative case is strong as a screening judgment, not as proof that no useful continuum model of school choice exists. A large-market QRE model could be worthwhile in its own right. The material here gives no reason to treat it as a continuous computational mirror of this paper.
