The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is limited by a missing anchor: this paper contains no named computational result. Its only numbered result is Theorem 2.1, proved here by adapting İnal’s proof. It says that when capacity is nonbinding, for every problem \( \tilde p=(A,\tilde P,n)\in PSWO \), every two stable matchings \( \mu,\mu'\in S(\tilde p) \), and every agent \(i\),
\[ \mu(i)\in\{i,c_0\}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\mu'(i)\in\{i,c_0\}. \]
That is a structural Lone Wolf theorem, not a result about an algorithm, complexity, or computational classification. So there is no qualifying anchor from which to give the requested one-question-per-anchor case.

The closest plausible mirror would be a large housing or student-pairing market with many agents from a small number of cohorts. A type could be a strict ranking over partner categories, the self-room option, and \(c_0\); \( \mu_t \) would be the fraction of agents of type \(t\). A solution would be a feasible mass matching, including mass assigned to pairs, self-rooms, or no room. One could ask whether every stable mass matching assigns the same amount of each type to self-rooms or no room. That is a recognizable population-continuous analogue, but it turns the paper’s individual-level theorem into a type-level structural question. It supplies no computational anchor, and the continuum stability definition would need to be made explicit.

That is the weak point, and it is decisive under ChoCo’s scope: the paper’s result is about a structural property of stable matchings, not the computational landscape of a problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorem 2.1 says that, if stable matchings exist, the same individuals are single or assigned to \(c_0\) in every stable matching. It gives no algorithm, complexity bound, or optimization problem. Its proof follows a chain of distinct named agents until finiteness yields a contradiction. The paper presents this as a structural extension of the Lone Wolf Theorem; it does not frame it as a computational question.

The proposed mass version—whether every stable mass matching assigns each type the same amount to self-rooms or \(c_0\)—is the strongest candidate mirror. A large student or housing market with a few preference cohorts makes that model plausible. But it asks for a new structural theorem. The paper’s agents rank named individuals, while the mass model must specify preferences over partner types and a stability notion for fractional assignments. That may be a worthwhile matching theory question; it is not a computational claim identified by this paper. Turning it into one, such as asking for the complexity of finding a stable mass matching or testing whether unmatched mass varies, would add a problem the paper does not formulate.

So the negative case is strong for ChoCo’s scope, but not universal: type-homogeneous roommate markets are a sensible continuous regime, and their computational study could be valuable in its own right. What is missing is a convincing computational mirror of this paper’s result.
