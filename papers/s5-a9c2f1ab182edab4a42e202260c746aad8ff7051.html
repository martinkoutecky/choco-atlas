The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is only a possible follow-on, not a mirror anchored in this paper’s results. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational-complexity result. Its “Result 1,” “Result 2,” and “Result 3” are experimental findings: respectively, comparisons of allocative efficiency across static treatments; a comparison of static and dynamic high-heterogeneity treatments; and a comparison of dynamic conclave with simple majority. The equilibrium work it cites is prior work, not a complexity result proved here. So there is no eligible anchor, and no anchor-based continuous problem to hand to a prover.

A plausible follow-on would take a rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over voter types \(t=(\text{preferred alternative},\text{valuation},\text{waiting cost})\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of a large body sharing that type. Each type would choose an exit-time strategy for a conclave; the question could be to compute an equilibrium and its allocative and net welfare. A legislature or jury with many members but relatively few preference-and-value types is a conceivable regime, and the paper’s focus on value heterogeneity gives this angle some connection to its subject.

The central weakness is that a continuum makes each voter non-pivotal: one person’s exit no longer determines when an alternative loses all support. That may erase the war-of-attrition incentives the paper studies, so this is not yet a convincing continuous version of its experiment. More fundamentally, equilibrium computation here would be a new computational question, not one of the paper’s named results. I would not present it as satisfying the requested anchor standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper to mirror. “Result 1,” “Result 2,” and “Result 3” report experimental welfare comparisons; they do not establish the complexity of a computational problem. The equilibrium analyses cited are prior work. So the proposed task of computing equilibrium and welfare from a distribution over voter types would be a new project, not a continuous counterpart to a result in this paper.

The strongest version of that project would model a large legislature or jury with a continuum of preference and value types, then ask how its conclave equilibrium compares with majority voting. But the paper’s mechanism turns on individual pivotality: an alternative is rejected when it loses its last supporter. In an atomless population, one voter’s exit cannot make that happen or affect the other side’s exit. That removes the war-of-attrition incentive the paper studies. Treating each type’s mass as exiting together restores influence only by making each type a collective player; adding a positive threshold or coordinated mass exits changes the mechanism and creates a different game.

A continuum of separate three-voter committees would preserve the experiment, but it would only aggregate outcomes across many finite games. With a finite support of committee types, average welfare is an expectation over those games, not a computational question about a continuous voting population. The paper’s 117 subjects and repeated committee assignments do not supply a hidden large electorate: each decision still involves three voters.

An imagined large-committee or mean-field extension might be worth studying on its own. The case against is that it would either lose the paper’s central strategic force or require a materially different model—and in neither case would it mirror one of this paper’s named computational results.
