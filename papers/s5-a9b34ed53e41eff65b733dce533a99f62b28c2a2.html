The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive route is the paper’s entropy-based diversity measure. There is one strict problem with the anchor requirement: the paper has no named computational-complexity result—none of its theorems classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its named results concern structural bounds on domains; some finite cases were checked computationally, but those checks are not complexity theorems.

The closest structural anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper using hypergraph Ramsey theory and Raynaud’s result. It says that for every \(k\), there are thresholds \(R_s(k)\le R_u(k)\) such that: if \(n\ge R_s(k)\), no domain on \(n\) alternatives is \((k,s)\)-abundant for \(s>2^{k-1}\); and if \(n\ge R_u(k)\), every domain has a \(k\)-alternative subset on which every triple satisfies the same never condition. The first clause gives the useful ceiling: for any such large domain, some \(k\)-subset has at most \(2^{k-1}\) distinct restricted orders.

A continuous population version, closely tied to the paper’s entropy discussion in Section 6, is Max-Min Condorcet Entropy Design. Given \(n\), \(k\le n\), a support cap \(\tau\), and an accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\), choose a set \(D\subseteq S_n\) of at most \(\tau\) rankings and rational masses \(\mu_\pi>0\) summing to one, with \(D\) a Condorcet domain, to maximize
\[ \min_{A\in\binom{[n]}{k}} H(\mu_A), \qquad \mu_A(\rho)=\sum_{\pi\in D:\,\pi|_A=\rho}\mu_\pi, \qquad H(\mu_A)=-\sum_\rho \mu_A(\rho)\ln\mu_A(\rho). \]
A solution is such a support and mass vector with value within \(\varepsilon\) of the optimum. Here a type is a complete ranking, and its mass is its fraction of the population. The regime is a large public-consultation panel or civic electorate with \(N\gg\tau\): many people share a relatively small catalog of rankings, and the institution wants a Condorcet-safe domain while avoiding a locally homogeneous population on any \(k\) options. The decision is which types to include and what population shares to assign.

The connection to Theorem 4 is concrete: its support bound implies an entropy ceiling of \((k-1)\ln 2\) for sufficiently large \(n\). The paper also notes that group-separable domains divide every \(k\)-subset into \(2^{k-1}\) equally sized parts, so a uniform mass distribution on such a domain reaches that ceiling when the support cap permits it. This is a plausible extension of the authors’ own profile-entropy measure, though the domain-and-mass optimization is a new problem, not a result they prove.

I’d expect the mass-allocation subproblem for a fixed \(D\) to be tractable by convex optimization. Choosing \(D\) as well introduces combinatorial structure over alternatives and rankings; I would provisionally expect that layer to be the harder one, but the paper gives no basis for a complexity classification. Natural follow-up questions are the complexity of the support-selection problem, the smallest \(\tau\) that permits the entropy ceiling, and how to round an optimal mass profile to a finite electorate.

The weakest point is decisive under the prompt’s strict rule: Theorem 4 is not a computational-complexity anchor. This is the best plausible continuous-population extension I see, because it lifts a profile-level entropy measure the paper actually defines. But I cannot honestly present it as mirroring a named computational result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor fails at the point the proponent already identifies: the paper has no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 4 is a structural bound on the restrictions of a Condorcet domain. Its proof uses Ramsey theory and a result about domain size; it defines no computational problem, input, algorithm, or running time. The finite searches reported elsewhere in the paper do not supply a complexity theorem either.

The strongest repair is the proposed max-min entropy design problem. It is a coherent way to put population masses over rankings, and Theorem 4 does imply an entropy ceiling of \((k-1)\ln 2\). But that connection lifts a support-size bound into a new optimization problem. The theorem concerns which rankings a domain contains; its abundance measure ignores how many people hold each ranking. Section 6 does define entropy using finite profile frequencies, so extending those frequencies to a distribution \(\mu\) is natural. Choosing both the domain and its masses, however, asks a new question about designing a population. A panel-design or stress-testing application could make that worthwhile, but it is not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The better versions have the same gap. With a fixed domain, one could choose masses to maximize the least entropy across \(k\)-alternative subsets. Or one could minimize the mass that must be shifted to improve that statistic. Those are plausible population-level optimization problems. Their objectives come from the paper’s diversity measures, but the paper does not pose those optimization tasks or establish discrete algorithms for them. Theorem 4’s entropy ceiling is useful context for such work; it does not provide the computational anchor the mirror was supposed to extend.

So the strongest negative case is narrow but firm: no proposed anchor survives the paper’s computational-result requirement. I would not claim this proves that no worthwhile continuous population project can be built from the paper. Its profile-entropy discussion gives a credible starting point for one. That possibility is a real weakness in the universal claim, while still leaving the proposed “mirror” unsupported as a continuization of a named computational result.
