The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make a compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result of the kind the brief requires as an anchor. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest results do not qualify. The unnumbered “Theorem (LIST AND PETTIT [2002])” states that no procedure producing complete and consistent collective judgments can satisfy universal domain, anonymity, and systematicity simultaneously; the paper attributes it to earlier work. The later, also unnumbered theorem says that for any group size divisible by \(k\), a sufficient increase in specialists’ reliability can make majority voting within specialist subgroups more reliable than voting across the full group. That is an analytic reliability claim, not a computational result.

A plausible population regime would be a large expert organization with many members sharing a small number of complete expertise and judgment profiles. One could study a distribution over those types and choose how to assign members to premises, aiming for rational collective judgments and truth tracking. But the paper does not formulate or analyze that computational problem. Making it one would create a new question, rather than mirror a named result of this paper. Its central results concern axiomatic aggregation and epistemic performance, so a population continuum could be relevant to a new study, but it does not supply the required anchor for this one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a continuous mirror is strongest at the level ChoCo is assessing: the paper offers no named computational problem whose complexity could be charted. Its two closest results do not supply one. The List–Pettit theorem characterizes which axioms an aggregation procedure can satisfy; the later theorem compares the reliability of two procedures under assumptions about expertise and group size. Neither asks for an exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithm. There is therefore no computational result here for a continuous population model to mirror.

A large expert organization could plausibly have many members of the same complete type: the same expertise, premise-specific reliability, evidence, and other features relevant to the model. Giving those types rational masses would be a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But applying the paper’s existing results to that society would lead toward mass-weighted aggregation axioms or limiting reliability calculations. Those are axiomatic or analytic questions, outside the programme’s computational scope.

The strongest alternative would add an algorithmic design problem: given masses of experts with different competence profiles, allocate them to premises or choose an aggregation procedure to maximize truth-tracking subject to constraints. The paper’s discussion of specialization makes that a plausible research direction. It would, however, add a search problem, an objective, and potentially allocation constraints that the paper does not formulate. That could be worthwhile work in computational judgment aggregation; it is not a continuous mirror of either result raised by the other analyst.

So no anchor survives: one result is an impossibility characterization, the other an analytic reliability comparison. The negative case is strong for this paper under ChoCo’s stated anchor requirement. The broader claim that no scenario could ever yield a worthwhile computational problem is harder to defend: adding design choices around specialist assignment might produce one.
