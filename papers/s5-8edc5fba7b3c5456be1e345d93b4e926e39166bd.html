What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only the pure-strategy equilibrium construction and absorbing-state claims of Proposition 4; it leaves the other equilibrium propositions, mixed equilibria, equilibrium selection, and Proposition 8's public-goods welfare comparison untouched.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is conditional: a meaningful population mirror exists, but this paper fails ChoCo’s strict computational-anchor gate. None of Propositions 1–8 asserts polynomial-time solvability, NP-hardness, parameterized complexity, approximation, or even an explicit algorithmic complexity bound. They are equilibrium-characterization and welfare results. Thus no fully compliant positive case can be claimed. The following is the strongest rescue.

The best candidate is Proposition 4, proved in this paper. It considers odd \(n\ge5\), \(n=2m+1\), an integer benefit supply \(\pi\), policy space \(X=\Delta_\pi^n\), and sufficiently large reconsideration probability \(\delta\). It constructs a pure-strategy stationary equilibrium. Its absorbing states are \(z^1=(\pi,0,\ldots,0)\) and the policies \(Y(e')\) having exactly \(m+1\) voters with \(e'\) benefits and exactly \(m-1\) voters with zero benefits. The policy rule fixes every absorbing state and maps every other default into \(Y\), so at most one proposal occurs on the equilibrium path. For every default \(d\), the equilibrium proposal lies in \(Y(e(d))\), gives the setter \(f_1(d)=\pi-(m+1)e(d)\), and gives at least \(m\) of its \(m+1\) paid voters types from \(L(d,e(d))=\{i\ne1:d_i\le e(d)\}\). The value functions are \(U_i(d)=d_i\) at absorbing states; otherwise the setter receives \((1-\delta)d_1+\delta[\pi-(m+1)e(d)]\), a paid voter receives \((1-\delta)d_i+\delta e(d)\), and an unpaid voter receives \((1-\delta)d_i\). The admissible payment level satisfies

\[ \underline e(d)=\min\{e'\in\mathbb Z_+:\lvert L(d,e')\rvert\ge m\} \]

and

\[ \overline e(d)=\max\{e'\in\mathbb Z_+:e'\le(\pi-d_1)/(m+1)\}. \]

The strongest continuous mirror would be the following.

Call it Mass-Protected Reconsideration Equilibrium. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), a distinguished agenda-setting office, rational initial default entitlements \(d_t\), a per-capita-scaled benefit supply \(B\), and reconsideration probability \(\delta\). A type includes its entitlement, utility parameters, and any institutional characteristics, so identical types really are interchangeable. The regime is a large legislature or party congress with \(N\gg\tau=|T|\) representatives: many districts repeatedly occupy a small number of party, regional, or entitlement classes, while one cabinet or prime-ministerial office has persistent proposal power.

A policy is a measurable allocation of benefits to voter mass together with the setter’s allocation. In the Proposition-4-type subclass, a proposal chooses a payment level \(e\) and masses \(s_t\le\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t s_t=\sigma\), where \(\sigma>1/2\) is the protected coalition mass. Mass \(s_t\) of type \(t\) receives \(e\), the remaining voter mass receives zero, and the setter receives \(B-\sigma e\). The value \(\sigma=(m+1)/(2m)\) is the exact finite analogue of Proposition 4’s \(m+1\) paid voters out of \(2m\); the limit has \(\sigma\downarrow1/2\). A proposal passes when the mass of voters whose continuation value weakly improves is at least \(1/2\), because the agenda setter supplies the remaining vote.

The computational problem is: given the typed distribution and default, compute an absorbing stationary equilibrium maximizing the agenda setter’s continuation value, or decide whether one exists with value at least \(\theta\). A solution consists of the proposal \(e,s\), the resulting absorbing allocation, and a finite certificate for the stationary rule and value functions satisfying

\[ U_t(d)=(1-\delta)u_t(d)+\delta U_t(f(d)), \]

with \(f(d)\) maximizing the setter’s value subject to the mass-majority constraint. In the Proposition-4-type restriction, the candidate \(e\) is governed by the weighted quantile

\[ \underline e(d)=\inf\left\{z:\sum_{t:d_t\le z}\mu_t\ge \frac12\right\}, \]

together with the setter-improvement bound \(e\le(B-d_a)/\sigma\). The finite certificate is therefore a weighted-threshold or small LP certificate over the type masses, not an encoding of the underlying bargaining problem.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it preserves the agenda setter, evolving default, majority approval, stationary continuation values, reconsideration, and the mutual protection mechanism. Only the population representation changes from named repeated legislators to rational masses of indistinguishable legislative types; scaling the benefit supply per capita is the natural co-modification required by a large legislature.

For the restricted Proposition-4-type problem I would expect Class A: the central choice is a weighted order statistic, and the remaining allocation can be expressed by a finite LP. The full equilibrium-selection problem may instead be continuum-specifically difficult, because the paper has multiple self-fulfilling equilibria and the stationary rule is a fixed point over default states. Further questions are whether finite equilibria converge to the mass model, whether the \(m+1\)-versus-\(m\) protection effect survives as \(\sigma\downarrow1/2\), and whether mixed equilibria from Propositions 6 and 7 admit a tractable mass formulation.

This mirror covers Proposition 4’s pure-equilibrium class only. It does not claim to continuize the mixed equilibria or Proposition 8’s public-goods welfare comparison.

The weakest point is decisive: Proposition 4 is not a computational result under the programme’s definition. Moreover, the strict protective buffer shrinks to zero in the literal population limit, and the mass model requires choices about splitting a type across paid and unpaid groups and selecting among multiple equilibria. So this is a defensible continuous research problem, but not a qualifying ChoCo case anchored in a computational theorem from the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is decisive at ChoCo’s admission gate: this paper has no computational anchor. Propositions 1–8 establish equilibrium existence, equilibrium characterizations, equilibrium selection, and welfare comparisons. None states a complexity bound, an algorithmic theorem, hardness result, approximation guarantee, or parameterized classification. Proposition 4’s construction may be described procedurally, but the paper never poses “compute such an equilibrium” as a computational problem, specifies an input encoding, or analyzes its running time. The proposed decision problem—compute an equilibrium maximizing the setter’s value—is therefore a new problem, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result.

The proposed Proposition-4 mirror also loses the paper’s central mechanism in the continuum. Among the \(2m\) non-agenda-setters, an absorbing policy in \(Y(e)\) pays \(m+1\) voters \(e\) and gives \(m-1\) voters zero. Since the setter supplies one vote, passage requires \(m\) voter votes. Thus the zero-paid voters are deliberately one voter short of being able to support the setter’s ideal policy. This one-voter gap is what makes the protected allocation absorbing.

Under the proposed mass rule, however, the paid fraction tends to

\[ \frac{m+1}{2m}\longrightarrow \frac12, \]

and the zero-paid fraction also tends to \(\frac12\). At the purported absorbing state, the zero-paid half is indifferent between the default and the setter’s ideal policy \(z^1\). Under the paper’s own weak-improvement voting convention, that entire mass supports \(z^1\). It therefore supplies exactly the \(\frac12\) voter mass needed for passage. The setter can then take everything, so the protected state is no longer absorbing. The finite “\(m+1\) rather than \(m\)” protection effect has vanished rather than become a continuous quantity.

One can repair this by imposing a fixed supermajority \(\rho>\frac12\), or by stipulating a positive protected buffer \(\sigma-\frac12\). But that inserts a new constitutional primitive. The original buffer is \(1/(2m)\), which disappears in the high-multiplicity limit; a fixed buffer is not its continuum limit. Keeping the buffer indexed by \(m\) instead leaves the population-size parameter in the model and does not produce the proposed continuous society. Either repair may define a worthwhile new bargaining model, but neither is a faithful mirror of Proposition 4.

The claimed weighted-quantile computation is also only a one-step calculation. Once mass \(s_t<\mu_t\) of a type is paid and the remainder is not, that type splits into agents with different current entitlements. The state is no longer described by one entitlement \(d_t\) per type: it must track a distribution of entitlements, or continually refine the type space. If splitting is forbidden, the model cannot represent the proposed mass allocation. If splitting is allowed, the asserted finite LP certificate does not describe the stationary fixed point over all default states and off-path deviations. Multiple self-fulfilling equilibria make equilibrium selection part of the problem, not a minor implementation detail.

A party-congress interpretation can make a mass bargaining model sensible, but it cannot cure the computational-anchor failure, and preserving a single named agenda setter while scaling the legislature requires further institutional choices about whose utility and benefits scale with population. Proposition 8 does not rescue the paper either: it is a welfare comparison, and its continuous calculation concerns the outcome space, not a continuous population.

So the honest verdict is red for ChoCo: no qualifying continuous mirror of this paper’s computational results exists, because the paper contains no such result to mirror and the strongest candidate’s defining finite-coalition mechanism degenerates at the continuum limit. A new mean-field legislative-bargaining paper could still be worthwhile, but it would be a new computational programme rather than a continuization of this one.
