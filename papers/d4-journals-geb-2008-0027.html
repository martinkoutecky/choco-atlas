What the mirror covers

The attempted extension concerns Proposition 3's minimum-salary equilibrium path; it leaves the other equilibrium results and examples without a continuous model.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is limited: this paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror on. It gives no result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered claims concern assignment and equilibrium properties, so I would not present any of them as complexity anchors. That leaves zero qualifying anchors and, under your criteria, no anchor-by-anchor prover question to offer.

The closest bridge is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It says that in the multistage game there is a stationary Markov perfect equilibrium path where, at each stage \(c=1,\ldots,\min\{n,m\}\), firm \(f_c\) offers applicant \(a_c\) the minimum competitive salary \(s_c^*\), and she accepts immediately. The resulting firm payoffs are \(u_i^*=Y_{ii}-s_i^*\) for \(i\le\min\{n,m\}\), and zero for firms left unmatched when \(n>m\). That is an equilibrium-existence result, not a claim about computing an equilibrium or the complexity of the underlying problem.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting behind it: large residency or graduate-hiring markets with many applicants and positions grouped into a modest number of complete types, such as specialty and productivity profiles. The paper itself discusses Shimer’s continuum-of-clones model, which supports the idea that such a regime is sensible; it also explains why Shimer’s game differs from theirs. A possible extension would put masses on firm and applicant types and ask whether an open-offer game has a stationary Markov equilibrium whose aggregate matching is assortative and whose type-level salaries equal the minimum competitive schedule. But that would be a proposed equilibrium-theory question, not a computational mirror anchored by a result in this paper.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s offers and rejections are actions by named, finite players, and its results rely on strict ordering and sequential rivalry. Moving to type masses requires specifying how aggregate offers reach applicants and how competition works among indistinguishable agents. That may change the game enough that the authors would not recognize the extension as their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no result of the kind ChoCo is set up to mirror. Its propositions characterize equilibrium outcomes and salary bounds; none asks for, or establishes the complexity of, a computational problem. That is the central gap here. It does not show that continuous matching is uninteresting. It means a complexity project would have to introduce a new computational question rather than continuize one the paper actually studies.

Proposition 3 is the closest candidate, but it says that a particular path is supportable as a stationary Markov perfect equilibrium. The strongest plausible mass version would put distributions over firm and applicant types, retain an output matrix, and ask whether an open-offer game has an equilibrium path realizing an assortative mass matching at minimum competitive salaries. But the paper’s strict supermodularity assumptions already yield an explicit minimum-salary formula. If the mass version is reduced to aggregate assignment flows and salary schedules, it becomes a competitive assignment problem; that does not capture the strategic offers, rejections, and state changes that make Proposition 3 a result about this paper’s game. If those strategic features are retained, the model needs rules for how individual offers reach indistinguishable agents and how pending offers and rejections evolve across type masses. That is substantial new game design, not a direct continuous mirror.

The proposed large hiring or residency market is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, and the paper’s discussion of Shimer’s continuum-of-clones model supports that regime. I would not treat either fact as an objection. But Shimer’s model changes how firms post wages and how applicants apply; it does not supply an aggregate version of this paper’s one-offer-per-firm game. The paper itself emphasizes that the two strategic setups differ. Retaining the bilateral protocol in a continuum therefore needs additional modeling choices before there is a computational problem to classify.

The same issue would remain if the anchor were shifted to Proposition 1 or Proposition 4: they characterize mixed-strategy payoffs or bound equilibrium salaries, but do not pose computational tasks. My case against spending ChoCo’s time on this paper is therefore strong on fit, not on impossibility. A continuous strategic matching project could be worthwhile in its own right; the paper does not give us a named computational result to mirror, and its closest result leads either to an aggregate assignment model that leaves out the game or to a newly specified continuum game.
