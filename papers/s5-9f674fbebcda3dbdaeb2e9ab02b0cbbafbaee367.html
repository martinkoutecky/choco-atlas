The continuous mirror question

Given \(K\) types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), initial colours \(c_t\in\{r,b,u\}\), a rational contact kernel \(q_{tu}\ge 0\) with each row sum at most \(1\), a finite integer horizon \(H\), and a seeding budget \(\beta\in[0,1]\), choose seeding fractions \(z_t\in[0,1]\) to maximize blue mass after \(H\) rounds. Require \(z_t=0\) for initially blue types and \(\sum_t\mu_t z_t\le\beta\). Initially set \(x_{t,b}(0)=1\) for blue types; for red types set \((x_{t,b},x_{t,r},x_{t,u})(0)=(z_t,1-z_t,0)\); and for uncoloured types set it to \((z_t,0,1-z_t)\). For \(s=0,\ldots,H-1\), let \(p_{t,c}(s)=\sum_u q_{tu}x_{u,c}(s)\) and update \(x_{t,b}(s+1)=x_{t,b}(s)(1-p_{t,r}(s))+(1-x_{t,b}(s))p_{t,b}(s)\), \(x_{t,r}(s+1)=x_{t,r}(s)(1-p_{t,b}(s))+(1-x_{t,r}(s))p_{t,r}(s)\), and \(x_{t,u}(s+1)=x_{t,u}(s)(1-p_{t,b}(s)-p_{t,r}(s))\). What are the exact and approximation complexities, in \(K\), input bit length, and \(H\), of maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{t,b}(H)\)?

The model it lives in

A finite typed continuum of users with masses μ, a rational type-level contact kernel \(q\), voter-model colour densities \(x\), and seeding decisions given by fractions \(z_t\); maximize blue mass at a finite horizon under a total mass budget.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is finite-horizon seed selection in a large platform whose users fall into behavioral and interaction cohorts. The paper’s anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper by reduction from Maximum Coverage. It states that, for every constant \(\epsilon>0\), no polynomial-time \((1-1/e+\epsilon)\)-approximation for Adoption Maximisation exists unless \(\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{DTIME}(n^{O(\log\log n)})\). That is an inapproximability result, not a claim that the paper proves exact optimization NP-hard.

A concrete continuous counterpart is Cohort Adoption Maximisation. An instance has \(K\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), summing to \(1\); an initial colour \(c_t\in\{r,b,u\}\) for each type; a rational contact kernel \(q_{tu}\); a finite horizon \(H\); and a seeding budget \(\beta\), measured as a fraction of society. Members of type \(t\) share their initial state and the same probabilities \(q_{tu}\) of contacting members of each type \(u\). Each row sums to at most \(1\); any remaining probability means no contact, so the member keeps their colour. A row of zero represents a stubborn type.

The decision is an allocation \(z_t\in[0,1]\): the fraction of type \(t\) directly seeded blue, with \(z_t=0\) for initially blue types and \(\sum_t\mu_tz_t\le\beta\). Initially, a red type has blue fraction \(z_t\) and red fraction \(1-z_t\); an uncoloured type has blue fraction \(z_t\) and uncoloured fraction \(1-z_t\). Let \(x_{t,c}(s)\) be the fraction of type \(t\) in colour \(c\) after round \(s\), and \(p_{t,c}(s)=\sum_u q_{tu}x_{u,c}(s)\). The type fractions evolve by the voter update rule: \(x_{t,b}(s+1)=x_{t,b}(s)(1-p_{t,r}(s))+(1-x_{t,b}(s))p_{t,b}(s)\); \(x_{t,r}(s+1)=x_{t,r}(s)(1-p_{t,b}(s))+(1-x_{t,r}(s))p_{t,r}(s)\); and \(x_{t,u}(s+1)=x_{t,u}(s)(1-p_{t,b}(s)-p_{t,r}(s))\). The objective is to choose \(z\) maximizing the blue mass after \(H\) rounds, \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{t,b}(H)\). An instance is encoded by the rational masses, kernel, budget, initial colours, and horizon; a solution is a feasible allocation attaining the optimum. Its approximation variants ask for an allocation whose objective is at least a specified fraction of that optimum.

A plausible regime is a large platform where millions of users have the same initial opinion, inertia, and feed probabilities across a modest number of cohorts. A campaign can reach a percentage of each cohort, rather than selecting named users one by one. The cohort definition includes every trait the model uses, and the number of users can be much larger than the number of types. The paper’s directed weighted contacts, stubbornness, initially uncoloured users, seed intervention, finite horizon, and expected-adoption objective all remain recognizable. Mean-field analysis could help describe the limit, but the question being asked is the computational one: how to allocate campaign mass.

My forecast is Class A for a \(1-1/e\) approximation, with exact optimization left open. The supporting bridge is the paper’s Theorem 4.6, proved here: for a fixed horizon, the finite-seed objective is monotone and submodular. Together with the paper’s polynomial-time objective calculation and greedy algorithm, that motivates asking whether the mass-allocation objective has a continuous diminishing-returns property and admits a continuous-greedy approximation. This is a forecast, not an implication of Theorem 4.1 or a result already proved for the mirror. Further questions are whether the continuous guarantee can beat \(1-1/e\), whether exact optimization is tractable, and whether finite cohort graphs converge to this model with a controlled error.

The weak point is the change in network regime and action granularity. The paper’s theorem selects whole nodes in a graph; this mirror uses exchangeable cohorts with a type-level contact kernel and allows fractional seeding within each cohort. Theorem 4.1 therefore does not establish hardness for the continuous problem. That limitation does not erase the mirror: the proposed problem still optimizes the same intervention against the same propagation rule and objective, in a plausible high-multiplicity regime. This case covers finite-horizon Adoption Maximisation; it does not mirror the paper’s separate convergence-time and convergence-period results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a fidelity objection to the proposed mirror. Theorem 4.1 optimizes over nodes in a fixed directed graph, where a seed’s value depends on which other nodes point to it. The cohort model replaces that incidence structure with a type-level contact kernel. For an arbitrary network, type masses and average contact probabilities need not determine the finite-horizon adoption process; preserving all relevant network roles can leave almost every node as its own type. Theorem 4.6’s submodularity result for sets of named nodes also does not, by itself, prove diminishing returns for the proposed mass-allocation objective. So the paper does not establish the proponent’s continuous-greedy forecast.

That objection does not defeat the better version of the mirror. A platform where large cohorts share an interaction law is a plausible regime, and allocating a fraction of each cohort to a campaign asks a computational question about population mass. A finite-step graphon or an explicit rational contact kernel could preserve meaningful interaction structure while making the high-multiplicity assumption precise. The model would differ from the paper’s fixed graph, but it still optimizes seeding against voter-model propagation. The prompt’s gate is whether the authors would recognize that as their problem; the material here gives no basis to rule that out.

Nor do the other grounds settle it. The paper has a named computational result, seed selection does not track who receives an outcome, and finite-horizon adoption mass does not vanish in the limit. The related mean-field literature described in the paper is analytic, so it is not evidence that this computational question has already been answered.

The negative case can therefore show that the proposed model is not automatically an exact limit of the paper’s network instances, and that the cited results do not yet certify its algorithmic claims. It cannot honestly show that no worthwhile mirror exists. A cohort or graphon version remains a credible candidate, so the universal claim is weak on the central anchor.
