The continuous mirror question

Given good-category values \(w_j\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and a positive rational threshold \(\theta\), decide whether there is a finite-support mass distribution \(x_{\mathbf b}\) over integral bundles \(\mathbf b\in\mathbb{N}^p\) such that \(\sum_{\mathbf b}x_{\mathbf b}=1\), \(\sum_{\mathbf b}x_{\mathbf b}b_j=q_j\) for every category \(j\), the allocation is EF1 across all positive-mass bundles, and \(\exp(\sum_{\mathbf b:x_{\mathbf b}>0}x_{\mathbf b}\ln u(\mathbf b))\geq\theta\), where \(u(\mathbf b)=\sum_jw_jb_j\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of agents with one identical-valuation type; each agent receives an integral bundle, while mass \(x_{\mathbf b}\) is assigned to each bundle configuration and category supplies scale with population. The objective is continuum NSW, the geometric mean of bundle utilities under that mass distribution.

What the mirror covers

Covers the offline final-allocation EF1-Identical-NSW threshold problem; leaves aside the approximation guarantees, online results, and PriorityNet experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of the bundle-pricing problem for this configuration optimization, and does the continuum threshold problem admit a polynomial-time algorithm or a continuum-specific hardness proof?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is the paper’s Theorem 3.1 (Strong NP-completeness). It says that EF1-Identical-NSW is strongly NP-complete: given \(n\) agents, goods with nonnegative integer values \(w_g\), a common additive valuation \(v(S)=\sum_{g\in S}w_g\), and an integer threshold \(T\), decide whether the goods can be partitioned into an EF1 allocation \(A\) with \(\operatorname{NSW}(A)\geq T\). The paper proves this result, while making clear that the hardness is inherited from earlier identical-additive NSW hardness; its appendix also gives a direct reduction from 3-Partition. [Theorem 3.1 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03846v4)

My lead mirror is *EF1-Continuum-Identical-NSW*. Imagine a large relief or public-benefit program serving a cohort of recipients with the same valuation of each standard good. There is one agent type, so \(\tau=1\), while the number \(N\) of recipients is very large. For each good category \(j\), the input gives its value \(w_j\) and its supply per recipient \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\). The inventory scales with the population: for a suitable \(N\), there are \(Nq_j\) units of category \(j\). A recipient still gets an integral bundle; only the population is continuous.

Formally, a bundle is \(\mathbf b\in\mathbb{N}^{p}\), with utility \(u(\mathbf b)=\sum_j w_jb_j\). Let \(x_{\mathbf b}\) be the mass of recipients assigned that bundle. Feasibility requires \(\sum_{\mathbf b}x_{\mathbf b}=1\) and \(\sum_{\mathbf b}x_{\mathbf b}b_j=q_j\) for every good category \(j\). The allocation is EF1 when, for every two bundles \(\mathbf b,\mathbf c\) in its positive-mass support, \(u(\mathbf b)\geq u(\mathbf c)-\max_{j:c_j>0}w_j\). Its continuum NSW is \(\exp(\sum_{\mathbf b:x_{\mathbf b}>0}x_{\mathbf b}\ln u(\mathbf b))\), with value zero if positive mass receives zero utility.

The question is: given the values \(w_j\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and a positive rational threshold \(\theta\), does there exist such a mass allocation satisfying EF1 and having continuum NSW at least \(\theta\)? A solution is a finite-support mass vector \(x\). This is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s allocation problem: agents who share a valuation are indistinguishable, and multiplying all population and inventory counts by a common factor gives the corresponding finite market. The mirror continues the population, not the bundles or the goods’ outcomes.

There is a concrete reason to expect the discrete hardness to change. In the paper’s 3-Partition construction, there are \(q\) agents, \(3q\) goods of values \(a_j\), and threshold \(B\), where \(\sum_j a_j=qB\) and \(B/4<a_j<B/2\). The mirrored instance has per-capita supply \(1/q\) of each good category and threshold \(B\). The average utility per unit mass is \(B\), so Jensen’s inequality says that reaching NSW \(B\) requires almost every recipient to have utility exactly \(B\). Each such integral bundle contains exactly three goods. But the mass allocation can mix those triples fractionally: checking whether their masses use each category to the required extent is a polynomial-size linear program over the triples that sum to \(B\). The discrete reduction requires an integral partition; its continuous version asks for a fractional configuration cover. Thus this particular 3-Partition proof does not transfer its hardness.

My provisional expectation for the full mirror is Class A, not because Theorem 3.1 settles it, but because there is a promising optimization route. With identical valuations, an optimum over mass assignments should itself be EF1: if positive mass has bundles \(\mathbf b,\mathbf c\) with \(u(\mathbf b)>u(\mathbf c)+w_j\) for a good \(j\) in \(\mathbf b\), shifting a small equal mass from \(\mathbf b\) to \(\mathbf b-\mathbf e_j\) and from \(\mathbf c\) to \(\mathbf c+\mathbf e_j\) preserves supplies and strictly improves the sum of log utilities. The unconstrained objective is linear in the masses, \(\sum_{\mathbf b}x_{\mathbf b}\ln u(\mathbf b)\), so the candidate is a configuration LP. The algorithmic question is whether its pricing problem over integral bundles can be solved in time polynomial in \(p\) and the input bit length \(L\).

That is also the weakest point. Bundle configurations remain integral, and pricing over them could retain hard combinatorics tied to the good categories; the configuration LP alone is not an algorithm. I would hand a prover the pricing problem and ask whether the full threshold problem admits a polynomial-time solution or has a new hardness proof. A pricing hardness result could reveal a continuum-specific boundary, but Theorem 3.1’s discrete hardness by itself would not establish it. The mirror covers the paper’s offline, final-allocation EF1-Identical-NSW result; it does not claim a continuous result for the online or learning components.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 3.1, and it is a genuine named complexity result. The strongest case against the mirror is that its hardness lives in the goods, not in population multiplicity. The 3-Partition reduction uses \(q\) interchangeable agents but \(3q\) individually weighted goods, each appearing once. In the proposed continuum instance, that becomes \(3q\) categories, each with per-capita supply \(1/q\). The category count grows with the population, so the reduction does not establish hardness in a fixed-support population limit. With a fixed catalog of recurring goods instead, mass can be split across integral bundle configurations; as the proponent observes, the reduction’s exact-cover condition becomes a fractional cover and its hardness may disappear.

That is the best negative point, but it does not defeat the mirror. A large cohort receiving bundles from a recurring stock of standard goods is plausible, and scaling per-capita supplies is expressly within scope. Each recipient still gets an integral bundle, while the population is the continuous object. The resulting configuration LP and its pricing problem are computational questions the programme cares about. Even if the 3-Partition hardness vanishes, discovering what replaces it is a worthwhile result.

So the honest negative case is weak: the theorem gives little reason to expect the paper’s particular hardness to transfer, but it does not show that no useful mirror exists. I cannot defend the universal claim that no scenario is worth studying.
