The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many claim types \(t\in T\), rational claims \(c_t>0\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and rational per-capita estate \(e\in(0,\sum_t\mu_tc_t)\), choose a common denominator \(D\) for the masses. For each positive integer \(k\), form a bankruptcy problem with \(kD\mu_t\) creditors of type \(t\), each claiming \(c_t\), and estate \(kDe\). Compute the exact limit \(a_t=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{kD\mu_t}\sum_{i\in N_t^{(k)}}x_i^{(k)}\), where \(x^{(k)}\) is the finite game's nucleolus.

A distribution \(\mu\) over claim types \(t\), with claim \(c_t\) and per-capita estate \(e\); output the exact per-type award \(a_t\) as the replication limit of finite nucleoli, computed by the weighted Talmud thresholds.

The case FOR (proponent)

On the anchor rule you gave, this paper has no qualifying computational result. Theorem 12, proved here but attributed to Aumann and Maschler [2], says that the CG-consistent (Talmud) solution of every bankruptcy problem is the nucleolus of its bankruptcy game. It is a substantive result, but it does not classify a problem as tractable or hard. The other numbered results are lemmas, observations, and a corollary about the same game-theoretic structure. The hydraulics are a proof method, not a computational analysis.

The strongest positive lead is a population version of Theorem 12, though it is a near miss, not a compliant anchor. Take finitely many claim types \(t\in T\), with claim \(c_t>0\) and rational population share \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(e\) be the per-capita estate, with \(0<e<\sum_t\mu_t c_t\). A creditor’s complete type is their claim; mass \(\mu_t\) means that fraction of creditors has claim \(c_t\).

One precise question is: choose a common denominator \(D\) for the \(\mu_t\). For each positive integer \(k\), form a finite bankruptcy game with \(kD\mu_t\) creditors of type \(t\), each claiming \(c_t\), and estate \(kDe\). Let \(x^{(k)}\) be that game’s nucleolus. Compute the limiting per-creditor award \(a_t\) for each type:
\[ a_t=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{kD\mu_t} \sum_{i\in N_t^{(k)}}x_i^{(k)}. \]
The output must be feasible, so \(0\le a_t\le c_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t=e\). This keeps the bankruptcy game and its nucleolus intact while making the population the object that grows.

The natural answer is the type-level Talmud rule. If \(e\le \tfrac12\sum_t\mu_tc_t\), then \(a_t=\min(c_t/2,\lambda)\), with \(\lambda\) chosen so \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t=e\). Otherwise \(a_t=\max(c_t/2,c_t-\eta)\), with \(\eta\) chosen to meet the same total-award constraint. The candidate is tractable by these threshold formulas. Its appeal is that the scenario has many creditors but relatively few claim types, and scaling the estate with population preserves the per-capita problem. Theorem 12 supplies the finite-game identity; the limiting formula is a consequence of that identity and scaling, not a complexity result stated in this paper.

I would restrict this mirror to Theorem 12. The paper’s other hydraulic observations do not provide additional computational anchors. The main weakness is decisive for the requested case: the proposed mirror is a plausible continuous extension of the paper’s result, but the paper supplies no named hardness or tractability result to mirror. Also, an intrinsic nucleolus for a continuum of creditors would need a further definition; the replication-limit question avoids claiming one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s sole plausible anchor is Theorem 12, but it identifies two solution concepts; it does not state a complexity result for a computational problem. The paper gives no hardness classification, algorithmic running time, or separation problem to continuize. That is the strongest objection here: the proposed mirror may be a sensible continuous extension of the theorem, but it is not a mirror of a computational result of this paper.

The proponent’s creditor types are a credible high-multiplicity scenario. If creditors with the same claim are indistinguishable, their masses and a per-capita estate make economic sense. That establishes a plausible population model, but it does not supply the missing computational target. Asking for the limiting awards in a replicated game is a limit question about the theorem’s allocation rule. Turning it into “given rational type masses, compute the awards” would add a computational task that the paper does not formulate.

A more direct continuum version runs into a different problem. The nucleolus here orders the profits of all coalitions lexicographically. A population distribution over claim types specifies creditor mass, but not a canonical way to order or aggregate the continuum of coalition profits. One could define a new continuum solution concept, but the paper does not point to a uniquely motivated computational question that such a definition would answer. The replication-limit construction avoids that issue, at the cost of remaining a statement about replicated finite games.

So the negative case is strong as a judgment about this paper’s fit with a programme on computational complexity: its result is a structural identification, not a computational result awaiting continuization. It is weaker as a universal claim. The creditor population is natural, and a worthwhile new continuous bankruptcy question could be built from it; the paper simply does not provide a computational anchor for that question.
