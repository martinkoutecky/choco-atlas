The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t supply a qualifying numbered anchor: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. The closest relevant claim is an unnumbered statement in §2.1, cited to earlier work rather than proved here: finding a stable matching that minimizes sex-equality cost, or balance cost, is strongly NP-hard. The paper’s own WeaveNet results are experimental, so they do not meet the requested anchor rule. I’ll use the sex-equality claim as the nearest basis for a positive case, but it is not a compliant numbered anchor.

The strongest mirror I see is Continuous Sex-Equality Stable Matching. Consider a large hiring round with many applicants and positions. An applicant type is a complete ranking of position types; a position type is a complete ranking of applicant types. Each type has a binary-rational mass, with the masses on each side summing to one. In a plausible regime, there may be millions of applicants and positions but only hundreds or thousands of distinct preference types.

A solution is a nonnegative flow \(x_{ab}\) from applicant type \(a\) to position type \(b\), satisfying \(\sum_b x_{ab}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}=\nu_b\). Thus every unit of mass is matched, though a type’s mass may be split across several partner types. Let \(r_A(a,b)\) and \(r_B(b,a)\) be the two sides’ ranks. The objective is to find a stable flow minimizing
\[ \left|\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}r_A(a,b)-\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}r_B(b,a)\right|. \]
The output is an optimal flow and its objective value.

Stability needs a precise population definition. For each pair \((a,b)\), let \(u_{ab}\) be the mass of type \(a\) assigned to positions it ranks below \(b\), and \(v_{ab}\) the mass of type \(b\) assigned to applicants it ranks below \(a\). Require \(u_{ab}=0\) or \(v_{ab}=0\) for every pair. If both were positive, some positive mass on each side would prefer the other type to its current assignment, so that pair would block. This retains the paper’s central condition—no blocking pairs—while allowing the aggregate assignment and rank totals to be fractional.

This seems like a plausible object for the authors to recognize: it keeps stable matching and sex equality as the actual problem, rather than merely carrying over the paper’s neural architecture or its \([0,1]\) outcome relaxation. The authors also discuss continuous preference scores and continuous relaxations of stability losses, though those are not population continuizations. I would scope the mirror to the sex-equality problem alone.

My prior is Class C: continuum-specifically hard, but that is a conjecture, not a result from this paper. The mass constraints are linear, while the no-blocking condition has a choice for each type pair: one side must have no mass assigned below that pair. Those choices interact with the fairness objective and may preserve substantial combinatorial difficulty. I would not claim hardness transfers just because discrete instances can be represented using singleton types: divisible flows can change which stable assignments are feasible and the optimum they achieve. The first questions for a prover would be whether exact optimization is NP-hard, whether hardness persists with a bounded number of types, and whether a tractable relaxation has an integrality gap that can be bounded.

The weakest point is the change in what agents rank. The paper ranks individuals, while this mirror ranks counterpart types; replacing named counterpart identities with interchangeable categories could change the problem. The case is strongest in settings where positions and applicants really are treated as preference cohorts, and weakest where identity-level rankings are essential. It also remains an unnumbered, cited hardness claim rather than the named anchor the prompt requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor does not meet the paper’s own-result bar. The paper reports an architecture and experimental comparisons; its claim that sex-equality and balance optimization are strongly NP-hard cites earlier work. There is no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary here asserting a computational result. So the proposed mirror is a follow-on to a cited result, not a continuous mirror of a result this paper establishes.

The cohort version is coherent, but it changes the matching model. The paper’s input ranks named individuals, and those ranks determine both stability and the sex-equality cost. Ranking counterpart *types* instead makes members of a type interchangeable and changes the ranks used in the objective. A more faithful version could use mass-weighted rank percentiles, but that still gives the authors a type-ranked market with a newly defined fairness cost. The paper’s discussion of ties and continuous preference scores makes that extension plausible; it does not make it the same computational problem as the cited hardness result.

That distinction defeats this particular anchor, though it does not show that the cohort problem is uninteresting. A large hiring market with repeated preference cohorts is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and optimizing sex equality among stable cohort flows could be worth studying. I can’t honestly claim that no such scenario could produce a worthwhile ChoCo question. The strongest defensible negative is narrower: the case offered does not establish a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper, and the broader claim that no useful mirror exists in any scenario remains weak.
