What the mirror covers

The proposed anchor is Theorem 1's characterization; the paper's examples, equivalence discussion, and mechanism construction remain finite-state results, and the proposed mirror establishes no continuous-population result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: mirror rationalizable implementation, the paper’s central problem, in settings where a large population acts through a small number of organized constituencies.

Call the mirror Continuous-Composition Rationalizable Implementation. Let \(\mathcal T\) be a fixed finite set of constituency types, with \(|\mathcal T|\ge2\). A society is a composition \(\mu\in\Delta^\circ(\mathcal T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of people in constituency \(t\). A type is a complete description of its members’ preferences and other relevant features. Assume each constituency acts through one representative who sends a message for the whole bloc. The representative’s utility from lottery \(y\) at composition \(\mu\) is
\[ U_t(y,\mu)=\mu_t\sum_{x\in X} y_x v_t(x,\mu), \]
where \(X\) is a finite outcome set and the per-member utilities \(v_t\) are rational, piecewise-affine functions of \(\mu\). The target social choice function \(F:\Delta^\circ(\mathcal T)\to\Delta(X)\) is also given by a finite rational piecewise-affine description.

The question is: does there exist one mechanism, with a nonempty countable message space for each constituency and an outcome rule from message profiles to lotteries, such that at every composition \(\mu\), rationalizable strategy profiles exist and every such profile yields \(F(\mu)\)? A solution is such a mechanism; a negative answer rules out every mechanism in this class. Restricting \(\mu\) to rational compositions gives the discrete link: a population with \(n_t\) members of type \(t\) has \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). With many constituents and fixed \(|\mathcal T|\), those finite populations range over increasingly fine grids of compositions.

The scenario is a large workforce, electorate, or bargaining environment organized into a few cohesive blocs. For example, many workers may belong to a small number of unions, each with a representative who negotiates over the group’s behalf. The population is large relative to the number of distinct bloc types, and the share of each bloc is the natural quantity. This fits the paper’s implementation setting better than treating millions of people as independently strategic players: its two-player case is explicitly motivated by contracting and bargaining.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that, for a finite state set \(\Theta\), a social choice function \(f:\Theta\to Y\) is rationalizably implementable if and only if it satisfies Iterative Monotonicity (IM). In the paper’s construction, each player’s deception maps a state to a set of states containing the truth-telling state. Starting from the truth-telling deception \(\beta^0=\beta^t\), the authors iterate their preference-reversal map \(R\), setting \(\beta^k=R(\beta^{k-1})\), and take the limit \(\beta^*=\sup_k\beta^k\). IM requires that whenever \(f(\theta)\ne f(\theta')\), the deception profiles \(\beta^*(\theta)\) and \(\beta^*(\theta')\) have no common message profile. Theorem 1 says exactly that this condition is necessary and sufficient for a mechanism whose rationalizable profiles are nonempty and all implement \(f\).

The continuous question keeps that underlying problem: existence of a mechanism that implements a target under rationalizability. It asks whether the paper’s iterative characterization can extend when the society itself ranges over a continuum of type compositions. It does not claim novelty for lotteries or continuous outcomes, which the paper already uses. Nor does Theorem 1 give a polynomial-time bound; it is a constructive characterization, not a complexity-class result.

For this mirror, my tentative expectation is Class C, continuum-specific difficulty: the least-deception iteration would now range over correspondences on an uncountable composition space, and the paper’s finite-state construction gives no finite convergence or certificate guarantee. That is a low-confidence research prior, not a hardness result. The natural next questions are whether a measurable, computable version of the fixed-point characterization exists; whether finite-population grids approximate its answer; and whether the two-player preference-intersection complications persist across a continuum of compositions.

The soft spot is the bloc assumption. It makes the problem recognizable as rationalizable implementation among a few strategic players, but it means a constituency acts as one player rather than as many independent people. If individuals are nonatomic and the mechanism depends only on aggregate reports, one person’s report has no effect on the outcome; reports can all be best responses, making nonconstant full implementation generally fail. The proposed mirror is credible when the paper’s players are naturally organizations or delegated groups. If they are supposed to be individual members of a mass society, this case weakens sharply.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the paper’s only strong anchor, but it is a characterization of mechanism existence, not a complexity result. Its iterative procedure operates on deceptions over a finite state set; the paper gives no running-time bound or finite encoding for its implementation test, and leaves the claimed computational advantage for future work. Replacing the state set by a continuum therefore does not, by itself, create a computational problem. The proponent’s piecewise-rational data gives finite descriptions of \(F\) and utilities, but the question still quantifies over arbitrary countable mechanisms and deceptions with no specified finite representation. Restricting those objects enough to make the question algorithmic might be worthwhile, but it would need a separate justification.

There is also a more basic problem with the proposed bloc model. For every \(\mu_t>0\), multiplying a representative’s utility by \(\mu_t\) leaves their best responses and rationalizable strategies unchanged. If members of a type have composition-independent preferences, \(v_t(y,\mu)=v_t(y)\), then every composition induces the same game. With one common mechanism and nonempty rationalizable strategy profiles at every composition, full implementation consequently forces \(F\) to be constant: any one rationalizable profile must yield \(F(\mu)\) at every \(\mu\). To avoid that collapse, the model must make preferences vary with composition. Then composition acts as a payoff-relevant state, while the mass of each bloc has no independent strategic effect.

The natural alternative, a nonatomic population whose members report individually, faces the opposite limit: a single member’s report has no effect on aggregate outcomes. If reports are payoff-equivalent, arbitrary reports survive rationalizability, so full implementation generally requires outcomes to be constant across the resulting report profiles. Making blocs atomic restores pivotality, but returns to the representative model, where positive mass only rescales utility. Replicating strategic players on finite population grids could define a different problem, but Theorem 1 does not characterize that growing-player model.

I would not count the proposed anchor as a ChoCo mirror in its current form. The negative case is not airtight: a carefully encoded continuous-state implementation problem could still be interesting. But it would need to establish both that composition affects the population’s incentives in a substantive way and that the resulting mechanism-existence question has a finite computational formulation. The paper does not supply that bridge. The cited continuous-implementation literature is relevant background, but it does not by itself answer this rationalizability question.
