What the mirror covers

The proposed limit construction targets Proposition 3's two-type pairwise-stability characterization; it leaves aside the paper's other trading-path, private-information, ranked-type, homogeneous-player, and strong-stability results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest foothold is Proposition 3, proved in the paper. It says that when every weak player has discount rate \(r_W\), every strong player has rate \(r_S\), and \(r_S<r_W\), a network \(g\) is pairwise stable if and only if (i) every pair of weak players is linked, (ii) every strong player has degree one, and (iii) the network is connected. That is an exact structural characterization of the stable networks. On an explicitly represented finite network, it also gives a direct polynomial-time recognition test, though the paper itself does not state a complexity classification. The paper contains no named hardness result, and no theorem about algorithms or complexity in the usual sense.

A plausible population regime is a large trading market with two standardized discount-rate groups: a fraction \(\alpha\) of impatient traders and \(1-\alpha\) of patient traders, with many traders of each type. The mirror would retain the endogenous trading links and pairwise-stability criterion; the mass records how much of the population has each type. It would not continuize bargaining time or uncertainty over discount rates.

One precise candidate is Continuum Core–Periphery Limit Recognition. Its input is rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), rates \(r_W>r_S>0\), and a finitely described candidate limit architecture for an atomless population: a dense link relation among weak agents, together with a periphery-to-core attachment plan that records the strong agents’ single links. The question is whether this architecture is a limit, as population size grows and the weak fraction tends to \(\alpha\), of finite pairwise-stable networks under the paper’s Definition 1. A solution is the equivalence class of such limit architectures, with dense core links and sparse peripheral links represented separately. There is no welfare-maximization objective in the paper; the criterion is pairwise stability.

Proposition 3 supplies a strong guide to the expected answer: the weak core is complete, each strong agent attaches to one weak agent, and the network is connected. Under this finite-approximation definition, recognition looks tractable because the finite theorem reduces it to checking those structural conditions. The scope is Proposition 3’s two-type stability characterization only; it does not cover the delay results or the private-information propositions.

The soft spot is substantial: this is an asymptotic structural question, not a computational result the paper proves. Ordinary graph limits can erase the strong agents’ individual links, so the attachment plan must preserve information that a standard dense limit loses. And because the question defines stability through finite approximations, the answer risks being a translation of Proposition 3 rather than a new continuum incentive analysis. I would offer it as a credible population-level extension of the paper’s main result, but not as a strong computational mirror under ChoCo’s gate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is a structural characterization of equilibrium networks, not a computational result. The paper never formulates a complexity problem: it characterizes which finite networks are pairwise stable. Given the network and its weak and strong players, checking the proposition’s conditions takes polynomial time. The proponent’s proposed limit-recognition problem therefore has no computational landscape to chart in the first place.

The large-market story is plausible. An OTC market with many patient and impatient traders could be a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and I would not object that traders’ identities disappear when they are grouped by discount rate. The obstacle is what happens to the *network* in the limit. The theorem’s stable networks have a dense clique of weak players and one individual link for each strong player. In a standard graph limit, those periphery links have vanishing density: the strong players look isolated, and the finite networks’ connectedness disappears. The limit then omits structure that Proposition 3 requires.

The proposed attachment plan repairs that loss only by carrying additional incidence information alongside the population distribution. With that information preserved, however, the recognition question largely reduces to the finite characterization: the weak core is complete, each strong player has one attachment, and the network is connected. Any attachment assignment satisfying those conditions is allowed. Asking whether such a structure has finite stable approximants is consequently a reformulation of Proposition 3, not a new continuous incentive or algorithmic problem.

One could instead define stability in an atomless society using positive-mass link changes. That may support a substantive continuum network-formation model, but it changes the paper’s bilateral deviation: a single link between two individuals has zero mass. Alternatively, one could use a tagged-player or rescaled payoff to retain the finite bilateral incentives; then the extra local network structure is needed, and Proposition 3 still supplies the answer directly. Either route may be worth studying as network theory, but neither turns this paper’s result into the kind of computational mirror ChoCo seeks.

The negative case is strongest on computational value, not on whether a continuous population regime is sensible. This paper offers a plausible high-multiplicity setting, but its highlighted result is already a simple structural description, and the proposed continuous recognition task adds no meaningful complexity question.
