The continuous mirror question

Given at most three categories \(M\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), complete monotone rankings over \(2^M\), per-capita supplies \(q_j\), baseline type budgets \(\bar b_t\), and a rational radius \(\epsilon>0\), consider positive budget vectors \(b'\) with \(\lVert b'-\bar b\rVert_\infty<\epsilon\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t b'_t=1\). Decide whether, for almost every such \(b'\), there are prices \(p\) and masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}=q_j\), and positive \(x_{t,S}\) is assigned only to bundles \(S\) that are affordable and most preferred among the affordable bundles for type \(t\) at budget \(b'_t\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic Fisher market with complete preference-budget types \(t\), masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita category supplies \(q_j\), prices \(p\), and allocation variables \(x_{t,S}\) assigning mass of each type to integral bundles. The question is equilibrium feasibility for almost every nearby normalized type-budget vector.

The objection that survived

The proponent does not explicitly connect the course-market question to Proposition 3.1’s at-most-three-item regime; the mirror can be made precise by restricting it to at most three replicated categories.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the small-item equilibrium-existence question suggested by Proposition 3.1. It does not cover Theorem 3.5’s nonexistence threshold or the paper’s later preference-class and welfare results.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

I do not see a qualifying anchor. The paper has named results about whether competitive equilibria exist, but none asserts a computational complexity classification or presents a formulation that the paper computes with. The closest negative result is Theorem 3.5, proved here: there are monotone strict preferences for two agents over five items such that no competitive equilibrium exists whenever \(\tfrac{4}{3}b_2>b_1>b_2\). Proposition 3.1 gives a positive existence result for monotone preferences with at most three items under its stated budget condition. Both concern equilibrium existence, not the complexity of deciding or finding an equilibrium. So I cannot honestly offer a continuous problem *per computational anchor*.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity follow-on to the paper’s subject. Picture a large course-registration market: many students, a modest number of preference and entitlement groups, and course-seat categories whose capacities scale with enrollment. A type \(t\) would include a student’s complete ranking over course bundles and their budget; \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s population share. Let \(q_j\) be the per-student supply of course category \(j\). Students still receive integral bundles, while the fraction of each type receiving each bundle can be fractional.

One precise question for that model is Replicated-Market Generic CE: given course categories \(M\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), monotone preferences, per-capita supplies \(q_j\), baseline type budgets \(\bar b_t\), and a neighborhood radius \(\epsilon\), decide whether a competitive equilibrium exists for almost every positive budget vector \(b'\) in that neighborhood, subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t b'_t=1\). For a particular \(b'\), an equilibrium witness is prices \(p\) and masses \(x_{t,S}\geq 0\) such that
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:\,j\in S}x_{t,S}=q_j, \]
and \(x_{t,S}>0\) only when \(S\) is affordable at \(p\) and no strictly preferred bundle is affordable. This is a concrete computational question about the paper’s equilibrium concept, but it is an extrapolation, not a mirror anchored in one of its computational results. I would leave its complexity classification open: fixing prices makes allocation among demanded bundles a feasibility problem, while finding prices that induce those demands can retain substantial bundle structure.

The positive case is that course cohorts are a credible regime with \(N\) large and support size \(\tau\) small, and the paper itself names course allocation as an application. The weakest point is that the mirror scales seat supplies with the population and perturbs budgets at the type level; that changes the market and the meaning of genericity. Theorem 3.5’s two-agent obstruction does not establish that it survives this change. Also, the paper notes prior work on continuum-agent markets, so any contribution here would have to be the computational analysis, which this paper does not provide.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s two named candidates do not supply computational anchors. Proposition 3.1 and Theorem 3.5 assert when a competitive equilibrium exists for finite markets; Theorem 4.1 and Proposition 5.5 do the same for special preference classes. Theorem 3.6 concerns supportability of an allocation, and Theorem 5.3 characterizes a preference class. None gives a complexity classification or studies an algorithmic problem. So there is no named computational result here to continuize.

The course-market model is a coherent follow-on, and I would not reject it just because supplies scale with the population or a type’s mass can receive different bundles. But it asks a new question: whether equilibrium can be computed or shown to exist generically in a continuum-agent market with per-capita supplies and fractional type-level allocations. That model is not what Theorem 3.5 or Proposition 3.1 studies: each of their markets has a finite partition of indivisible goods among named agents. The proposed model changes the input regime and allocation object, then adds a computational question the paper never asks. It may be worth studying on its own, but it cannot rescue the missing anchor.

That is the strongest negative case I can make, and its limit is real: the course-registration scenario makes a continuous computational study plausible. The paper gives no basis for claiming that such a study would be uninteresting; it gives no computational result for the programme to mirror.
