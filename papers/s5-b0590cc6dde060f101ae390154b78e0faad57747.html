Why no mirror

Propositions 1 and 2 are named computational guarantees: the shift procedure terminates, and its outputs are Nash-stable, although neither supplies a runtime bound. The proposed continuum replaces whole-provider deviations and Shapley payoffs with infinitesimal entry and Aumann–Shapley payoffs, for which the paper gives no finite-replica limit. That yields a coherent nonatomic coalition-formation study, while changing the solution concept enough that the anchored results no longer mirror the paper’s question.

What the mirror covers

The attempted mirrors cover Proposition 1’s termination guarantee and Proposition 2’s Nash-stability guarantee; they leave the allocation formulation, coalition-value and core results, and experiments outside the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is for a narrow high-multiplicity version of energy-aware federation formation among regional edge-cloud operators. The operators would be numerous but drawn from a small set of standardized profiles: each type records its per-provider host mix, VM workload mix, VM revenues, energy price, and migration costs to each provider type. Think of many small operators using a few standard infrastructure and service configurations. The population is the mass of providers of each type; individual providers still make integral VM and host decisions, while allocations averaged over a large type mass can be fractional. This regime is plausible, though it is not a good model of a federation among a handful of hyperscalers.

The paper has two named algorithmic anchors, both proved here. It has no named result establishing a complexity class such as P or NP-hardness, so there is no hardness anchor and no direct discrete-hardness-versus-continuum test.

My lead is Proposition 2: *Any final partition produced by the algorithm in Figure 2 is Nash-stable.* In the paper’s terms, each provider weakly prefers its current coalition to joining any other current coalition or acting alone. The preference relation includes the history rule: a coalition the provider has previously left is assigned preference \(-\infty\). This is a stability guarantee for the federation-formation algorithm, not a complexity bound.

A precise mirror is Continuous Nash-Stable Federation. An instance gives a finite set of complete provider types \(T\) and rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). For a coalition with type-mass vector \(x\), define \(V(x)\) as its total revenue minus the minimum energy and migration cost for placing its VM mass on its host mass, using the mass-scaled version of the paper’s allocation model. The host-on and VM-assignment variables are continuous aggregate quantities; per-provider decisions remain integral. To define type payoffs, use the Aumann–Shapley allocation of this value function: for \(x_t>0\), the aggregate payoff to type \(t\) is
\[ P_t(x)=x_t\int_0^1 \partial_t V(sx)\,ds, \]
and its per-provider payoff is \(\pi_t(x)=P_t(x)/x_t\). For the piecewise-linear value functions arising from the allocation model, the derivative is taken where it exists along the path.

A solution is a finite list of coalition mass vectors \(x^1,\ldots,x^K\) summing to \(\mu\), together with their allocation and payoff variables, such that every type present in a coalition weakly prefers that coalition to entering any other listed coalition or forming a singleton. Entry payoffs are the limiting per-provider payoffs as an infinitesimal mass of that type joins. This asks for a stable federation structure over a continuous population, not for fractional outcomes.

For this search problem, I would tentatively expect Class C: existence and computation may be hard for continuum-specific reasons, since the finite algorithm’s convergence guarantee does not automatically provide a stable mass partition. That is a conjectural direction, not a result of this paper. The mirror also generates questions about whether stable partitions exist, whether finite replicas approximate them, and how the answer changes with the payoff rule.

The supporting anchor is Proposition 1: *Starting from any initial coalition structure \(\Pi_0\), the proposed algorithm always converges to a final partition \(\Pi_f\).* The authors prove it here using the finiteness of discrete partitions and the history rule. Its mirror is Continuous Federation-Shift Convergence: given rational type masses, an initial finite mass partition, and history states, repeatedly move an eligible type cohort to a coalition it strictly prefers under the continuous payoff rule. Ask whether every legal sequence of such shifts terminates after finitely many moves, and return a terminating path and final partition if so. I would also place this question in Class C, or leave it open: a continuum permits infinitely many coalition compositions, so the paper’s finite-state argument no longer applies. Proposition 1 does not establish polynomial time even in the discrete setting.

This mirror covers the paper’s coalition-formation stability and convergence results. It does not claim a complexity classification for the VM-allocation MILP, which appears as a formulation used in the paper’s computations, not as a numbered complexity result.

The weakest point is substantive: Shapley payoffs in a finite federation do not uniquely dictate Aumann–Shapley payoffs in the continuum, and the paper’s history mechanism tracks coalitions an individual provider has left. In a mass model, that history may need its own evolving distribution, potentially increasing the number of types. I think the authors could recognize the stable federation question as theirs, because it preserves provider types, federation membership, energy-aware allocation, and unilateral incentives. Whether they would accept this particular continuous payoff and history model is the main uncertainty.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper is a weak anchor for ChoCo before choosing any mirror: it proves no runtime bound or complexity classification. Proposition 1 proves termination of a finite, history-dependent procedure; Proposition 2 says its terminal state is Nash-stable under those history-dependent preferences. Neither tells us how hard it is to find a federation or how computation changes when the population becomes continuous. A continuous search problem could be invented here, but it would be a new question rather than a continuation of a computational result the paper established.

Proposition 2 has a more basic modelling gap. A player is a whole provider with an integral host inventory and workload, and its deviation moves that provider between coalitions. In an atomless population, one provider has zero mass, so its move does not change coalition composition. The proposed Aumann–Shapley payoff tries to recover a payoff from an infinitesimal marginal contribution, but the paper’s value function comes from a MILP with on/off hosts and discrete VM placements. Its marginal value can jump at packing and activation thresholds, so that derivative is not a canonical limit of the paper’s Shapley payoffs. A finite-replica limit is the strongest repair: replicate providers, scale the aggregate value, and derive type payoffs from the finite games. But that requires a particular scaling and a limit to exist; it does not follow from Proposition 2. The paper gives no reason to privilege that limit over other continuous payoff rules.

Proposition 1 runs into the same unit-of-deviation problem. If an entire type cohort moves together, the procedure has finitely many type-level partitions, but it forbids identical providers from splitting across coalitions. If arbitrary submasses may move, there are continuously many states and no finite-state termination argument. One could ask for asymptotic convergence, or study finite replicas as their size grows. Those are possible dynamical-systems or mean-field-game projects; the first changes the paper’s termination claim, and the second supplies no uniform algorithmic guarantee from the proposition.

The proposed regional edge-cloud scenario is the best case for a population model, and it keeps the negative case from being airtight. Still, a provider’s complete type here includes its host mix, workload, energy price, revenue policy, migration costs, and history. Migration costs are indexed by source and destination provider, so location and network relationships can make types nearly unique. A large population of interchangeable operators would need substantial standardization. The paper’s small-provider examples do not establish that regime, though they cannot rule it out.

So I would not treat either proposition as a strong ChoCo mirror. The negative case is strongest on fit: making a continuous model requires new payoff, deviation, and convergence choices, while the paper supplies no complexity result to anchor them. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists; a replicated edge-provider model could be interesting. Its case would be for a new mean-field coalition-formation study, rather than a compelling continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
