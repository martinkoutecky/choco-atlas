The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly give a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results: it has no named result in the requested complexity-theoretic sense. There is no classification of a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar.

The closest candidate is Theorem 3, proved in the paper: when there are at least three players and the environment is economic, a social choice rule is behaviorally strongly implementable if and only if there is a collection of opportunity sets coalitionally consistent with that rule. Theorem 2 gives the necessary direction more generally, and Theorem 4 gives a sufficient condition for status quo rules. These are results about whether a mechanism exists, not about the complexity of deciding or computing something. Proposition 1 is explicitly attributed to de Clippel (2014), and likewise concerns behavioral implementability.

One could propose a new continuum-agent extension in which a type is a complete state-contingent choice rule, mass is the fraction of agents with that rule, and the mechanism must implement a target outcome while resisting coalition deviations. But the paper’s results concern a finite set of strategically distinct players, and their mechanisms and coalitions depend on those player identities. Defining a continuum version would therefore require a new equilibrium and implementation theory; it would not mirror a named computational result in this paper. That is the limiting point for the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper supplies no computational problem to mirror. Theorem 3, Theorem 2, and Theorem 4 are results about whether a social choice rule is implementable, not about the complexity of deciding implementability or finding a mechanism. Theorem 2’s necessity condition follows directly from the equilibrium definition, and its proof is omitted as such. Proposition 1 is an earlier behavioral-implementation result attributed to de Clippel, not a result of this paper. Those results may motivate computational questions, but none defines one.

The best continuous proposal would give mass to complete state-contingent choice-rule types, restrict attention to anonymous mechanisms, and ask whether a target rule can be implemented against deviations by measurable coalitions. That sounds coherent. But it requires choices the paper does not make: how types are encoded, what mechanisms are allowed, how a coalition’s mass changes the outcome, and what counts as a block. With unrestricted choice rules over menus, state spaces, and mechanisms, there is no evident finite input whose running time to analyze. A computational study could impose useful restrictions, but its complexity landscape would be created by those restrictions; it would not be the continuous counterpart of a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 3 is the most tempting anchor, but its economic-environment assumption is tied to named players. Each player must have a distinct outcome \(x[i]\) that is uniquely chosen whenever available. In a continuum population, that assigns a distinct witness outcome to each individual; with a finite outcome set, this cannot hold. In exchange examples, the witness gives the entire aggregate endowment to one named player, which does not naturally become a feasible allocation to a zero-mass individual. One could replace it with type-level or per-capita outcomes, but that changes the condition the theorem characterizes. The resulting theorem might be valuable, yet it would be a new implementation theory with no computational claim supplied by the original.

Theorem 2 and Theorem 4 fare no better as computational anchors. Their conditions quantify over coalitions, opportunity sets, and choice behavior; in a continuum model, those become measurable coalitions and mass-indexed deviations. Finite support could make some of that tractable, but the paper offers no algorithmic structure that says which finite representation to use or what complexity question to ask. Proposition 1 can likewise be reimagined for a population with repeated bounded-rationality types, but its finite agenda and player-specific allocation of alternatives would need their own mass-based reformulation. That would be a new mirror of de Clippel’s result, not a computational mirror of this paper.

So the negative case is strong for the programme’s stated purpose: none of the proponent’s anchors is a named computational result, and the paper’s core objects do not provide a ready-made computational problem that continuization makes accessible. It is not an airtight claim that no worthwhile continuum model could be built. A large population sharing choice biases could be an interesting implementation setting. The honest objection is that developing it would be a separate research direction, not the continuous computational-social-choice mirror this paper offers.
