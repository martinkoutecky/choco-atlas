Why no mirror

The paper's named theorems are structural representation results, not computational results of the kind required by the programme. A distribution over streaming-profile types gives a coherent high-multiplicity evaluation reformulation for \(P\), \(U\), and \(Sh\), but it does not supply a computational anchor. The proposed mass-transfer and inverse problems would import new objectives absent from the paper.

What the mirror covers

The closest reformulation covers Theorem 1's three index identities and the family correspondences suggested by Theorems 2 and 3; it does not cover a computational optimization result because the paper has none.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is real, but modest. The paper contains no named theorem asserting NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), parameterized complexity, or an explicit LP formulation. Its named results are structural. Thus there is no genuine complexity anchor in the strict sense required by the programme. The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper.

The natural mirror makes users the continuous population and keeps artists finite. Let \(A=\{1,\ldots,n\}\) be the artists, and let \(\mathcal X\subseteq\mathbb Z_+^n\setminus\{0\}\) be a finite set of complete user-profile types. A type \(x\in\mathcal X\) records how many times that user streams each artist during the accounting period. Let \(\mu_x\) be the fraction of users of type \(x\), with \(\sum_x\mu_x=1\). The type includes every feature used by the rule: the entire streaming vector, and, in an extension with different subscription prices or budgets, those features as well.

There may be millions of users but only \(\tau=|\mathcal X|\) recurring profiles: for example, users in a fixed subscription cohort, country, or listening-programme class with identical monthly streaming profiles. This is a genuine high-multiplicity regime, not an assertion that every streaming platform has few types. With \(N\) actual users, the discrete instance has \(N\mu_x\) users of type \(x\); the continuous instance records only \(\mu\). Total revenue is \(N\) if each user pays one unit, so the continuous rewards below are per-capita rewards and actual payments are \(N\) times those values.

For \(x\), write \(q_x=\sum_i x_i\) and \(S_x=\{i:x_i>0\}\). Define aggregate streams by
\[ A_i(\mu)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal X}\mu_x x_i, \qquad A(\mu)=\sum_i A_i(\mu)=\sum_x\mu_xq_x. \]

My lead mirror is Continuous Streaming Revenue Allocation. Its instance is \((A,\mathcal X,\mu)\) together with one of the paper’s indices \(P,U,Sh\). Its solution is the exact per-capita reward vector \(r\), namely
\[ r_i^P=\frac{A_i(\mu)}{A(\mu)}, \]
\[ r_i^U=\sum_x\mu_x\frac{x_i}{q_x}, \]
and
\[ r_i^{Sh}=\sum_x\mu_x \frac{\mathbf 1[i\in S_x]}{|S_x|}. \]
The decision variable is the payout vector \(r\), or equivalently a type-level payout certificate; the objective is exact evaluation of the selected streaming rule. This is an allocation problem rather than an optimization problem, which is an important limitation of the mirror.

Theorem 1 states, in full, that for every finite streaming problem and its associated multi-issue claims problem: there is a weighted proportional rule reproducing pro-rata rewards; there is a weighted proportional rule reproducing user-centric rewards; no weighted proportional rule reproduces the Shapley rewards; pro-rata equals the two-stage proportional–proportional rule \(R^{P,P}\); user-centric equals the constrained-equal-awards–proportional rule \(R^{CEA,P}\); and Shapley equals the constrained-equal-awards–constrained-equal-awards rule \(R^{CEA,CEA}\). The theorem is proved in the paper.

Every statement survives the mirror by replacing sums over named users with mass-weighted sums over types. In particular, a type \(x\) has claim \(q_x\) in the first-stage claims problem. The proportional first stage gives the pro-rata formula, while constrained equal awards gives every user one unit because \(q_x\ge1\) and the total endowment is one per capita. Applying proportional allocation within a type gives \(r^U\); applying constrained equal awards within a type gives \(r^{Sh}\). The nonexistence statement also survives: the paper’s one-user counterexample is already an atomic continuous society.

This mirror should be tractable, in Class A, but only in the elementary sense that its exact answer is computable in polynomial time—indeed with \(O(n\tau)\) arithmetic operations once the types are explicit. The paper’s identities become exact continuum identities, not approximations. The high-multiplicity dictionary is also clean: a rational discrete profile with counts \(N_x\) maps to \(\mu_x=N_x/N\), and multiplying the continuous reward by \(N\) recovers the discrete payout.

The original authors should recognise this as their problem’s continuous analogue. Their rules depend on user columns only through sums, supports, and per-user normalization. Replacing
\[ \sum_{j\in M} f(t_{\cdot j}) \]
by
\[ N\sum_x\mu_x f(x) \]
is therefore a direct population continuization, while the artist set and the underlying streaming technology remain discrete. The fact that rewards are divisible is inherited from the paper’s revenue-allocation model; it is not the object being continuized.

The mirror also generates more substantive follow-up questions. One can ask whether a target reward vector is realizable by a type-local probabilistic index, or whether it can be obtained by a user-weighted index. Those are natural inverse problems over the same \(\mu\), and the paper’s Theorems 2 and 3 suggest linear or linear-fractional formulations. One can also allow mass transfers between streaming types—campaigns that move a fraction of users from one listening profile to another—and minimize the cost of making an artist reach a target revenue share. That would be a genuine ChoCo optimization problem built on the paper’s model rather than a change of subject.

The weakest point is decisive: the paper itself studies no computationally difficult decision problem. The mirror therefore does not show that discrete hardness dissolves in the continuum; it merely gives an exact and sensible high-multiplicity version of an already easy allocation calculation. Moreover, exact streaming profiles may produce very many types, so the regime is weaker than the canonical voting example. Still, the model is honest, the authors’ Theorem 1 transfers exactly, and it provides a legitimate Class A starting point rather than pretending that this paper contains a complexity result it does not.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper has no computational anchor in the programme’s sense. Theorem 1 is a structural identity between allocation rules, not a theorem about an algorithm, complexity class, approximation guarantee, or optimization problem. It specifies no computational input model beyond a finite matrix \(t\), no decision problem, and no objective to optimize. Theorems 2 and 3 are even more plainly representation theorems: they identify broad families of indices with broad families of claims rules. Consequently, there is no named computational result whose population axis can be continuized.

The proposed mirror is mathematically legitimate, but that is not enough. With user types \(x\) and masses \(\mu_x\), the three outputs are simply

\[ r_i^P=\frac{\sum_x\mu_x x_i}{\sum_x\mu_x q_x}, \qquad r_i^U=\sum_x\mu_x\frac{x_i}{q_x}, \qquad r_i^{Sh}=\sum_x\mu_x\frac{\mathbf 1[i\in S_x]}{|S_x|}. \]

These are direct expectations of the paper’s per-user formulas. Theorem 1’s equalities then persist by linearity. That establishes a sensible high-multiplicity regime, but not a worthwhile continuous computational problem. The continuum has supplied a measure-theoretic notation for aggregating identical columns; it has not supplied a search space, feasibility question, intervention, robustness query, or nontrivial optimization object from the paper.

There is also a repairable but revealing flaw in the proponent’s CEA explanation. If all users of type \(x\) are compressed into one type-agent with claim \(q_x\), ordinary CEA does not give that type-agent its mass \(\mu_x\). To recover the intended result one must retain an atomless population of individual users, or explicitly introduce a mass-weighted CEA rule with aggregate claim \(\mu_xq_x\) and aggregate endowment \(\mu_x\). The authors would likely accept that repair, so it is not the fundamental objection. But after the repair, the result is still only the same per-user identity integrated against \(\mu\).

Theorem 2 does not rescue the case. If probability systems are restricted to anonymous type-local rules, the attainable reward vectors are just

\[ \left\{\sum_x\mu_x p_x: p_x\in\Delta(S_x)\right\}, \]

a product of simplices mapped linearly. If the paper’s permitted dependence on the named user \(j\) is retained, then users with the same streaming vector need not be the same type, and the finite high-multiplicity compression disappears unless every user-specific parameter is separately included in the type. Thus the theorem either becomes an elementary polyhedral description after adding anonymity, or fails to produce a bounded type space under its original generality.

The same dichotomy affects Theorem 3. Under a type-local weight \(w_x\), the aggregate reward has the form

\[ r_i= \frac{\sum_x\mu_x w_x x_i} {\sum_x\mu_x w_x q_x}. \]

That is a direct normalized weighted sum. With arbitrary user-dependent weights, the proposed type compression again ceases to be valid. Theorem 4 has the same issue in a narrower form. None of these results creates a computational problem; they merely describe which formulas can represent which allocations.

The suggested mass-transfer extension is the strongest possible rescue: introduce transfers \(z_{xy}\) between listening-profile types, costs for changing profiles, and a target artist share. That could become a genuine ChoCo transport or intervention problem. But it is not a mirror of any result in this paper. The paper fixes \(t\) and studies revenue-sharing rules; it contains no campaign operation, conversion cost, target share, or robustness objective. Those ingredients would be imported from the continuization programme. The resulting project might be worthwhile on its own, but it cannot make this paper a computational anchor under the stated rules.

So the negative verdict is strong under the programme’s eligibility criterion: the paper supports a clean high-multiplicity reformulation, but no worthwhile continuous mirror of its actual contribution. The universal claim is not literally provable—one can build a new intervention problem on top of streaming profiles—but that would be new computational social choice inspired by the paper, not continuization of one of its named results.
