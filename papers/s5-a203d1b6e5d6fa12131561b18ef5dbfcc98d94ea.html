The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that Theorem 5 describes a phenomenon with a natural population-level analogue. But it is not a qualifying computational anchor: it proves a welfare comparison, not a complexity or algorithmic result. The same is true of the paper’s other main results.

Theorem 5 says that, in any finite market with fixed submitted rank-order lists and fixed priorities at other colleges, if criteria at the focal college agree on acceptability, reversing two terminal criterion blocks weakly benefits each criterion when its own block is later under the Boston mechanism. Theorem 4 gives a large-market DA result: under regularity, the expected fraction of colleges where either comparison fails tends to zero; with sufficient thickness, the failure probability tends to zero uniformly over colleges. Theorem 2 gives the corresponding comparison for a fixed applicant set. These are substantive comparative statics, but none asserts that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

A plausible continuous extension would model a large public-admissions cohort. A type would specify a submitted school list and the applicant’s acceptability and priority class at each school; \(\mu_\theta\) would be that type’s share of the cohort. School capacities and criterion-block sizes would be fractions of cohort size. In each Boston round, type mass applies to its next school, and each criterion accepts eligible mass in its type-priority order, possibly splitting mass at a cutoff.

One concrete computational question is: given rational type masses, school capacities, priority orders, and two precedence orders that differ by a terminal \(A\)-/\(B\)-block reversal at a focal school, compute the final matching mass under each order and compare the resulting admitted mass under supplied criterion utilities. In a repeated-cohort setting with many applicants but relatively few shared types, this is a sensible population model. On finite support, I would expect the Boston outcomes to be computable by round-by-round mass updates, so the natural prediction is tractability. Theorem 5 motivates the comparison to investigate, but does not establish this continuous computational result.

The main weakness is that the paper gives every applicant a distinct position in each criterion’s strict ranking. A type that captures all those ranks may be unique to one applicant, eliminating the high-multiplicity regime. Grouping applicants into shared priority types therefore requires a coarser priority model, which the authors might regard as a meaningful extension but which they do not study.

So there is a credible population-continuous follow-on centered on Theorem 5, but no named computational result in this paper to anchor the requested continuization programme case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative starts with what the paper actually proves. Theorems 2, 4, and 5, along with the other named results, are comparative statics about who is admitted under different precedence orders. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 5 is the proponent’s best anchor, but its continuous analogue would not continue a computational result from the paper: it would add an outcome-computation task the paper never poses.

That task is coherent. In a repeated admissions cohort, a type could record a submitted school list and a profile of criterion scores or priority classes, with its mass giving the cohort share of that profile. Capacities could scale with the cohort, and a cutoff could split a type’s mass. This avoids treating each applicant’s identity as essential; grouped score profiles are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. With fixed precedence orders, the Boston mechanism then updates type masses round by round. That is a sensible population model, but it is a procedure for implementing a fixed mechanism, not a computational question the theorem addresses. Choosing an order to optimize some policy objective would be more ambitious, but it would add an objective absent from the paper. Such a project might be worthwhile on its own; the paper does not supply the computational problem that would make it a mirror.

Theorem 2 has the same gap in a simpler form. One can replace the applicant set with a distribution over criterion score profiles, scale the seats with population, and compare the resulting admitted masses under a terminal-block swap. That is a nondegenerate and plausible continuization. But the operation remains sequential selection by criterion priority. The theorem states a welfare comparison of the two outputs; it does not study the cost of finding either output or optimizing over precedence orders. The continuous version could produce a meaningful comparative-static result, but it would not provide a complexity question to classify.

Theorem 4 faces a more specific scale problem. Its large-market result keeps each college’s capacity bounded while the number of colleges grows. If students are normalized to unit population mass, the capacity of any one college—and thus the focal college’s admitted class—has vanishing mass. If capacities instead grow to keep each college’s mass positive, the model no longer satisfies the theorem’s bounded-capacity regime. A continuum model could study a continuum of colleges or finite-size rejection-chain effects, but those are substantial new modeling choices. In particular, the return event central to Theorem 4 is an individual rejection chain; an aggregate mass flow may erase that event. So the natural limit does not straightforwardly retain the theorem’s central object.

These points do not show that a continuous population model is impossible or that its answer would be uninteresting. They show why the proponent’s strongest construction remains a follow-on model rather than a mirror of this paper’s computational contribution. The strongest-looking anchor, Theorem 5, is still an analytic welfare result; the other anchors have the same issue, and Theorem 4 also loses its focal scale under the most direct population limit.

The honest limit of the negative case is that a continuous Boston admissions model with shared score profiles is quite plausible. The objection is narrower: the paper gives no named computational result for the continuization programme to carry over. To get one, the analyst must supply a new optimization or robustness problem, and its worth cannot be established just from these theorems.
