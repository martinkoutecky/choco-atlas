The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive case that meets your anchor requirement: the paper has no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or a parameterized complexity classification.

Its main result, the *Expected Multi-Utility Theorem* (proved in the paper), is a representation theorem: for a compact metric prize space, independence and continuity characterize when an incomplete preference over lotteries can be represented by a set of continuous bounded utility functions. That is an existence result, not a computational result. The paper gives no named complexity anchor to mirror.

One could imagine a new problem about a large population of consumers with a small number of shared incomplete preferences over lotteries. But the paper studies one decision maker, and its set of utility functions represents that person’s incompleteness; it is not a population of voter types. Treating it as one would change the problem rather than continuize it. And the paper’s continuous lottery space is continuity of outcomes, which your scope explicitly excludes as the goal. So there is no honest, anchor-based continuous problem to hand to a prover from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no computational anchor, and that is the strongest case against a mirror. Adding consumers and an aggregation objective would create a new social-choice problem, with no result in this paper to continuize.

One could also ask for an algorithm to check the axioms or construct a representation. The paper gives no finite input model for that task: it allows arbitrary compact metric prize spaces and preference relations over lotteries, so the computational problem would depend on additional encoding choices. Such a project might be worthwhile on its own, but it would not be a continuous-population mirror of a result here.
