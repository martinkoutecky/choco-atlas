The continuous mirror question

Given three candidates, a target \(c^\star\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over transitive partial preference reports \(p\), is there an allocation \(x_{p,\rho}\geq 0\) of each report's mass among its linear extensions \(\rho\), with \(\sum_{\rho \text{ extends } p} x_{p,\rho}=\mu_p\), and an agenda under which \(c^\star\) wins by majority? Resolve pairwise ties using a fixed candidate priority order.

The model it lives in

Types are the reported partial orders \(p\), with mass \(\mu_p\). Decision variables \(x_{p,\rho}\) split each report cohort among its complete rankings; the agenda is chosen from the finite three-candidate agendas. There is no cost objective: the question is whether some feasible allocation and agenda make the target win under majority voting with fixed tie-breaking.

The objection that survived

The paper's formal weighted profile gives each weighted vote one completion, while its unit-voter expansion allows those voters to complete independently; the cohort scenario makes this extension plausible, but the paper does not explicitly equate the two semantics.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5 and, for three candidates, the fair-agenda hardness of Theorem 7; it leaves the Condorcet-winner results, the profile-versus-majority-graph comparisons, and the variable-candidate complexity landscape untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • For variable numbers of candidates, what is the decision complexity in the number of candidates, support size, and input bit length?
  • When can a continuous winning allocation be rounded to a finite electorate while preserving the winner?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: given an incomplete weighted profile with at least three candidates and a designated candidate, deciding whether that candidate is a weak possible winner is NP-complete. Here, that means asking whether some completion of the voters’ incomplete preferences and some agenda make the candidate win. The paper proves hardness by reducing from number partitioning, already with three candidates. I would mirror that three-candidate hard case.

Call the mirror Three-Candidate Continuous Weak Possible Winner. Its input is three candidates, a target candidate, and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over incomplete preference reports \(p\), with \(\sum_p\mu_p=1\). A report is a transitive partial order; its possible completions are the linear rankings extending it. The question is whether there is an allocation \(x_{p,\rho}\geq 0\) of each report’s mass among its completions, satisfying
\[ \sum_{\rho\text{ extends }p}x_{p,\rho}=\mu_p, \]
and an agenda such that the target wins. Pairwise contests use strict majority of mass, with a fixed priority order to resolve ties. A solution is the allocation and agenda witnessing the win. This is an existence question, so it has no cost objective.

The regime is a large electorate with many people sharing one of relatively few disclosed partial rankings—for example, a standardized elicitation process that has left the same comparisons unknown for many respondents. Mass records the fraction of society with each report. Crucially, members of a report cohort may have different underlying rankings, so their mass can split among its completions. The agenda remains the chair’s finite decision; the population is the continuous object.

I expect this three-candidate mirror to be tractable (Class A). Each report has at most six linear extensions, and there are only finitely many three-candidate agendas and pairwise outcome patterns. For each pattern, the mass-conservation equations and the required pairwise margins give a linear feasibility problem. Checking this finite collection of LPs decides whether the target can win. Strict-majority constraints can be handled with an auxiliary slack variable. With rational input, this gives a polynomial-time decision procedure.

The partition reduction shows why the relaxation matters. Its variable-weight incomplete ballots all report \(A>B\); their weights encode the integers \(k_i\). In the discrete construction, each weighted ballot must be completed as a whole, so arranging the needed pairwise results encodes an equal partition. In the continuous model, that report mass can be divided between \(A>B>C\) and \(C>A>B\). Splitting it equally gives the reduction’s needed cycle without solving the partition instance. The hardness is driven by indivisible population blocks, which the fluid model removes.

With three leaves, every full binary agenda is balanced. So the same formulation also covers the fair-agenda version at three candidates. It does not settle the variable-candidate problem: there, the many possible rankings and agendas could introduce a distinct source of difficulty. Further questions include whether the general continuous problem is tractable in \(m,\tau,L\), and when a fluid yes-instance can be rounded to a finite electorate while preserving a winner with a sufficient margin.

The weakest point is the interpretation of weights and incomplete reports. The mirror treats weight as cohort size and lets people with the same report complete their preferences differently. That is a natural high-multiplicity reading, supported by the paper’s definition of \(U(P)\), which replaces a weight-\(k\) vote with \(k\) identical unit voters. But if the authors intend each weighted ballot to be an indivisible bloc whose members must share one completion, this mirror changes the completion semantics. The case is strongest for the paper’s population interpretation, and deliberately limited to its three-candidate hardness core.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is a real computational anchor, so the strongest objection is about what its continuous mirror means. In the reduction, each weighted partial ballot must receive one completion for its entire weight. The authors’ expansion \(U(P)\) shows the distinction: if those unit voters may complete independently, the set of possible completions grows. Indeed, the paper notes that weights can make weak possible winner computation hard where the unweighted case is easy. The hardness is tied to that shared completion across a weighted block.

The proposed LP allows the mass of one partial-report cohort to split among rankings. That is a sensible model if many people gave the same partial report but may have different hidden preferences. But it changes the reduction’s completion semantics: it removes the very block correlation that encodes the partition. Preserve that correlation, and the mass must act as an indivisible bloc; allowing it to split instead answers a different question about uncertain populations. The paper does not settle which interpretation its authors would accept as the mirror.

There is also a scope concern. With three candidates, there are only constantly many partial orders, completions, pairwise outcomes, and agendas. The proposed algorithm is therefore a small LP enumeration; it shows that fractional completion erases the fixed-candidate hardness, but it does not yet provide a broader complexity landscape. Extending the model to variable \(m\) could be more substantial, though exponentially many rankings and agendas then enter, and this paper’s three-candidate reduction does not resolve that problem.

Those points make the mirror narrower and its relationship to the paper’s semantics less direct than the proponent suggests. They do not defeat it: a large population with shared reports and heterogeneous hidden rankings is plausible, and showing that its continuous weak-winner problem is tractable could be worthwhile. I cannot honestly make a strong universal case that no scenario produces a useful mirror; the negative case rests mainly on the unresolved completion semantics and the limited scope of the proposed three-candidate result.
