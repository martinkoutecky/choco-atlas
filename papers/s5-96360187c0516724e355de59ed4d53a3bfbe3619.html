The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), ballot types \(R\in\mathcal T\subseteq\Gamma(C)\), a prefix \(\ell\), and independent random nonnegative rational weights \(X_R\) with explicitly listed finite distributions on a common grid and \(\sum_R X_R>0\), set \(\mu_R=X_R/\sum_S X_S\). After eliminating \(\ell\) and transferring each type’s mass to its highest-ranked remaining candidate, compute exactly the probability that a specified remaining candidate \(A_i\) has strictly less first-choice mass than every other remaining candidate.

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over ballot-ranking types, with each realized mass vector obtained by normalizing independent random weights \(X_R\). There is no intervention to choose: the output is the exact probability of the strict-minimum event at a specified elimination prefix; the common normalization does not change the comparisons.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Lemma 7’s strict-minimum probability calculation at an elimination prefix; it does not separately anchor the full elimination-tree algorithm, tie handling, empirical examples, or prediction accuracy.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of this probability calculation in the bit length and support size of the weight distributions, as well as in \(m\) and \(\tau\)?
  • Can the calculation be extended efficiently to correlated probability laws over ballot-type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: the paper has no numbered theorem or lemma classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or similar. Its best named computational anchor is Lemma 7, proved in the appendix and used by the algorithm. It says that, after an elimination prefix \(\ell\), if the candidates’ first-choice totals are independent, then the probability that \(A_i\) has *strictly fewer* first-choice votes than every other remaining candidate is

\[ \widehat E^\ell_{\{A_i\}} = \sum_{k\in B} \tau^\ell_{A_i}(k) \prod_{j\ne i}\bigl(1-\kappa^\ell_{A_j}(k)\bigr), \]

where \(\tau^\ell_{A_i}\) is the distribution of \(A_i\)’s total and \(\kappa^\ell_{A_j}\) is the cumulative distribution of each other candidate’s total. The formula computes a round’s elimination probability; Lemma 6, also proved here, supplies the independence of the aggregated totals needed as the algorithm recurses. I would use Lemma 7 as the sole anchor, rather than imply that the paper proves a complexity bound it does not.

Lead mirror: Continuous-mass IRV prediction. An instance has candidates \(C\), a set \(\mathcal T\subseteq\Gamma(C)\) of \(\tau\) ballot types (complete, possibly truncated rankings), and an independent random nonnegative weight \(X_R\) for each \(R\in\mathcal T\). Each weight has an explicitly listed finite distribution on a common rational grid, with binary-rational probabilities; assume the sum of the weights is positive in every possible outcome. In a draw, the society is the distribution over ballot types with masses

\[ \mu_R=\frac{X_R}{\sum_{S\in\mathcal T}X_S}. \]

Run IRV on that society: each type’s mass goes to its highest-ranked remaining candidate, or exhausts if it ranks none. Resolve a tie for elimination uniformly among the tied candidates. The question is to compute the exact probability of each elimination sequence and each candidate winning. A solution is that probability vector and elimination tree. There is no action to choose or objective to minimize: this is a prediction problem, as in the paper.

This is a continuous population because each realized society is a distribution of fractional mass over ballot types; no individual ballots are enumerated. The rational grid is an input representation for uncertain masses. In the natural regime, a large RCV electorate—say hundreds of thousands of voters—has relatively few distinct ballot types, especially with three to five candidates. Each type is the exact ballot ranking, and its mass is its fraction of the electorate. Partial counts or polling can supply the uncertainty over those fractions. This is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and the authors should recognize the mirror: the ballot types, transfer rule, and winner-prediction task all remain theirs. The mirror covers the paper’s recursive probability calculation for partial-count and recount predictions, not the accuracy of its polling or recount assumptions.

I expect a tractable, parameterized version for the paper’s small-agenda regime. On an explicit common grid, the type totals and the totals transferred after elimination can be computed by convolutions; Lemma 7’s formula then gives the probability of a strict minimum, with the paper’s tie calculation handling tied minima. The full elimination tree has at most a factorial number of branches in \(m=|C|\), so this supports fixed-parameter tractability in \(m\) and polynomial time for fixed \(m\), measured against the explicit input-table size, \(\tau\), and grid size. It does not establish polynomial time in unbounded \(m\). The further questions are whether the full tree can be computed more efficiently as \(m\) grows, how to model correlated type masses, and how the result changes when the mass distributions are not given on an explicit grid.

The weak point is that this is close to a rescaling of the paper’s model: rational masses can be scaled back to integer counts, and dividing count totals by a common factor leaves IRV outcomes unchanged. So the mirror is a sensible continuous-population formulation and a high-multiplicity bridge, but Lemma 7 does not show that continuity removes hardness or unlocks a new algorithm. The paper also makes no named hardness claim for this prediction problem; any claim that hardness transfers would need a discrete stochastic-prediction hardness result to transfer from.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Lemma 7’s formula for the probability that one candidate has strictly fewer first-choice votes than all the others. The strongest objection is that this is a probabilistic identity for independent vote-count distributions, not a complexity result about a continuous population. For a fixed mass vector \(\mu\), IRV’s elimination order is deterministic; to preserve the paper’s prediction question, the mirror needs a probability law over mass vectors as well.

That objection does not defeat the proponent’s formulation. If the independent random counts are \(X_R\), normalizing them to \(\mu_R=X_R/\sum_S X_S\) gives genuine societies over ballot types. The normalization preserves every elimination comparison, so Lemma 7’s calculation carries over. This makes the mirror very close to the paper’s existing count model, but closeness or an unchanged answer is not enough to make a well-posed mirror unworthy.

A stronger version would put a correlated probability law directly on the simplex of ballot-type masses and ask for the probability of each IRV elimination sequence. That fits the paper’s prediction aim and its stated interest in correlations. For a specified law—say, a Dirichlet law or a piecewise polynomial density—the task is well-defined: compute the probability mass of the regions in which each elimination sequence occurs. The work may then center on probability integration rather than Lemma 7’s convolution formula, but I cannot establish that this makes the question uninteresting or outside the authors’ problem.

So the negative case is weak. The paper does not prove a complexity classification, and its named computational anchor does not by itself show that continuization opens a new algorithmic landscape. But the population has many voters per ballot type, the mass model retains the election’s relevant information, and a continuous random-society version is natural. I can argue that the proposed mirror may be mostly a reformulation; I cannot honestly defeat every worthwhile continuous mirror of this anchor.
