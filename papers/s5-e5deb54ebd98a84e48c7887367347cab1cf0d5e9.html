What the mirror covers

The proposed restatement covers Proposition 1's oracle optimization and strategy-proofness; it does not create a new continuous mirror of that result. The identification and sample-based regret results in Propositions 2 and 3 concern the paper's statistical procedures.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s oracle treatment rule, not of its experimental design or its statistical treatment rules.

The paper has no numbered result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or similar. Its best computational anchor is Proposition 1, proved here: assuming people prefer lotteries that give their true preferred treatment with higher probability, an optimal individualized treatment rule is determined by \(\beta_1=\Pr(T=1)\tau(1)\) and \(\beta_0=\Pr(T=0)\tau(0)\), where \(\tau(t)\) is the conditional average treatment effect for true preference type \(t\). The proposition gives the following optimal rule \((\delta(1),\delta(0))\), where each coordinate is the probability of treatment \(1\) for that stated type:

  • If \(\beta_1>0,\beta_0>0\), or \(\beta_1>0,\beta_0=0\), or \(\beta_1=0,\beta_0>0\), the rule is \((1,1)\).
  • If \(\beta_1>0,\beta_0<0\), it is \((1,0)\).
  • If \(\beta_1=0,\beta_0=0\), it is \((\epsilon,\epsilon)\) for any \(\epsilon\in[0,1]\).
  • If \(\beta_1=0,\beta_0<0\), or \(\beta_1<0,\beta_0=0\), or \(\beta_1<0,\beta_0<0\), it is \((0,0)\).
  • If \(\beta_1<0,\beta_0>0\), it is \((0,0)\) when \(\beta_1+\beta_0<0\), \((\epsilon,\epsilon)\) when \(\beta_1+\beta_0=0\), and \((1,1)\) when \(\beta_1+\beta_0>0\).

In every case the rule is strategy-proof. This is an exact optimization result, though the paper does not state a complexity-class bound.

A continuous version I would put to a prover is Strategic Treatment Mass Optimization. An instance consists of two treatments \(0,1\); rational masses \(\mu_0,\mu_1\geq 0\) with \(\mu_0+\mu_1=1\); rational conditional effects \(\tau(0),\tau(1)\); and a population of unit mass in which type \(t\) strictly prefers treatment \(t\). Each agent knows the rule and reports a type. The decision is a pair \(\delta(0),\delta(1)\in[0,1]\), giving the treatment-\(1\) probability for each report. Agents maximize expected utility, so truthful reporting is optimal when \(\delta(1)>\delta(0)\), false reporting is optimal when \(\delta(1)<\delta(0)\), and reports are immaterial to treatment probability when the coordinates are equal. The task is to return a rule maximizing expected population outcome after these strategic reports; a solution is any maximizing pair \((\delta(1),\delta(0))\). The baseline \(\mathbb E[Y(0)]\) can be included to report the welfare value, but it does not affect the optimizer.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for a large treatment programme with two standardized preference and response types: millions of eligible people, but only two decision-relevant types, with type masses representing population shares. Members of a type share the preference and treatment-response information the rule uses; there are no individual prices, costs, or capacity constraints. Here \(N\) is large and the support size is \(\tau=2\). For rational input, Proposition 1 gives a direct algorithm using arithmetic and sign comparisons, hence polynomial time in the input bit length. That complexity observation follows from the proposition; it is not a complexity theorem stated by the paper. This is a Class A result: the objective’s structure yields an explicit optimum.

The fit to the authors’ problem is unusually direct: the mirror retains their two treatments, strategic preference reports, lotteries, mean-outcome objective, and type-specific treatment effects. It covers Proposition 1’s oracle optimization result only. It does not turn the experimental designs or the sample-to-rule regret bounds into social-choice problems. Further questions include how the optimal rule changes with more than two treatments, and how a finite cohort’s realized welfare compares with the mass model when treatment assignments are randomized.

The main weakness is also substantial: the paper already models a population as a probability space and optimizes expected welfare using the distribution of true types. This mirror may therefore be judged a restatement of the paper’s existing population model, rather than a new continuous analogue. It supplies no discrete hardness result whose complexity might dissolve, either. Its positive case is that the high-multiplicity, finite-support representation is a natural and computationally explicit way to state the authors’ own oracle problem—not that the paper has discovered a new continuum model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject the proposed binary-treatment mirror as a collision. The paper already models its population as a probability space and writes welfare as an expectation over the joint distribution of outcomes and true preferences. The proposed mass vector \((\mu_0,\mu_1)\) is simply the distribution of \(T\) under that probability measure, and its \(\beta_t=\mu_t\tau(t)\) are exactly the aggregate quantities Proposition 1 uses. The proposed rule is therefore the proposition’s own optimization problem in mass notation.

This holds for a finite cohort too. With \(N\) people, give each person mass \(1/N\); then \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), and \(\beta_t\) is the average treatment effect contribution of type \(t\). Proposition 1 applies directly, without a limit or a new high-multiplicity model. Calling its sign comparisons a polynomial-time algorithm is valid, but it does not supply a distinct continuous question: the paper already states and solves that population-level optimization. The large-programme scenario makes the model plausible; it does not make this proposed mirror new.

The statistical regret results do not create a separate population mirror either. Their \(n\) is the number of experimental observations used to estimate the population quantities. Continuizing that axis would concern sampling or estimation, while the population in the paper is already distributional.

The negative case is weaker against every possible extension. The paper itself points to more than two treatments, where the optimal rule is unclear. A computational study of that extension could be worthwhile, and the paper does not classify its complexity. Yet it would enlarge the treatment and incentive problem while retaining the paper’s existing probability-space population; it would not show that this paper’s population needed continuization. So I find the case against the named anchor strong, but I cannot honestly rule out worthwhile computational work on extensions of the paper’s already distribution-based model.
