What the mirror covers

The proposed query would study weighted-majority acceptability; it covers none of the paper's axiomatization and validity results or its noncompactness result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best foothold is the group-preference example. A candidate continuous problem would take a finite set of alternatives \(C\) and a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, given by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and binary-rational masses. Set \(a\prec_\mu b\) when more than half the population ranks \(b\) above \(a\). Given properties \(\psi\) and \(\phi\) of alternatives, ask whether every acceptable alternative among those satisfying \(\psi\) also satisfies \(\phi\); if not, return an acceptable counterexample. This fits a large population with relatively few ranking types, and mirrors the paper’s use of acceptability for a group’s cyclic majority preferences. The paper’s relation is more general, though: it need not come from an electorate or a majority rule.

There is no named computational-complexity result here that can anchor that question. Theorem 50, proved in this paper, says that for ACC semantics over unconstrained preference relations, semantic entailment from any premise set is equivalent to derivability in the paper’s sound and strongly complete axiom system ACC. Theorem 126, also proved here, says ACC semantics on total, anti-symmetric models is not compact; Corollary 127 concludes that no sound and strongly complete axiomatization exists for that case. These are results about axiomatizability and compactness, not whether a computational problem is in P, hard, or parameterized tractable. I would not present them as such. The population query above is a plausible application to investigate, but it is not an honest mirror of a qualifying anchor from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Its main theorems, including Theorem 50, characterize semantic entailment by axiomatization. Theorem 126 and Corollary 127 establish noncompactness and the resulting limit on axiomatizability. None states the complexity of a computational problem, and the paper gives no finite input encoding or algorithmic task whose complexity a population distribution could change.

The best version of the proposed mirror is coherent: represent voters by a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, induce a pairwise majority relation on alternatives, and ask whether every acceptable alternative satisfying \(\psi\) also satisfies \(\phi\). That is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, and Example 18 gives it a foothold in the paper. But the article uses majority preference there to illustrate its semantics; its formal results take the preference relation as primitive and range over general models. Computing this query from \(\mu\) would study weighted-majority aggregation followed by an acceptability query. That could be worthwhile ChoCo research, but it is a new computational problem motivated by an example, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

Theorem 126 offers no better anchor: its noncompactness construction concerns an infinite family of formulas and preference models, not population mass. A finite-alternative society distribution does not supply that structure merely by having fractional masses.

So the negative case is strong against calling this paper a continuous mirror: there is no computational result here to mirror, and the strongest proposed alternative changes the research question. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile project could grow from the paper. Weighted-majority acceptability could be a useful new direction; the paper itself simply does not establish it as a mirror.
