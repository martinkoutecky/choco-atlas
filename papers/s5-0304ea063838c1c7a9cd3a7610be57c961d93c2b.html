What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror; Theorem 3 is the proposed population-limit hook, while Theorem 1, its corollary, Proposition 1, and Theorem 2 are also analytic claims.

The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot identify a named computational result in this paper that qualifies as an anchor. Theorem 1 and its corollary characterize when causal networks imply different forms of opinion independence; Proposition 1 relates opinion independence to independence of correctness events; Theorems 2 and 3 prove jury-theorem claims about how majority correctness changes with group size. They are mathematical results, but none classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithm or computational formulation.

The strongest population-continuum reading is Theorem 3: jurors form a homogeneous population, and as its size grows, the probability of a correct majority converges to the probability that the problem is easy. One could describe a unit mass of jurors and measure the fraction holding the correct opinion in each problem. That is a natural analytic reading of the paper’s limit result. It supplies no computational task, however, and turning it into one would require adding a problem the paper does not study.

So there is no honest per-anchor continuous computational question to hand to a prover under the requested criteria. The paper’s subject is opinion independence and jury-theorem analysis, not computational social choice.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the strongest anchor. The direct continuum lift is simple: represent a large jury by masses \(\mu_t\) of juror types, where a type captures competence and relevant evidence exposure. For a problem \(\pi\), let \(p_t(\pi)\) be type \(t\)’s probability of being correct, and suppose opinions are independent conditional on \(\pi\). The correct-opinion mass then converges to
\[ q(\pi)=\sum_t \mu_t p_t(\pi). \]
With a strict margin from \(1/2\), the majority is correct in the limit exactly when \(q(\pi)>1/2\). Across problems distributed by \(\nu\), the limiting probability of a correct majority is \(\nu(\{\pi:q(\pi)>1/2\})\). The paper’s easy/hard case is recovered when every juror has competence \(p\) on easy problems and \(1-p\) on hard ones: the limit is the mass of easy problems.

That calculation alone is analytic and likely too easy to merit a computational study. The stronger mirror comes from the paper’s explicit suggestion that a social planner can design the environment to induce desired independence. For large pools of fact-checkers, expert panels, or voters with repeated competence and evidence profiles, one could ask for the least-cost transfer of mass among juror types—through training or changes to evidence exposure—that raises majority accuracy above a target across a distribution of problems. This is a population-control question grounded in the paper’s concern with jury accuracy and causal environments. It uses population mass, not fractional outcomes or a noise model. Its computational landscape could be nontrivial once intervention costs and constraints across problem types are specified.

There is a real limit to this case: the paper does not define intervention costs, a feasible design space, or a computational problem, and it has no named complexity result. So this is a plausible extension of its planner-design discussion, not a direct mirror already present in the paper. The proponent is right about that narrow point. But it does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists: Theorem 3 and the planner-design passage together support a credible continuous jury-design question, provided the authors recognize that extension as part of their problem.
