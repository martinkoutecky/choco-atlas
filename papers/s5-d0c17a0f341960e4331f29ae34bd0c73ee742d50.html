The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates, committee size \(k\), fixed lexicographic tie-breaking, a focal voter of type \(\theta^\star=(u^\star,\succ^\star,\lambda^\star)\) and rational mass \(\alpha>0\), and a rational distribution \(\nu_{\theta,A}\) of total mass \(1-\alpha\) over opponents’ types and current ballots, compute a ballot \(B\subseteq C\) maximizing the focal voter’s OWA utility from the committee selected by approval masses \(p_c+\alpha\mathbf{1}[c\in B]\), where \(p_c=\sum_{\theta,A:c\in A}\nu_{\theta,A}\). Return an optimal ballot and its resulting committee.

The model it lives in

A society represented by rational masses of voters with complete preference and utility types and current ballots, together with one focal type of mass \(\alpha>0\) choosing a ballot. Approval scores are masses, the outcome is the top-\(k\) committee with fixed tie-breaking, and the objective is the focal type’s OWA utility. The paper’s result predicts an optimal sincere ballot, found by evaluating the \(m+1\) sincere prefixes.

The objection that survived

The positive-mass focal voter remains an atomic decision maker, so the mirror does not capture a fully atomless setting where an individual has no influence.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s sincere best-response computation; it leaves the paper’s lazy and sincere equilibrium results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

I see one defensible mirror, and it is narrow: the sincere best-response result, rather than the paper’s equilibrium analysis.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that under a non-degenerate best-\(k\) approval rule, for any voter \(i\) and any fixed ballots \(A_{-i}\) of the other voters, there is a sincere ballot that maximizes \(i\)’s utility over the election outcome. The paper then draws out the computational consequence: sincere ballots are just the \(m+1\) preference prefixes, so one can find a best response by checking those instead of all \(2^m\) approval sets. The theorem does not print a complexity-class label, but this is a constructive polynomial-time result. The paper contains no comparable named hardness result, so this is the appropriate algorithmic anchor.

Mass-Sincere-BR. Take \(m\) candidates, a committee size \(k\), AV with fixed lexicographic tie-breaking, and a focal voter of complete type \(\theta^\star=(u^\star,\succ^\star,\lambda^\star)\). The type includes the voter’s cardinal utilities and OWA weights, since the paper’s utility over a committee depends on both. Let the other voters be represented by a rational-mass distribution \(\nu_{\theta,A}\) over intrinsic types \(\theta\) and their current ballots \(A\), with total mass \(1-\alpha\). The focal voter has mass \(\alpha=1/N\), the normalized weight of one voter in a population of scale \(N\).

For each candidate \(c\), let \(p_c=\sum_{\theta,A:c\in A}\nu_{\theta,A}\). If the focal voter chooses ballot \(B\subseteq C\), the approval mass is \(p_c+\alpha\mathbf{1}[c\in B]\). The question is to return a ballot \(B\) maximizing
\[ U_{\theta^\star}(W(B))=\sum_{j=1}^k\lambda^\star_j u^\star(c_j), \]
where \(W(B)\) is the top-\(k\) committee under those masses and the fixed tie-break, and \(c_1\succ^\star\cdots\succ^\star c_k\) are the winners in the focal voter’s order. The solution must include an optimal ballot and its resulting committee; Theorem 3 predicts that an optimal sincere ballot exists. For rational utility data, the direct algorithm evaluates the \(m+1\) sincere prefixes, in time polynomial in \(m\), the joint support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A).

A plausible regime is a large association election: perhaps hundreds of thousands of members, a few dozen candidates, and hundreds or thousands of repeated preference-and-utility types. The intrinsic type must include the shared committee utility as well as preferences; current ballots are actions, so the society input can record how mass of each type is distributed across ballots. The focal voter has one-voter mass \(1/N\). This is a high-multiplicity version of precisely the paper’s complete-information unilateral response problem: the focal voter still chooses an approval ballot, while the opponents’ profile is summarized by approval masses. That makes it plausible the authors would recognize the question as theirs. The extension from counts to masses preserves the score comparisons and the monotonicity behind Theorem 3.

This case covers Theorem 3’s sincere best-response computation. It does not claim to mirror the lazy-PNE non-existence result or the paper’s equilibrium-existence statements; those are different questions, and the paper does not give them as computational complexity results. The mirror raises a natural follow-up: can sincere best responses be assembled into an efficiently computable equilibrium of the mass game? A second question is whether the sincere-best-response guarantee survives ballot-length restrictions.

The weak point is pivotality. For a large \(N\), a voter of mass \(1/N\) changes the committee only when approval masses are close enough at the cutoff; in a strictly atomless model where each individual has exactly zero influence, the response problem collapses. I would therefore defend this as a continuous-background, focal-voter mirror of one named algorithmic result, not as a mirror of the paper’s full strategic equilibrium analysis. The finite mass \(\alpha\) is also what keeps the paper’s unilateral decision recognizable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that Theorem 3 is not a computational-complexity result. It proves that a sincere best response exists and observes that one can find one by checking the \(m+1\) sincere ballots. The paper does not formulate a computational problem, specify an input encoding, or give a complexity classification. Turning that observation into “compute a best response from rational approval masses” is a plausible new algorithmic question, but it is a computationalization of a game-theoretic lemma, not a mirror of a named computational result of the kind ChoCo is charting.

The continuum creates a further mismatch. If voters are infinitesimal, one voter’s ballot cannot change the approval masses, so the paper’s unilateral pivotality disappears. The proposed focal-voter model keeps a finite mass \(\alpha=1/N\) precisely to preserve it. That leaves a distinguished individual acting against a continuous background; it does not make the strategic actor part of the population-scale object. Giving a positive mass cohort a joint ballot choice avoids that issue, but changes the deviation from one voter’s best response to a coordinated group action.

This is a real objection to the anchor, but it is not airtight. The programme allows societies with rational masses, and a focal mass of \(1/N\) fits that representation. The paper also explicitly presents Theorem 3 as an efficient way to find a sincere best response. A reader could reasonably accept the proposed focal-voter problem as a narrow, recognizable mirror. So the negative case is weakest exactly there: it can argue that the anchor is not a named complexity result and that the faithful atomless limit loses pivotality, but it cannot rule out the hybrid formulation without narrowing what counts as a continuous society.
