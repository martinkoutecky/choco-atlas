The negative case is strongest at the anchor stage: this paper has no computational result to continuize. So there is no named computational question here whose continuous version could fill ChoCo’s gap.

There is also a deeper obstacle to making the axiomatic results into mirrors: their input is already effectively a continuous society. The paper allows arbitrary positive vote weights and treats scale as irrelevant. For party \(j\), normalize its weight to \(\mu_j=v_j/\sum_i v_i\); these are masses of the party-support types. The rules already map such weight vectors to integer seat allocations, and exactness explicitly studies limits of input vectors. Calling the weights population masses would largely rename the existing model. The integer seat output is intrinsic to apportionment, but that does not create a new continuous-population question.

The best new candidate is the proponent’s vote-transfer idea: move mass between party-support types at a specified cost, and minimize the transfer needed to reach a target allocation. That is a plausible high-multiplicity control problem. But it adds the intervention objective, cost model, and choice of apportionment rule; none is studied in this note. The paper’s abstract rules can even be arbitrary set-valued mappings, with no finite algorithmic representation to analyze. A transfer problem could be worthwhile as a separate project, but it does not mirror any result here.

So the negative case is strong against a *continuous mirror of this paper*, though weaker against new research inspired by apportionment. The paper already accepts continuous vote weights, and its results are axiomatic rather than computational; any computational project would need to supply its central question from outside the paper.
