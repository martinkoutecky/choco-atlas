The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of schools, rational per-capita capacities, and a finite menu of types specifying complete school rankings and priority tiers, does there exist a rational finite-support society distribution \(\mu\) over those types and a pure or mixed acceptance equilibrium in the truthful-report, two-stage fluid deferred-acceptance system such that almost every student weakly prefers their final assignment to their one-stage fluid deferred-acceptance assignment, with a positive mass strictly preferring it? Keep the two stages finite; in each stage, priority tiers fill capacity in order, splitting a boundary tier proportionally, while first-stage acceptances retain their seats and remaining mass enters the second stage under residual capacities.

The model it lives in

Types carry a complete ranking and priority tier at each school; a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) is the society, and type-level acceptance fractions determine the two fluid deferred-acceptance runs. The decision is whether some such \(\mu\) and equilibrium fractions produce a Pareto improvement over one-stage fluid deferred acceptance.

The objection that survived

The proposed problem selects \(\mu\) as part of a certificate instead of taking a society distribution as input, making it a population-synthesis question rather than computation on a supplied society.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The question mirrors Theorem 2(ii)’s constructive improvement-existence result; it does not establish the full acyclicity characterization in Theorem 2(i) or cover the strategic-reporting SPNE results in Theorems 3–4.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do mass-based analogues of the capacity-priority cycles characterize when a strict welfare improvement is possible?
  • Can rational society and equilibrium certificates be bounded and decided efficiently in the school count, type support, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive hook is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that when students report preferences truthfully and are strategic only about accepting or rejecting first-stage offers: (i) if the capacity-priority profile is acyclic, every strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium and produces the one-stage DA outcome; and (ii) if the profile has a cycle, there is a preference profile with a two-stage Nash equilibrium whose outcome Pareto dominates the one-stage outcome. The cycle is specific: there are schools \(c,c'\) and students \(s,s',s''\) with \(s \succ_c s' \succ_c s'' \succ_{c'} s\), plus disjoint sets of other students above \(s'\) at \(c\) and above \(s\) at \(c'\), of sizes \(q_c-1\) and \(q_{c'}-1\). Part (ii) is established by a construction.

There is a qualification about the anchor: the paper has no named computational-complexity result—no theorem classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized. Theorem 2 is a structural equilibrium-welfare characterization. I would use it as the best modeling hook for a new computational question, not present it as a complexity result the authors already proved.

A plausible regime is a large school district enrolling a very large cohort through a fixed set of schools. The agents are students; a type records a complete ranking of schools and the outside option, plus the student’s priority tier at every school. Students of the same type have the same preferences and priorities in every respect the model uses. The regime is persuasive only where the number \(n\) of students is far larger than the number \(\tau\) of supported types. School capacities scale with population, so the input gives each school a rational capacity per unit of population. This retains the paper’s central issue—how priority structure shapes strategic acceptance and welfare—while treating tied members of a priority tier proportionally when a capacity cuts through it.

I would ask the following as the lead mirror:

Fluid Two-Stage Improvement Existence. The input is a finite set of schools, rational per-capita capacities, and a finite menu of preference-and-priority-tier types with a priority order over tiers at each school. Is there a rational finite-support society over those types and a pure or mixed acceptance strategy profile that is a nonatomic Nash equilibrium of the truthful-report, two-stage fluid DA system, with a final allocation that gives almost every student a weakly preferred school than one-stage fluid DA and gives a positive mass a strictly preferred school? A solution is a society distribution, equilibrium acceptance fractions, and the resulting stage allocations that certify “yes”; the question is whether such a certificate exists.

In each stage, type mass applies down its ranking as in DA; schools retain higher-priority mass up to capacity, splitting a boundary tier proportionally if needed. First-stage acceptances keep their seats. Rejected and unmatched mass enters the second stage under the residual capacities. In a nonatomic equilibrium, each individual’s accept-or-reject choice is a best response holding aggregate behavior fixed. The quantity being decided is the possibility of a Pareto improvement; there is no cost-minimization objective in Theorem 2 to preserve.

My initial expectation is Class A, at least with a finite tier system: fluid DA consists of priority ordering and capacity cuts, and the finite theorem’s cycle witness suggests a structural characterization may survive. That is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 2. The new questions are what replaces its integer \(q_c-1\) thresholds with masses, whether an exact cycle criterion exists, and whether the decision algorithm is polynomial in the school count, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\).

The weakest point is that nonatomicization changes the strategic problem. A single student’s action no longer changes aggregate admissions, while the paper’s finite equilibrium constructions depend on individual students affecting who reaches the next stage. Tiered priorities and proportional treatment at a boundary also change the strict-priority setting used in the theorem. So this is a recognizable continuous-population version of the paper’s welfare question, but the answer may reveal that its key result relies on atomic agents. The mirror covers only Theorem 2’s truthful-report acceptance game; it does not claim to continuize the strategic-reporting SPNE results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the only plausible anchor, but it gives a structural equilibrium-welfare characterization, not a computational result. For a fixed finite capacity-priority profile, it characterizes when some preference profile admits an improving equilibrium. The proposed mirror makes the society itself part of the witness: the input is a type menu and capacities, and the question is whether some rational population and equilibrium can be constructed. That is a synthesis question about which population to create, not an algorithmic question on a society supplied as an instance.

One could repair this by supplying a fixed distribution \(\mu\) and asking whether a Pareto-improving equilibrium exists, or how to compute one. That would be a well-posed computational question, but it adds an algorithmic problem the paper never formulates. The paper compares equilibrium outcomes; it does not ask for an equilibrium-computation procedure or study its complexity. Whether that extension is one the authors would recognize as their problem is the main uncertainty in the negative case.

There is also a real change in the strategic mechanism. The finite constructions use students’ accept-or-reject decisions to alter the remaining capacities and who participates in the next DA run. In a nonatomic model, an individual deviation leaves those aggregate capacities and admissions unchanged. A positive-mass group can still reject, but a group-wide change is not a unilateral deviation in nonatomic Nash equilibrium; its mass must arise from individual best responses to the aggregate outcome. That makes the equilibrium question different from the pivotal acceptance game in Theorem 2.

This is not a decisive objection. A continuous model can represent the relevant priority groups as types, scale capacities with population, and allow fractional acceptance at indifference. A finite cycle pattern might then have a faithful mass counterpart. So the strongest case against is that the proposed anchor does not yet yield a computational problem on a given society, and its repaired version changes the strategic question. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror: the fixed-society equilibrium-computation problem is plausible, and the population-limit model may preserve the welfare phenomenon. The negative case is therefore weak at its most important point.
