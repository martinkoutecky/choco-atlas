The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many applicant types with rational masses summing to one, strict rankings over a finite contract menu, and specified hospital mass-choice rules satisfying mass analogues of IRC and HK, does every finite terminating schedule yield the same terminal contract-mass vector? A schedule selects positive measurable submasses of eligible types to make their most-preferred fresh acceptable offers; if outcomes differ, provide two finite schedules witnessing the difference.

The model it lives in

An atomless cohort of applicants partitioned into finitely many complete types \(p\), with masses \(\mu_p\). Each applicant receives at most one contract. Hospitals use specified rational piecewise-linear, type-anonymous mass-choice rules to map cumulative offer-mass vectors to retained contract-mass vectors, with feasible selections that respect each type’s available mass. The decision is the schedule of submasses making offers; there is no optimization objective.

The objection that survived

The mass-choice rule class and its encoding are newly stipulated, so the paper gives no basis for a complexity claim over those rules.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s outcome-independence claim for finite schedules. It leaves Proposition 1’s equivalence of choice conditions and other finite-agent results unchanged; the countably infinite schedule extension would continuize time and is outside this population mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which rational piecewise-linear mass-choice rules do mass-level IRC and HK suffice for finite-schedule outcome independence?
  • Can the common terminal contract-mass vector be computed efficiently from a specified encoding of the mass-choice rules?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is about the paper’s central order-independence result, though there is a qualification: the paper has no numbered result classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its closest named computational anchor is Theorem 1, an algorithmic result rather than a complexity classification.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that for fixed preferences and hospital choice functions satisfying the HK condition—with IRC assumed throughout—the outcome of the generalized cumulative offer process is independent of which eligible doctors make offers in each round. Thus every GCOP schedule, including single-offer and simultaneous-offer schedules, produces the same set of contracts reserved by hospitals.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large placement market: for example, a cohort of many medical residents applying to a relatively small set of hospital systems. There might be tens of thousands of applicants but only dozens of distinct types. A type would specify a complete ranking of the available hospital-contract packages and every applicant characteristic used by hospitals’ choice rules. Its mass \(\mu_p\) is the fraction of the cohort with that full description. Applicants within a type are interchangeable for the model’s purposes. This makes the population continuous; proposal rounds remain discrete.

My lead mirror is Mass-GCOP Outcome Independence. An instance has a finite set of applicant types \(P\), a finite contract menu \(X\), rational type masses \(\mu_p\) summing to one, and strict rankings for each type. Each hospital has a type-anonymous mass-choice rule: given the cumulative masses of offers it has received, the rule specifies the masses of contracts it retains. Specify the rules by rational piecewise-linear maps, with compatible selections of actual submasses so that no applicant is held under two contracts. Require mass versions of IRC and HK: wholly rejected new offer mass leaves existing choices unchanged; and adding offer mass from one type cannot cause a previously unchosen, distinct type to become chosen when both types were unchosen before.

In each round, the schedule chooses any positive measurable mass of currently eligible applicants, and each chosen applicant makes their most-preferred fresh acceptable offer. Hospitals reconsider cumulative offers, and the process ends when no unheld applicant mass has a fresh acceptable contract. The problem is: do all finite terminating schedules yield the same terminal contract-mass vector? Return that vector if they do; otherwise, return two schedules with different vectors. The action is the mass of each eligible cohort making its next offer. There is no optimization objective: the question is whether scheduling choices affect the resulting allocation.

I expect the invariant outcome, making this a Class-A-style structural extension: it is the population-mass analogue of the paper’s central theorem, and finite replications provide a plausible route to it when the mass-choice rules behave continuously. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a complexity result established by this paper. Further questions include whether countably infinite fair schedules also converge to the same allocation, and whether the common outcome can be computed efficiently from the rational mass-choice maps.

The authors would likely recognize this as a mirror of their problem: their contribution is precisely that arbitrary proposal groupings and asynchronous processing do not change the outcome. This proposal changes the population representation while keeping the matching, contracts, hospital choices, and schedule question. It does not continuize time, outcomes, or incentives.

The weak point is substantive: the paper’s proof works with finitely many named doctors and offers, while this mirror needs hospital choice rules over divisible masses. The theorem does not automatically extend to those rules, and splitting a type’s mass can produce schedules with infinitely many rounds. The paper also supplies no hardness anchor, so this case supports a continuous research question about the algorithm’s outcome, not a claim that the paper identifies a complexity boundary.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the one serious anchor: it proves that the paper’s generalized cumulative offer process has the same outcome under every permitted schedule. But it is an outcome-independence theorem, not a complexity result. The paper gives no running-time classification, optimization problem, or algorithmic boundary for ChoCo to map across. A continuous version could extend the theorem, but that would not by itself fill the programme’s stated gap in complexity and algorithms.

The strongest version of the mirror is a large placement market with finitely many applicant types, rational masses, and hospital capacities that scale with the cohort. Replace each hospital’s choice function with an anonymous rule selecting masses of contracts, and ask whether every finite schedule produces the same terminal contract-mass vector. This is a sensible population-continuous question; the proposed resident cohort is plausible, and aggregating identical applicants does not lose information the model uses.

The problem is that this mirror needs a new model at its center. The paper’s HK condition is defined for choices from sets of contracts involving named doctors; it supplies no canonical rule for selecting fractional masses. If the mass-choice rules are restricted by direct analogues of IRC and HK, the project becomes a structural extension of those conditions and the finite proof’s schedule-reordering argument. Any genuinely new challenge would likely be about how fractional choices and their limits behave, rather than the computational landscape ChoCo targets. If the rules are left broad enough for schedules to produce different outcomes, that variation may instead reflect the chosen mass-choice model. Asking how efficiently to compute the common outcome does not resolve this gap: the paper supplies no class of mass-choice rules or input encoding on which to base a complexity question. The proposed rational piecewise-linear maps are a start, but different choices of map class could make the maps, rather than the offer process, determine the computational problem.

The additional question about countably infinite fair schedules changes the time axis as well as the population, so it is outside the programme’s stated scope. And, as framed, it asks about convergence, not a computational classification.

This is a case against spending ChoCo effort on *this paper*, not a proof that no valuable mirror exists. The finite type space and large-cohort placement scenario make population continuization credible. The negative case is weaker precisely there: a well-specified mass-choice model could support a worthwhile theorem. But the paper itself does not provide the complexity anchor or the mass-choice structure needed to make that a clear fit for this programme.
