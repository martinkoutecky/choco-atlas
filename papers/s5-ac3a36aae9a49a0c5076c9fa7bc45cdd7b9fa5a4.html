The continuous mirror question

Given \(K\) offices with disjoint nonempty candidate sets \(A_1,\ldots,A_K\), and a finite support of approval types \(t\subseteq A\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), does there exist an allocation \(X=\{x_j\}_{j=1}^K\), with \(x_j\in A_j\), such that for every \(z\) with \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t z_t\ge 1/K\), if \(\left(\bigcap_{t:z_t>0}t\right)\cap A_j\ne\varnothing\) for some office \(j\), then \(X\cap\left(\bigcup_{t:z_t>0}t\right)\ne\varnothing\)?

The model it lives in

A society is a finite support of approval types with rational masses; binary decision variables \(x_{j,a}\in\{0,1\}\) choose one candidate per office, and the resulting integral allocation must satisfy GJR for every coalition submass of at least \(1/K\).

The objection that survived

The paper’s GJR guarantee is false even for a finite electorate: two disjoint groups of size \(n/K\) can require different candidates for the same office, so no allocation satisfies the axiom.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only the GJR allocation question anchored by Proposition 1; it does not cover the GPJR, partial representation, unequal-power, GreedyPAV simulation, or replacement results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize feasibility and determine the complexity of continuous GJR allocation as a function of \(K\), the number of candidates, support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\).
  • Identify restrictions on approval types or office candidate sets under which every society admits a GJR allocation and an efficient construction.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest clean anchor is Proposition 1 in Section 5.1, proved in this paper. It says that an office allocation satisfying Global Justified Representation (GJR) always exists and can be computed in polynomial time. Specifically, for every group \(V'\) of at least \(n/k\) voters and every office \(j\), if the group unanimously approves some candidate for office \(j\), then the allocation must include some candidate approved by at least one voter in \(V'\). The selected candidate need not be for office \(j\). The paper’s named results do not offer a hardness anchor, so this is the positive algorithmic result I would build on.

Consider a large political party choosing ministers for the offices it received in coalition negotiations. It might have hundreds of thousands of members but far fewer distinct approval ballots: members in the same type have exactly the same approvals over the candidates for every office. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for this paper’s own setting: \(n\) is large, while the support size \(\tau\) of recurring ballot types is moderate. Each type’s mass is its fraction of the party membership.

Here is the mirror I would give a prover: Continuous-GJR Allocation. An instance has \(K\) offices with nonempty, disjoint candidate sets \(A_1,\ldots,A_K\), and a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of approval types \(t\subseteq A=\bigcup_j A_j\). Each type has a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t=1\). A coalition of the continuum is represented by masses \(z_t\in[0,\mu_t]\); its total mass is \(\sum_t z_t\), and its participating types are \(\mathcal T_z=\{t:z_t>0\}\). The question is to find one candidate \(x_j\in A_j\) for each office, forming \(X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_K\}\), such that for every \(z\) of total mass at least \(1/K\), and every office \(j\),
\[ \left(\bigcap_{t\in\mathcal T_z}t\right)\cap A_j\ne\varnothing \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X\cap\left(\bigcup_{t\in\mathcal T_z}t\right)\ne\varnothing. \]
The solution is the integral office allocation \(X\); the population is what becomes continuous.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. The paper’s greedy proof translates directly: compute each candidate’s approval mass among the remaining types; while some candidate has mass at least \(1/K\), elect one to its office and remove the mass of types approving that candidate. Fill any remaining offices arbitrarily. If a coalition of mass at least \(1/K\) remains unsatisfied at termination, none of its mass was removed by a winner it approves. Its common approved candidate therefore still has approval mass at least \(1/K\), contradicting termination. The computation is polynomial in \(K\), the number of candidates, \(\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\).

This is a faithful mirror: it preserves the paper’s ballots, office constraints, GJR condition, and integral output. It does not continuize the outcomes. For a finite electorate with type counts \(n_t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) gives the same GJR condition: any coalition’s approval union and common approvals depend on its participating types, and a discrete coalition of those types can include all their voters. The continuous encoding can therefore represent a high-multiplicity electorate compactly and let the algorithm run in terms of \(\tau\), rather than listing all \(n\) voters.

I would keep the scope to Proposition 1. I would not use the simulation evidence for GreedyPAV or stretch this case to the paper’s stronger axioms, unequal-power setting, or replacement procedure. Further questions include whether the paper’s GPJR result admits an FPT mirror parameterized by \(\tau\), and how far the same type-mass approach extends to offices with unequal power.

The soft spot is that this is a modest continuation: the weighted greedy algorithm is an almost direct translation of the paper’s finite proof, and GJR is a relatively weak proportionality guarantee. It does not show that continuization dissolves hardness or creates a new algorithmic boundary. Still, the mirror is the same computational task in a credible large-membership regime, stated with a support-based input and a precise mass interpretation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the right anchor to challenge, but its claimed guarantee fails even before continuization. Let \(K=2\), \(A_1=\{a,q\}\), \(A_2=\{b\}\), and let half the population approve only \(a\), while the other half approve only \(q\). Each half is a cohesive group of mass \(1/K\), with a common approved candidate for office 1. GJR therefore requires the allocation to include both \(a\) and \(q\), since each group approves no other candidate. But the model permits only one winner for office 1, and \(b\) represents neither group. No valid allocation exists.

The proposed continuous greedy algorithm fails on the same instance. It can select \(a\), filling office 1, after which the \(q\)-approving mass remains unrepresented; filling office 2 arbitrarily cannot help. The obstacle is office capacity, which Proposition 1’s proof overlooks when it argues that disjoint groups can be handled in at most \(K\) iterations.

One can repair the proposed mirror by asking whether a GJR allocation exists for a given type distribution, rather than promising one always exists. But that is a feasibility problem correcting the paper’s finite axiom result; its obstruction is already present with two ordinary voter types and does not arise from continuization. Making the problem always feasible would require revising the axiom or adding restrictions on ballots, which would move away from the result the proponent anchored on.

That defeats the claimed Continuous-GJR construction as stated. It does not support the broader claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario: a large party with many members and repeated approval ballots is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and a corrected computational question might still be worth studying. The honest negative case is therefore narrow: reject this anchor as a valid transferred guarantee, but the material here does not establish the universal no-mirror claim.
