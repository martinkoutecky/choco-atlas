What the mirror covers

The population reading captures the expected-distance asymptotics in Theorems 1 and 2 and Corollaries 1 and 2; it yields no computational classification.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is a natural population model already visible in the paper’s limit. A type is a complete ranking \(t\in\mathcal P\), and its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of society holding that ranking. For a fixed ranking \(\sigma\), the population’s average distance to it is
\[ D_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal P}\mu_t d(t,\sigma). \]
The paper’s asymptotic analysis uses exactly this quantity: as the electorate grows, expected average distance to a constant rule converges to \(D_\mu(\sigma)\).

The closest named anchor is Theorem 2, proved here. For a fixed set of alternatives and independent voter preferences whose average distribution is \(\mu\), it says that the expected-distance gap between any sequence of anonymous social choice rules and a constant rule tends to zero exactly when the minimum and maximum of \(D_\mu(\sigma)\) over rankings \(\sigma\) are equal. This gives a credible population-level reading of the paper’s indifference result. It covers the asymptotic expected-distance claim; it does not give a computational algorithm for choosing or evaluating a social choice rule.

That limitation is decisive under the requested anchor criterion. Theorem 2, Theorem 1, and Corollaries 1 and 2 are asymptotic and analytic results, not numbered results asserting a complexity classification or an algorithmic bound. The paper mentions the NP-hardness of computing a Kemeny–Young ranking, but cites that result from elsewhere; it is not a numbered result established here. So there are no qualifying computational anchors, and therefore no per-anchor continuous computational questions I can honestly put forward. Turning the condition \(\min_\sigma D_\mu(\sigma)=\max_\sigma D_\mu(\sigma)\) into a decision problem would be a new computationalization of the theorem, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper.

The weak point is also the reason this foothold does not carry a ChoCo case: the paper already studies large-society limits of expected distances, but its contribution is not a computational question over a society supplied as a mass distribution. Its continuous-population interpretation is plausible; a continuous computational mirror anchored in the paper’s named results is not.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the proponent’s only anchor, and it is not a computational result: it characterizes when asymptotic expected distances coincide. The paper gives no algorithmic bound or complexity classification for that claim. A continuous population is a natural reading of its limit, but that alone does not supply the missing computational question.

The most faithful computational version would take a supported distribution \(\mu\) over rankings and decide whether
\[ \Delta_d(\mu)=\max_{\sigma}\sum_t\mu_t d(t,\sigma)-\min_{\sigma}\sum_t\mu_t d(t,\sigma) \]
is zero. For the paper’s highlighted distances, that test has a simple form. With Kendall distance, swapping adjacent alternatives \(a,b\) changes the expected distance by their pairwise margin. Thus \(\Delta_d(\mu)=0\) exactly when every pairwise margin is zero, checkable from the \(\tau\) supported rankings. For the rank-difference distances in Corollary 2, define \(g_a(k)=\sum_t\mu_t f(r_t(a)-k)\); constancy reduces to checking adjacent-swap equalities among the \(g_a(k)\). That too is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). Under the paper’s uniformity assumptions, the condition already follows from the theorem’s hypotheses. These are useful checks, but they do not yield the complexity landscape the programme is meant to chart.

One can make the question harder by asking for the numerical extrema rather than whether they coincide. For Kendall distance, that becomes weighted Kemeny optimization; for the rank-separable examples, it becomes an assignment-style optimization. Those are legitimate population problems, but they ask for an optimal consensus ranking, whereas the paper’s result concerns indifference among arbitrary anonymous rules. They are a separate computational project, not a computational mirror supplied by Theorem 2. The fact that ranking types can have high multiplicity makes such a project sensible; it does not turn it into a result of this paper.

So the proponent has identified a plausible continuous interpretation, but no surviving computational anchor. The negative case is strongest against a mirror of this paper’s results. It is weaker as a universal claim: someone could independently argue that the weighted optimization problems deserve ChoCo’s attention. That would need its own case, rather than being carried by this paper’s indifference theorem.
