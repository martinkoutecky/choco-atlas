The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a research opportunity, not a computational result established by this paper. The paper is unusually close to ChoCo’s population dimension: its economies vary in population, and it studies how incumbents’ welfare changes when new agents enter. But the paper does not study complexity.

The nearest result is Theorem 1(a), proved in this paper. In the single-task case, among Groves mechanisms that satisfy population monotonicity and individual rationality, a mechanism minimizes the deficit in every economy if and only if, for each agent \(i\), its Groves function is
\[ h_i(c_{-i})=(c_{-i}^{\alpha})_{[n-1]}, \]
the highest task cost among the other \(n-1\) agents. This is a useful characterization, but it does not assert that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. There are no named computational-complexity results in the paper that qualify as anchors under your criteria. Theorem 1 is not an in-P result merely because its formula can be evaluated by sorting.

A plausible regime for a follow-on mirror is a large agency assigning recurring packages of indivisible duties across many households. A type would be a complete cost function over task bundles; agents of the same type would have identical costs and other mechanism-relevant attributes. The society would be a rational mass distribution over a small support of types, with total population \(M\) much larger than the number of types \(\tau\). The recurring task supply would scale with \(M\), so aggregate assignments can be fractional by type while every individual still receives an integral bundle.

One concrete question to hand a prover would be: given a finite task set, a finite set of cost-function types, rational type masses, and a per-capita task supply, compute the minimum per-capita deficit of an assignment-efficient, dominant-strategy truthful mechanism that is population monotonic and individually rational. A solution would be a mechanism defined over the supported type distributions, assigning each individual an integral bundle and transfer, with incumbents’ utilities weakly increasing under every population extension. This is recognizable as an extension of the paper’s mechanism-design problem. My tentative expectation is Class A in a finite type and task setting, because aggregate allocation variables and efficiency constraints are linear; the hard part would be representing truthfulness and population monotonicity across all distributions. This is a conjecture for the proposed extension, not a consequence of Theorem 1(a).

The weak point is substantial: with a fixed finite supply—especially the paper’s single indivisible task—an atomless population makes the recipient a measure-zero individual. Scaling task supply with population avoids that degeneracy, but changes the single-task problem Theorem 1(a) characterizes. So the best positive claim is that the paper supplies a compelling *population-varying foundation* for a continuous research question. It does not supply a qualifying computational anchor, and the proposed mirror needs a co-modification that the authors might or might not accept.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest-looking anchor, Theorem 1(a), is not a computational result. It characterizes the Groves functions that minimize deficit subject to population monotonicity and individual rationality in a single-task model. The paper gives no computational problem, input encoding, or complexity claim to mirror. Computing the displayed order statistic may be easy, but that is not the objection: the theorem’s contribution is an axiomatic characterization, not an algorithmic one.

The proposed continuous version has to supply the missing computational problem. With one fixed indivisible task, a society described only by type masses does not say which individual receives it: the recipient has measure zero. A model could retain a tagged recipient, but then that feature is outside the mass distribution. Alternatively, scaling task supply with population avoids the issue, but changes the theorem’s single-task setting into a repeated or multiple-task problem. The paper does not characterize the proposed minimum-deficit mechanism there; it notes that the transfer functions become more complicated. That may be a worthwhile new project, but it is not a computational mirror of Theorem 1(a).

The proponent’s finite-type formulation also leaves the central algorithmic burden open. Truthfulness and population monotonicity constrain a mechanism across reports and population extensions, not just at the given distribution. Linear aggregate allocation constraints do not by themselves show that those mechanism-wide conditions have a finite representation or can be optimized efficiently. Restricting possible reports to a finite type set may help, but that is an additional domain choice, not a computational result supplied by this paper.

So the best negative case is about fit: this paper supplies no named computational anchor, and the proposed repair either loses the single-task recipient in the aggregate or changes the model enough to pose a new problem. That defeats the case for treating this paper as a strong ChoCo mirror. It does not establish that no continuous mechanism-design question inspired by the paper is worth pursuing; that universal claim would be stronger than the evidence allows.
