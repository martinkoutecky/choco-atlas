The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) items, \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), nondecreasing symmetric submodular valuations \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), and nondecreasing rational piecewise-linear item cost curves \(c_j:[0,1]\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\) with \(c_j(0)=0\) and a supplied \(\alpha\) satisfying \(\alpha c_j(x)/x\ge c_j(y)/y\) for all \(0<x\le y\le1\), can a polynomial-time direct mechanism return masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) of type \(t\) receiving whole bundle \(S\subseteq M\), and per-capita payments, such that \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), individual rationality and no positive transfers hold, no positive-mass coalition can make every member strictly better off by misreporting, total payments lie between \(\sum_j c_j(q_j)\) and \(\alpha\sum_j c_j(q_j)\), and social cost is at most \(2\alpha^3H_\tau\) times the minimum feasible social cost, where \(q_j=\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\)?

The model it lives in

A finite-support population with type masses \(\mu_t\), each unit of mass receiving an integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\), and allocation variables \(x_{t,S}\). Item \(j\) costs \(c_j(q_j)\), where \(q_j\) is its served mass; the objective is \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}(v_t(M)-v_t(S))+\sum_jc_j(q_j)\). The computational task is to construct a direct mechanism and per-capita payments meeting the stated incentive, budget-balance, and approximation guarantees.

The objection that survived

The paper permits costs that distinguish which players are served, while the proposed cost curves depend only on total served mass; the case does not establish that this anonymous regime captures those identity-sensitive instances.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.4 for symmetric submodular valuations and separable, anonymous per-capita item costs. It leaves out identity-dependent cost functions, the general-valuation results of Section 4, and non-separable costs.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the sequential mechanism and its incentive proof be adapted to mass allocations with deviations by positive-mass coalitions, with the approximation depending on \(\tau\) rather than headcount?
  • Can the model retain costs that depend on the served mass of each type, rather than only total served mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is Theorem 3.4’s iterative ascending cost-sharing mechanism. The paper has no numbered hardness result to anchor here; its useful anchor is an algorithmic one.

Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper, says that when valuations are symmetric submodular and nondecreasing, and each item’s nondecreasing cost function is \(\alpha\)-average decreasing, IACSM runs in polynomial time, satisfies individual rationality, no positive transfers, and weak group-strategyproofness, is \(\alpha\)-budget-balanced, and gives a \(2\alpha^3 H_n\)-approximation to social cost. Here, \(\alpha\)-average decreasing means that for \(S\subseteq T\), \(\alpha c(S)/|S|\ge c(T)/|T|\). The result is a good anchor because it states an algorithm and its guarantees for the underlying mechanism problem, rather than merely supplying an encoding.

A credible regime is a large population using several shared services, such as a city’s sensor or connectivity services. Residents fall into a relatively small set of valuation types: each type has the same value for every bundle of services and the same diminishing returns. The number of residents can be very large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct types stays small. Service costs depend on the fraction served; the costs and values are normalized per capita so that the model remains meaningful as the population grows. This captures the paper’s multi-item cost-sharing setting and its social-cost objective, rather than making continuity in the outcome space the point.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Type-Mass IACSM. An instance has \(m\) items, a finite type set \(\mathcal T\) of size \(\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to one, and for each type \(t\), a nondecreasing symmetric submodular valuation \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\). Each item \(j\) has a nondecreasing rational piecewise-linear cost curve \(c_j:[0,1]\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), with \(c_j(0)=0\), and a supplied bound \(\alpha\) such that
\[ \alpha\,\frac{c_j(x)}{x}\ \ge\ \frac{c_j(y)}{y} \qquad\text{for all }0<x\le y\le1. \]

A feasible allocation is a collection of masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the *whole bundle* \(S\subseteq M\), and \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\). Thus the type-level allocation can be fractional, while every unit of population receives an integral bundle. Item \(j\) is served to mass \(q_j=\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\). The social cost is
\[ \pi(x)=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\bigl(v_t(M)-v_t(S)\bigr)+\sum_j c_j(q_j). \]

The problem is to give a polynomial-time direct mechanism, in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length, for every finite-support report profile. It must return such an allocation and per-capita payments, satisfy IR and no positive transfers, and be weakly group-strategyproof against deviations by any positive-mass coalition. Its total payments must lie between \(\sum_j c_j(q_j)\) and \(\alpha\sum_j c_j(q_j)\). Its social cost must be at most \(2\alpha^3H_\tau\) times the minimum feasible social cost. A solution is a finite algorithm for computing the outcome and payments, together with proofs of those guarantees. The \(H_\tau\) target is deliberate: it asks whether the approximation can depend on the number of types rather than the headcount.

I expect this restricted problem to be Class A. The mechanism’s cost shares are based on average cost along a sequence of shrinking served populations, and that quantity has a direct mass-based counterpart. With few types, the mechanism could process type cohorts rather than individual residents. The resulting theorem would be recognizable to these authors: it keeps their services, valuations, costs, incentives, and social-cost measure, while asking for the high-multiplicity algorithmic version.

The main weakness is real: Theorem 3.4 does not imply the \(H_\tau\) bound. Its mechanism removes players one at a time, and its harmonic analysis is indexed by \(n\). In a continuum, there is no next individual to remove; splitting a type’s mass can affect both the trace argument and group-strategyproofness. So the proposed problem is a genuine new theorem, not a limit argument. That difficulty is also where the computational question lies. The mirror covers Theorem 3.4’s restricted separable-cost, symmetric-submodular setting; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s general-valuation or non-separable results. Further questions are whether the type-support bound holds, whether the method extends to type-specific costs, and where it fails when average cost is not controlled.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that it changes a consequential part of the paper’s model. Theorem 3.4 allows each item’s cost to depend on the *set* of people served. The proposed cost curve \(c_j(q_j)\) depends only on the total mass served, so it removes cost differences tied to who receives the service. That is natural for some shared services, but it leaves out cost structures such as Set Cover and Vertex Cover, where users’ identities encode the combinatorial instance.

There is a harder version: let costs depend on the vector of served masses across types, or on the support of the served types. That retains more of the original combinatorial structure. But it also exposes the main gap in the proposed mirror. The paper’s proof processes named players one at a time, and its incentive argument relies on that order and on each player’s bundle. In a mass model, the authors would need to define how type mass is split and processed, what deviations by a positive-mass coalition mean, and how payments and group-strategyproofness work for those allocations. The \(H_\tau\) target is a new guarantee, not a consequence of the paper’s \(H_n\) analysis.

That is a real research risk: the attractive type-mass model may be a new mechanism-design problem rather than a direct high-multiplicity version of the theorem. Still, it does not defeat the anchor. Theorem 3.4 is a named polynomial-time result, and large populations of users with a small number of valuation types are credible. The programme permits changing costs when population continuation requires it, and the proof difficulty is exactly the sort of algorithmic question the programme seeks. I cannot make an honest case that no worthwhile mirror exists; the best objection is that the proposed mirror needs sharper modeling and a genuinely new incentive proof.
