The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-based positive case under your rules. The paper has no named computational result classifying a problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered Results 1–6 are empirical findings; Propositions B.1–B.4 describe Bayesian and DeGroot learning dynamics, not the complexity of a computational problem. So there are no eligible anchors, and no honest complexity class to predict for a mirror tied to one.

There is a plausible follow-on question, though it is not an anchor from this paper. Imagine a large forecasting organization with a star communication structure. A type records an agent’s private information, confidence calibration, and updating behavior; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of agents of type \(t\). One coordinator observes the population’s reports, while each other agent observes only the coordinator’s reports. Given a prior over the true state, \(\mu\), a fixed set of leader-selection procedures, and the type-specific update rules under each announced procedure, ask which procedure minimizes expected final aggregate error, \(\mathbb{E}[|\sum_t \mu_t x_t^{(R)}-\theta|]\). The solution is the best procedure and its induced final reports. A large forecasting team is a more credible regime for this model than the paper’s four-person experimental groups.

The paper gives this extension a recognizable mechanism: Result 2 reports that selecting the most accurate leader does not improve performance over random selection, while selecting the most confident leader worsens it. Result 4 reports that publicly declaring the leader’s accuracy or confidence increases followers’ weight on that leader’s opinion. Those findings motivate a population-scale model of selection and influence, but they do not establish the complexity of the proposed optimization problem. The weakest point is that the paper studies experimental economics, not computational social choice, and does not itself formulate or solve this problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no eligible anchor in this paper. Results 1–6 report experimental findings, and Propositions B.1–B.4 characterize learning under specified assumptions. None states the complexity of a computational problem or presents an algorithm whose continuous counterpart could be classified. Results 2 and 4, which motivate the proposed extension, are empirical effects of leader selection and disclosure—not computational results.

The proposed optimization over leader-selection procedures is a new question, not a mirror of those results. With the paper’s three treatments, \(\{T_0,T_1,T_2\}\), it amounts to comparing three expected errors under a newly specified population model. Preserving the four-person teams and taking a continuum across teams reduces to averaging repeated team outcomes. Scaling each team instead requires a special model for one distinguished leader and a continuum of followers: the leader has zero population mass but still broadcasts to everyone. That hybrid model is possible, but the finite-round DeGroot dynamics the paper studies have explicit update formulas, and the paper supplies no larger policy space or constraints that would make choosing among procedures a substantive optimization problem. Adding those ingredients might create one, but it would be a separate project.

So the case against a computational mirror of this paper is strong: the required computational result is absent, and the proposed question depends on choices the paper never makes. The broader claim that no continuous social-learning model could ever be worthwhile is weaker. A leader coupled to a large, heterogeneous population could be a valuable research question, but the paper does not establish it as a continuous computational mirror.
