The case FOR (proponent)

The closest result is Proposition 1, proved here. It gives sufficient conditions for a round-robin qualifying tournament to be incentive compatible with respect to seeding: exactly one team qualifies; all teams qualify; or each qualified team’s seeding value equals the maximum coefficient among teams ranked below it. That is a structural result about a mechanism’s incentive property. It does not classify the complexity of deciding or finding a manipulation, or analyze an algorithm’s running time.

A continuous population of clubs could be imagined, but turning the proposition into a question about mass-ranked teams would require new choices about types, rank quantiles, and what group manipulation means. The proposition does not anchor those choices, and its individual team ranks make a small-support, high-multiplicity regime difficult to motivate. Since there are no qualifying anchors, there is no per-anchor continuous problem to state.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent supplies no qualifying anchor. Proposition 1 is a sufficient-condition result about incentive compatibility and a proposed seeding rule; the paper gives no algorithmic problem, complexity classification, or running-time result. Its examples witness possible incentives, but do not define a computational task. In particular, Definition 1 compares rankings before and after a “manipulation” without specifying a feasible action set or encoding how match results can be changed. Asking whether a beneficial manipulation exists would require supplying that missing game and its input model. That could be worthwhile research, but it would be a new computational problem, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The strongest possible mirror would imagine a very large qualifying league with a distribution of club types, rank teams by performance quantiles, and ask whether a positive-mass cohort can manipulate its seeding position—or design a mass-level strategyproof seeding rule. But the paper’s incentive condition is about a named team that qualifies both before and after, and the number of other qualified teams with higher seeding values. A cohort deviation changes the strategic unit from one team to a coalition. Keeping one focal team inside a continuum preserves the individual question, but its action and effect on its rank still need a separate model; the distribution alone does not specify them. A fractional bracket or mass assignment would change the tournament outcome model as well.

The type problem is more fundamental than the difficulty of defining quantile ranks. The paper’s argument depends on how particular teams’ coefficients, standings, and qualification routes interact. A type description coarse enough to aggregate many teams may lose those relationships; one detailed enough to preserve them may need to encode much of the team-level state. A continuous reformulation of the proposition may still be possible, but it would be an axiomatic mechanism result. The programme explicitly seeks computational questions about a continuous population, so that reformulation does not provide the needed anchor.

This is a strong negative case for a computational mirror of this paper, not a proof that no related sports model could ever be worthwhile. A large open competition might support a new mass-level game, but its appeal would come from that new model, not from a computational result established here.
