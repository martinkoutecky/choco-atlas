Its results concern strategic properties of the capacity-allocation game: equilibrium existence, weakly dominant strategies, and hospitals’ preferences over equilibrium outcomes. Those are meaningful theorems, but they are not computational anchors for this programme. [The paper’s MPRA text](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/9239/1/MPRA_paper_9239.pdf)

There is a plausible population mirror nearby. In a large residency market, intern types could be complete rankings over hospitals, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of interns of each type. Hospitals choose regular-market quotas, and the two matching periods produce assignments of mass by intern type and hospital. A natural question is whether a pure-strategy quota equilibrium exists and, if so, to find a quota profile and its matching outcomes. The regime would be many interns but relatively few preference types, with capacities scaling per capita.

That mirror fits the paper’s subject, but its question is equilibrium analysis, not the computational complexity of a matching problem. It supplies no named result to anchor the requested continuous problem, and I can’t honestly assign it a tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hard classification from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would vote against a ChoCo mirror of this paper. Its results concern equilibrium existence and strategic properties of hospital quota choices; the paper gives no theorem classifying the complexity of a computational problem. So there is no named computational result here to anchor a mirror.

The strongest candidate is the proponent’s large residency market: represent interns by a finite set of complete preference types, scale hospital capacities with population, and ask for a pure-strategy quota equilibrium and its two-period matching outcomes. That is a coherent continuous-population question. But it makes equilibrium computation a new contribution. The strategic players in the paper are the hospitals, a finite set of individually specified actors; the continuum would describe the interns whose preferences feed the matching process. Computing an equilibrium of that hospital game could be worthwhile on its own, but the paper supplies no computational result that makes it a mirror.

A closer attempt would ask for an algorithm to compute the matching outcomes under given quotas. That centers an allocation problem the paper uses inside its strategic analysis, rather than one of its stated results. Preserving distinctions among applicants that determine who remains unmatched for the aftermarket can also require many types; aggregating applicants with different rankings or priorities changes those distinctions. A restricted market with genuinely interchangeable cohorts could avoid that problem, though it would add assumptions and still leave the missing computational anchor.

The negative case is therefore strong against calling this paper a computational mirror, but weak against the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project could arise from it. The large-market equilibrium problem is plausible; the case against it is that it is a new computationalization of the paper’s game-theoretic analysis, not a continuization of a computational result the paper establishes.
