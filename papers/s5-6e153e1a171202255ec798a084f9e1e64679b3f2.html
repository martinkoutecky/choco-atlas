The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is about Theorem 4, but it cannot meet your anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 4, proved here, says that for \(n\) conditionally i.i.d. experts, every aggregation scheme has regret at least \(1/4-3\sqrt{\log n/n}\). Theorem 1, also proved here, gives the exact minimum regret for two Blackwell-ordered experts: the precision scheme attains \( \frac18(5\sqrt5-11) \), and no scheme does better. These are minimax performance results, not claims that an underlying problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, or otherwise classified. The paper’s Matlab maximizations do not supply such a result. So there is no qualifying anchor, and no anchor-based Class A/B/C case to make.

There is still a plausible follow-on mirror, with Theorem 4 as its motivation rather than a valid computational anchor. Imagine a very large cohort of experts forecasting the same binary event, all using the same signal protocol and receiving conditionally i.i.d. signals. For the aggregation task, take a type to be an expert’s posterior forecast \(x\); mass \(\nu(x)\) is the fraction of experts reporting \(x\). The cohort has many members but, as in the construction behind Theorem 4, only two forecast types at a time.

One precise candidate is Exact-Mass Robust Forecast Aggregation. An instance gives a finite rational forecast alphabet \(X\) and a finite list \(\mathcal C\) of possible rational information structures. Each structure \(I\) specifies a prior and state-conditional distributions \(\rho_I^0,\rho_I^1\) over \(X\), satisfying the Bayes-calibration condition that each reported \(x\) is the expert’s posterior. In the continuum society, the observed profile is exactly \(\nu=\rho_I^\omega\), the mass distribution of forecasts in the realized state. The aggregator knows \(\mathcal C\), but not which \(I\) generated the profile. It must choose a forecast rule \(F\) mapping profiles to \([0,1]\). Its regret against the omniscient aggregator, who knows \(I\), is
\( \max_{I\in\mathcal C}\mathbb E_I[(F(\nu)-h_I(\nu))^2] \),
where \(h_I(\nu)\) is the omniscient posterior after observing the exact population profile. A solution is a rule minimizing this worst-case regret, together with its value.

The large-expert regime is plausible in the paper’s own terms: the experts share a common information structure, while the number of people is unbounded and the forecast support can stay at \(\tau=2\). The authors would likely recognize this as a continuum version of their many-i.i.d.-experts question. Their Appendix D construction gives reason to expect the unrestricted continuum version to have minimax regret \(1/4\): its adjacent state-conditional forecast distributions coincide across information structures, creating long chains of indistinguishable profiles for the ignorant aggregator, while the exact profile distinguishes the state for the omniscient one. That is an inference from their construction, not a theorem stated in the paper. For an explicitly listed finite class \(\mathcal C\), the optimization over forecasts at the finitely many possible profiles is a convex minimax problem, so I would expect it to be tractable by continuous optimization.

The weakness is decisive for ChoCo: even this promising mirror is a newly posed decision problem, not a computational problem classified by the paper. Theorem 4 studies finite samples and asymptotic regret; it does not establish the complexity of computing a continuous-population aggregation rule. This is a credible bridge to population continuization, but it cannot honestly be presented as an anchored complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror starts with a mismatch in what the paper proves. Its numbered results are about minimax regret and forecast accuracy. Theorem 1 gives an optimal regret for two Blackwell-ordered experts; Theorem 2 bounds regret for two conditionally independent experts; Theorem 4 gives a lower bound for \(n\) conditionally i.i.d. experts. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. So the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror.

Theorem 4 is the strongest candidate because its experts are exchangeable and the forecast support in its construction stays small. That makes a high-multiplicity regime plausible. I would grant the proposed co-modification too: in a continuum cohort, the mass distribution of realized forecasts can represent the exact signal frequencies. But this changes the task from aggregating a finite random sample to acting on an exact population distribution. The theorem’s sample-size parameter and its \(n\)-dependent construction do not themselves specify a computational problem for that limit.

For the proposed finite-list version, the exact profile can only be one of at most \(2|\mathcal C|\) state-conditional distributions. The aggregator’s choices are therefore values \(F(\nu)\) at finitely many profiles. With rational input, minimizing worst-case regret over an explicit list of structures is a finite convex minimax problem: each structure contributes a weighted sum of squared errors, and the constraints can be represented as second-order-cone constraints. That is a coherent continuous-population model, but its natural finite encoding leads directly to standard convex optimization. It does not reveal a hard pricing problem or a complexity landscape in the paper.

If the class is instead all conditionally i.i.d. information structures, the candidate no longer gives a finite input describing which structures matter. In the unrestricted limit, the paper’s chain construction suggests that the minimax value is \(1/4\): adjacent structures can induce the same population profile in opposite states, while the vanishing endpoint mass leaves the aggregator nearly at chance; the constant \(1/2\) forecast gives the matching upper bound. This is an inference from the construction, not a theorem in the paper. It identifies an information-theoretic limit, but does not provide an input-dependent computation problem to classify.

The two-expert results are weaker anchors still. Their distinctive structure is a relation between two signal channels—Blackwell ordering in Theorem 1, conditional independence in Theorem 2—not a population-size parameter. Replicating expert pairs either creates many separate forecasting tasks or turns the model into one with many experts observing a common state. The latter is a worthwhile model choice, but it leads back to the many-expert question, not to a population version of either two-expert theorem.

That is the strongest negative case I can make, but it does not establish the universal claim. The paper’s many-expert setting makes a continuous population sensible, and a more carefully chosen restricted family of information structures could yield a nontrivial optimization problem. Such a problem would need its own input model and computational motivation; this paper does not provide them. The proponent has made a plausible case for a population extension, but not for an anchored computational-complexity mirror.
