The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Statement 1 proves that a beneficiary’s excess allocation and the excess influence power of its supporters are proportional; Statement 2 equates two ways of measuring differences in influence power. Neither is a computational result. The reported comparisons—including Table 1’s simulated average \(F_{a1}\) values for six methods—are empirical findings, not results that place a problem in P or prove it hard. I can’t honestly supply an anchor or a continuous problem *per anchor*.

The best positive opening, though, is a continuous-population version of the paper’s per-apportionment \(F_{a1}\) criterion. Take many voters, grouped into \(\tau\) complete ballot types, where each type supports one beneficiary. Let \(\mu_i\) be the fraction of voters of type \(i\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and let \(M\) be the number of seats. For a named apportionment method \(A\), compute its seat allocation \(x^A\), where \(\sum_i x_i^A=M\). Put beneficiaries in descending order of \(\mu_i\); let \(L\) and \(S\) be the larger and smaller halves, splitting a tie at the boundary consistently. Their ideal allocations are \(D_i=M\mu_i\). The output is the exact value and sign of

\[ F_{a1}^{A}(\mu,M) =\sum_{i\in L}(x_i^A-D_i)-\sum_{i\in S}(x_i^A-D_i). \]

The regime is an electorate with \(N\) voters and \(N\gg\tau\): for example, a large election with many voters but relatively few distinct party-support ballots. Mass \(\mu_i\) is the fraction holding ballot type \(i\); \(x_i^A\) is the seats allocated to its beneficiary. This keeps seats integral while making the population continuous. The paper’s own entitlement formula \(D_i=MV_i/V\) becomes \(D_i=M\mu_i\), so I think the authors would recognize this as their per-apportionment question. Computing it for the divisor methods discussed in the paper should be tractable; further questions include comparing the methods’ \(F_{a1}\) values over specified societies and finding their worst-case favoring.

This mirror covers the paper’s per-instance criterion \(F_{a1}\), not its simulation-based claims about average method bias. Those averages require a law over societies; treating that law as the main continuous object would move toward continuizing profiles, rather than the population. My weakest point is more basic: this candidate is not anchored in a named computational result, and because the methods use vote proportions, the continuous input is already close to the standard apportionment input. It is a plausible high-multiplicity formulation, but it does not establish the kind of complexity-landscape contribution the programme seeks.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to mirror. The numbered statements establish identities between measures of favoring; the method rankings come from simulation, while the majorization discussion is an analytical characterization. None asserts the complexity of a computational problem.

The strongest proposed mirror is the per-apportionment \(F_{a1}\) calculation for a large electorate with a few ballot types. That is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario. But the paper’s apportionment input is already the vector of beneficiary weights \(V_i\), and the allocation methods and \(F_{a1}\) depend on those weights, not on the identities of the voters behind them. Replacing \(V_i/V\) by masses \(\mu_i\) therefore gives the same calculation: for rational masses, clearing denominators recovers integer voter counts, and scaling the weights does not change the allocation. The continuous population is already represented by the paper’s aggregate input.

That equivalence matters because the proposed task adds no computational object: compute the apportionment, then evaluate the paper’s criterion. The population is not a variable being transferred, controlled, or optimized over; it only supplies weights to a method that already takes weights as input. So this mirror does not bring the paper within reach of continuous optimization machinery or open up a complexity question the paper’s model lacked.

A stronger proposal might maximize or minimize \(F_{a1}\) over societies. That could be a new research problem, but it is not among the paper’s results, and its value would depend on adding a domain or constraints that the paper does not study. Averaging over societies would instead require choosing a probability law over mass vectors; that makes the law over profiles central, rather than the population itself.

The negative case is strongest for this paper, not universal: its natural population regime exists, and the aggregate input makes it easy to describe. What is missing is a compelling computational mirror of a result the paper actually establishes. I would not treat the proposed \(F_{a1}\) evaluation as such.
