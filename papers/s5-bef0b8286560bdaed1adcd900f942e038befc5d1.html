The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no qualifying anchor: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition establishing a complexity or algorithmic classification. Its pivotality derivation in §§2.3–2.5 is unnumbered; Algorithms 1 and 2 give pseudocode, and §4 reports simulations. So there is no anchor-specific continuous problem or class prediction to restate.

The strongest plausible mirror I can offer is therefore unanchored. Take a finite candidate set \(C\), a ballot-length limit \(L\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over the allowed ranked ballots, an expected electorate size \(N\), a voter utility function \(u\), and a tie-breaking rule. For each ballot type \(b\), draw the background count \(X_b\) independently from \(\mathrm{Pois}(N\mu_b)\). Let \(W(X)\) be the IRV winner. The problem is: find a ballot \(\beta\) maximizing
\[ G(\beta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[u(W(X+e_\beta))-u(W(X))\right], \]
where \(e_\beta\) adds the focal voter’s ballot. Its associated pivotal probability is \(\Pr[W(X+e_\beta)\ne W(X)]\). Here ballot rankings are the types, \(\mu_b\) is their population share, and \(N\) preserves the scale needed for one voter to matter.

A plausible regime is a large city or state election with millions of voters and a small candidate set: even with five candidates and truncated ballots, the possible ballot types are bounded while the electorate is much larger. This covers the paper’s central question about direct and indirect pivotality and the expected utility of a ballot. The paper explicitly allows alternative joint probability models, so using one shared Poisson realization across IRV rounds is a defensible extension of its problem. But the paper provides no result from which to classify this mirror; that status would need new analysis.

The weak point is fundamental: in a strictly atomless electorate, one person has zero mass and cannot change the winner. Keeping \(N\) and a one-voter increment preserves the authors’ question, but makes the model close to their existing Poisson voting framework. A referee might see it as a reparameterization of the paper’s expected ballot counts rather than a new continuous-population problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case for identifies no qualifying anchor in the paper: its pivotality formulas, pseudocode, and simulations are not numbered complexity results. That is a real limitation for placing the paper in ChoCo’s tractability trichotomy, though it does not by itself make a mirror uninteresting.

Its proposed objective \(G(\beta)\) is the strongest candidate, but its voters remain discrete. The Poisson variables \(X_b\) are integer counts, \(N\) sets their scale, and \(e_\beta\) is one focal voter’s unit ballot. Thus \(\mu\) parameterizes a stochastic model of finite profiles; it does not replace the society with a mass distribution on which IRV is run. A shared Poisson realization makes the model more coherent than the paper’s independence assumptions, but it remains a probability model over discrete elections. That may be a worthwhile computation, but under ChoCo’s stated scope it is not yet a continuous-population mirror.

On an exact continuum, IRV runs on the type masses and has a deterministic winner. Changing one person’s ballot changes zero mass, so it cannot change that winner. This defeats the paper’s central pivotality question; indirect pivotality relies on the same one-voter event, and the IRV–SMDP comparison then has no nonzero individual pivotal probabilities to compare. Giving the strategic voter mass \(\varepsilon\) restores an effect, but changes the object: a fixed \(\varepsilon\) is a bloc or mass-control question, while \(\varepsilon=1/N\) with finite \(N\) returns to a finite voter perturbation. In the large-\(N\) limit, individual pivotality vanishes away from tie boundaries.

That leaves a plausible boundary-scaled question: study pivotality near elimination ties, perhaps with an asymptotic scaling in \(N\). It could be computationally interesting, but it asks for a limiting stochastic analysis rather than the paper’s stated continuous-society computation. This is where the negative case is weakest: a tagged voter against a high-multiplicity background, or a carefully scaled tie-boundary model, might be accepted by the authors as a useful mirror. So the strongest case against is that the proponent’s concrete mirror keeps the population finite and that the literal continuum erases individual pivotality—not that every conceivable related question lacks value.
