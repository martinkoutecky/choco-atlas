What the mirror covers

No named result is covered by a continuous computational mirror; the paper's symmetry and uniqueness results remain analytic equilibrium statements.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly give this paper a qualifying computational anchor. The closest result is Theorem 3.1, proved here: in any two-agent symmetric auction with rank-and-bid-based allocation, bid-based payments, and i.i.d. values from a continuous bounded-support distribution, every Bayes–Nash equilibrium has the two agents playing identical strategies except on a measure-zero set of values. Corollary 3.3, also proved here, extends that conclusion to all agents in the stated \(n\)-agent setting. Theorem 4.5 proves uniqueness of the symmetric equilibrium under its additional rank-based and win-vs-tie-strict assumptions. These are structural equilibrium results, not complexity classifications or algorithms that compute equilibria. The paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor.

There is a plausible follow-on question. Model a large auction population with a finite set \(T\) of complete advertiser types, rational masses \(\mu_t\), and an anonymous allocation and payment rule depending on a bidder’s bid and the mass of higher or tied bids. Ask for the type-indexed bid distributions \((\sigma_t)_{t\in T}\) that form an equilibrium: each type’s distribution must be supported on bids maximizing its utility against the aggregate bid mass induced by all the \(\sigma_t\). A possible regime is a large batch of interchangeable ad opportunities with many advertisers in a small number of shared archetypes.

That is a concrete mass-equilibrium computation problem, but I would not present it as a mirror anchored by this paper. The paper’s continuum is the prior \(F\) over values for a finite number of strategic bidders; it does not make the bidder population continuous. A mass model changes finite bidder ranks into ranks against aggregate bid mass, and may require scaling auction capacity as well. Theorem 3.1 does not establish a complexity result for that changed game, so the paper gives no basis for predicting whether it is tractable or hard. My weakest point is therefore decisive: the proposed model is a plausible neighboring question, but the paper supplies no computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a mirror starts with a gap in the anchors: none of the named results states a computational problem or gives a complexity bound or algorithm. Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 3.3 say that, in a finite-player Bayesian game, agents with identical value distributions use the same strategies in equilibrium, up to measure-zero exceptions. Theorem 4.5 says that a symmetric equilibrium is unique under stronger assumptions. These are equilibrium structure results, not computational results of the kind ChoCo sets out to continuize.

A mass model is possible, but it does not carry those conclusions over in a way that gives the programme a clear target. In a continuum of advertisers, a single advertiser has zero mass. If inventory remains fixed as the population grows, each advertiser’s allocation can vanish. Scaling inventory with the population avoids that degeneration, but creates a different game in which allocation depends on aggregate bid mass or rank quantiles. Theorem 3.1’s comparison of two named players’ strategies then has no direct counterpart: individual asymmetries may disappear from the aggregate, while different value types may properly use different strategies. The mass-equilibrium question in the case for is therefore a neighboring model, not a computational version of the theorem.

Theorem 4.5 is the strongest candidate for a better mirror: in a position-auction setting with scaled inventory, ask for the unique symmetric equilibrium strategy. But the paper gives no computational formulation for that task. Its inputs are arbitrary continuous distributions and payment and allocation functions; it specifies neither a finite encoding nor an output representation or approximation criterion for an equilibrium function. Restricting those objects to, say, rational piecewise descriptions could make a computational question well posed, but that is additional research design, not a question anchored by Theorem 4.5. It might be a worthwhile project in its own right; the theorem’s uniqueness proof does not establish that it is.

So I would reject the proposed anchors as evidence for a worthwhile ChoCo mirror. The honest limit of this negative case is that a carefully specified computational study of continuum-auction equilibria could still be worthwhile. What the paper supplies is an analytic reason to consider such a study, not a computational result to continuize.
