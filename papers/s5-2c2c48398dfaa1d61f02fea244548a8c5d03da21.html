The continuous mirror question

Given item classes \(G\), at most four additive valuation types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), compute a complete allocation of mass to integral bundles. Represent a bundle by \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\), and let \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(B\). Require \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}B_g=q_g\) for each \(g\in G\), and \(v_t(B)\ge (2/3)v_t(B'-\mathbf{1}_h)\) whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\), \(\sum_s x_{s,B'}>0\), and \(h\) is a good copy in \(B'\). What is the complexity of computing such an allocation in the number of item classes and the input bit length?

The model it lives in

A feasibility and construction problem over type masses \(\mu_t\) assigned to integral bundle vectors \(B\), with variables \(x_{t,B}\), per-capita item supplies \(q_g\), and the \(2/3\)-EFX constraints. There is no welfare objective; the task is to output a feasible allocation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s complete \(2/3\)-EFX guarantee for at most four valuation types. It leaves Theorem 4’s \((1-\varepsilon)\)-EFX charity bound and the paper’s other results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one compute a feasible rational mass allocation in time polynomial in the number of item classes and input bit length, without expanding the population to a common-denominator number of agents?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is structurally plausible, but I can’t give it a valid anchor under your stated rule: the paper has no named result that classifies an underlying computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Its main contributions are existence guarantees.

The closest lead is Theorem 3, proved here: for at most four valuation types, a complete \(2/3\)-EFX allocation exists. The paper gives an algorithm for constructing one, but its named termination bound is Lemma 7: at most \(T^2\) steps, where \(T\) is the total number of configurations. That is not a polynomial-time bound in the input size. Theorem 4, also proved here, guarantees a \((1-\varepsilon)\)-EFX allocation with \(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{k/\varepsilon})\) goods in charity for at most \(k\) types; it too is an existence result, not a complexity classification. The rainbow-cycle results it invokes, Theorems 1 and 2, are cited from earlier work.

The natural mirror of Theorem 3 would be a complete \(2/3\)-EFX allocation for a continuous population with at most four types. A type is an additive valuation vector \(v_t\), and \(\mu_t\) is its share of the population. To preserve indivisible bundles while taking a population limit, let goods be replicated item classes with per-capita supplies \(q_g\). Let \(x_{t,B}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(B\). Require \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}b_g=q_g\), where \(b_g\) is the number of copies of item class \(g\) in \(B\). Ask whether such an allocation exists in which every recipient’s bundle is worth at least \(2/3\) as much, by their own valuation, as every other allocated bundle after removal of any one good. This is a sensible high-multiplicity question: the paper itself motivates few valuation types in settings such as public housing, where the population can be large relative to the number of types.

I would expect that continuous feasibility question to be tractable for fixed \(k=4\), but the paper’s existence theorem does not establish that. The item-supply scaling is also a material modeling choice: it gives a coherent continuum limit while requiring repeated goods or seat classes.

So the paper offers a credible *existence-theorem* mirror, especially through Theorem 3, but no qualifying computational anchor for the requested complexity programme. The main weakness is not whether the population regime makes sense; it is that the paper does not establish a complexity result for the underlying problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the proponent’s only anchor, and it establishes existence: every instance with at most four valuation types has a complete \(2/3\)-EFX allocation. The paper gives a construction, but Lemma 7 bounds its iterations by \(T^2\), where \(T\) is the number of configurations; it gives no runtime in the input parameters. So the paper supplies no complexity classification of the kind this programme asks to continuize.

The proposed mass model is coherent. With complete valuation vectors as types, EFX does not need agent identities: one can let \(x_{t,B}\) denote the mass of type \(t\) receiving integral bundle \(B\), and scale item supplies per capita. But asking whether those masses can satisfy EFX is another existence question. Asking for an algorithm and its complexity is a sensible next research problem, yet the paper does not establish a computational claim that carries over to it. Its envy-graph construction moves bundles among named agents; adapting those exchanges to populations split across bundles would require new machinery. That makes the model plausible, but does not make this paper a computational anchor for it.

Theorem 4 does not improve the case: it is also an existence guarantee, and its charity bound counts individual goods. Under per-capita supply scaling, that count does not directly express a nonzero mass of charity; a mass-normalized bound would be a new statement.

So the strongest negative case is narrow but decisive for the stated programme: this paper does not anchor a computational continuous mirror. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous EFX question exists would be too strong. Few valuation types are a natural high-multiplicity regime, and the proposed model is worth studying on its own; it is simply not a continuization of a computational result established here.
