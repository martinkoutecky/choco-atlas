The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a fluid interview-assignment problem built around Theorem 4.1. It is a genuine population continuization: firms and workers become masses, interview links become a mass-constrained information network, and the final hiring outcome remains a unit-capacity deferred-acceptance matching. But there is an important qualification: this paper contains no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, approximation, or other formal complexity result. Thus the case below is a substantive mirror of the paper’s main structural results, not a qualifying complexity anchor in the strictest ChoCo sense.

A plausible regime is a large, segmented labor market: many hospitals and medical residents in one quality tier, or many firms and graduates recruited through the same campus channel. Agents within a tier share the same interview cost, quota, and distributions of match surplus, while their realized private values are independent shocks. The number of agents is large and the number of persistent types—side, tier, quota, cost, and value distributions—is fixed or moderate. This is particularly plausible because the paper itself motivates ex-ante homogeneity by partitioning markets into groups of similar-quality agents.

Let \(F=W=[0,1]\) denote unit masses of firms and workers. In the homogeneous case, an interview assignment is a measurable relation \(\eta\subseteq F\times W\) satisfying \(\int_W\mathbf{1}_{\eta}(f,w)\,dw=\alpha\) for almost every firm and \(\int_F\mathbf{1}_{\eta}(f,w)\,df=\alpha\) for almost every worker. Here \(\alpha=x/N\) is the per-capita interview rate, the continuous counterpart of \(x\) interviews in a finite market. Interview cost is therefore \(c\alpha\) per firm, or \(c\alpha\) per unit population. This scaling is essential: keeping \(x\) fixed while \(N\) grows would make the interview mass vanish.

Given \(\eta\), each interview generates a private firm surplus drawn from \(H\), and worker preferences are generated from \(G\), as in the paper. The final outcome is the measure-valued firm-proposing deferred-acceptance matching supported on \(\eta\). It is not a fractional employment relaxation: each infinitesimal firm and worker can still receive at most one match. The objective is expected unmatched mass, \(U(\eta)=1-\mathbb{E}[\lVert \pi_\eta\rVert]\), where \(\pi_\eta\) is the continuum FOSM.

For \(\alpha=1/k\), perfect overlap means partitioning both populations into blocks of mass \(\alpha\), with every firm interviewing every worker in its own block. More generally, one can restrict attention to regular symmetric interview relations whose connected components all have the same mass. A component of mass \(s\alpha\) corresponds to the paper’s finite symmetric assignment with degree of non-overlap \(s-1\). Perfect overlap is the case \(s=1\).

The lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It states: for any \(x\) such that \(N\) is divisible by \(x\), there exists \(\epsilon>0\) such that, if \(\Pr(\delta<0)<\epsilon\), an interview assignment with perfect overlap and \(x\) interviews per firm and worker minimizes expected unemployment among all other symmetric interview assignments in which every firm and worker receives \(x\) interviews. The theorem is not a complexity theorem; it is an optimization statement over interview assignments. Its proof compares an upper bound on unemployment under perfect overlap with a strictly positive lower bound for every non-perfect-overlap assignment, using Hall’s theorem.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous Overlap Design:

Given \(H\), \(G\), the interview rate \(\alpha\), finite type masses, and a regular symmetric interview-assignment class \(\mathcal{E}_\alpha\), find \(\eta\in\mathcal{E}_\alpha\) minimizing \(U(\eta)\), and output the optimal interview relation and its expected unemployment. The principal decision question is whether the unique optimizer, under an appropriate fluid analogue of \(\Pr(\delta<0)<\epsilon\), is the perfect-overlap block design.

This is a credible continuous analogue of the paper’s underlying problem. It preserves the costly-information stage, the externality from shared interview partners, and deferred acceptance; it does not merely replace a finite matching by a fractional one. I would expect the homogeneous fixed-quota version to be Class A: perfect overlap has a compact mass description, and the paper’s structural argument suggests that the continuous optimum can be certified without searching over arbitrary networks. The general heterogeneous version may instead be continuum-specifically difficult because evaluating \(U(\eta)\) for an arbitrary interview kernel requires solving a fluid deferred-acceptance process.

A second, supporting anchor is Proposition A.4, proved in the appendix. It states that if there exist \(N,c\) for which a symmetric pure-strategy perfect-overlap equilibrium exists with \(x\) interviews per firm and worker, then for every \(\epsilon>0\), there is \(N_0\) such that every \(\widetilde N>N_0\) admits a correlated equilibrium in which each firm interviews \(x\) workers, each worker receives \(x\) interviews, and, with probability at least \(1-\epsilon\), a firm obtains the same expected profit as under the perfect-overlap equilibrium.

Its continuous counterpart is Fluid Correlated Interview Equilibrium:

Given \(H\), \(G\), \(c\), \(\alpha\), and \(\epsilon>0\), find a probability distribution \(\Pi\) over \(\alpha\)-regular interview relations such that \(\Pi\) is a correlated equilibrium of the continuum first-stage interview game, every firm and worker has interview mass \(\alpha\), and a random firm receives the perfect-overlap equilibrium payoff with probability at least \(1-\epsilon\). A solution must provide the distribution over interview relations and certify that almost every firm has no profitable measurable deviation to another interview neighborhood of mass \(\alpha\).

I would expect this problem also to be tractable in the homogeneous setting, through a random block-partition construction and a characterization of the allowable interview-cost interval. It generates useful new questions: whether exact perfect-overlap equilibrium exists for heterogeneous types; how the equilibrium cost interval depends on \(\alpha\); whether the finite-\(N\) correlated equilibria converge quantitatively to the fluid equilibrium; and what happens when quotas, costs, or preference distributions vary across types.

The mirror covers only the paper’s interview-assignment results, especially Theorem 4.1 and Proposition A.4. It does not claim to continuize the simulations, the finite-\(N\) formulas, or every equilibrium-existence proposition.

The weakest point is serious. In the paper, realized preferences are idiosyncratic rankings over named counterparties. If those realized rankings must themselves be included in the type, then the number of types is effectively enormous, and the high-multiplicity interpretation becomes weak. The defence is that the paper’s first-stage problem is explicitly ex ante: firms optimize expected profit under a common preference law, so the persistent type can be the tier and stochastic environment while the realized values are native uncertainty. A stricter referee may reject that move.

A second weakness is scaling. The theorem’s finite bound \(\Pr(\delta<0)^x\) does not transfer mechanically when \(x\) becomes proportional to \(N\); an atomless model has a different law-of-large-numbers structure. Establishing the correct fluid limit is therefore new work. That is also why this is a genuine mirror rather than a restatement—but it means the positive case is a plausible research programme, not an already-proved continuization.

So the best positive verdict is: the paper supports a natural and recognizable continuous-population interview-assignment model, with a strong Class A conjecture in the homogeneous regime. Under ChoCo’s strict requirement that the anchor itself be a named computational classification, however, this paper has no qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong under ChoCo’s own gate: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Propositions 3.1 and 3.2, Theorem 4.1, and Proposition A.4 are equilibrium-existence, structural, and asymptotic statements. None asserts a complexity classification, an algorithm, an approximation guarantee, or parameterized tractability. The proposed “Continuous Overlap Design” is therefore an invented optimization problem, not a continuization of a computational result in the paper.

Even setting that gate aside, Theorem 4.1 does not have a nondegenerate fluid analogue under the best natural scaling. Its proof relies on finite interview degree. Perfect overlap gives the finite bound

\[ U_{\mathrm{PO}}\leq \Pr(\delta_{1:x}<0)=\Pr(\delta<0)^x, \]

while non-perfect overlap admits a positive-probability finite preference profile producing unemployment. If \(x/N\to\alpha>0\), then \(x\to\infty\), so the first quantity tends to zero whenever \(\Pr(\delta<0)<1\). Each firm now interviews a positive-measure continuum of workers and almost surely has a positive-measure set of acceptable candidates. The finite Hall obstruction that drives the theorem is washed out by density: its witness is a finite configuration whose probability has no uniform positive lower bound and typically becomes a null event.

This is not merely the uninteresting conclusion that “continuity does not help.” The primitive phenomenon has disappeared. Deferred acceptance is no longer the paper’s finite proposal process: a firm has a continuum of candidates, generally no attained top-ranked candidate under iid atomless values, and no ordinary measurable sequence of proposals. One must replace it with a cutoff or flow model. With finite type-level preferences, that replacement becomes an aggregate matching model and removes the paper’s identity-based overlap externality.

The alternative is to keep \(x\) fixed as \(N\) grows. That preserves the paper’s finite-degree combinatorics, but the interview relation then has edge density \(x/N\to0\). Under the proposed mass-based formulation it becomes a null relation; under the paper’s literal per-interview cost, it remains a sparse identity-level random graph. The relevant limit is consequently a sparse graph or local-neighborhood limit, not a society represented by a distribution over finitely many types. That could motivate a new random-graph matching project, but it is not the continuous-population mirror envisaged by ChoCo.

The type-space defence does not repair this. The paper’s firms and workers are ex ante homogeneous, but the realized pairwise values \(\delta_{w,f}\) and \(\zeta_{w,f}\) determine acceptability, rankings, and the deferred-acceptance outcome. Omitting those realizations gives only an ex ante stochastic environment, not a continuous society that determines the matching. Including the realized rankings or interview neighborhoods in the type makes types effectively individual- or edge-specific. Replacing them by common type-level rankings is a legitimate new model, but it no longer preserves the paper’s mechanism.

Proposition A.4 has the same scaling problem. Its construction keeps \(x\) fixed while \(N\) grows, partitioning the market into components of \(x\) firms and \(x\) workers. Those components have vanishing population mass in the continuum. A random partition into finite components is therefore not a positive-mass interview kernel; its useful information lives in sparse local identity structure. If instead \(x=\alpha N\) is used to obtain positive-mass blocks, the proposition’s proof no longer applies, and one returns to the dense-limit degeneration above.

The proposed “Fluid Correlated Interview Equilibrium” also has no computational representation: it asks for a probability measure over measurable interview relations and certification against all measurable deviations. The block-partition construction is either merely a finite latent segmentation—already representable by finitely many types—or a sparse identity-level construction outside the finite-type mass model.

There is a real weakness in claiming an absolute impossibility: a carefully designed sparse, multi-type random-graph limit might be an interesting new matching theory. But it would be a new mean-field network model, not a computational mirror of this paper’s named results. Under ChoCo’s stricter standard, the paper should receive a red verdict: no qualifying computational result, and both proposed anchors collapse into either a dense fluid model that erases the overlap phenomenon or a sparse graph model that is no longer continuization by population mass.
