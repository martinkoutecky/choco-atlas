The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) alternatives, a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and rational thresholds \(\theta_c^k\), each type assigns its mass to its highest-ranked surviving alternative at round \(k\). Let \(q_c^k\) be the total mass assigned to \(c\), and eliminate each surviving \(c\) with \(q_c^k<\theta_c^k\). Under the condition that the surviving thresholds sum to more than \(1\) at every nonterminal round, compute the exact sequence of survivor sets and vote masses through termination, including the number of rounds.

The model it lives in

A finite electorate is represented by a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types. Each type carries mass and votes sincerely for its top surviving alternative; thresholds are fractions of the population. The task is to compute the resulting elimination sequence under the normalized condition \(\sum_{c\text{ surviving}}\theta_c^k>1\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: this paper gives no named hardness result and no complexity-class theorem. Its best anchor is Proposition 1, proved here. It says that if, at every round \(k\), the sum of the surviving alternatives’ thresholds exceeds the total number of votes, then at least one alternative is eliminated each round and the game lasts at most \(m_1-1\) rounds. That is a termination bound, not a stated classification such as “in P”; the polynomial-time consequence below is for the continuous problem I formulate.

My lead mirror is Continuous Sincere Threshold Elimination. An instance has \(m\) alternatives, a finite support \(T\) of strict preference rankings, rational type masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and a rational threshold schedule \(\theta_c^k\) for each round and candidate. The schedule may be supplied directly or generated by a rational threshold-updating rule. At round \(k\), each type places all its mass on its most-preferred surviving alternative. Thus candidate \(c\)’s vote mass is
\[ q_c^k=\sum_{\substack{t\in T:\\c\text{ is top-ranked in the current survivor set}}}\mu_t. \]
Eliminate each surviving \(c\) with \(q_c^k<\theta_c^k\). Restrict instances to schedules for which \(\sum_{c\text{ surviving}}\theta_c^k>1\) at every nonterminal round—the population-normalized version of Proposition 1’s condition. The requested solution is the exact sequence of survivor sets and vote masses, through the terminal set, together with the number of rounds. There is no strategic decision or optimization objective: sincere voting fixes each type’s allocation, and the survivor set is the evolving state.

A plausible regime is a large primary or other staged election where many people share one of a modest number of stable ranking patterns. The type is a complete ranking; mass is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking; each threshold is a minimum fraction of the electorate. For example, a million-person electorate represented by dozens of ranking types is a sensible high-multiplicity instance. The paper’s Proposition 1 supplies the same aggregate-threshold condition, while the continuous version replaces integer vote totals with rational masses. This is a direct mirror of its sincere threshold-elimination process, not a claim that the paper studies strategic voting or that its simulation table is a complexity result.

The expected direction is tractable. With the promised threshold condition, Proposition 1’s bound gives at most \(m-1\) rounds. At each round, the top surviving choice for every supported ranking and the resulting vote masses can be computed exactly; with the schedule explicitly encoded, this takes polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the rational input bit length \(L\). This is a consequence for the stated mirror, not a complexity classification proved in the paper. A further question is how much mass must move between ranking types to change the terminal survivor set.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s only numbered anchor is a simple finite-termination result, and the discrete process is already straightforward to simulate. So this makes a credible, author-recognizable continuous problem, but not a compelling case that continuization dissolves discrete hardness or exposes a deep new complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the paper has no named computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Proposition 1 proves a termination guarantee: when the surviving thresholds sum to more than the total number of votes, some alternative is eliminated each round, so the game lasts at most \(m_1-1\) rounds. It says nothing about an algorithm, complexity, optimization, or approximation problem. The authors also say they assume sincere voting and do not analyze the game strategically; the simulations illustrate behavior rather than establish a computational result.

The proponent’s continuous society is sensible: many voters can share a ranking type, and vote totals and thresholds can be expressed as population shares. I would not object to that model. But its requested output—the sequence of survivor sets and vote masses—is a new computational framing of the paper’s process, not a computational question the paper poses. With an explicitly encoded rational threshold schedule, one can tally the \(\tau\) supported types and advance through at most \(m-1\) rounds. That calculation is polynomial in the input size. The point is not that an easy answer is uninteresting; it is that the source result is a termination theorem, not an algorithmic result whose continuous complexity is being charted.

The stronger alternative is the proponent’s closing question: given a society, how much mass must move between ranking types to change the terminal outcome? That could be a worthwhile computational problem, especially since the paper mentions stability as related work. But it adds an adversarial perturbation objective and a cost model that Proposition 1 does not contain. It does not repair the lack of a computational anchor in this paper.

So I can make a strong case against treating the proposition as a computational mirror. I cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could arise from this model: continuous outcome robustness is a plausible extension, and may be worth studying in its own right. That is where the negative case is weakest.
