The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that this paper has no qualifying named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Appendix A.7, “Main Result,” says that under its assumptions Diet COLA is incentive-compatible and the team with the longest active streak without a playoff series win has the highest lottery index—and therefore the highest chance of the first pick. The paper proves that claim here through a case analysis of game incentives. But A.7 is not a numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition about a computational problem’s complexity or algorithms, so it does not meet the anchor requirement.

A possible mirror would describe a large league by a distribution \(\mu\) over team types, where a type records lottery index, playoff history, strength, preferences, and pick ownership; mass would represent the fraction of franchises of each type. The mechanism would allocate lottery chances and picks, subject to incentive constraints. I do not find that a convincing mirror of this paper’s problem: its league has 30 distinct franchises, and its playoff bracket, draft slots, and team-specific strategic choices are finite. Replacing them with a continuum would require redesigning those central features, rather than simply making the population continuous.

One could instead imagine a distribution over media analysts’ survey responses, but the survey is an implementation component, not the paper’s central result; it involves fewer than 100 respondents, and the paper gives no named computational result about it either. So I cannot state a continuous problem per qualifying anchor without inventing one the paper does not supply.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no result in ChoCo’s target category. Appendix A.7 establishes an incentive claim for Diet COLA under stated preferences and assumptions; it does not give the complexity of a computational problem or an algorithm for one. The lottery odds, poll cutoff, and RBTS scores are calculations, but the paper makes no complexity claim about them. So there is no qualifying computational result here to anchor a continuous question.

The best candidate is a distribution over complete team types: lottery index, playoff history, strength, preferences, and pick ownership. Franchise identity alone is not a valid objection; high multiplicity could be sensible in a larger league. But a useful mirror would need to preserve the interactions that drive the paper’s claim: standings, playoff qualification, the bracket, and each team’s effect on other teams’ lottery eligibility. In the NBA’s fixed-size league, the population is just 30 teams. In a continuum with a fixed number of playoff places and premium picks, those prizes have vanishing mass. Scaling the places with the population avoids that limit, but also requires a new league and competition structure. Replicating 30-team leagues leaves each lottery separate, so the distribution across leagues does not simplify any one league’s incentive problem.

One could propose a new computational task: given a distribution of team states and preferences, choose a lottery line and carry-over rules that ensure incentive compatibility while favoring weaker teams. That may be worth studying, but it is mechanism synthesis added to the paper, not a continuous mirror of a computational result it proves. With the paper’s assumptions, its central incentive argument is already a short case analysis; adding a detailed model of strategic play, schedules, and league scale would be substantial new work.

The media-survey alternative is weaker. The paper’s survey has fewer than 100 respondents, with peer comparisons and conflicts of interest. A distribution over responses would not capture those respondent-level relationships, and the six-option line rule itself is simple aggregation. The paper proves no computational result about truth elicitation to mirror.

So I would argue there is no worthwhile ChoCo mirror of this paper as written. The limitation is that “in any scenario” is hard to establish: authors could develop a scalable league and a new mechanism-design problem that the paper’s proposal motivates. But that would be a new project, not a continuous-population version of one of this paper’s computational results.
