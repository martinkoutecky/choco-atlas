That changes the stability concept, and the paper does not establish this as an analogue of its ISE theorem.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive anchor is Theorem 1: “There always exist an Individually Stable Equilibrium in the group formation game.” The paper proves it by applying the improvement-update algorithm from [15]. Its argument uses a potential consisting of the sorted utilities and the fact that the finite player set has only finitely many partitions. This is an existence result, though—not a complexity classification such as polynomial time or NP-hardness. If the required anchors must assert a complexity classification, this paper has none.

A plausible regime is a large emergency-response or smart-infrastructure network with many standardized units. Let a type record an agent’s sector, exact staging location, and resource vector. Thousands of units might share a few hundred such types, so mass represents the fraction of units of each type. This uses the paper’s stated applications and preserves its strategic question: agents form groups to balance pooled resources, sectoral diversity, and spatial cohesion.

Here is a concrete continuous question that mirrors Theorem 1. Call it Continuous ISE Construction. An instance gives a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and the utility function. For a type \(t\), the sector, location \(x_t\), and resource vector \(r_t\) are fixed. A group is a nonzero mass vector \(q\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{T}\), with \(\sum_g q^g=\mu\) across the groups. Define
\[ R_c(q)=\sum_{t:\,c_t=c}q_t r_{t,c},\qquad D(q)=\sum_{t,u\in T}q_tq_u\lVert x_t-x_u\rVert, \]
and use the paper’s heterogeneity boost \(h\) and group utility \(U(q)=f(R(q),D(q))\). The question is to output a finite or countable group partition that is stable to an infinitesimal member of any type leaving for an existing group or a singleton: no such move both strictly improves the mover’s utility and is weakly accepted by the destination group. For a computational version, \(f\) must be fixed or have a finite representation; for example, one could fix \(f(R,D)=R/(1+D)\). There is no planner’s objective here. The output is a strategically stable partition, as in the paper.

I would provisionally expect this to be continuum-specifically difficult, or even to expose failures of existence, because the finite-potential proof does not carry over to mass partitions. This is low confidence: a compactness argument from replicated finite games might instead yield existence and a constructive route. Useful follow-up questions are whether every rational \(\mu\) has a stable mass partition, whether finite ISEs converge to one, and whether one can construct it efficiently.

This is a narrow mirror of Theorem 1’s stable-group existence claim. It does not mirror the simulation findings as complexity results. Its weakest point is also decisive: infinitesimal entrants barely affect group utility, while consent in the finite game depends on the effect of an individual joining. The first-order acceptance rule above is a reasonable mean-field limit, but it needs validation by the authors; changing that rule could change the equilibrium question. The paper offers a sensible setting for a population model, but its sole named theorem is a weak anchor for the programme’s computational-complexity agenda.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper gives ChoCo no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 proves existence of an individually stable equilibrium using finiteness of the player set and its partitions; it gives no complexity bound or computational classification. The simulation findings are empirical results for small finite games. A continuous equilibrium existence theorem could be interesting, but it would not mirror a complexity result the paper establishes.

There are plausible high-multiplicity settings: a large emergency-response fleet, for example, could have many units sharing a sector, depot, and resource level. So I would not argue that this model has no sensible repeated-type regime. The harder problem is that its stability notion depends on an individual deviation. In an atomless population, one person has zero mass: moving them changes neither a group’s resource total nor its spatial coverage. Acceptance by the destination group then becomes automatic, while a singleton has zero mass and may have no meaningful utility under the group formula.

The proponent’s derivative-based acceptance rule is the strongest repair. It preserves the sign of the effect a small entrant has on incumbents, but it replaces unilateral deviation with a limiting perturbation rule. The paper does not select that rule, and it need not capture finite-game stability when the first-order effect is zero: then stability can depend on higher-order changes. The paper also allows any monotone \(f\), which need not have a derivative. Fixing a smooth \(f\) and choosing a derivative rule would define a coherent new model, but its connection to the paper’s equilibria would need to be established.

One could instead ask for a continuous algorithm: given finitely many types and their masses, construct a stable partition. But that requires specifying how to represent the collection of groups and their compositions, how singleton deviations work, and what stability means at ties. Those choices are central to the answer, not just implementation details. The resulting problem may be worthwhile as new work in computational game theory; the paper itself does not anchor it as a continuous computational question.

So my negative case is real but limited. The absence of a complexity result and the loss of atomic deviations make this a weak mirror for ChoCo. They do not prove that no author-recognizable, useful continuous formulation exists; the first-order version is a plausible candidate, provided the authors accept and validate the changed stability concept.
