The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(X\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^d\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_x\) summing to \(1\), labeled candidate sites \(C\) at locations \(p_c\in\mathbb{Q}^d\), a committee \(T\subseteq C\), and a failed set \(J\subseteq C\), compute a replacement \(R\subseteq C\setminus J\), with \(|R|\le |T\cap J|\), minimizing \(\max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\min_{c\in(T\setminus J)\cup R}\|x-p_c\|_2\).

The model it lives in

Types are occupied rational locations \(x\in X\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_x\); candidates are labeled sites \(c\in C\) at \(p_c\in\mathbb{Q}^d\). The input includes \(T\) and \(J\); replacements are subsets \(R\subseteq C\setminus J\) with \(|R|\le |T\cap J|\), and the objective is worst-case distance over \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 11’s hardness claims for ORP, FTS, and OFTC in dimensions \(d\ge2\). It leaves the one-dimensional algorithms in Theorems 1, 6, and 9, the approximation results in Lemmas 17, 19, and 23 and Theorem 22, and the bicriterion result in Theorem 28 unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(d\), are any of the three mirror problems fixed-parameter tractable in the support size \(\tau\), the failure bound \(f\) where applicable, or both, with polynomial dependence on \(m\) and encoding length \(L\)?
  • Which approximation guarantees from the paper persist with running times stated in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is their fault tolerant spatial coverage problem with the population represented by masses over locations. Optimal Replacement is the lead: it preserves the paper’s worst case service objective and isolates the replacement decision, while Theorem 11 also gives corresponding hardness results for the other two variants.

A plausible regime is a large city choosing a committee of service locations from a finite list of proposed sites. Residents in the same building or residential complex have the same location type and the same distance to every proposed site. There may be millions of residents but far fewer occupied locations, so the number of agents \(n\) can greatly exceed the number of types \(\tau\). A type is a location \(x\in\mathbb{Q}^d\); its mass \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of residents there. The candidate sites remain a finite, labeled set \(C\), with location \(p_c\in\mathbb{Q}^d\) for each candidate. The labels matter even when two sites coincide, since they can fail independently.

For a committee \(S\subseteq C\), define its score by
\[ \sigma_\mu(S)=\max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\min_{c\in S}\|x-p_c\|_2. \]
This is exactly the paper’s min–max Chamberlin–Courant score, now evaluated over the locations with positive population mass. Given a failing set \(J\subseteq C\), let
\[ \operatorname{rep}_\mu(S,J)= \min_{\substack{R\subseteq C\setminus J\\ |R|\le |S\cap J|}} \sigma_\mu((S\setminus J)\cup R). \]

The three continuous problems are then precise.

  • Continuous Optimal Replacement (lead): Given the rationally weighted location types, candidate sites, an existing committee \(T\), and a failing set \(J\), find a replacement set \(R\subseteq C\setminus J\) with \(|R|\le |T\cap J|\) minimizing \(\sigma_\mu((T\setminus J)\cup R)\).
  • Continuous Fault-Tolerance Score: Given the location distribution, candidate sites, committee \(T\), and integer \(f\), compute

\[ \max_{\substack{J\subseteq C\\ |J|\le f}}\operatorname{rep}_\mu(T,J). \]

  • Continuous Optimal Fault-Tolerant Committee: Given the location distribution, candidate sites, \(k\), and \(f\), find \(S\subseteq C\), \(|S|\le k\), minimizing

\[ \max_{\substack{J\subseteq C\\ |J|\le f}}\operatorname{rep}_\mu(S,J). \]

Here \(d\ge 2\) is fixed, the coordinates and positive masses are given in binary, and \(\sum_x\mu_x=1\). The failures remain failures of named candidates; it is only the voter population that is represented by mass.

The anchor is Theorem 11, proved in this paper. It states that Optimal Replacement, Fault-Tolerance Score, and Optimal Fault-Tolerant Committee are all NP-hard under the Euclidean norm in every dimension \(d\ge 2\), with committee size \(k\) and failure parameter \(f\) as input. The theorem is proved here by a reduction from Planar Monotone 3-SAT; although its construction follows a scheme used in earlier work, the theorem’s fault-tolerant reduction and result are the authors’ own. I use its three problem-level claims as a single joint anchor, and its Optimal Replacement claim is the strongest part of my case.

The expected direction for all three mirrors is hardness transfers. Given any finite voter set from the paper, put mass \(n_x/n\) at each distinct voter location \(x\), where \(n_x\) is the number of voters at that location. For every committee \(S\),
\[ \sigma_\mu(S)=\max_{v\in V}\min_{c\in S}\|v-p_c\|_2. \]
Thus every replacement score is unchanged; the same is true after maximizing over failures or minimizing over committees. This is an exact embedding, with \(\tau\le n\) and polynomial size rational masses. A polynomial algorithm in \(m,\tau,L\) for these continuous problems would therefore solve the paper’s NP-hard instances in polynomial time. The reduction’s combinatorics lie in the geometry and candidate choices, not in large voter multiplicities.

The authors should recognize these as their problems: the criterion, committee choices, failure model, and adaptive replacement all stay intact. Their own applications include facility location, and a worst served resident is a natural objective for a public service committee. The mirror covers Theorem 11’s hardness claims for all three variants; I am not claiming here to mirror the paper’s one dimensional algorithms or its approximation results.

The weakest point is that \(\mu_x\) has no effect on this min–max score once it is positive: a type with tiny mass still counts just as much as a heavily populated one. So this mirror makes a population continuous in representation and supports a high multiplicity regime, but it does not create mass-sensitive computation or make these particular instances easier. That limitation is also faithful to the paper’s stated worst voter objective: it is defensible if every occupied type must receive the same worst-case protection. It would not be a good mirror if the intended question were about average service or the fraction of the population served.

This leaves useful follow-up questions: whether any of the three problems becomes fixed parameter tractable in \(\tau\) or \(f\); which approximation guarantees persist with running time expressed in \(m,\tau,L\); and what representation is suitable for an extension to a non-atomic spatial distribution. Those are further questions, not changes to the mirror above.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the proponent’s joint Theorem 11 anchor is that its proposed continuum has no role for population mass. For any committee \(S\),
\[ \sigma_\mu(S)=\max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\min_{c\in S}\|x-p_c\|_2. \]
So ORP, FTS, and OFTC depend only on the support locations. Changing the positive masses while keeping that support fixed changes none of their values or solutions. The proposed “continuous” input is therefore just the paper’s finite set of voter locations, with duplicate locations merged. The paper’s algorithms can work on those unique locations directly, and its hardness results carry over by the same exact encoding. That is useful compression when many voters share a location, but it does not create a new computational role for a continuous population or its distribution.

The residents-in-buildings scenario is plausible, and the authors could recognize worst-case service over occupied locations as their problem. But the mirror’s only high-multiplicity gain is that repeated copies of a location need not be listed repeatedly. A location with one resident constrains the worst-case score just as much as one with a million. That is faithful to the paper’s objective, so it is not a modeling error; it is why the mass representation contributes no new question. This is a weaker research case than one where aggregating mass changes the optimization problem or brings a new continuous-optimization method into play.

The stronger escape is to give the population a genuinely continuous spatial support, such as a region. Then the objective becomes worst-case coverage of that region by the finite candidate sites, including after failures. But the masses still disappear: the computation is over the support geometry, whose description now replaces the finite location list. Continuous-demand facility-location work already studies geometric demand regions, while fault-tolerant \(k\)-center work studies related but different failure models; neither source establishes that this paper’s adaptive replacement variant has already been solved continuously. [Fekete, Mitchell, and Weinbrecht on continuous-demand location](https://www.ibr.cs.tu-bs.de/users/fekete/hp/publications/PDF/2000-On_the_Continuous_Weber_and_k-Median_Problems.pdf), [Khuller, Pless, and Sussmann on fault-tolerant \(k\)-center](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-3975(98)00222-9). That route may be worthwhile computational geometry, but it is not a mass-sensitive continuation of the proposed finite-type mirror. Replacing the worst-case score with an average or a population-coverage threshold would make masses matter, but would change the objective and would need its own case as a mirror.

I cannot honestly make this a decisive objection to Theorem 11. Finite-support distributions are within the programme’s model; co-located residents are a sensible high-multiplicity regime; and the three named NP-hardness claims transfer exactly. The negative case is that this particular anchor yields a restatement and deduplication result rather than a substantive use of continuization. That may make it a poor use of ChoCo’s time, but it does not establish that the mirror is ill-posed or that no worthwhile version exists.
