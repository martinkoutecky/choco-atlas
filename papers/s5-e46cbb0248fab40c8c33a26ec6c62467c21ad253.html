The continuous mirror question

Given two nonatomic populations partitioned into finitely many types with rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\), acceptable type pairs, strict type rankings, and costs \(c_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\), find a minimum-cost measurable one-to-one matching plan with no Pareto-improving alternative. Compare the same individuals across plans; an alternative is a Pareto improvement if almost every individual weakly prefers their new assignment and a positive mass strictly prefers it.

The model it lives in

A two-sided nonatomic matching market with type masses, type-specific rankings and costs, and measurable matching plans that pair individuals one-to-one. The objective is \(\sum_{ij} c_{ij}x_{ij}\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass matched between types \(i\) and \(j\); Pareto efficiency is evaluated by comparing each individual's assignment across plans.

The objection that survived

The paper ranks named neighbors strictly, while the proposed type market makes partners within a type interchangeable; the case asserts that this is a plausible cohort version but does not show that the original authors would accept that preference change.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover Theorem 12's minimum-cost Pareto-optimal matching hardness and Theorem 3's minimum-cost popular maximum matching algorithm. They leave the paper's other structural results and its discrete extended formulation unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the paper's 3SAT reduction survive when each type's mass can be split among partners?
  • Can this continuous problem be represented and optimized using input size polynomial in the number of supported types and the mass bit lengths?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a high-multiplicity matching market: a very large cohort of applicants and a very large cohort of one-to-one positions, with many agents sharing each type. For example, applicants may share a qualification profile and ranking of position types, while positions share a role profile and ranking of applicant types. Let \(I\) and \(J\) be the types on the two sides, with rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) encoded in binary. A type specifies the agent’s strict ranking of acceptable opposite-side types; the acceptable type pairs and their per-match costs are also fixed by type. The regime of interest has many agents but relatively few supported types, \(\tau_A=|I|\) and \(\tau_B=|J|\).

A matching is still integral for each person: no person gets a fractional partner. Formally, represent each type’s population by an interval of the specified measure, and a matching by a measurable partial bijection between the two sides. Its aggregate flow \(x_{ij}\) is the mass paired across types \(i,j\), and its cost is \(\sum_{ij}c_{ij}x_{ij}\). When comparing matchings, follow the same individuals in both plans and compare the types of their partners, with being unmatched ranked below acceptable partners. This makes the population continuous while keeping preferences, Pareto efficiency, and popularity about the matching of people. It is not a fractional outcome for an individual.

Lead: Theorem 12, proved here. Given a bipartite graph with strict preferences and edge costs in \(\{0,1\}\), the paper proves that computing a minimum-cost Pareto-optimal matching is NP-hard; it also proves that approximating this problem within any multiplicative factor is NP-hard. The paper’s Section 4 proves this by reduction from 3SAT. The continuous mirror is Type-Pareto Min-Cost Matching: given the type masses, acceptable type pairs, strict type rankings, and costs \(c_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\), find a minimum-cost matching among all matchings with no Pareto improvement—no alternative matching on the same population makes almost every agent weakly better off and a positive mass strictly better off. The output is a matching plan, not just its cost.

I tentatively expect hardness to transfer (Class B): the reduction’s combinatorics are in the preference graph and its clause gadgets, rather than in a large number of repeated individuals. The relevant questions for a prover are whether the reduction survives mass splitting, whether integer supplies per type recover enough of its gadget structure, and whether the inapproximability statement has a continuous counterpart. This is an expectation, not a consequence of Theorem 12: the theorem proves hardness for discrete agents, and a direct continuous reduction remains to be supplied.

A second, distinct anchor: Theorem 3, also proved here. For a bipartite graph with strict preferences and edge costs \(c:E\to\mathbb R\), the paper proves that a minimum-cost popular maximum matching can be found in polynomial time. Its proof gives a compact extended formulation for the popular max-matching polytope, using a map from stable matchings in an auxiliary graph. The corresponding question is Type-Popular Max-Mass Matching: among all matchings of maximum total mass, find one of minimum cost such that no other maximum-mass matching receives more total preference mass than it does. Here a voter is a member of either side, and preference mass is measured by the population mass of agents who strictly prefer one plan’s assignment to the other’s. For this mirror, take rational costs encoded in binary.

I expect this second problem to be tractable (Class A), perhaps through a type-level extended formulation or a separation algorithm. The paper’s formulation is a concrete reason to ask the question, but it does not settle it: its auxiliary construction has levels tied to \(|A|\), and expanding a type of large rational mass into individual copies need not be polynomial in the mass’s bit length. The follow-ups are whether a formulation can be bounded by the number of supported types and input bit length, and whether popularity comparisons can be separated efficiently over measurable matching plans.

The mirror covers these two preference-based matching optimization problems. It does not claim that the paper’s discrete algorithms or hardness proofs already hold for the continuous versions. The weakest point is most acute for the lead: the NP-hardness reduction is built from discrete gadget choices, and the continuous population permits agents within a type to be split across different partners. The type-level scenario is still a plausible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem; whether that splitting preserves the hardness is precisely an open question the mirror makes visible.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s comparisons are identity-based. Each node ranks named neighbors, and \(\phi(M,N)\) compares the assignments of the same people in two matchings. A type must therefore preserve the full neighbor list, not just a ranking of role or applicant categories. Replicating a population changes those lists; replacing them with rankings over types makes partners within a type interchangeable and changes the preference model. That is a real gap between the paper’s stated problem and both proposed mirrors.

It does not defeat the better version of either mirror. A large market with cohorts that rank counterpart types is plausible, and the programme allows the natural mass-scale version of Pareto improvement: no one is worse off, with a positive mass strictly better off. Theorem 12’s reduction does not establish hardness for that version, but the missing reduction is not a reason the question is unworthy. Theorem 3 has the same issue: popularity must compare the same individuals across plans, so a type-flow matrix alone may not encode preference gains. Modeling full measurable matching plans or the relevant couplings makes the question harder to formulate computationally, but that difficulty could itself be the research problem.

The paper’s results on popular fractional matchings do not close this gap: fractional assignments to a finite set of people are not a continuous population. Neither result is already answered continuously by the paper.

So the negative case is weak. I can argue that the proposed mirrors are not automatic continuizations of the theorems, and that the bridge from named-neighbor preferences to type preferences needs careful justification. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile cohort-based model that the authors would recognize. Both anchors survive that objection.
