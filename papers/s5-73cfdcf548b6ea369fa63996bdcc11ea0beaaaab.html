The strongest candidate regime would be a large school-choice market with many students but relatively few complete preference rankings, and object capacities that scale with population size. A continuous assignment model could represent each ranking \(t\) by mass \(\mu_t\), each school by per-capita capacity \(\lambda_o\), and choose assignment masses \(x_{t,o}\) to minimize \(\sum_{t,o}\mu_t R(o,t)x_{t,o}\), subject to the type-mass and capacity constraints. That is a sensible continuous allocation problem, but it is not an anchored mirror of Theorem 1 or 2. In a nonatomic population, one student’s report changes no mass and so cannot affect the assignment; allowing a positive-mass cohort to deviate changes the paper’s individual dominance question into a coalition question. The URM’s uniform choice over finitely many optimal deterministic assignments also has no immediate canonical counterpart in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 establish strategic dominance properties; Proposition 3 concerns equilibrium and waste. They do not give the complexity of a computational problem, or an algorithm for one. So none is a valid anchor under the stated brief.

The proposed school-market model is sensible: represent preference rankings by masses \(\mu_t\), and scale capacities with the population. Minimizing total reported rank then gives a transportation LP. That is a legitimate continuous population model, but it mirrors the assignment rule’s optimization problem, not the paper’s strategic results. And it does not by itself mirror the URM: the LP describes the face of rank-minimizing assignments, while URM selects uniformly among finite deterministic optima. A continuum does not specify a corresponding selection rule.

The best attempt to recover the strategic results would be a mass-deviation question: how much population must switch from truthful reports to ODS reports to change the assignment or make it wasteful? That could be a useful campaigning problem. But a positive mass acting together is a coalition, whereas Theorems 1 and 2 concern one agent deviating against every profile of other agents. In a nonatomic population, a single agent has measure zero and cannot change the aggregate allocation. Giving that agent positive mass changes the question rather than rescuing the theorem’s mirror.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchored computational mirror. It is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be built from it: the mass-deviation problem is a credible follow-up, but it would be a new computational question, not a continuization of a computational result in this paper.
