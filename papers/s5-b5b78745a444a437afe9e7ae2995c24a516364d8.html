What the mirror covers

No qualifying computational result is mirrored; the paper’s fixed-problem and variable-problem CJTs give probabilistic asymptotic conclusions, while the rest analyzes whether their assumptions are justified under different notions of uncertainty.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is that the paper’s jury setting naturally admits a population description: a type could be a voter’s conditional response probabilities \(p^0\) and \(p^1\), and its mass the fraction of voters with those probabilities. The aggregate question would concern the mass voting correctly as the electorate grows.

But that is only an analytic reformulation of the paper’s subject, not a mirror of a computational result. The “fixed-problem CJT” and “variable-problem CJT” are unnumbered probability limit theorems: each says that, under stated competence and independence assumptions, the probability of a correct majority tends to one. The paper gives no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity classification or algorithmic result to anchor a ChoCo question.

So there are no qualifying anchors here, and I can’t honestly state a continuous computational problem per anchor. The type-mass sketch also leaves out the paper’s central issue: dependence caused by shared circumstances. A distribution over individual response types alone does not capture that dependence.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no computational result to mirror. Its fixed-problem and variable-problem CJTs are genuine limit theorems, but they say what follows probabilistically from competence and independence assumptions. The paper asks whether those assumptions are justified under different notions of uncertainty. It gives no computational problem, encoded input, algorithm, or complexity classification. That is a strong reason to reject a ChoCo mirror anchored in this paper.

The response-type sketch does not supply the missing target. Let \(t\) specify conditional response probabilities \(p^0(t)\) and \(p^1(t)\), with mass distribution \(\mu\). Under conditional independence, the relevant average competence is \(\bar p^x=\int p^x(t)\,d\mu(t)\). For finite rational support, computing it is just \(\sum_t\mu_t p^x(t)\); the CJT then supplies the asymptotic conclusion when \(\bar p^x>1/2\). That is a direct population description of the theorem’s assumptions, but it yields no computational question of the kind the programme studies.

The strongest repair is to represent shared circumstances explicitly: use a common context \(C\) and a type distribution \(\mu_c\) conditional on each context. This captures the paper’s central distinction and can support useful analysis of how majority reliability varies across contexts. A computational task could also be built around a compact model of those contexts, or around interventions on voters. But it would require adding an inference or optimization objective that the paper does not formulate. It would be a new project adjacent to the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.

The population setting is plausible, and treating voters in large electorates by type and mass is sensible. The negative case does not rest on denying that. Its limit is narrower: the paper offers no computational anchor, and the most faithful continuization remains an analytic restatement. The claim that no related computational problem could ever be worthwhile would be too strong; the case that this paper merits a ChoCo mirror is weak.
