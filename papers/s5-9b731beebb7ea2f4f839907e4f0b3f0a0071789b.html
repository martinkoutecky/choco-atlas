The continuous mirror question

Given three facilities at rational positions on a line, normalized rational capacities \(\kappa_j\), a threshold \(\alpha\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) on supported report profiles \(t=(\pi_t,A_t)\), let \(R_t\) be the locations consistent with each report. A deterministic rule observes the report masses and chooses \(x_{t,j}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{t,j}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,j}=\kappa_j\). Nature may place each assigned portion of report mass at any location in its \(R_t\); compare the resulting assignment cost with the full-information minimum cost for that same placement and capacities. What is the infimum, over such rules, of their worst-case distortion over inputs and consistent placements; is it \(2\)?

The model it lives in

A continuum of applicants on a line, with mass \(\mu_t\) for each observed report profile and per-capita facility supplies \(\kappa_j\). The rule allocates each report mass across facilities; latent locations remain uncertain within the profile’s consistency set, and the benchmark is the optimal full-information mass assignment for the realized locations.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5.7’s three-facility lower bound and Theorem 5.6’s general \(2\)-distortion algorithm. It leaves aside the other information combinations, their distortion bounds, and the separate lower bound for \(\mathrm{ORD}+\mathrm{APP}\).

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the lower bound of \(2\) persist when every facility has per-capita capacity bounded below by a fixed positive constant?
  • What is the complexity of computing an optimal mass assignment for a fixed report profile under this robust distortion objective?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible population mirror here because the paper’s lower bound already relies on a large electorate. Its central question is still the authors’ question: how well can a matching algorithm minimize total distance when it sees only partial information about agents’ locations?

The natural regime is a large school district or employment system assigning applicants to facilities along a corridor. There may be hundreds of thousands of applicants but relatively few distinct reported profiles. A type is the complete information available to the assignment rule: an ordinal ranking \(\pi\) and an approval set \(A\), under a known threshold \(\alpha\). The type’s feasible location set is the set of line positions consistent with those reports and the known facility locations. Applicants with the same type are indistinguishable to the rule; their actual positions are hidden states over which the robust guarantee must hold. Facility capacities become per-capita supplies, and the rule assigns fractions of each type’s mass to facilities. That fractional action is the natural aggregate form of assigning each applicant to one facility.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 5.7, proved in this paper. It states that every algorithm using \(\mathrm{DIST}+\mathrm{APP}+\mathrm{ORD}\) has distortion at least \(2\), even with three facilities. The paper’s construction makes the population-size connection especially clear: for \(\alpha<3\), it uses two large groups and a middle facility with unit capacity; as \(n\) grows, that facility’s per-capita capacity vanishes and the lower-bound ratio tends to \((5\alpha+1)/(3\alpha-1)>2\). For \(\alpha\ge3\), the proof uses a separate lower-bound case. The result is proved here; it is a distortion lower bound, not an NP-hardness theorem.

A prover-ready mirror is Three-Facility Mass Distortion. An input gives three rationally located facilities on a line, normalized rational capacities \(\kappa_j\) summing to \(1\), a threshold \(\alpha\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) on the supported report types \(t=(\pi_t,A_t)\). For each type, let \(R_t\) be its set of consistent locations. A rule chooses \(x_{t,j}\ge0\), with \(\sum_jx_{t,j}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tx_{t,j}=\kappa_j\). Nature may place each type’s mass anywhere in \(R_t\), including distributing it differently among the portions assigned to different facilities. The rule’s distortion is the supremum, over those placements, of its total distance cost divided by the minimum cost achievable with full knowledge of the placed population and the same capacities. The question is whether every deterministic rule has worst-case distortion at least \(2\) on some three-facility input; a lower-bound solution is a report profile and adversarial placements forcing ratio arbitrarily close to \(2\), whatever feasible mass assignment the rule chooses.

The companion anchor is Theorem 5.6, also proved in this paper. It says that the authors’ \(\mathrm{GeneralAlgorithm}\), using \(\mathrm{DIST}+\mathrm{APP}+\mathrm{ORD}\) with \(\alpha=3\), achieves distortion at most \(2\) for every instance. Its continuous mirror uses the same input model and asks for a feasible mass assignment whose distortion is at most \(2\) for every consistent placement. A solution is an allocation rule from mass profiles to feasible matrices \(x\), with that guarantee. The paper’s recursive algorithm fills capacities from ordered groups of agents; its mass version fills them by portions of report-type groups. The Theorem 5.7 lower bound is the stronger anchor for the continuous case, because its construction already takes a population-size limit. Together, the two anchors suggest that the worst-case distortion of this continuous problem is exactly \(2\).

For the upper-bound question, I expect a Class A result: the paper supplies the structural allocation rule, and its mass version appears directly implementable from the supported types and capacities. The lower-bound question asks whether the finite obstruction survives normalization; the theorem’s limiting construction gives a strong reason to expect that it does. Further questions include whether the bound changes when every facility must have a capacity bounded away from zero per capita, and whether the exact fixed-profile robust assignment problem has an efficient formulation. The anchors establish a \(2\)-distortion rule and a matching worst-case barrier; they do not establish polynomial-time solvability of that exact robust optimization problem.

The paper contains no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, or parameterized-hard. Its relevant named results are approximation guarantees for distortion, so the mirror inherits an algorithm-design question rather than the programme’s usual complexity-classification question.

The weakest point is the type model. The reports \((\pi,A)\) do not determine an agent’s true distances; those remain hidden and enter through the adversarial realization. This mirror is convincing if the object of study is a robust assignment rule over masses of observationally indistinguishable agents. It is less direct if ChoCo requires every type to specify known, exact costs. The authors should nevertheless recognize the question: it keeps their facilities, assignment constraints, information channels, social-cost objective, and worst-case distortion, while replacing finite groups of applicants with masses. The paper itself studies finite assignments, so its result is not already a continuous-population formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that both anchors require a robust hidden-location model that does not fit ChoCo’s usual type model. I can’t make that objection airtight: the mass extension looks plausible, and the paper’s use of large groups is evidence for a high-multiplicity regime.

Theorem 5.7 is the stronger anchor, and its \(n\to\infty\) construction is genuine support for the proponent. But a report type \((\pi,A)\) does not determine an agent’s distances or their contribution to either the assignment cost or the full-information optimum. Agents with the same report can occupy different locations. Treating \((\pi,A)\) as the type therefore groups agents whose costs differ. Expanding the type to include exact location fixes that, but if the rule is given the location-type masses, it receives information the paper’s algorithms do not have. To preserve the paper’s information restriction, the mirror must instead give the rule report masses and let an adversary choose hidden locations consistent with them. That is a coherent robust uncertainty problem, but it is not simply a population distribution over complete types.

The same issue affects Theorem 5.6. Its recursive algorithm appears easy to extend from counts to masses, and its proof may carry over by replacing sums over agents with sums over mass. But the resulting guarantee is for the robust hidden-location extension, not for a society whose supplied type masses determine its costs. The distinct, potentially harder task—choosing a mass assignment that minimizes worst-case distortion for a given report profile—adds an objective and uncertainty set that the paper does not specify.

So the best negative case is a scope objection: these anchors do not directly yield ChoCo’s standard computational problem without deciding how unobserved locations are represented and who knows their distribution. If the authors accept the robust model, however, the objection largely gives way: Theorem 5.7 is a strong lower-bound anchor, and Theorem 5.6 likely has a fractional analogue. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror survives; this is a weak negative case, and its weakness is exactly the plausibility of that robust formulation.
