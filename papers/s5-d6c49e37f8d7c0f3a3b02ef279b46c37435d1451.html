The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize when choice rules can be represented by normalized welfare orderings or functions. Theorem 3 characterizes relative fair choice rules; Proposition 1 and Proposition 2 characterize relative maximin and relative utilitarian rules; Proposition 3 characterizes the KS choice rule; and Theorem 4 gives a two-person characterization. These are axiomatic results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class.

The paper’s \(\arg\max\) formulas do not supply a computational anchor: they represent choice over an arbitrary compact feasible set \(X\), without an input encoding, algorithm, or complexity analysis. So there is no continuous problem per qualifying anchor to hand to a prover under the requested standard.

A plausible follow-on would model a large workforce as a distribution over finitely many skill and productivity types, with a policy inducing feasible per-capita utility vectors and type-specific ability maxima. Computing a relative-fair choice over those vectors would put population mass at the center of the model. But that would be a new computational project inspired by the paper’s welfare criteria; this paper gives no result about its complexity. Its existing continuity is in the feasible outcome space, while the number of individuals remains finite.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case starts with a clean point: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize which choice rules admit normalized welfare representations; Theorem 3 characterizes the relative fair rules; Propositions 1–3 and Theorem 4 give further axiomatic characterizations. None states an algorithmic or complexity result. Their \(\arg\max\) expressions range over an arbitrary compact feasible set \(X\), with no input encoding or representation of \(X\). So none supplies a computational problem whose continuous-population version this programme could chart.

The strongest proposed mirror would take a large workforce partitioned into finitely many skill types, describe feasible utility assignments by type, and optimize a relative-fair welfare criterion over those assignments. That is a coherent possible project, but the paper does not specify its computational object. The abilities \(b_i(X)\) are maxima derived from the whole feasible set, not fixed attributes of workers. And a type’s average utility generally does not retain the distribution needed to evaluate individual maximin or other inequality-averse criteria. Keeping that distribution requires a new feasible-set model over utility assignments. Whether the resulting problem is easy or hard would then depend on the production, resource, and allocation constraints supplied by that model. The paper contributes a possible objective, not the computational structure.

This also defeats a stronger version of the same mirror: carry the population distribution through exactly, rather than aggregating each skill type to an average. That may yield an interesting infinite-dimensional welfare-optimization problem, but its domain, encoding, and algorithmic questions all have to be chosen afresh. With a polyhedral finite-type model, some criteria may reduce to linear or convex optimization; with discrete technologies, hardness may come from those technologies. Neither outcome is a complexity result latent in the paper’s axioms.

So the paper-specific negative case is strong: there is no computational anchor here, and the workforce proposal is an adjacent research idea rather than a continuization of a result. The universal claim is harder to defend honestly. A computational study of relative-fair choices over a well-specified type-based allocation model could be worthwhile. But that would establish the value of a new model, not a continuous mirror of this paper.
