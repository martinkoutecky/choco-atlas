The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest possible positive angle is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s Karma price-design problem. But the paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its results are presented through equations, algorithms, figures, and tables. The stationary Nash equilibrium existence claim is attributed to earlier work, not stated as a numbered result proved here. So there is no eligible named anchor, and I can’t honestly supply an anchor-based case.

The unanchored candidate is: take a large commuter population with rational masses \(\mu_t\) over a small support of types \(t\), where each type specifies its discount factor, urgency process, and relevant costs. Given the Karma rules, initial balances, and route travel-time functions, find a threshold \(q\) and a stationary social state \((\pi,d)\) that satisfy agents’ best responses and the population’s stationarity conditions, while minimizing aggregate travel time. The output would be \(q\), \(\pi\), \(d\), and the resulting mass using each route. A large, repeated commuter cohort with relatively few distinct urgency and preference types is a plausible regime, and the authors would recognize this as their bridge-pricing problem.

The weakness is substantial: the paper already models population shares over type, urgency, and Karma balance, computes stationary equilibria, and searches for prices that produce the desired traffic split. That makes this candidate close to a restatement of the paper’s existing population model. The paper offers useful computational material, especially its bridge-tolling results, but those results are not the kind of numbered complexity anchor required here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to continuize: the paper proves no complexity theorem about a discrete problem, and its stationary-equilibrium existence claim is attributed to earlier work. Its own contributions are a model, a numerical procedure, software, and a bridge-tolling case study. The proponent was right to offer no named anchor; that leaves no paper result for a ChoCo mirror to take as its starting point.

The strongest candidate is to give commuter types rational masses and optimize a Karma price or threshold \(q\), minimizing travel time subject to a stationary equilibrium. But the paper already represents its population by the distribution \(d_p[\tau,u,k]\), computes the social state \((\pi_p,d_p)\), and searches prices to obtain target route shares. Recasting that state distribution as a society over types would restate the paper’s existing population model. An exact or approximation-complexity analysis of its equilibrium solver could be useful, but it would be algorithmics for an already population-based game—not a discrete-to-continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

That is a strong case against a worthwhile *ChoCo mirror*, though not a proof that no adjacent research question could be valuable. The paper already makes the population aggregate and computationally studies its behavior; the missing piece is complexity analysis, not continuization.
