The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying case for a continuous computational mirror: the paper has no named result asserting that a problem is tractable, hard, or in a parameterized complexity class.

The nearest result is Theorem 4, proved here. It says that a binary social choice function on the full weak preference domain is group strategy-proof if and only if it is powerless revealing, and that it has a unique generating ineffective family. Theorem 5, also proved here, gives the strict-preference version: group strategy-proof functions are exactly those represented by a unique downward-inclusive family \(H\), with \(a\) selected exactly when the set preferring \(b\) belongs to \(H\). These are functional characterizations, not computational anchors.

There is a tempting population angle: the authors allow \(I\) to be infinite, so one could take the agents to form a continuum. But the paper has no measure over preference types, mass-valued decisions, or computational problem. Merely taking \(I\) to be a continuum does not supply the continuous-population model or complexity question ChoCo requires. A mass-based, anonymous restriction might motivate a follow-on problem, but it would be a new question, not a mirror of a named computational result here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 4 characterizes binary group strategy-proof functions by ineffective families; Theorem 5 gives the strict-preference characterization by downward-inclusive families. Neither states an algorithmic problem or a complexity bound. Their uniqueness claims are representation results, not computational ones. So neither theorem is a surviving anchor under ChoCo’s criteria.

The strongest population version is easy to state. Let a society be a distribution over the two strict preference types, or over the three weak preference types, and restrict attention to anonymous rules. The strict case reduces to the mass \(p\) of agents who prefer \(b\); the weak case lies in a two-dimensional simplex. One could characterize which regions of those spaces define group strategy-proof rules. That is a sensible axiomatic follow-on, and the paper’s results motivate it. It still asks for a characterization of rules, not a computational question.

To turn that into computation, one would have to choose an input representation for a rule and add a task: evaluate it, test strategy-proofness, or find the least mass transfer that changes the outcome. The paper chooses none of these. With a succinct quota or polyhedral rule, evaluation and many natural checks concern a fixed, low-dimensional space. With an arbitrary rule, its encoding determines the complexity. A mass-transfer task also needs a cost model the paper does not supply. Those could become worthwhile projects, but they would be newly specified problems, not continuous mirrors of the paper’s named results.

The negative case is strongest at the boundary of “mirror”: a continuous, anonymous axiomatic extension is plausible, but it does not meet this programme’s computational criterion. This cannot rule out a valuable later project built from the paper; it does show that the paper itself supplies no computational anchor for one.
