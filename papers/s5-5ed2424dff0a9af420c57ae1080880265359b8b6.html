Why no mirror

The replicated EFX-feasibility question is a coherent continuous-population extension. But Theorems 1 and 2 prove nonexistence examples; they do not give an algorithm, a computational hardness result, or another computational claim to mirror, so bit (a) fails.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only the EFX nonexistence results in Theorems 1 and 2, with separate XOS and matroid-nullity cost restrictions; it leaves the cited positive results and the open submodular case alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the replicated XOS profile from Theorem 1 admit an EFX allocation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is to keep the chores indivisible for each person and continuize only the population. Consider a large organization with employees in a fixed set of cost types and recurring chore templates. Copies of a type have identical cost functions; each employee still receives an integral bundle. The allocation variable is the mass of each employee type assigned each bundle. Thus fractional mass records how many interchangeable employees receive a bundle; it does not give any employee a fractional chore.

For the paper’s word gadget, let \(C\) be its 53 chore templates: the two full cyclic orbits of \(a=011111100011001110\) and \(b=011101110010011001\), together with the nonzero cyclic shifts of \(d=100010101011010111\). Let \(i\in[18]\) be the employee types, with mass \(1/18\) each, and let each chore template have supply \(1/18\). This is the normalized large-population regime: with \(q\) employees of each type and \(q\) copies of each chore template, there are \(18q\) employees and \(53q\) chores, while the numbers of employee and chore types stay fixed.

For precision, write \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{C}\) for an individual’s finite multiset of chores, counting each copied chore separately. A candidate allocation is a family of masses \(y_{i,b}\ge 0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_b y_{i,b}=\frac1{18}\quad\text{for each }i, \qquad \sum_{i,b} y_{i,b}b_g=\frac1{18}\quad\text{for each }g\in C. \]
Require finite expected bundle size and require EFX between every pair of bundles that occur with positive mass. In particular, a type-\(i\) employee receiving \(b\) must, for every supported bundle \(b'\), satisfy
\[ c_i(b)\le c_i(b') \quad\text{or}\quad c_i(b-e_g)\le c_i(b')\ \text{for every }g\text{ with }b_g>0. \]
Here \(e_g\) removes one copy of chore \(g\). This is a feasibility question: find such an EFX allocation, or determine that none exists. The paper has no welfare objective to preserve. The continuous part is the distribution of people over bundles, not the bundles themselves.

My lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that there is an instance with 18 agents and 53 chores in which every cost function is monotone XOS with binary marginals, no complete EFX allocation exists, and each cost function is the maximum of two binary additive functions. Its continuous mirror is Continuous Replicated XOS-EFX Feasibility: given rational masses of employee cost types, rational supplies of chore templates, and for each type a cost function that is the maximum of two binary additive clauses, decide whether a mass assignment to integral multiset bundles satisfies the supply equations and EFX constraints. The paper’s word gadget with the masses and supplies above is a distinguished instance of this problem.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. It states that there is an instance with 18 agents and 53 chores in which every cost function is monotone supermodular with binary marginals, no complete EFX allocation exists, and every cost function is the nullity function of a rank-two partition matroid. Its mirror is Continuous Replicated Matroid-Nullity-EFX Feasibility: the same mass-allocation question, restricted to type costs of the form
\[ c_i(b)=\max\{z_i(b)-1,0\}+\max\{o_i(b)-1,0\}, \]
where \(z_i(b)\) and \(o_i(b)\) count chore copies in the two parts of type \(i\)’s partition. Theorem 2 uses a separate cost profile on the same chore templates, so it gives a separate mirror question.

These are named existence counterexamples, not NP-hardness or polynomial-time theorems. Neither anchor proves that its continuous mirror is infeasible: once each cost type has many interchangeable employees, mass can be split across different integral bundles, and the finite obstruction may disappear. For the general feasibility families, my low-confidence forecast is Class C: masses are continuous, but selecting a compatible support of indivisible bundles remains combinatorial. That is a conjecture to investigate, not a consequence of either theorem; there is no hardness-transfer argument here. A finite-support theorem or a useful pricing formulation could instead put either problem in Class A.

The scenario is plausible at the level of repeated chore assignment: many employees share a fixed set of cost profiles, and task templates recur as the organization grows. The word signatures themselves are deliberately constructed, though, and may be hard to motivate as a real cost profile. More fundamentally, the paper studies whether EFX allocations exist, not their computational complexity. The mirror therefore covers only the two exact nonexistence results, and its strongest next questions are whether either replicated profile admits EFX, whether that answer persists at large finite \(q\), and whether the general feasibility problem has a finite-support or algorithmic characterization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that Theorems 1 and 2 are finite nonexistence results, not computational results. They give no algorithm, complexity bound, or parameterized problem. The proposed replicated feasibility problems therefore introduce a new computational agenda; they do not continuize a computational result established by this paper. The two anchors also share one gadget and one matching argument, so they amount to one new feasibility question with two cost-function restrictions.

The gap is concrete. The proofs depend on exactly 18 agents and 53 one-off chores: bundle counts force a set \(R\) of size \(r\), and the resulting \(r\) disjoint pairs contradict a matching bound on the 53-word graph. Replicating agents and chore templates changes those counts into cohort masses and chore supplies. The paper supplies no weighted version of its matching lemma, and no algorithmic structure for choosing a compatible support of integral bundles. EFX imposes pairwise conditions across every bundle in that support, making the proposed problem a substantial new feasibility model rather than an LP or pricing problem suggested by the paper.

That is a reason to rank this mirror below papers whose results already define a complexity landscape. It does not defeat the improved mirror. A large organization with recurring task templates and a fixed set of complete worker cost types is a coherent high-multiplicity regime. Each worker can still receive an integral bundle, and EFX remains an individual comparison. Neither multiplicity nor the meaning of the property disappears in the limit. The paper’s finite gadget may also be artificial, but the same allocation problem can be posed for a plausible repeated-task setting.

So the honest negative case is limited: these theorems do not themselves establish a ChoCo-style computational contribution, and their proof gives little help in formulating one. I cannot honestly rule out the replicated EFX-feasibility question as worthwhile. The proponent’s strengthened formulation survives the strongest objections available here; asserting that no scenario supports a useful mirror would go beyond what the paper warrants.
