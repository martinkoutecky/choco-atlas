The continuous mirror question

Given a finite item catalogue \(J\), a periodic turn word \(w\) over at most eleven agent types, an \(\mathit{OXS}\) valuation template \(u_t\) for each type, rational per-capita supplies \(r_j\), and accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\), consider every admissible scale \(N\): \(N\) agents follow \(w\), each category \(j\) has \(Nr_j\) distinct indivisible copies, and agents play the resulting finite round-robin game. For each scale, form the type-conditioned histograms of integral bundles over all SPNEs. Compute a rational histogram within \(\varepsilon\) in \(L_1\) distance of some subsequential limit of these histograms as \(N\to\infty\).

The model it lives in

The society has type masses \(\mu_t\) given by the frequencies in \(w\); each type shares its complete valuation template and schedule role. Each scale retains indivisible goods and finite-game SPNE. The output \(\pi_t(b)\) records the limiting fraction of type \(t\) receiving each integral bundle \(b\).

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a scaling-limit mirror of Theorem 17, proved in this paper. The theorem says that \(\textsc{PromiseRoundRobinSPNE}_{11}\) is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard even when all agents’ utilities are \(\mathit{OXS}\). In the promise problem, the input gives the agents’ utilities, a target agent \(i\), and a threshold \(v\); it is promised that either every SPNE gives \(i\) utility at least \(v\), or every SPNE gives \(i\) utility below \(v\), and the task is to decide which case holds. The paper proves this by reducing from SAT.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large draft among organizations that share a small number of exact roster-valuation templates. The paper’s sports-team motivation fits: \(\mathit{OXS}\) valuations model assigning drafted players to positions. Imagine many clubs with one of eleven recurring valuation profiles, ordered in a repeating draft schedule. Each profile’s mass is its share of the clubs. The player pool grows in proportion to the number of clubs, with each player category supplied at a fixed rate per club. This requires genuine homogeneity: clubs in a type share the same valuation and the same position in the schedule. The many clubs are individuals in the game, not a single coordinated bloc.

I would call the mirror Limit-\(\mathit{OXS}\)-RoundRobin-SPNE. Its input is a finite catalogue \(J\) of item categories, a periodic turn word \(w\) over \(K\leq 11\) types, rational per-capita supplies \(r_j\) for \(j\in J\), and an \(\mathit{OXS}\) valuation template \(u_t\) for each type. The type masses are the frequencies \(\mu_t\) in \(w\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Item copies are distinct indivisible goods but have the same category-specific values.

At each scale \(N\), take \(N\) individual agents whose type sequence is \(w\) repeated, and \(Nr_j\) copies of category \(j\), for scales where these counts are integers. Run the paper’s round-robin game and consider the allocations induced by its SPNEs. Record, for each type \(t\), the fraction \(\pi_t(b)\) of its agents who receive each integral bundle \(b\). A solution is an aggregate allocation \(\pi=(\pi_t)_t\) that is a limit point of such histograms along some sequence of scales and SPNE allocations. Thus the population is continuous at the type level, while every individual still receives an integral bundle. A computational version takes an accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\) and asks for a rational histogram within \(\varepsilon\) of some valid limit point. The individual objective remains utility maximization at every subgame; the reported type-level utility is \(\sum_b\pi_t(b)u_t(b)\).

The authors could recognize this as a continuous form of their underlying problem: it keeps round-robin, indivisible goods at every finite scale, \(\mathit{OXS}\) preferences, and SPNE as the equilibrium concept. It aggregates only the outcomes of agents who share a complete type. The mirror covers the equilibrium-computation result in Theorem 17, rather than the paper’s fairness or equilibrium-count results.

I would initially expect this limit problem to be Class A. As \(N\) grows, one agent’s pick changes a category’s stock by \(1/N\), so the individual strategic feedback from steering future availability disappears in the limit. What remains is a dynamic allocation of mass among a fixed number of \(\mathit{OXS}\) types, a plausible target for a configuration-flow LP or related continuous optimization. A central question is whether subgame-perfect behavior in the finite games converges to a tractable flow description; another is whether those limits are unique.

The weak point is substantial: Theorem 17 is hard with exactly eleven agents, and its SAT reduction uses strategically contested, distinct items. Scaling the population and cloning item categories changes that contest. The theorem’s hardness therefore does not establish hardness of this limit problem, and the limiting game may suppress precisely the strategic effects that make the authors’ problem difficult. The mirror is strongest as a concrete, testable question about whether their SPNE complexity survives in a large homogeneous draft; it is not a claim that their reduction already survives continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 17 is a real computational anchor: \(\textsc{PromiseRoundRobinSPNE}_{11}\) is \(\mathsf{NP}\)-hard for \(\mathit{OXS}\) utilities. The case against a mirror cannot rest on a lack of computational results, or on the fact that high-multiplicity elections already exist. The stronger objection is that this theorem’s object is an atomic sequential game, and the proposed limit does not preserve that object in an evident way.

In the finite game, an agent’s pick changes what the next named agent can choose. SPNE requires optimal behavior after every history, including histories that do not occur on the equilibrium path. The SAT construction uses those exact, alternating choices and credible continuations. In a population scaled by \(N\), one person is mass \(1/N\). In a true atomless limit, that person cannot change the aggregate state. A mass-flow or mean-field formulation can make sense, but it replaces individual turns and subgame-perfect strategies with a different equilibrium problem. Retaining the individual turns instead means carrying an ordered history and contingent strategy tree whose size grows with \(N\); a histogram of bundles does not encode whether the continuations are credible. This is a change in the strategic object, not a technical issue about how to extend indivisible goods or ties.

The proposed histogram limit faces a related problem. The paper asks whether one designated agent gets utility above a threshold in every SPNE. Replacing that agent’s payoff with the average payoff of a type is a sensible population-level objective, but it is a different predicate: the average can hide a small number of agents with very different outcomes. Keeping a tagged agent preserves the theorem’s objective, but that agent remains an atom whose behavior need not be represented by the population distribution.

The strongest repair is to repeat many copies of the eleven-player SAT game, with one isolated item pool per copy, and aggregate their outcomes. That keeps the strategic logic intact, but the computation is still the SPNE computation of a finite eleven-player game; aggregation adds no algorithmic content. Sharing a pool across copies restores a single large draft, but then picks couple the copies and the original subgame structure no longer follows. A periodic schedule and repeated valuation templates make the large draft plausible, but they do not resolve this fork.

This is a substantial negative case, not a proof that no useful mirror exists. One could study computational complexity of a dynamic mean-field equilibrium, or ask whether finite-game SPNE outcomes converge to a tractable aggregate description. Either could be worthwhile. The weakness is that the second question keeps the closest link to Theorem 17, while the first changes its equilibrium concept. I would therefore resist treating the proponent’s limit as an established continuous mirror of the theorem, while conceding that a carefully specified convergence problem could still make a strong one.
