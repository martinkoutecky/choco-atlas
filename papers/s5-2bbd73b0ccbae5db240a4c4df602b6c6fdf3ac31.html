The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) over complete worker types \(t\), two task types \(x,y\), their weights \(w^x,w^y\), per-worker task supplies \(s_x,s_y\), and each type's valuations and productivities, compute a market-clearing, per-capita balanced DPM equilibrium and its aggregate productivity, including any mass split at the relative-valuation cutoff.

The model it lives in

Use a population with mass \(\mu_t\) of each worker type and type-level expected assignments \(z_{t,x},z_{t,y}\). Enforce aggregate supplies \(\sum_t\mu_t z_{t,j}=s_j\), equal expected workload per type, and DPM equilibrium demand at common task prices with type-specific purchasing-power ratios; report productivity \(\sum_t\mu_t(Y_t^x z_{t,x}+Y_t^y z_{t,y})\). In the two-task case, the allocation follows the relative-valuation cutoff, with the boundary type's mass splittable.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying named computational anchor in this paper. The numbered results concern equilibrium existence and welfare (Theorems 1–3), balance guarantees (Theorem 4), productivity formulas and limits (Theorems 5–6), and threshold structure (Proposition 7). They do not classify a computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized, or give an algorithm with a running-time guarantee.

The closest candidate is Theorem 6, proved here: under its two-task statistical assumptions, it gives an exact expression for DPM’s expected productivity using binomial CDFs. Proposition 7 supplies the threshold allocation structure behind that expression. These are useful characterizations, but calling either an “in P” result would overstate what the paper proves.

There is a plausible continuous-population follow-up: model workers as a distribution over complete preference and productivity types, scale task arrivals with workforce size, and ask for a productivity-maximizing assignment of type mass to task types subject to per-capita balance. But that would be a new computational question, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper. Since there are no qualifying anchors, I can’t honestly supply the requested one-problem-per-anchor case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Its propositions and theorems establish balance, efficiency, equilibrium existence, asymptotic behavior, and productivity formulas. The proof of balanced-equilibrium existence uses a fixed-point argument; it does not give a computational algorithm or a complexity guarantee. So there is no result here that classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard.

The closest anchor is Proposition 7 and Theorem 6: workers are sorted by relative valuation, and the resulting DPM allocation yields an expected-productivity formula. A continuous population of workers is quite plausible for the call-center or agency settings. In the paper’s two-task binary-productivity model, workers could be grouped by their complete valuation and productivity types, with type masses and task arrivals scaled per worker. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; worker identity is not a sound objection.

But the proposed type-mass optimization changes the question. The paper characterizes DPM’s allocation and productivity; it does not optimize a planner’s assignment of type mass. One could instead ask for a continuous evaluation of DPM’s productivity under a type distribution, but that would extend a statistical characterization, not mirror a computational result. The paper already studies a large-population limit analytically, including the productivity gain under its restricted assumptions. That limit is not a continuous computational treatment, but it makes the gap to a ChoCo-style complexity result especially clear.

So the negative case succeeds only in the narrower sense: this paper supplies no named computational anchor, and the suggested optimization is a new problem rather than a mirror of one of its results. It does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The high-multiplicity workplace setting is credible, and computation of the equilibrium that DPM requires could be a worthwhile adjacent question if the authors recognize it as part of their problem. The honest negative case is therefore weak beyond the absence of a qualifying anchor.
