The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support distribution μ over positive rational valuation vectors \(v\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^k\), represented by τ supported vectors and positive binary-rational masses summing to 1, return every supported \(v\) for which there is no other supported \(w\) with \(w\succ v\). The output may include each maximal type’s mass μ_v.

The model it lives in

Types are complete valuation vectors \(v\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^k\), each carrying mass μ_v. The decision is which supported types are Pareto maximal; there is no additional optimization objective, and the masses can be reported with the maximal types.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers frontier computation in Proposition 1, with τ distinct valuation types as the support; it leaves the strategic mechanism results, including Theorems 2 and 3, aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s classical Maximum Vector problem, not its strategic payment mechanism. A type is a complete \(k\)-dimensional valuation vector \(v\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}^k\); \(\mu_v\) is the fraction of a large population with that vector. The input is the \(\tau\) supported types and their binary rational masses. The task is to return
\[ \{(v,\mu_v):v\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu),\ \nexists w\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\text{ with }w\succeq v\text{ and }w\ne v\}. \]
That is, identify the Pareto-maximal types and report the population share at each. There is no additional optimization objective: the decision is which supported types belong to the frontier.

A plausible regime is a large procurement or service platform with many providers in standardized cohorts, where providers in a cohort have the same valuation vector over the criteria used for selection. The population can be large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct profiles stays modest. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s underlying problem: the original task computes the frontier of vectors, and the type-level answer compactly represents which providers are on it.

My anchor is Proposition 1, cited from Kung, Luccio, and Preparata (1975), not proved in this paper. It states that for a finite set \(F\) of \(k\)-dimensional vectors, \(MAX(F)\) can be computed in \(O(|F|\log |F|)\) time for \(k=2,3\), and in at most \(O(|F|(\log |F|)^{k-2})\) time for \(k\ge4\). In the mirror, \(|F|=\tau\); for fixed \(k\), exact comparisons of binary rational coordinates give polynomial running time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). The expected outcome is therefore tractable.

This covers only the computation of the maximum-vector frontier. It does not continuize the paper’s strategic contribution. In particular, Theorem 2, proved here, says that mechanism \(\mathcal M\) is equilibria-truthful under the distinct-values (DV) assumption. That is an incentive result, not a complexity classification; repeated high-multiplicity types can also violate DV. I would not claim this mirror captures that theorem or the payment construction.

The weakest point is that masses do not affect which types are maximal. The continuous input is a weighted, compressed representation of the finite support, and the cited frontier algorithm already runs in time based on the number of vectors. That makes this a modest mirror of the paper’s computational primitive, not a continuization of its main mechanism-design results. It leaves natural follow-up questions—whether any type-level payment model preserves the incentive guarantees, and whether a genuinely mass-sensitive frontier problem remains faithful to the authors’ question—but Proposition 1 alone does not answer them.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor is the one place this paper resists a strong negative case. Proposition 1 really is a named computational statement: it gives running times for computing \(MAX(F)\). I cannot honestly claim the paper contains no such result. But it is introduced as a known result from Kung, Luccio, and Preparata, while the paper’s own contributions concern incentive compatibility and payments.

The best version of the mirror is coherent: treat each valuation vector as a type, give each supported type a rational mass, and return the maximal types, perhaps with their shares. The concern is that this barely uses the population model. The frontier depends on the supported vectors, not their masses. Adding the shares to the output is an extra report about the input distribution; it does not change the frontier computation. The paper’s mechanism results do not supply a mass-based computational problem to make that population structure consequential.

Trying to make the mirror more faithful to the strategic part exposes a different gap. The mechanism reasons about a named agent’s unilateral misreport and individual vector payment. In a non-atomic population, one agent has zero mass; if instead an entire cohort reports together, that is a different deviation model. And the paper’s mechanism explicitly removes identical vectors, while its positive result for \(MAX\) assumes distinct values. So the type-cohort interpretation does not carry over the paper’s incentive guarantees as stated.

That makes this a weak negative case, not a decisive one. The static frontier mirror remains well-posed, and the fact that it has an easy, already-known algorithm cannot by itself disqualify it under the programme’s criteria. I can argue that it is a thin population mirror of background material rather than a mirror of the paper’s main results, but I cannot rule out the proponent’s formulation as a worthwhile computational question.
