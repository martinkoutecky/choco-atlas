The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuum version of the paper’s common-prior information-pooling rule. But there is a hard limit on the case: the paper has no numbered computational-complexity result of the kind the brief requires as an anchor. Its named results are axiomatic pooling theorems. Section 8 gives a recursive aggregation procedure and discusses its complexity, but not as a numbered complexity theorem.

The closest mathematical anchor is Corollary 1, proved here. Under Individual Bayesian Rationality, conditionally independent private information, Collective Bayesian Rationality, acceptance of each person’s likelihood assessments, and a common prior \(p\), it states that the collective posterior is \(\pi \propto \pi_1\cdots\pi_n/p^{n-1}\). That is a substantive result, but it is a formula for pooling beliefs—not a named claim that a computational problem is in P or otherwise has a complexity classification. I would not present it as a qualifying computational anchor.

Still, it suggests one precise candidate mirror: Common-Prior Continuum Evidence Pooling. Take a finite hypothesis set \(H\), a positive rational common prior \(p\), and a finite set \(T\) of expert types. Each type \(t\) has a positive rational posterior \(q_t\) over \(H\), based on the common prior. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a population scale \(N\). Here, \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of experts of type \(t\); \(N\mu_t\) is their agent-equivalent mass.

The problem is to return the collective belief, with solution \(\Gamma_{\mu,N}\) given by \(\Gamma_{\mu,N}(H) \propto p(H)\prod_{t\in T}(q_t(H)/p(H))^{N\mu_t}\), normalized to sum to one. There is no optimization objective in this problem: the output is the pooled belief itself. If every \(N\mu_t\) is an integer, the expression is exactly Corollary 1 applied to a finite group with \(N\mu_t\) experts of each type.

The regime could be a very large forecasting or sensor network whose members use a shared prior and a finite set of standardized evidence protocols. Many members use the same protocol and produce the same posterior; the number of agents is large while the number of distinct belief types is small. A type includes every feature the pooling rule uses, including its prior and posterior. The paper’s independent-information assumption remains essential: the repeated types must represent independently acquired evidence, not repeated copies of the same signal.

For this posed function problem, I expect tractability: the exact aggregate can be represented compactly by the input weights and rational exponents, with work proportional to \(|H||T|\) to construct the expression. That is an implication of the proposed formulation, not a complexity result proved in the paper. If the desired output is instead a rounded numeric vector or the most likely hypothesis, the bit complexity and precision requirements need to be specified.

This mirror covers only the common-prior, independent-information pooling rule of Corollary 1. It does not capture the paper’s general distinct-prior case or its recursive treatment of subgroup information in Theorem 4. It is plausible that Dietrich would recognize it as a continuous extension of his pooling formula, since integer masses recover that formula exactly.

The weakest point is also decisive: fractional evidence mass is not derived from the paper’s axioms. The finite theorem counts independent evidence from individual agents, so population size affects the posterior; a distribution of type fractions alone loses that scale. Adding \(N\) and allowing fractional likelihood exponents makes a coherent continuation, but it is an extra modeling choice. Combined with the absence of a qualifying computational anchor, that leaves a plausible continuous analogue of one result—but not a strong, paper-anchored computational-complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem for ChoCo is that the paper proves no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Corollary 1 characterizes a collective belief under axioms; Theorem 4 constructs another such belief recursively. Section 8 estimates the recursion’s cost, but that estimate is not a complexity theorem. There is therefore no computational result here for a continuous mirror to classify. This is a strong case against a paper-anchored ChoCo contribution, though it does not by itself show that continuous Bayesian pooling is uninteresting.

The proponent’s proposed mirror is the best candidate. With a finite population scale \(N\), the formula
\[ \Gamma_{\mu,N}(H)\propto p(H)\prod_{t\in T}\left(\frac{q_t(H)}{p(H)}\right)^{N\mu_t} \]
is a natural extension of Corollary 1. Integer counts recover the finite formula, and a large network of experts or sensors using standardized protocols could be a credible high-multiplicity setting. I would not object that agents cannot be grouped into types: if the pooling rule uses only their prior and posterior, agents with the same pair are indistinguishable for that purpose.

The trouble is that \(N\) carries the evidence strength. The society distribution \(\mu\) sums to one, so it records type proportions but not how many independent pieces of evidence the group has. If \(N\) is fixed at one, duplicating the society leaves the aggregate unchanged; the result pools one unit of average evidence, rather than all the information in the group. If \(N\) grows with the population, write \(\ell_\mu(H)=\sum_t\mu_t\log(q_t(H)/p(H))\). Then \(\Gamma_{\mu,N}(H)\propto p(H)e^{N\ell_\mu(H)}\), which concentrates on the hypothesis maximizing \(\ell_\mu\) whenever that maximum is unique. The limiting belief becomes degenerate, unlike the paper’s strictly positive opinions. A finite intensity measure with fractional exponents is coherent as weighted or tempered evidence, but the paper’s conditioning axioms do not select that intensity or establish that fractional evidence has the same meaning as conditioning on everyone’s information. The proposed formula is thus a plausible new model, not a canonical continuous version of the paper’s Bayesian group agent.

One could add a computational task, such as finding the least mass of experts to alter so that a target hypothesis becomes most likely. That would be a new intervention problem; the paper neither poses it nor proves a computational result that anchors it. So the negative case is strong on the ChoCo criterion: this paper offers no demonstrated complexity landscape to continuize, and its cleanest proposed extension depends on an extra choice about evidence scale. The broader claim that *no* worthwhile continuous mirror exists is weaker. Continuous weighted evidence pooling is mathematically sensible, and Dietrich might recognize it as a useful extension of his formula. What I cannot establish is that it would be a worthwhile computational mirror of this paper.
