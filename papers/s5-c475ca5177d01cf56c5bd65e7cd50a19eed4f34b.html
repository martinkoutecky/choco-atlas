The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind this programme normally anchors on: it has no theorem classifying a problem as in P, NP-hard, or similar. Its nearest named anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper (using results from Teo et al.): for identically and independently distributed demand processes, every associated distribution-center consolidation game has a nonempty core. In full, the theorem says there is a division of the grand coalition’s minimum cost among the centers such that no coalition is allocated more than it would cost to serve its own demand. Corollary 4.3, also proved here, strengthens this to total balancedness: every subgame of such a consolidation game has a nonempty core. These are structural existence results, not complexity results; I use Theorem 4.2 as the anchor, and Corollary 4.3 as supporting evidence.

A credible continuous setting is a supply network with a fixed, modest number \(n\) of distribution centers and a very large population of small demand points—say, retail outlets with similar demand streams. Let type \(t_i\) consist of an outlet’s demand process and its incumbent center \(i\), so the type support is at most \(n\) while the population can be much larger. Restrict to identical Poisson demand streams, a standard subcase of the paper’s stochastic model. A rational mass \(\mu_i\) records the share of outlets initially served by center \(i\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\); \(P\) is the population scale. The demand points can be indifferent to which center serves them, as in the paper. Here, mass is a share of the outlet population, and fractional reassignment means that a fraction of a large, otherwise identical cohort changes center.

I would pose Continuous DC Core Computation as the mirror of Theorem 4.2. An instance gives the centers \(N\); their setup costs \(f_j\), lead times \(L_j\), holding and penalty rates \(h_j,p_j\), and ordering-cost functions \(k_j\); the rational masses \(\mu_i\); and the population scale \(P\). For a coalition \(S\), let \(z_{ij}\geq0\) be the mass originally attached to \(i\in S\) that it sends to \(j\in S\), with \(\sum_{j\in S}z_{ij}=\mu_i\). Center \(j\) then serves mass \(q_j=\sum_{i\in S}z_{ij}\). Under the Poisson restriction, its lead-time demand is \(D_{j,q_j}\sim\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda Pq_jL_j)\), where \(\lambda\) is the per-outlet demand rate. Define \(G_j(y;q)=\mathbb E[h_j(y-D_{j,q})^+ + p_j(D_{j,q}-y)^+]\) and \(I_j(q)=\inf_{r,Q>0}\{\lambda Pq\,k_j(Q)+\frac{1}{Q}\int_r^{r+Q}G_j(y;q)\,dy\}\), with \(I_j(0)=0\). The coalition cost is \(c_\mu(S)=\min_z\sum_{j\in S}\bigl(f_j\mathbf 1[q_j>0]+I_j(q_j)\bigr)\). The problem asks for an allocation \(x\in\mathbb R^N\) satisfying \(x(N)=c_\mu(N)\) and \(x(S)\leq c_\mu(S)\) for every coalition \(S\); such an \(x\) is the solution.

The expected direction is tractable for a fixed number of centers, assuming exact evaluation or optimization access to the inventory-cost functions. Theorem 4.2 predicts that a core allocation exists in this iid continuous regime. With fixed \(n\), one can evaluate coalition costs and solve the finite system of core constraints; with \(n\) as an input, that system has exponentially many constraints, and the paper provides no polynomial-time construction. So this is a plausible Class A lead in the fixed-infrastructure regime, but the theorem by itself establishes existence, not an efficient algorithm. Natural follow-up questions are whether the result survives multiple demand types, what happens when \(n\) grows, and how the continuous core relates to core allocations in finite economies.

The strongest point for the mirror is that the paper’s coalition costs already depend on aggregated demand, and its main theorem is proved through inequalities that scale coalition demand. It is natural to ask whether that stability result persists when the customer population itself is represented by mass. The weakest point is substantial: the paper’s cooperative-game players are distribution centers, not customers, and it studies neither computation nor a continuous population. Its stochastic demand processes do not make its population continuous. The mirror is therefore a plausible high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s cost game, but a constructive core-computation problem would be a new computational question rather than one of the paper’s stated results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a mismatch between the theorem’s game and the population the proposed mirror makes continuous. Neither Theorem 4.2 nor Corollary 4.3 is a computational result: they prove core existence and total balancedness using the Bondareva–Shapley characterization. They give no algorithm, complexity bound, or finite input model. “Compute a core allocation” is a reasonable new question, but it is not a computational result from this paper being mirrored.

The distinction also matters for the continuous model. The game’s players are the distribution centers \(N\). Its coalitions are subsets of centers, and its core allocation \(x\in\mathbb R^N\) divides costs among centers. The demand points \(M\) are loads that centers may reassign; they do not receive allocations or form coalitions. In the proposed model, outlet mass changes the cost of serving a coalition while leaving the game’s players finite. Under the identical-demand assumption, those outlets contribute aggregate demand rates, quantities the inventory model already represents. A large cohort of similar outlets is a plausible high-multiplicity inventory input, but it does not make the society in Theorem 4.2 continuous.

Making customers the core players would require a new cooperative game: the paper gives no way to define a customer coalition’s cost or its incentive to deviate. Making the centers continuous instead changes the game’s participants. Their types would need to encode location, setup cost, lead time, inventory parameters, and initial load; in realistic networks, the number of distinct types may grow with the number of centers. Grouping identical centers into mass also requires deciding how to represent indivisible facilities and fixed setup costs. Allowing fractional centers changes those choices, while an atomless model removes individual center deviations. Such a model could be developed, but it is not a forced continuation of this theorem.

Corollary 4.3 does not provide a separate surviving anchor: its subgames are still formed from subsets of the same finite set of centers. Continuous demand can leave that statement intact without making the game’s population continuous.

There is a real weakness in this negative case. A large network with many similar outlets and interchangeable depots could motivate a continuous inventory model, and the theorem’s scaling argument makes it natural to investigate whether core existence extends to real demand masses. That may be worthwhile adjacent work. The case against is strongest when the requirement is a continuous-population mirror of this paper’s results: the paper’s central result concerns stability among facilities, while the proposed continuum concerns demand inputs, and the paper supplies no computational claim to anchor the proposed computation.
