The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\) of size \(d\), a partition into groups \(G_1,\ldots,G_g\), rational fairness bounds \(\alpha_i,\beta_i\), block parameters \(k,b\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) supported on \(\tau\) rankings, find a block-fair permutation \(\sigma\) whose expected Kendall tau distance \(\sum_{\pi}\mu_\pi\mathcal K(\pi,\sigma)\) is at most \(3\) times the minimum over feasible permutations. Feasibility requires \(\alpha_i\ell\le |P_\ell(\sigma)\cap G_i|\le\beta_i\ell\) for every \(i\) and every prefix length \(\ell\ge k\) divisible by \(b\).

The model it lives in

The population consists of voter types \(\pi\in T\subseteq\mathfrak S_d\), each carrying rational mass \(\mu_\pi\), with \(\sum_{\pi\in T}\mu_\pi=1\). The decision variable is one output permutation \(\sigma\), subject to the paper's block-fairness constraints on candidate groups, and the objective is its expected Kendall tau distance from the voter-type distribution. The weighted-support algorithm has polynomial dependence on \(d,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), with arbitrary group count.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the \(q\)-mean block-fair Kendall result of Corollary 4.4 for arbitrary group counts and the \(q\)-mean strict-fair Ulam result of Corollary 4.6 for constant group counts. It does not cover the separate \((3-\varepsilon)\)-approximation of Theorem 4.8 or turn the standalone closest-fair-ranking results into population aggregation results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the \(3\)-approximation for expected block-fair Kendall aggregation be improved while keeping runtime polynomial in \(d,\tau\), and \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s fair rank aggregation problem with the *ranking population* continuized. A type is a complete ranking \(\pi\) of the \(d\) candidates. Its mass \(\mu_\pi\) is the fraction of people who submit that ranking. This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime for a large electorate ranking a modest candidate slate: many voters can share ballots, so the number of distinct rankings \(\tau\) may be far smaller than the electorate size. Ranking is the voters’ preference input; the output remains a single permutation subject to the paper’s group-fairness constraints.

For the lead mirror, Fractional Block-Fair Kendall Aggregation takes a candidate set \(C\) of size \(d\), a partition into groups \(G_1,\ldots,G_g\), rational fairness bounds \(\alpha_i,\beta_i\), block parameters \(k,b\), and a distribution \(\mu\) with rational masses on \(\tau\) rankings. A feasible output is a permutation \(\sigma\) whose every prefix of length \(\ell\ge k\) divisible by \(b\) satisfies
\[ \alpha_i\ell\le |P_\ell(\sigma)\cap G_i|\le\beta_i\ell \quad\text{for every }i. \]
For the median version, its cost is the expected Kendall tau distance
\[ \Phi_1(\sigma;\mu)=\sum_{\pi}\mu_\pi\,\mathcal K(\pi,\sigma). \]
The question is to return a feasible \(\sigma\) whose cost is at most three times the minimum feasible cost. Feasibility can be promised. This mirrors Corollary 4.4, proved in the paper: for any \(q\ge1\), a \(3\)-approximate \(q\)-mean block-fair aggregation under Kendall tau can be found in \(O(nd^2+n^2d\log d)\) time, for arbitrary numbers of groups. The corollary follows from the paper’s fair-aggregation framework and its exact closest block-fair ranking algorithm; the Kendall-distance computation it uses is cited from elsewhere.

The weighted version has the same algorithmic shape. Compute a closest fair ranking for each support ranking, then choose the candidate minimizing the weighted aggregate objective. The approximation argument still applies: choose a support ranking nearest to an optimal fair aggregate, and the triangle-inequality bound holds for every support type, hence for their weighted objective. Replacing the list of \(n\) ballots by its \(\tau\) distinct types gives the expected running time \(O(\tau d^2+\tau^2d\log d+\operatorname{poly}(L))\), where \(L\) includes the mass and fairness-parameter bit lengths. I expect this mirror to be tractable, in Class A.

A second, distinct mirror is Fractional Strict-Fair Ulam Aggregation. It has the same candidate groups and rational distribution over rankings, but requires every prefix of length \(\ell\ge k\) to satisfy
\[ \lfloor\alpha_i\ell\rfloor\le |P_\ell(\sigma)\cap G_i| \le\lceil\beta_i\ell\rceil \quad\text{for every }i, \]
and measures expected Ulam distance. Its median question is again to return a feasible ranking within factor three of optimum. Corollary 4.6, proved in the paper using its Ulam dynamic program and a cited Ulam-distance computation, gives a \(3\)-approximation for every \(q\ge1\) when \(g\) is constant, in \(O(nd^{g+2}+n^2d\log d)\) time. The support-weighted mirror should therefore run in \(O(\tau d^{g+2}+\tau^2d\log d+\operatorname{poly}(L))\) time for constant \(g\), and I expect it to be tractable as well. The first mirror is the stronger case: it allows arbitrary group counts and uses the paper’s prominent Kendall tau result.

These are population continuizations because the society is the distribution of preference rankings. The output ranking and its fairness constraints stay exactly in the paper’s discrete form. For an electorate with \(n_\pi\) voters of each ranking type, \(\mu_\pi=n_\pi/n\); the expected objective differs from the paper’s summed objective only by normalization, which does not change approximation ratios. The authors should recognize these as weighted, high-multiplicity versions of their fair aggregation questions. With fixed \(q\), the same weighted-support argument extends beyond the median objective; \(q=1\) gives the clean rational-cost formulation above.

There is no named hardness result in the supplied paper to anchor a hardness-transfer mirror. The two anchors are algorithmic, so this case supports tractability rather than claiming that any hardness dissolves.

My weakest point is that the paper writes its input as a *set* \(S\) of rankings and does not discuss a rational distribution over them. The mirror thus makes the standard rank-aggregation move of representing repeated ballots by weights. That extension seems natural to the authors’ underlying objective, which adds one distance contribution per input ranking, but it is the main point on which they might decline the mirror. The intended regime also depends on \(\tau\ll n\); large electorates alone do not guarantee a small support.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper formally aggregates a *set* of \(n\) distinct rankings. A measure over ranking types with repeated mass changes that input: it treats the rankings as ballots from interchangeable voters, rather than as distinct data points. If the rankings are independently curated sources, multiplicity may have no meaningful population interpretation.

That objection does not defeat the better mirrors. In a large electorate ranking a modest candidate slate, a voter’s complete type is their ranking; voters with the same ranking are indistinguishable to these objectives. The output remains a single fair permutation, while the electorate is the distribution over rankings. Candidate identity is not the issue: voter identity never enters either objective. And a potentially large support is an instance-regime question, not a reason to reject the model.

Both anchors survive this version. For Corollary 4.4, the weighted Kendall median objective is \(\sum_\pi \mu_\pi \mathcal K(\pi,\sigma)\), with block-fairness on the output; its weighted-support algorithm and approximation argument follow the paper’s structure. For Corollary 4.6, the same population model with strict fairness and expected Ulam distance gives the corresponding mirror when the number of candidate groups is constant. Rational masses make these proper high-multiplicity inputs, with support size and bit length available as parameters.

So the negative case rests on a real but narrow mismatch with the paper’s literal set-valued input. The natural electorate scenario removes it, and neither the discrete output nor the fact that the weighted extension may be straightforward defeats a well-posed population-computational question. I cannot make an honest universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
