The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a type-mass version of \(P\)-stable Nash recognition, anchored by the paper’s main result. Theorem 2, proved here, says that given true preferences \(P\) and submitted preferences \(Q\), one can decide in \(O(n^4)\) time whether \(Q\) is a \(P\)-stable Nash equilibrium: no woman can change her list, get a strictly better partner under her true ranking, and leave a matching stable under \(P\). The paper also proves Theorem 1, which characterizes every partner a woman can obtain by changing her list and supplies a witnessing list for each. These are algorithmic results in the paper, not results cited from elsewhere; the paper credits earlier work for the underlying exploration algorithm. [The paper on arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.04344)

Here is a continuous matching market for those questions. Let \(A\) be the proposer-side types and \(B\) the responding-side types. Type \(a\in A\) has rational mass \(\alpha_a\), and type \(b\in B\) has rational mass \(\beta_b\), with both sides’ masses summing to \(1\). Each type records its true ranking \(P\) and submitted ranking \(Q\) over the opposite side’s types. A matching is a flow \(x_{ab}\geq 0\) whose row and column sums are \(\alpha_a\) and \(\beta_b\). Define mass deferred acceptance by running synchronous proposal rounds: free proposer mass proposes to its next type, and each responding type retains the highest-ranked proposal mass up to its capacity, splitting mass at the cutoff and rejecting the rest. Those rejected continue down their lists. Thus the mechanism and its tie handling are part of the problem definition.

Call a matching \(P\)-stable if there is no pair of types \(a,b\) for which positive mass on each side would prefer that pair to their current partners under their true rankings. The continuous counterpart of Theorem 1 is: given a marked subcohort of rational mass \(\delta>0\) from responding type \(b\), report \(Q\), and all other reports fixed, return every proposer type \(a\) for which some replacement ranking of the marked cohort makes the mass-DA outcome match all \(\delta\) to \(a\), together with a witnessing report and flow. This is the attainable-partner set, with a cohort’s partner category taking the place of an individual’s partner.

The lead question, the counterpart of Theorem 2, is Positive-Mass \(P\)-Stable Manipulation Recognition. First compute the baseline mass-DA flow under \(Q\). Choose a responding type \(b\), a baseline partner type \(a_0\) with positive flow to \(b\), and a rational submass \(\delta>0\) of \(b\) currently matched to \(a_0\). Allow that marked submass to switch to one common complete ranking \(\widehat Q_b\), leaving every other report fixed. Ask whether some such choice produces a new mass-DA flow that is \(P\)-stable and matches every member of the deviating submass to a type strictly preferred to \(a_0\) under \(P_b\). A “yes” solution consists of \(b,a_0,\delta,\widehat Q_b\), and the resulting flow; “no” means no positive-mass safe manipulation exists. The decision criterion preserves the paper’s central conjunction: a better outcome under true preferences and stability under the true profile.

The plausible regime is a large centralized market with recurring preference classes, such as a residency or hiring market: many applicants and many positions, but relatively few types of applicants and positions with the same true and submitted rankings over counterpart categories. For example, the population may be large while the supported type lists remain modest. A type’s mass is its share of applicants or positions; the flow records the fractions matched across types, though each realized individual still receives one integral match. The response-side subcohort’s list change is the mass-level action. This puts the population itself into the model, rather than merely making outcomes fractional.

I expect both continuous questions to be Class A candidates. For Theorem 1’s mirror, the output is limited to the supported proposer types; a mass analogue of the paper’s active-proposer exploration could potentially find them without enumerating every ranking. For the lead question, \(P\)-stability is a finite set of type-pair conditions, and once reports are fixed, the flow constraints are linear. The hard step is extending the paper’s structural pruning of possible manipulations to splittable mass. If that works, the reachability result could feed the stable-manipulation test much as Theorem 1 feeds Theorem 2. This is a proposed research direction, not a complexity consequence already proved by the paper. In particular, there is no hardness anchor here from which to expect hardness transfer.

The follow-up questions are concrete: does mass deferred acceptance give a canonical outcome independent of how one represents or replicates the mass; do the active-proposer characterization and the paper’s uniqueness lemma survive when types split across matches; and can the test run in time polynomial in the numbers of supported types and the rational input bit length? A further bridge question is whether the mass problem recovers the finite problem when the smallest permitted deviating mass is \(1/n\).

The weak point is also the main modeling change. In an atomless society, one individual has zero mass, so an individual’s report cannot change an aggregate distribution-based outcome; literal unilateral Nash manipulation may collapse. The proposed questions instead let a positive-mass subcohort change its report. That is a safe-manipulation problem with the paper’s two-sided market, deferred-acceptance mechanism, true-versus-submitted rankings, and \(P\)-stability condition, but its strategic unit is a cohort rather than one woman. If the authors would not recognize this mass-level action as their problem, the mirror fails. Its best defense is that the programme’s high-multiplicity model treats mass as the operative unit, and the paper’s core computational task—finding beneficial manipulation subject to true-profile stability—survives that change.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has real computational anchors: its attainable-partner result and its \(O(n^4)\) algorithm for recognizing \(P\)-stable Nash equilibria. The strongest objection is that both results take one named woman’s deviation as their strategic unit, and that unit does not survive a population-only continuum.

In an atomless matching market, one woman’s report has zero mass. If the mechanism’s state is the distribution of types, changing her list leaves that state—and therefore the aggregate matching flow—unchanged. The recognition question then becomes vacuous: no individual deviation changes the outcome. The attainable-partner question fares worse: a flow over types does not say which individual is paired with a named woman. A tagged woman could be added, but the mass state still does not determine her particular partner or the effect of her blocking pairs. An almost-everywhere stability test would ignore such a singleton; tracking it would require extra individual-level structure.

The best repair is the proponent’s marked subcohort of mass \(\delta>0\). That is a coherent continuous matching problem, and matching preference classes are plausible in large hiring or residency markets. But it changes the action from one woman’s unilateral report to a coordinated deviation by a positive-mass group. The attainable-partner result becomes a question about which partner types a bloc can reach; Nash recognition becomes a search for safe coalitional manipulation. Those could be worthwhile problems, but the paper’s algorithms do not establish that they are mirrors of its individual-manipulation results. Shrinking \(\delta\) toward zero restores the individual scale while removing its effect on the aggregate. Keeping \(\delta\) positive retains an effect by keeping the coalition.

There is a related modeling choice. Preserving the paper’s strict rankings over named individuals requires retaining those identities in the types, which undermines aggregation. Ranking counterpart *types* instead is a sensible co-modification, but it gives the model category-level preferences and fractional matching flows. Those flows can represent aggregate stability and outcomes without recovering the individual partner comparisons that drive the paper’s safety condition.

So the negative case is a boundary argument, not a proof that no useful continuous question exists. If the authors adopt mass-level coalitions as the strategic unit, the proponent’s question is plausible and may be valuable. The paper itself gives no clear reason to treat that coalition problem as its unilateral \(P\)-stable Nash problem; that is the point on which the case against rests.
