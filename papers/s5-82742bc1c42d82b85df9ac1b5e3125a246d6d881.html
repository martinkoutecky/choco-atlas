The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive hook is Theorem 1.3: it studies larger populations of bounded degree, with independent initial opinions, and proves a polylogarithmic bound on the expected time to reach \(\varepsilon\)-consensus. One could imagine a high-multiplicity regime where many agents share the same local interaction role, with each role a type and its population share represented by \(\mu_t\). That makes a population-level model plausible. The caveat is that the graph’s connections matter to the dynamics, so type masses alone would not preserve the problem; the mirror would also need an interaction kernel.

But there is no eligible computational-complexity anchor in this paper. Theorem 1.3, proved here via Theorem 5.1 and Remark 5.3, says that for a connected undirected graph or an Eulerian directed graph of bounded maximum degree, with \([0,1]\)-valued i.i.d. initial opinions, \(\mathbb{E}[\tau_\varepsilon]\le(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)^C\) for a universal \(C\), when \(n\) is sufficiently large depending on the degree bound. Here \(\tau_\varepsilon\) is the elapsed time of the stochastic opinion process—not the running time of an algorithm. Theorem 1.2 and the exponential-time example in Claim 6.2 likewise bound the process’s convergence time; Theorem 1.1 is a known convergence result. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

So I cannot honestly give a continuous problem per anchor, or name a lead anchor. One could formulate new computational questions about predicting consensus time or choosing interventions in a typed interaction model, but those would be extensions of the paper’s subject, not mirrors of its named computational results. The positive case is therefore limited to the plausible repeated-role population regime around Theorem 1.3; it does not meet the task’s anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper states no computational complexity result to mirror. Theorem 1.3 bounds \(\mathbb{E}[\tau_\varepsilon]\), the elapsed time of a stochastic opinion process with Poisson clocks. Theorems 1.2 and 1.4 and Claim 6.2 likewise concern convergence, variance, or elapsed process time. None gives an algorithmic running time, hardness result, or computational problem with an input and output. A continuous-population question about convergence could be mathematically worthwhile, but it would be an analytic question of the kind ChoCo distinguishes from its target.

The strongest version of the proposed Theorem 1.3 mirror would give mass to recurring local interaction roles and preserve their links with a sparse interaction structure. That could be sensible for selected graph families. But the role masses alone do not encode who interacts with whom, and DeGroot updates can create correlations that type-level average opinions lose. A richer continuum model could preserve local structure and ask whether the polylogarithmic convergence bound survives. That would be a genuine continuous-population dynamics question; the result sought would still concern process convergence, not computational complexity. Adding a question about efficiently computing or optimizing the dynamics would introduce a new problem, rather than mirror a named computational result in this paper.

The same obstacle defeats a stronger reading of the other finite-graph results. One could ask for continuous analogues of the spectral-gap bounds, the variance estimates, or the exponential consensus-time example. Those would transfer analytic statements to a continuous model. The in-paper exponential example is also an exponential *elapsed convergence time*, not an exponential-time algorithm or a hardness reduction.

So the case against a ChoCo mirror is strong: the paper may support continuous models of social learning, but it supplies no computational result for the programme to continuize. The universal claim is less secure if “mirror” includes a newly designed intervention or optimization problem motivated by the paper. Such a problem might be worth studying, but its computational core would have to be supplied from outside the paper.
