The continuous mirror question

Given worker types \(I\) and task types \(J\), with rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\), integer per-worker capacities \(b_i\), task values \(q_j>0\), and compatibility edges \(E\), find a feasible assignment \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}q_jx_{ij}\), subject to \(\sum_{j:(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\le b_i\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_{i:(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\le\beta_j\).

The model it lives in

A maximum-cost flow on the type graph: worker type \(i\) supplies at most \(b_i\alpha_i\), task type \(j\) receives at most \(\beta_j\), and each compatible assignment has value \(q_j\). The flow records mass assigned while individual workers still receive at most \(b_i\) tasks.

What the mirror covers

Mirrors the maximum-value allocation guarantee of MBFS and MDFS for repeated worker and task types; it does not establish task truthfulness, group strategyproofness, or the paper's other manipulation results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give an exact bit-complexity bound for computing an optimal assignment from binary-rational masses and values.
  • Determine when an aggregate optimal flow can be implemented as a distribution over integral task bundles for workers of each type.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper gives no named NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity result. The strongest clean computational anchor I see is Theorem 18, proved in Section 4.2. For task edge manipulation, it states that MBFS, MDFS, and MAP are truthful; MBFS and MDFS have approximation ratio \(1\), while MAP has ratio \(2\). The authors prove the theorem here; the proof uses the cited result [38] for MAP’s approximation bound and gives a tight example. I use its ratio-\(1\) claim as the anchor: MBFS and MDFS compute an optimal maximum vertex-weighted \(b\)-matching.

A natural high-multiplicity setting is a large hiring or assignment market. Many workers share the same skills, eligible job categories, and per-worker capacity; many job slots share the same value and eligible worker types. A city or sector might have thousands of workers and vacancies but only tens or hundreds of such types. This is a plausible instance regime for the paper’s matching problem, rather than a claim that all matching markets are large-population settings.

Here is the mirror I would ask a prover to study: Continuous Maximum Vertex-Weighted \(b\)-Matching. An instance has finite sets of worker types \(I\) and task types \(J\). Worker type \(i\) has rational mass \(\alpha_i\), capacity \(b_i\) per worker, and an eligibility set; task type \(j\) has rational mass \(\beta_j\), public value \(q_j>0\), and compatible worker types. The input lists only positive-mass types and their binary-rational masses. A solution is a nonnegative rational assignment \(x_{ij}\) on compatible pairs satisfying
\[ \sum_{j:(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\le b_i\alpha_i \quad\text{for every }i\in I, \qquad \sum_{i:(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\le \beta_j \quad\text{for every }j\in J. \]
The question is to find a feasible \(x\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{(i,j)\in E}q_jx_{ij}. \]
Each infinitesimal worker still receives an integral bundle of tasks, and each infinitesimal task is assigned at most once; \(x_{ij}\) records the mass of those assignments. Scaling task supplies and worker capacities with population size is part of making this matching mirror coherent.

I expect this problem to be tractable: it is a maximum-cost flow on the type graph, with capacities \(b_i\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_j\). It admits an exact algorithm polynomial in the numbers of types, compatible pairs, and input bit length. The connection to Theorem 18 is the underlying allocation problem computed by its ratio-\(1\) mechanisms. This is the authors’ matching problem aggregated by complete worker and task types, not an encoding problem.

The mirror is credible as a high-multiplicity version of their allocation problem, but it has a real limitation: the paper’s task-truthfulness result concerns individual discrete tasks. It does not establish truthfulness for whole task-type cohorts, and the paper separately shows that task-side group strategyproofness can fail. So this formulation mirrors Theorem 18’s optimal allocation guarantee; it does not claim to carry over all of that theorem’s strategic content. A natural follow-up is whether a continuous type-level mechanism can preserve individual task truthfulness, and how its answer changes when a cohort can coordinate. The mirror’s weakest point is that its optimization core is already a familiar flow problem, so it may be a sound positive mirror but a modest contribution to a programme seeking a rich new complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed flow question keeps the allocation primitive and drops the distinctive content of Theorem 18. In Section 4, tasks have utility \(1\) if matched and \(0\) otherwise, and the theorem says that each individual task cannot improve by hiding edges. The proposed LP asks for a maximum-weight assignment of type mass; it has no reports or incentive condition. Its exact-flow guarantee therefore mirrors the allocation computed by the mechanisms, but not their task-truthfulness result.

That gap matters in a continuum. If a task has zero mass, hiding its edges changes no aggregate input, so individual truthfulness is vacuous or undefined in a type-mass model. Letting an entire task type change its report gives a positive-mass deviation, but that is a cohort or coalition incentive question. The paper treats group strategyproofness separately in Theorems 16–17; it is not the individual-task claim of Theorem 18. A more faithful continuous mirror would need to define and study such type-level deviations, rather than infer the theorem’s strategic guarantee from a flow formulation.

The worker/task-type scenario itself is credible: repeated eligibility profiles, capacities, and task values can produce a genuine high-multiplicity market. The mass constraints and the flow variables are coherent. So the negative case cannot honestly rest on lack of multiplicity or on the fact that fractional assignments appear.

Nor can I dismiss the exact-flow question just because it is likely easy or already familiar; under the programme’s rules, a well-posed tractable mirror still counts. The case against is therefore limited: Theorem 18’s most distinctive claim does not carry into the proposed continuum, while its surviving allocation claim is a thinner mirror. That weakens the proponent’s anchor, but it does not defeat it outright.
