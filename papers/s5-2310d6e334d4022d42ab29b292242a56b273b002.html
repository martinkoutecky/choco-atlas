The strongest positive case is for continuizing the paper’s central coalition-formation model. A plausible regime is a large DAO or civic group drafting a shared document, where many members fall into a relatively small number of preference and behavior types. A type would be an ideal document point together with the parameters determining its approval behavior; \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s share of the membership. A coalition’s state would record how much of each type supports its current compromise document. When the mediator proposes a new document, the mass of each type that accepts it moves according to the paper’s voting model, and the stopping condition becomes whether a coalition holds at least a fraction \(Q\) of the population. That is recognizably their process, with population mass replacing individual counts.

A natural follow-up would ask whether, from such a finite-support society and a specified mediator and constitution, a coalition of mass at least \(Q\) can form within \(H\) rounds. But that would be a new computational question, not a mirror of a named result in this paper.

The weak point is that the paper’s own experiments generate individual ideal sentences, so their support may grow with the number of agents rather than remain small. The proposed DAO regime needs a genuine concentration of members into shared types—plausible for standardized policy positions, but not established by the paper. More fundamentally, the paper supplies no named computational result for continuization to explain or classify.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

A continuous analogue of that guarantee would be an analytic convergence question, outside ChoCo’s computational focus.

The proposed DAO model is coherent if many members share ideal documents and behavioral parameters. Tracking each type’s mass and asking whether a coalition reaches quota \(Q\) preserves the paper’s population process. But the suggested question—whether this happens within \(H\) rounds—is a new computational problem, not a mirror of a result in this paper. With the mediator fixed, it amounts to running the specified process; to make it an optimization problem, one must choose among merger sequences or proposals, which adds the forward-looking planning problem the paper leaves for future work.

The text setting makes that extension harder to pin down computationally: the paper uses an LLM to generate proposals, but gives no finite proposal universe or computational model for the LLM. A finite set of standardized preference types could make the population side clean, but it would not supply the missing computational result or formalize the proposal-generation part. The paper’s experiments also generate individual ideal sentences, so they do not establish that support remains small as the population grows.

This is a strong case against a *paper-backed* mirror, not proof that no useful continuous model could ever be built. The DAO type scenario is plausible, and studying it may be worthwhile as new work.
