The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is two-hop threshold mass welfare. The paper’s anchor is Theorem 23, proved by the authors: in the paper’s 2-hop, 2-step setting, maximizing social welfare is APX-hard, and for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is no polynomial-time \((1-1/e+\varepsilon)\)-approximation unless \(P=NP\). The problem remains APX-hard with only two items, though with a larger hardness constant. The paper attributes the reductions to Max Coverage and SAT; some proof details are in its cited full version.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large online community with many users in each of a modest number of recurring network and product-interest roles. Members of a role share the same intrinsic values and the same pattern of connections to other roles. For a clean model, take a finite type graph \(H\): every member of type \(t\) is connected to every member of each adjacent type. The population has unit mass, with rational type masses \(\mu_t\), and \(N\gg\tau\) users when represented at population scale \(N\), where \(\tau\) is the number of positive-mass types. This is a stylized block-mixing network, but it gives each type a complete description of the preferences, values, and network role used by the problem.

Here is the question I would hand to a prover. The input is a finite item set \(I\), a symmetric type graph \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational intrinsic values \(\lambda_{ti}\), and rational mass thresholds \(\eta,\theta>0\). A solution is a matrix \(x_{ti}\ge 0\), where \(x_{ti}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned item \(i\), with \(\sum_i x_{ti}=\mu_t\). Thus each individual still receives one item; \(x\) records the population share receiving each item.

Write \(\Gamma_1(t)\) for the types adjacent to \(t\), and \(\Gamma_2(t)\) for the types at distance two, excluding \(t\) and \(\Gamma_1(t)\). The support mass for type \(t\) receiving item \(i\) is
\[ q_{ti}(x)=\sum_{u\in\Gamma_1(t)}x_{ui} +\sum_{v\in\Gamma_2(t)}x_{vi}\, \mathbf 1\!\left[\exists u\in\Gamma_1(t)\cap\Gamma_1(v):x_{ui}\ge\eta\right]. \]
The question, named 2-Hop Threshold Mass Welfare, is to maximize
\[ \sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\sum_{i\in I} \lambda_{ti}x_{ti}\,\mathbf 1[q_{ti}(x)\ge\theta]. \]
For the paper’s two-neighbor threshold, the natural per-capita normalization at scale \(N\) is \(\eta=1/N\) and \(\theta=2/N\): a bridge needs at least one person’s mass, and support needs at least two. The mass variables remain divisible, so the question asks whether the high-multiplicity relaxation makes the underlying welfare problem easier.

This is recognizable as the authors’ problem: it still assigns unit-demand agents unlimited-supply items to maximize the same intrinsic-value-weighted, two-hop, step-function welfare. The population is what becomes continuous; the product choices and the network externality remain central. The mirror covers Theorem 23’s step-function hardness result only, not the paper’s linear or concave results or its approximation algorithms.

My initial expectation is hardness transfers (Class B). The anchor’s two-item hardness suggests the difficult choices lie in the network-role and item incidence structure, rather than in the number of agents. The continuous question retains that structure in \(H\), and increasing the population by repeating each role need not remove it. But this is a research hypothesis, not a consequence of Theorem 23: a transfer must show that splitting each type across items does not destroy the reduction’s approximation gap. Further questions include whether an optimal mass allocation can be rounded without substantial welfare loss, and how approximability depends on \(\tau\), \(m\), and the threshold bit lengths.

The soft spot is the network regime. The paper allows arbitrary graphs of individually described agents; this mirror uses repeated, block-mixing roles. Encoding a hard graph may require \(\tau\) to grow with the reduction, even while each type has many members. And divisible assignments could change the answer. So the theorem gives a strong reason to ask this continuous question, but it does not establish its complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor raised is Theorem 23: APX-hardness for two-hop step-function welfare, including a two-item version. It is a genuine computational result, so the objection cannot be that the paper lacks a problem to mirror. The difficulty is preserving its two-hop threshold meaning when the population becomes continuous.

In the proposed block model, one person’s mass is \(1/N\). A bridge type can therefore put mass \(x_{ui}=1/N\) on item \(i\), while a distance-two type puts \(\Theta(1)\) mass on \(i\). The bridge condition is met, the distance-two mass enters the target’s support, and the target can receive positive welfare per capita. As \(N\) grows, the bridge mass tends to zero while the welfare it enables stays positive. At the limiting allocation, the bridge has mass zero; with a zero threshold, however, every positive bridge mass enables the effect. The limiting welfare therefore depends on vanishing mass that the continuous society has erased. This is a consequence of the paper’s local “there exists a same-item neighbor” condition, not a tie-breaking or strict-inequality issue.

The cleanest repair is to hold \(\eta\) and \(\theta\) at positive constants. That gives a coherent model in which bridges and support require population shares. It also makes the threshold a macroscopic cohort requirement: an agent’s externality depends on whether a non-negligible fraction of the relevant groups participates. That may be a good problem for communities with prevalence-based effects. Theorem 23, whose threshold is two neighbors, gives no reason to expect its reduction or its approximation boundary to carry over to that model. It would need its own motivation and complexity analysis.

The network creates a second constraint on the mirror. A finite type graph preserves the paper’s topology only for block-structured networks where members of each role have the same connections to whole other roles. In sparse networks, agents with the same broad role can have different neighbors and different overlaps among their two-hop neighborhoods. Those overlaps determine who supplies externality to whom. Encoding them in the type description can make the types as numerous as the agents; averaging them away changes the optimization problem. So the proposed model either narrows the network to dense block structure or loses incidence information central to the paper’s objective.

This is a serious weakness in the direct mirror of Theorem 23, though it does not prove that no worthwhile variant exists. A block-network model with fixed population-share thresholds could be worth studying on its own. The negative case is weakest there: the authors might recognize that rescaling as a natural co-modification. What remains unsupported is that it is a continuization of the paper’s two-neighbor hardness result, rather than a new prevalence-threshold problem.
