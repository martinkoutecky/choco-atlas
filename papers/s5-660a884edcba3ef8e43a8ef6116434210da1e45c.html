What the mirror covers

The proposed question targets Theorem 1.1; Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 have no stated continuous questions.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous rule-design mirror here, but the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Theorem 1.1 proves an impossibility about tournament rules: there is a finite \(k\) for which no Condorcet-consistent rule is \(k\)-\(\mathrm{SNM}\)-\(1/2\). The paper gives the concrete instance \(k=939\), \(n=1878\). Theorem 1.2 gives a Condorcet-consistent \(k\)-\(\mathrm{SNM}\)-\(2/3\) rule for every \(k\), and Theorem 1.3 gives a cover-consistent, \(2\)-\(\mathrm{SNM}\)-\(1/3\) rule. These are substantive rule-existence results, proved here, but none classifies a computational problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise. The NP-completeness result about Banks-set membership is cited prior work, not an anchor from this paper.

The strongest *near-case* is therefore Theorem 1.1, treated as a rule-feasibility question rather than as a complexity result. A possible mirror is Continuum Tournament-Rule Feasibility at Coalition Mass \(1/2\). Let a society be a unit-mass population partitioned into finitely many tournament-role types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\). A measurable tournament kernel \(W(x,y)\) records which member of the population beats which; within-type interactions are represented by a regular kernel, and members of a type share the same exogenous role. A rule \(F\) maps each such society to a probability measure \(\nu_W\) over its members. A coalition is a measurable set \(S\) with \(\mu(S)\leq 1/2\); its action replaces \(W\) only on \(S\times S\). Its gain is \(\nu_{W'}(S)-\nu_W(S)\).

The question is whether there is an anonymous, block-Condorcet-consistent rule \(F\) for this model whose gain is at most \(1/2\) for every such coalition and every allowed internal change. A solution is the rule itself, with an effective representation for evaluating it on finite descriptions of the types and kernels. This covers the paper’s large-coalition, approximate-strategyproofness result, not its separate \(2\)-team or cover-consistency results. It asks a tournament-rule question the authors could plausibly recognize, while making the population the continuous object.

The high-multiplicity story would be many teams occupying a small number of recurring tournament roles, such as large cohorts of teams in the same league or competition format, with similar external match relations. The paper itself gives some motivation for that regime: its lower-bound argument expands a small tournament into groups of copies with balanced internal tournaments. That makes a small-role limit worth asking about. It does not establish that these copies are identical types under the full model, though; the internal match pattern is part of the issue, not a detail to wave away.

My expectation would be a no-go in some finite-step versions, because the paper’s obstruction may survive in a sufficiently faithful role-based limit. Whether it survives in a genuinely atomless model is open; the theorem does not prove that. Useful follow-ups are whether the \(939/1878\) obstruction persists at fixed support size, what happens for coalition mass below \(1/2\), and whether a continuum rule can be characterized or computed from a finite kernel description.

The soft spot is substantial: tournament winners are individual teams, whereas a continuum winner rule is naturally a probability measure over a population. The Condorcet condition and the effect of changing matches inside a positive-mass coalition both need careful continuum definitions. Those co-modifications might still produce a model the authors recognize, but they prevent presenting this as a direct limit theorem. And under the required anchor standard, the candidate remains a rule-feasibility mirror, not an anchored computational-complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named result about computational complexity. Theorem 1.1 rules out a class of tournament rules; Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 construct rules with specified incentive and fairness properties. The LP is proof machinery for those claims. The paper does not classify the complexity of computing a manipulation, checking an SNM guarantee, or finding a rule. The NP-completeness result it mentions concerns Banks-set membership and is prior work. That makes Theorem 1.1 the strongest possible anchor here, but it is a rule-feasibility result, not the kind of computational result ChoCo is set up to mirror.

The proposed mass-\(1/2\) question is the best version of that anchor. Its mass does not vanish: the paper’s lower bound uses a coalition of 939 among 1878 teams. But an atomic society with 1878 types simply restates the finite theorem. Collapsing those teams into a few roles loses structure that the proof uses. In its expansion, each of the six original teams becomes 313 copies whose internal matches form a balanced tournament. Those copies have different match relations within their group. Treating them as one complete type erases those relations; treating each position as a separate type leaves support size growing with the number of teams. Either way, the finite-support high-multiplicity model does not carry the construction as proposed.

The strongest repair is a continuum tournament kernel \(W(x,y)\), with coalitions as measurable sets and outcomes as probability measures over teams. That is a coherent new model, but it changes what the paper’s fairness condition can say. A graphon description identifies inputs that differ on measure-zero sets, while a Condorcet winner is an individual whose match outcomes matter pointwise. Requiring a winner at the type-block level avoids that problem only by changing the winner from an individual team to a role or positive-mass group. These are substantive changes to the object and its axioms, not just the usual mass version of a discrete condition. The paper supplies no limit theorem connecting its finite SNM guarantees to this kernel model.

The case against is therefore strongest as a scope and model objection, not as proof that no one could study continuum tournament rules. The \(1/2\)-mass obstruction and the paper’s replication argument make such a project plausible. But it would be a new theory of strategic tournament kernels, without a computational result from this paper to anchor it. I would reject it as a ChoCo continuous mirror; I would not claim the broader graphon question could never be worthwhile.
