What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is a large school and housing allocation cohort. Let each household’s type record its initial school and housing endowments and its full utility over school–housing pairs; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of households of type \(t\). Each type reports rankings separately to the two sector authorities, which run TTC. A natural computational question would be whether a welfare-best Nash equilibrium of this two-sector reporting game achieves the first-best allocation. That continues the paper’s central strategic issue—coordination across separately run mechanisms—into a population with \(N\) households and a potentially much smaller number \(\tau\) of types.

But this is a research lead, not a mirror anchored in the paper’s results. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its numbered formal item is Definition 1, defining TTC; its own findings are experimental comparisons, while the theoretical results it discusses are cited from other work. None supplies the required anchor, so there is no honest anchor-specific problem or complexity direction to report.

The lead also has a substantial modeling gap: the experiment’s three players are individually pivotal, and applying separate TTC mechanisms to a continuum requires specifying how mass cycles work and how assignments across sectors pair up for households of the same type. Those choices could change the strategic game. The paper’s central contribution is observed behavior and treatment effects, not the complexity of computing equilibria. I would therefore describe it as a possible source of a new high-multiplicity question, but not as a paper with a qualifying continuous mirror anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Its numbered formal item is Definition 1 of TTC. Its findings are experimental comparisons: how often subjects use Pareto-dominant rankings, the resulting welfare, and differences across games and treatments. The implementation results it discusses belong to cited papers. None is a complexity result proved here, so none supplies an anchor for a computational mirror.

The school-and-housing cohort is a plausible setting for a new project. One could give each type its endowments in both sectors and utility over paired outcomes, represent the cohort by type masses, define TTC through mass flows, and track joint assignments across sectors. But asking for a welfare-best Nash equilibrium in that model changes the paper’s object: it studies what people do under repeated play, random rematching, and different elicitation timings. Computing an equilibrium would address predicted strategic possibilities, not the observed behavior or treatment effects. The equilibrium notion also needs a substantive choice: are deviations by individuals, which have zero mass, or by whole type blocks?

A continuum model of this kind may be worth studying on its own, and I would not argue that no such scenario can ever be useful. But it is a research lead rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper. The negative case is strong for rejecting this paper as an anchored ChoCo mirror; the broader claim that every possible continuization is unworthy would go beyond what the evidence supports.
