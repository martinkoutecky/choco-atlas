The continuous mirror question

Given rational creator-type masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), audience-share densities \(a_{it}\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t a_{it}=1\) for each user type \(i\), user masses \(q_i\), and usage rates \(\lambda_i\), choose admitted creator masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), a subscribing set \(S\subseteq\{1,2\}\), and a subscription fee \(z\) to maximize \(z\sum_{i\in S}q_i-\sum_t r_t x_t\), subject to \(z\le\lambda_i\sum_t a_{it}x_t\) for each \(i\in S\), where \(r_t=\max_{u\ge0}u\sum_{i:\lambda_i a_{it}\ge u}q_i\); include zero profit as an option.

The model it lives in

A continuum of small creators with finitely many complete audience-profile types: type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t\), each unit receives an integral join-or-decline decision, and the platform admits mass \(x_t\) while paying each admitted unit its solo-market reservation value \(r_t\). In a finite catalog of size \(N\), type-\(t\) creators have audience shares \(a_{it}/N\), so their individual outside options scale as \(r_t/N\) and aggregate payments remain finite.

The objection that survived

The continuized population is the provider side rather than the paper’s already mass-based user side, and the long-tail limit scales each creator’s audience share with catalog size; this makes the ChoCo fit less direct.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is an artist-mass portfolio mirror of Proposition 6, which is proved in this paper. Proposition 6 asserts that the platform’s arbitrary-allocation-rule portfolio problem (18) is NP-complete; the proof reduces from Subset Sum, whose NP-completeness the paper cites from elsewhere. In full, the discrete problem takes user masses \(q_1,q_2\) with \(q_1+q_2=1\), usage rates \(\lambda_2>\lambda_1>0\), and artist listening probabilities \(\pi_{ij}\), with \(\sum_j\pi_{ij}=1\). For each artist, the solo outside option is \(R_j=\max_{r\ge0} r\sum_{i:\lambda_i\pi_{ij}\ge r}q_i\). The platform chooses a portfolio \(J\) and subscribing user types \(S\subseteq\{1,2\}\) to maximize \(\bigl(\min_{i\in S}\lambda_i\sum_{j\in J}\pi_{ij}\bigr)\sum_{i\in S}q_i-\sum_{j\in J}R_j\), with an empty portfolio yielding zero. This is the underlying portfolio problem, not its integer-programming representation.

The regime I find plausible is a large catalog of independent small creators or rights-holders: many providers, but relatively few distinct profiles of audience across the two listener segments. A type \(t\) records that complete relevant profile. Let \(\mu_t\) be its share of the creator population, and let \(a_{it}\) be its listening-share density for listener type \(i\), normalized so \(\sum_t\mu_t a_{it}=1\). In a realization with \(N\) creators, there are \(N\mu_t\) creators of type \(t\), each contributing listening share \(a_{it}/N\). Thus \(N\) can be large while the support size \(\tau\) stays moderate. A type includes the outside-option economics implied by its audience profile; any additional artist-specific costs or contract terms would have to be included in the type too.

The continuous question is: given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), profiles \(a_{it}\), and listener parameters \(q_i,\lambda_i\), choose how much mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each creator type to admit, and what subscription fee to charge, to maximize platform profit while paying admitted creators their solo-market reservation value? The outside-option payment per unit of creator mass is \(r_t=\max_{u\ge0}u\sum_{i:\lambda_i a_{it}\ge u}q_i\). For a proposed subscribing set \(S\), the platform’s fee is bounded by \(z\le\lambda_i\sum_t a_{it}x_t\) for every \(i\in S\); its profit is \(z\sum_{i\in S}q_i-\sum_t r_tx_t\). A solution gives the admitted masses \(x_t\), fee \(z\), and reservation payments to those masses. As in the paper’s set-of-user-types formulation, enumerate the three nonempty choices of \(S\), plus zero profit. If an omitted user type could pay the chosen fee, including it at that same fee only raises revenue, so an optimum can be represented by an actual subscriber set.

For each \(S\), this is a linear program: maximize \(z\sum_{i\in S}q_i-\sum_t r_tx_t\), subject to those fee bounds and \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\). With finite rational support, the continuous problem is therefore solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. I expect Class A: tractable. The subset-selection hardness can dissolve because a continuum cohort can be admitted in any proportion; each real creator still joins or declines as a whole. The model preserves the paper’s platform tradeoff between subscriber value and artist outside options, rather than replacing portfolio choice with a fixed portfolio.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their portfolio problem in a high-multiplicity catalog regime: Proposition 6 is explicitly about choosing which artists join, and the mirror keeps that decision, the outside option, and the platform’s profit objective. The paper already assigns masses \(q_i\) to its two listener types, so this is not a claim that continuous population is absent from the paper. It extends the mass-based model to the creator side, where the discrete portfolio choice drives Proposition 6’s hardness.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Proposition 6’s arbitrary-allocation-rule portfolio optimization, not the NP-completeness results for pro-rata or user-centric portfolios, nor the paper’s comparisons of payment rules. The main follow-up is the high-multiplicity dictionary: how closely can an optimal mass portfolio be implemented by whole creators in a large finite catalog? The paper’s Theorem 3, which gives an arbitrary-rule solution within the outside-option value of one artist, makes that a concrete question, but does not itself establish this mirror’s rounding guarantee.

The weak point is the scenario. Many creators may have distinct audience profiles or contracts, and the paper allows an “artist” to be a large catalog owner rather than a small interchangeable provider. The mirror is most convincing for a platform whose relevant supply consists of many small, comparable rights-holders; it is much less convincing for a handful of major labels or superstar artists. The type model handles genuine shared profiles, but it cannot make a sparse, idiosyncratic catalog into a high-multiplicity regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Proposition 6 as a ChoCo mirror is that its hard choice is over a catalog, not over a society making a collective decision. The platform is a single profit maximizer choosing which distinct content providers to carry. The paper already represents its user population by masses \(q_1,q_2\); the NP-completeness is in selecting artists. Continuizing the users therefore does not mirror Proposition 6. Continuizing the artists instead gives a continuous-supply portfolio problem in platform economics, which may be useful, but is a stretch from the programme’s stated computational social-choice gap.

There is also a real modeling step in the proposed artist-mass mirror. In the paper, each artist has probabilities \(\pi_{ij}\), and those probabilities sum to one across artists for each user type. To have many creators with bounded support, the mirror sets \(\pi_{ij}=a_{it}/N\) for creators of type \(t\). Then each creator’s solo revenue is \(r_t/N\), while aggregate reservation payments remain finite. This is coherent as a *diffuse long-tail catalog* model, but it is not ordinary duplication of fixed artist types: the individual audience shares shrink as \(N\) grows, and the LP’s \(a_{it}\) are audience densities introduced for that limit. The proponent’s unanswered finite-to-continuous dictionary is therefore central to validating this as the same economic problem.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. A platform with many small, independent rights-holders and a few stable audience-density profiles is a plausible regime. The paper’s model makes audience probabilities and outside options the relevant features, so providers sharing those features can be treated as interchangeable; the aggregate outside-option cost need not vanish just because each provider’s share is small. The proposed LP preserves portfolio choice and profit, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as their problem under that regime. The scaling is a substantial co-modification, but the programme explicitly allows such co-modifications when continuizing the population requires them.

So I cannot honestly sustain the universal negative. The strongest objection is about whether a continuum of content providers belongs in ChoCo’s scope, and whether the proposed scaling has a convincing economic interpretation. Neither rules out the long-tail scenario. There is no basis here for an “already done” objection: the paper’s continuous user masses and high-multiplicity neighbors do not already answer the continuous-provider portfolio question. Proposition 6 survives the negative case, though its value to ChoCo depends on accepting the supply side as the population being continuized.
