The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-institution endowments \(z_t\), and per-institution liabilities \(\ell_{ts}\) owed uniformly across type-\(s\) mass, compute the greatest proportional clearing profile. Equivalently, solve for \(\lambda_t\in[0,1]\) maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\lambda_t\sum_{s\in T}\ell_{ts}\), subject for every \(s\in T\) to \(z_s+\frac{1}{\mu_s}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\lambda_t\ell_{ts}-\lambda_s\sum_{u\in T}\ell_{su}\geq 0\); set \(\lambda_t=0\) by convention if type \(t\) has no liabilities.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population with finitely many complete institution types. Each type has a mass, an endowment per institution, and total liabilities per institution to each creditor type; those liabilities are spread uniformly across the creditor type’s mass. The decision variable \(\lambda_t\) is type \(t\)’s proportional payment rate, and the objective maximizes total payments per unit of population subject to nonnegative type-level equity.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers greatest clearing under proportional division rules, anchored by Theorem 4.2, for uniformly pooled type-to-type exposures. It leaves the constrained-awards, constrained-losses, and priority formulations, the multiplicity examples, and decentralized selection of the least clearing matrix aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a type-mass version of the paper’s proportional-rule clearing problem. The paper’s Theorem 4.2 says that, for a finite financial network in which all agents use proportional division rules, the greatest clearing payment matrix is the unique solution of an LP: maximize \(\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}\) over nonnegative payments \(P\) and nonnegative \(\lambda_i\), subject to \(P_{ij}=\lambda_iL_{ij}\), \(\lambda_i\leq 1\), and \(z+P^\top\mathbf 1-P\mathbf 1\geq 0\). The paper identifies this result as previously shown by Eisenberg and Noe (2001), and places it as a special case of its own Theorem 4.1. It is an algorithmic anchor, not a new hardness result; the paper gives no named hardness result to mirror.

Call the continuous problem Type-Mass Greatest Proportional Clearing. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of \(\tau\) institution types, with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) has a per-institution endowment \(z_t\), and each type-\(t\) institution owes total liability \(\ell_{ts}\) to type-\(s\) creditors. Obligations to a type are spread proportionally across that type’s mass. Types include the full relevant profile: endowment, exposure to every creditor type, and division rule.

The question is to compute the greatest clearing payment profile. Write \(\lambda_t\) for the fraction of type \(t\)’s liabilities that it pays, so its per-institution payment to type \(s\) is \(\lambda_t\ell_{ts}\). Its assets per institution are \(z_s+\frac{1}{\mu_s}\sum_t\mu_t\lambda_t\ell_{ts}\). The problem is to find the payment profile maximizing total paid liabilities per unit of population, \(\sum_t\mu_t\lambda_t\sum_s\ell_{ts}\), subject to \(0\leq\lambda_t\leq1\) and, for each type \(s\), \(z_s+\frac{1}{\mu_s}\sum_t\mu_t\lambda_t\ell_{ts}-\lambda_s\sum_u\ell_{su}\geq0\). The payment profile is the solution; \(\lambda_t\) for a type with no liabilities can be fixed by convention.

This is an LP with \(\tau\) payment-rate variables and \(\tau\) equity constraints, built from a \(\tau\times\tau\) exposure table. With binary-rational input, it is solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). I expect Class A: the finite greatest-clearing computation remains tractable and compresses to type-level input.

The regime I find plausible is a large payment or supply-chain network of small firms or intermediaries with standardized balance sheets and diversified, pooled exposures to a relatively small number of creditor classes. There might be many thousands of institutions but only tens or hundreds of distinct complete profiles. The paper itself notes supply chains as an application of its financial-network framework. Here the shares \(\mu_t\) affect the balance equations: a larger debtor mass relative to creditor mass changes per-institution receipts, so population mass is doing substantive modeling work.

There is also an exact finite-to-mass correspondence for this structured regime. Given \(n_t\) institutions of each type, let \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and have each type-\(t\) institution owe \(\ell_{ts}/n_s\) to each type-\(s\) institution. A type-symmetric finite clearing solution then reduces to the LP above; conversely, rational masses can be realized by choosing a common denominator and constructing that finite network. This does not cover arbitrary bilateral networks, but it makes the mirror a faithful high-multiplicity version of the authors’ greatest-clearing question.

The scope is deliberately limited to the proportional-rule result in Theorem 4.2, with Theorem 4.1’s general characterization as support. It does not cover the paper’s constrained-awards, constrained-losses, or priority-rule formulations, its examples of multiplicity, or its discussion of decentralized selection of the least clearing matrix. Those other division rules suggest follow-up questions about whether type-mass formulations retain their mixed-integer structure and whether special cases become tractable.

The weak point is the required exchangeability: if institutions of one type have different named counterparties or materially different exposures within a creditor class, they must be split into more types, potentially eliminating the compression. The mirror also lands on a known LP result rather than exposing a discrete hardness result that might disappear in the continuum. Its positive case is therefore about a credible and computationally useful high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s exact clearing problem, not about a new complexity phenomenon.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor is the right one to test: Theorem 4.2 gives a named computational result, and its greatest-clearing LP supplies a clear target for a mirror.

The strongest objection is that a financial network is defined by who owes whom. In a general network, an institution’s asset value depends on payments from specific counterparties, whose payments depend on their own counterparties. Grouping institutions by balance sheet and total liabilities to each creditor class loses that structure. Two networks with the same class-level liability totals can send money to different creditors and produce different asset values and clearing payments. To preserve the paper’s exact problem, types may need to encode the full pattern of counterparties; then the number of types can grow to the number of institutions.

The proposed LP avoids that problem by imposing a special network structure: every institution in a class spreads its liabilities uniformly across each creditor class. That gives an exact finite-to-mass correspondence, but it is an additional complete-diversification assumption. It is not implied by having many institutions with similar balance sheets. A more general graphon or exposure-kernel model could retain richer relationships, but it would need a finite, explicit representation of that kernel to pose the same kind of computational question; with a finite block representation, it returns to a structured class of networks.

This is a real limit on the mirror’s reach, but it does not defeat the best version. Pooled or class-level exposures in a large, standardized network are a coherent scenario, and the authors could plausibly recognize greatest clearing in that setting as their problem. The proponent’s version is narrow, yet the prompt’s universal burden makes that enough: I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous mirror. The negative case is therefore weak; it argues against treating the mirror as representative of general financial networks, but the type-mass mirror survives for the pooled-exposure regime.
