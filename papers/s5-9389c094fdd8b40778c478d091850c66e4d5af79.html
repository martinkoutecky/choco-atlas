The continuous mirror question

Given finite chores \(B\), threshold \(\theta\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over complete types \(t\in T\), and rational profiles \((d_t,w_t)\), promised that the positive-mass disutility graph is a disjoint union of complete bipartite components whose induced exchange graph is strongly connected, output prices \(p\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{B}\) with \(\sum_jp_j=1\) and per-unit-type bundles \(x_{tj}\) satisfying \(x_{tj}>0\Rightarrow d_{tj}<\theta\), \(d_{tj}/p_j\le d_{t\ell}/p_\ell\) for every \(\ell\), \(\sum_jx_{tj}p_j=\sum_jw_{tj}p_j\), and \(\sum_t\mu_tx_{tj}=\sum_t\mu_tw_{tj}\) for every chore \(j\).

The model it lives in

A finite chore set with a distribution \(\mu\) over complete disutility-endowment types; prices \(p\) and per-unit-type allocations \(x\) are the decision variables, and the objective is feasibility of competitive equilibrium under minimum-pain-per-buck, earnings, and mass-weighted market-clearing constraints.

The objection that survived

The reductions use formula-sized chore and gadget structures and may require many distinct complete agent types, so they establish hardness of a compressed population representation rather than hardness caused specifically by population multiplicity or a small standardized type catalogue.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 45 and 16, while Theorems 4 and 35 serve only as existence guarantees and the remaining fixed-point lemmas and auxiliary results receive no separate computational mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does PPAD-hardness persist when \(|T|\), the number of chores, or the number of disutility components is bounded?
  • Can equilibria be computed efficiently for a fixed chore set and a fixed catalogue of complete types?
  • Can one distinguish hardness carried by population multiplicity from hardness carried by the growing chore and gadget structure?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a genuine high-multiplicity version of the paper’s competitive chore-division problem. I would lead with Theorem 45 and use Theorem 16 as a second, independent anchor. Both mirrors are Class B: the paper’s hardness survives continuization because its combinatorics live in chore types, disutility structure, and price gadgets rather than in the mere number of named agents.

A type is a complete agent profile. In the exchange model, type \(t\) consists of a disutility vector \(d_t=(d_{tj})_{j\in B}\) and an endowment vector \(w_t=(w_{tj})_{j\in B}\). A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many such types. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population having exactly that disutility and endowment profile. The per-capita supply of chore \(j\) is therefore \(s_j=\sum_t\mu_t w_{tj}\). This is not continuizing the chores: the paper’s chores are already divisible. The new continuous object is the population, while the chore set remains finite.

The natural regime is a large service economy with many repeated employment or household-contract types. For example, a nationwide organization may have thousands of workers on each of a modest number of standardized contracts. Their disutilities describe the burdens of different chore-hours, while their endowments describe the duties or service credits they initially bring. The relevant population size can be much larger than the number \(q\) of distinct \((d,w)\)-types. A finite instance with \(N_t\) agents of type \(t\) becomes \(\mu_t=N_t/N\); conversely, any rational \(\mu\) can be expanded into a finite high-multiplicity population.

My lead problem would be Continuous Competitive Chore Equilibrium under Strong Connectivity. The input consists of a finite chore set \(B\), a threshold \(\theta\), a finite type support \(\mathcal T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational type data \((d_t,w_t)\). Form the disutility graph on positive-mass types and chores, with an edge \(t j\) exactly when \(d_{tj}<\theta\). Require this graph to be a disjoint union of complete bipartite components. Form the exchange graph on those components, with an arc from component \(K\) to component \(L\) when every chore in \(L\) has positive endowment from some positive-mass type in \(K\), and require this exchange graph to be strongly connected.

The question is to output a price vector \(p\in\mathbb R_{>0}^{B}\), normalized by \(\sum_jp_j=1\), and a per-unit-mass allocation \(x=(x_{tj})\), such that, for every type \(t\) with \(\mu_t>0\),

\[ x_{tj}>0 \Longrightarrow d_{tj}<\theta \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{d_{tj}}{p_j}\le \frac{d_{t\ell}}{p_\ell} \ \text{for every chore }\ell, \]

\[ \sum_j x_{tj}p_j=\sum_j w_{tj}p_j, \]

and, for every chore \(j\),

\[ \sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=\sum_t\mu_t w_{tj}. \]

Thus the objective is to find any competitive equilibrium, not to optimize a secondary welfare function. The allocation \(x_{tj}\) is the bundle received by one unit of type-\(t\) population; the aggregate amount assigned to that type is \(\mu_t x_{tj}\).

The anchor is Theorem 45, proved in this paper. It states that if \(\mathcal I\) is the class of chore-division instances satisfying Conditions 1 and 2 from Section 3—respectively, the disutility graph being a disjoint union of complete bipartite graphs and the exchange graph being strongly connected—then chore division is PPAD-hard even when restricted to \(\mathcal I\). The existence theorem preceding it says that every such instance admits a competitive equilibrium, so this is hardness of finding an equilibrium in a promised-solvable class, not hardness caused by nonexistence. The PPAD-hardness reduction is proved here from normalized polymatrix games, whose PPAD-hardness is cited from Chen, Paparas, and Yannakakis [CPY17]. In the reduction, an equilibrium price vector yields a polymatrix equilibrium through a transformation of the form

\[ z_i=\frac{p(b_i^K)-(1-\alpha_K)}{2\alpha_K}, \]

and the resulting \(z\) satisfies \(z_{2i-1}+z_{2i}=1\) together with the normalized polymatrix implications comparing \(z^\top M_{*,2i-1}\) and \(z^\top M_{*,2i}\).

This theorem transfers directly to the continuous problem. Given a finite hard instance, assign each named agent’s complete \((d,w)\)-profile a rational population mass. The aggregate supply equations are just the original market-clearing equations divided by the population size. Conversely, a type-level equilibrium can be expanded by giving every agent of a type the same bundle. If desired, every type can be replicated \(r\) times without changing the continuous instance, making the actual population arbitrarily larger than the number of distinct types. The price vector—and therefore the polymatrix strategy extracted from it—does not change. I therefore expect this mirror to be PPAD-hard, with hardness transferring in Class B.

The paper’s use of highly structured price gadgets actually strengthens the mirror’s plausibility. The reduction already reasons through groups of agents with common disutility patterns and through aggregate endowment and price equations. Replacing repeated groups by masses is faithful to the algebra rather than a simplification of the underlying economic question. The continuous question asks exactly what the paper asks—whether there are prices and an allocation satisfying minimum-pain-per-buck demand, earnings, and market clearing—only with a distribution of agent profiles instead of a list of names.

The second anchor is Theorem 16, also proved in this paper. It states that determining an \((11/12+\delta)\)-competitive equilibrium, for any \(\delta>0\), in chore division with fixed earnings is strongly NP-hard. The proof is by a reduction from 3-SAT: a satisfying assignment yields an exact competitive equilibrium, while an unsatisfying formula yields no \((11/12+\delta)\)-competitive equilibrium. The construction uses variable gadgets with two local equilibria and clause gadgets whose prices reveal whether a clause is satisfied. All disutility and earning values are constant-sized, which is why the theorem is stated as strong NP-hardness. Remark 17 further explains how fixed earnings can be converted to equal earnings by making identical copies of agents.

The corresponding continuous problem, Continuous Fixed-Earnings Chore Equilibrium, has a finite type distribution \(\mu\), disutilities \(d_{tj}\), fixed earning requirements \(e_t\), threshold \(\theta\), and per-capita chore supplies \(s_j\). The solution is a price vector \(p>0\) and per-unit-mass allocation \(x\) satisfying

\[ x_{tj}>0 \Longrightarrow d_{tj}<\theta \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{d_{tj}}{p_j}\le\frac{d_{t\ell}}{p_\ell} \ \text{for all }\ell, \]

\[ \sum_j x_{tj}p_j=e_t \]

for every positive-mass type, and exact clearing

\[ \sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=s_j. \]

Its approximation version replaces exact clearing by

\[ \alpha s_j\le\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le \frac{s_j}{\alpha}, \qquad \alpha=\frac{11}{12}+\delta. \]

The decision question is whether such an \(\alpha\)-competitive equilibrium exists; the search question is to produce one. To recover the paper’s unit-supply finite instances exactly, take a finite population scale \(N\), set \(\mu_t=N_t/N\), and use \(s_j=1/N\). Then the continuous clearing equations are simply the original equations normalized per agent. Equivalently, as the paper observes, fixed earnings can be represented in the exchange model by setting \(w_{tj}=e_t\) for every chore \(j\), after normalizing prices.

I again expect hardness to transfer. The 3-SAT construction can be represented by a rational distribution over its variable-gadget and clause-gadget types; duplicating every type preserves the equilibrium conditions while making the underlying population arbitrarily large. The hardness is carried by the finite chore-and-gadget structure, not by one-person targeting. This mirror is therefore also Class B. A natural follow-up is whether the problem becomes tractable when the number of chore types, disutility components, or population types is fixed, and whether the paper’s equal-earnings simulation remains polynomial under a compressed type representation.

The scope is deliberately limited. These mirrors cover the paper’s exchange and fixed-earnings competitive-equilibrium problems, especially Theorems 45 and 16. Theorem 35 is useful as the existence guarantee behind the first promised search problem, but I am not presenting it as a separate computational anchor. I am also not claiming that the paper’s fixed-point proof automatically supplies a polynomial algorithm in the continuous model; it supplies the equilibrium object, while Theorem 45 says that finding it can remain PPAD-hard.

The weakest point is that the high-multiplicity interpretation is more convincing mathematically than empirically. The hardness reductions contain many finely engineered agent profiles, and after aggregation the number of distinct types still grows with the source instance. Repeating those profiles establishes a legitimate continuum population, but it does not show that PPAD-hardness is caused by population heterogeneity or that the continuous model is a realistic market. There is also a normalization choice for fixed earnings—per-capita supplies must scale with population size—which a referee would expect to see stated explicitly. Those are real limitations, but they do not undermine the central positive case: the paper’s equilibrium problems admit precise population-continuum formulations, their finite instances embed exactly through rational type masses, and the named hardness results survive that continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed mirrors establish only a cloning property, not a genuinely population-continuous problem. In both reductions, the hard information lies in the support of the type distribution and in the named chore structure.

For Theorem 45, each agent \(a^K_{i,j}\) carries a row-specific endowment encoded by \(M_{i,j}\), and its disutility neighbourhood is tied to particular chore pairs. These agents are therefore generally distinct complete types. Replacing each by mass \(1/N\) merely turns a list of named agents into a histogram. Duplicating every type \(R\) times leaves the normalized distribution \(\mu\) unchanged, so the claimed arbitrarily large population is invisible in the continuous input. The PPAD reduction still depends on the growing number of chore pairs, components, and distinct type profiles—not on population multiplicity.

The same criticism applies to Theorem 16. Variable agents and clause agents are wired to formula-specific chores, with clause-specific disutility profiles and earnings. A fixed catalogue of standardized household or employment types cannot represent arbitrary 3-SAT instances without putting the instance back into the growing chore set or into essentially one type per named agent. Thus the proposed mirror does not demonstrate that a natural high-multiplicity chore economy inherits the theorem; it demonstrates that the equilibrium problem is closed under duplicating an already arbitrary finite economy.

This is a real limitation on the positive case, but it does not defeat the best mirror. The paper’s equilibrium constraints use no individual identity beyond the complete profile \((d,w)\) or \((d,e)\). If many agents share such a profile, their allocations can be aggregated exactly:

\[ \sum_t \mu_t x_{tj} = \sum_t \mu_t w_{tj}. \]

Conversely, a type-level allocation can be assigned identically to every agent of that type. Divisibility and linearity mean that even splitting a type’s mass across several bundles introduces no new phenomenon. The same is true for fixed earnings.

Consequently, neither anchor suffers from identity dependence, degeneration, or a mismatch between the finite and continuous models. The stronger continuous formulation proposed by the other analyst is mathematically legitimate, and the paper contains named computational results—Theorems 16 and 45—that transfer to it. No existing continuous treatment appears to have already answered those population-computational questions.

So the honest negative conclusion is narrow: the proponent has not shown that the mirror captures a genuinely population-driven regime rather than a compressed encoding of an arbitrary finite market. But that is not enough for the requested universal rejection. Under ChoCo’s rules, the high-multiplicity equilibrium mirror remains a worthwhile Class B candidate, and the negative case is ultimately weak.
