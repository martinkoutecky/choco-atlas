The continuous mirror question

Given \(r\) good kinds, two complete additive valuation types \(v^1,v^2\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}^r\) of mass \(1/2\) each, and per-capita supply \(1/2\) of every kind, decide whether there is a finitely supported rational lottery over feasible profiles satisfying the following conditions, and construct one when it exists. A profile consists of masses \(x_{a,B}\ge 0\) on integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^r\), with \(\sum_B x_{a,B}=1/2\) for each type \(a\) and \(\sum_{a,B}x_{a,B}B_g=1/2\) for each good kind \(g\). Define \(\operatorname{MMS}^{\mathrm{cont}}_a=\sup_{\pi:\,\mathbb{E}_{B\sim\pi}[B_g]=1/2\ \forall g}\operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{B\sim\pi}v^a(B)\). In every profile in the lottery's support, each occupied type-\(a\) bundle \(B\) must satisfy \(v^a(B)\ge(4/5)\operatorname{MMS}^{\mathrm{cont}}_a\), and every pair of occupied bundles \(B,C\) of types \(a,b\) must satisfy \(v^a(B)\ge v^a(C\setminus\{g\})\) for every \(g\in C\) with \(v^a_g>0\). The lottery must also be ex-ante envy-free: for its expected masses \(\bar x\), \(\sum_B\bar x_{a,B}v^a(B)/(1/2)\ge\sum_B\bar x_{b,B}v^a(B)/(1/2)\) for all types \(a,b\). What is the complexity of deciding feasibility and constructing such a lottery, in \(r\), the valuation bit length, and the output size?

The model it lives in

A population of two complete additive valuation types, each of mass \(1/2\), shares goods whose per-capita supplies scale with the population. Decision variables are masses on integral bundles and a finite lottery over feasible mass profiles; each profile must meet the EFX and continuous-MMS conditions, while the lottery meets ex-ante envy-freeness.

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends Theorem 8 and its running-time result, Lemma 9, to the two-type goods population. It leaves the chores results, including Theorem 13, and the three-agent limitation in Lemma 15 outside this anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every two-type instance admit a lottery satisfying ex-post EFX and the \(4/5\) continuous-MMS guarantee alongside ex-ante envy-freeness?
  • When such a lottery exists, can feasibility and construction be solved efficiently with finite support?
  • How does the continuous MMS benchmark compare with the MMS of finite replicas as the cohort sizes grow?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high-multiplicity replica of the paper’s two-agent fair division problem. The paper has no named NP-hardness result to anchor a hardness mirror. Its Section 4 Lemma 15 constrains probabilities for a restricted class of ECE randomizations; it is not a complexity-hardness theorem. The best anchors are therefore the paper’s algorithmic results.

Lead: Two-Cohort Replica Fair Division for Goods. Theorem 8, proved here, says that for every two-agent additive goods instance, Algorithm 4 returns a lottery whose supported allocations are each EFX and \(4/5\)-MMS, while the lottery is ex-ante EF. Lemma 9, also proved here, gives running time \(O(m\log m)\). The theorem’s guarantees use an ordered-instance EFX and MMS result from [9], but the theorem and running-time analysis are given in this paper.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover. The input is \(r\) kinds of goods and two complete agent types, with additive rational valuations \(v^1,v^2\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^r\). Each type has mass \(1/2\), and the per-capita supply of each good kind is \(1/2\). This is the replica regime in which, for \(N=2k\) agents, there are \(k\) agents of each type and \(k\) copies of each good kind. The types are complete because members of a cohort have identical values for every good kind.

A continuous allocation profile is a set of masses \(x_{a,B}\ge0\), where \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^r\) is an integral bundle and \(x_{a,B}\) is the mass of type \(a\) receiving it. Every profile must satisfy
\[ \sum_B x_{a,B}=\tfrac12 \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{a=1}^2\sum_B x_{a,B}B_g=\tfrac12\quad\text{for each good kind }g. \]
The mass records how many people receive each bundle; it does not make an individual’s bundle fractional.

Define the continuous MMS of type \(a\) by
\[ \operatorname{MMS}^{\mathrm{cont}}_a = \sup_{\pi:\,\mathbb{E}_{B\sim\pi}[B_g]=1/2\ \forall g} \operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{B\sim\pi} v^a(B). \]
This is the maximin share in the replica limit: a unit mass of integral bundles uses exactly the per-capita supply. The question is whether one can compute a finitely supported rational lottery \(D\) over feasible profiles such that, in every profile in its support, every occupied type-\(a\) bundle \(B\) satisfies \(v^a(B)\ge(4/5)\operatorname{MMS}^{\mathrm{cont}}_a\), and every pair of occupied bundles satisfies the paper’s goods-EFX condition. Also require ex-ante envy-freeness: writing \(\bar x_{a,B}=\mathbb{E}_{D}[x_{a,B}]\), for all types \(a,b\),
\[ \frac{1}{1/2}\sum_B \bar x_{a,B}v^a(B) \ \ge\ \frac{1}{1/2}\sum_B \bar x_{b,B}v^a(B). \]
In words, a type-\(a\) person’s expected value for their own bundle must be at least their expected value for a type-\(b\) person’s bundle. This also gives ex-ante proportionality by averaging over the population. The computational question is the feasibility and construction of such a lottery, with complexity in \(r\), the valuation bit length, and the output size.

I would provisionally predict Class A, because there are only two complete valuation types, the supply and ex-ante conditions are linear in bundle frequencies, and the finite two-agent result shows that correlated randomization can meet strong ex-post and ex-ante guarantees together. A plausible route is to bound the relevant bundle patterns and then solve a continuous optimization problem over their masses. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 8. Questions it raises include whether the needed support can be bounded efficiently, whether the guarantees hold for every two-type instance, and how the continuous MMS compares with finite-replica MMS.

Secondary anchor: Two-Cohort Replica Fair Division for Chores. Theorem 13, proved here using a chores guarantee from [9], says that for every two-agent additive chores instance, Algorithm 6 returns a lottery supported on allocations that are EFX and \(7/6\)-MMS, and is ex-ante EF. Lemma 14 gives running time \(O(m\log m)\). Its mirror uses the same types, masses, integral bundles, and supply constraints, with \(d^a=-v^a\) denoting disutility. Define the continuous maximin cost share as
\[ \operatorname{MMSCost}^{\mathrm{cont}}_a = \inf_{\pi:\,\mathbb{E}_{B\sim\pi}[B_g]=1/2\ \forall g} \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{B\sim\pi} d^a(B). \]
The problem is to find a finite rational lottery over feasible profiles in which every occupied type-\(a\) bundle has disutility at most \((7/6)\operatorname{MMSCost}^{\mathrm{cont}}_a\), each profile is EFX under the paper’s chores definition, and the lottery is ex-ante EF under the same type-to-type comparisons above. I would also tentatively expect Class A, for the same fixed-type and mass-optimization reasons, while asking whether the chores EFX constraints admit a compact representation.

The regime is plausible: two large cohorts with fixed additive valuations divide a stock of standardized goods or chores whose copies scale with population. With \(N=2\), this is exactly the paper’s two-agent input format; as \(N\) grows, cohort masses and per-capita supplies give the high-multiplicity limit. Fractional mass describes the fraction of cohort members receiving each bundle, while every individual bundle remains integral.

The soft spot is substantial: both anchors concern exactly two agents. Neither proves that EFX and the stated MMS guarantees survive when each type represents many individuals, and the continuous MMS benchmark can be stronger than the finite two-agent benchmark. So the paper gives a sound anchor for asking the replica question, but not an algorithm for answering it. That gap is precisely why this looks like a useful continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that the paper’s results are specifically about two recipients. To turn them into population questions, one must move to \(2k\) agents and \(k\) copies of each good or chore kind. That creates a recognizable high-multiplicity model, but it is a new scaling regime; Theorems 8 and 13 do not themselves establish their guarantees there. Keeping the stock fixed as the population grows is less promising: almost everyone receives nothing, so the fairness benchmarks degenerate. The scaled-supply model avoids that problem, though, and the programme explicitly allows this kind of co-modification.

For the goods anchor, there is a useful lift: replicate each two-agent allocation across the two cohorts. Every member of a cohort gets the same integral bundle. The original EFX and ex-ante EF guarantees then carry over to those two bundle types. The difficult remaining part is the continuous MMS benchmark: the paper’s \(4/5\)-MMS guarantee is relative to a two-agent partition, and does not settle the stronger mass-based benchmark in the proposed mirror. That leaves a real computational question, even if the paper’s algorithm is a weak guide to answering it.

The chores anchor has the same limitation. Replicating the two-agent allocations carries over EFX and ex-ante EF, but does not establish the \(7/6\) guarantee against continuous MMS cost. So the chores mirror is not independently defeated; it inherits the same open gap.

Section 4 does not supply a stronger objection. Lemma 15 rules out a specified family of two-phase-online randomizations on a three-agent instance. It is neither a complexity result nor evidence that a large-population version is meaningless.

I therefore cannot make the universal negative case convincingly. The proponent overstates the evidence for Class A: linear mass constraints alone do not make bundle choice, EFX, or the continuous MMS benchmark easy to optimize. But two cohorts of people with shared valuations dividing scaled supplies is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the resulting questions are ones the authors could recognize as fair division. That is enough to leave a worthwhile mirror standing.
