The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population mirror here, but the paper has no qualifying named computational result to anchor it. Its only numbered proposition, Proposition 1, says that attaching any tree of \(m\ge1\) arguments to an attacker increases the issue’s h-categorizer value, while attaching one to a defender decreases it. The paper proves that semantic property, but it does not assert a complexity result about a problem. The paper’s H1–H6 findings are experimental correlations, not numbered theorems about tractability or hardness. For example, in the no-votes protocol, greater learning probability correlates with the final value being closer to the merged value (\(R=-0.55029\)) and with lower average dissatisfaction (\(R=-0.70346\)); these experiments use seven agents and twenty arguments. They motivate a computational question, but they cannot serve as the required anchors.

The strongest plausible mirror would use a large online debate around one issue. Agents could share complete behavioral types consisting of their initial opinion graph, comfort-zone width, and learning probabilities. A platform with many users but a moderate number \(\tau\) of such cohorts is a credible high-multiplicity regime: mass is the fraction of users in each cohort. As users learn, mass splits across current opinion-graph states. The shared public graph and the h-categorizer semantics remain those of the paper, so this continues its debate problem rather than replacing it with a problem about fractional outcomes.

A precise *prospective, unanchored* question is Infinite-Population Debate Outcome. An instance gives a finite issue-oriented universe graph, a rational distribution over the cohorts and their behavioral parameters, and a rational threshold \(q\). Starting from the issue-only public graph, evolve the no-votes protocol in its deterministic population limit: each cohort’s mass splits among its eligible moves according to the paper’s random-choice rule and among learning outcomes according to its learning probabilities; any argument chosen by positive mass is added to the public graph. The question is whether the terminal issue value under the h-categorizer is at least \(q\). A solution is the yes/no answer, together with the terminal public graph and cohort-state distribution. This keeps the population as the continuous object and makes prediction of the paper’s debate outcome the computational task.

I would leave this proposed problem’s complexity open. The paper’s results give no basis for calling it tractable, transferring hardness, or predicting continuum-specific hardness. The weakest point in the positive case is therefore decisive for the requested grading: the mirror is plausible, but none of the paper’s named results establishes the complexity landscape that the programme asks analysts to connect to a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Its numbered Proposition 1 is a property of the h-categorizer under adding argument trees. H1–H6 are experimentally observed correlations, not complexity claims. So the proponent’s proposed *Infinite-Population Debate Outcome* is a new prediction problem motivated by the experiments, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

For the no-votes protocol, there is also a more fundamental modeling problem: the public graph changes when an argument is played even once. In a population limit, any move with positive probability is made by positive mass; under independent finite-population choices, it appears with probability approaching one as the population grows. Thus the graph’s evolution depends on which cohort states have positive mass, not on their proportions. The terminal h-categorizer value depends on that graph, so changing a cohort’s mass from a tiny positive amount to a large one need not matter, while setting it to zero can change the outcome abruptly. The proposed continuum therefore reduces to support and reachability, rather than treating aggregate population behavior as the evolving object.

That objection does not dispose of the strongest repair. The paper’s voting protocol makes argument weights depend on vote shares, so cohort masses can affect the public graph’s h-categorizer values and, in turn, agents’ eligible moves and learning. A large debate platform with many users in a moderate number of cohorts sharing opinion graphs and behavioral parameters is plausible. One could ask whether the terminal issue value crosses a threshold for a rational distribution over those cohorts. That is a genuine population-based computational question, and the paper explicitly studies the voting protocol.

I can argue that this threshold problem is prospective rather than anchored to a complexity result in the paper. I cannot honestly argue that the voting version fails the programme’s population test or is unworthy of study. The negative case is strong against the proposed no-votes mirror, but weak against the best available mirror; a universal “no worthwhile continuous mirror” verdict would overstate what the paper supports.
