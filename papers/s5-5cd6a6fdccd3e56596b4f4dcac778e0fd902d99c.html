The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a named computational result in this paper to anchor a valid continuous mirror. Its results concern existence and structure of voting rules, not the complexity or algorithms for a computational problem.

The closest foothold is Theorem 16, proved here: for \(m=q^2+q+1\), where \(q\) is a prime power, and \(n=11m\), it constructs an odd, monotone, unbiased voting rule that is not transitive-symmetric and has winning coalitions of size \(\lfloor1.508\sqrt n\rfloor+5\). That is a constructive existence theorem, but it does not classify a computational problem as tractable or hard, so it does not meet the requested anchor standard.

One could imagine taking mass over the \(2^{11}\) vote patterns of repeated 11-voter blocks and aggregating their local rule outcomes. But that changes the object into a population of blocks, and the paper provides no computational question or result for that model. Presenting it as a mirror of Theorem 16 would overstate the case. The honest positive-side conclusion is that this paper supplies no eligible anchor for the requested continuization case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a continuous mirror is strong because the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 proves that unbiasedness does not imply transitive symmetry; Theorems 5, 6, 7, and 16 establish existence and structural properties of voting rules. Even Theorem 16’s explicit construction is not a complexity result: it gives a family of rules, not an algorithmic problem with a stated input, objective, or complexity classification. That leaves no eligible anchor under the programme’s criteria.

The proposed 11-voter-block interpretation does not supply one. The paper studies how each voter’s vote can be pivotal within a rule whose voter positions matter. A distribution over the two vote types, \(+1\) and \(-1\), loses those positions and the graph structure that makes the rules unbiased but not transitive-symmetric. Preserving that structure by making each position a distinct type retains \(n\) distinct roles rather than a high-multiplicity population. Aggregating blocks and defining a new computational task could be interesting, but it would add a problem the paper does not study; it would not mirror Theorem 16.

A continuum also removes the paper’s central unit of analysis: an individual voter’s pivotality. In an atomless population, changing one agent has measure zero. One could define type-level or block-level influence instead, but that changes the question and still supplies no computational result from this paper to continue. The negative case is therefore persuasive for this paper’s fit with the programme. The broader claim that no adjacent computational project could be worthwhile is less secure; such a project would be new work, not a continuous mirror of these results.
