The continuous mirror question

For each round, given advertiser types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), slot classes \(J\), eligibility sets \(S_t\), integral demand caps \(\kappa_t\), per-capita supplies \(s_j\), and feasible rational marginals \(x_{t,j}\), compute rational masses \(\lambda_{t,A}\geq 0\) for bundles \(A\subseteq S_t\), \(|A|\leq\kappa_t\), such that \(\sum_A\lambda_{t,A}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{A:j\in A}\lambda_{t,A}=x_{t,j}\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,j}=s_j\).

The model it lives in

Advertiser types encode value, budget, eligibility, and demand cap; each type carries population mass \(\mu_t\). The input gives type-level fractional slot marginals, and the output gives masses \(\lambda_{t,A}\) over integral bundles, with no optimization objective. This is a uniform-matroid decomposition for each type; per-round distributions can be coupled over the persistent population.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 4's assignment-realization step through a type-mass bundle decomposition; leaves the auction guarantees in Theorems 1–3 and 6 and the impossibility result in Theorem 7 untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a coupling across rounds preserve the paper's ex-post Pareto-optimality guarantee for every realized allocation?
  • Can this decomposition be integrated into the full clinching mechanism while retaining its incentive guarantees under a suitable large-market strategic model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow mirror of Theorem 4, proved in this paper’s Appendix E. It is the paper’s clearest named algorithmic result: given a matrix \(M\) with \(|J|\) rows and \(|C|\) columns, whose entries are values from \(I\), with each value occurring at most \(|C|\) times, a swapping algorithm finds a matrix \(M'\) of the same size such that every value occurs just as often in each row as it did in \(M\), and at most once in each column. The paper uses this to turn fractional slot assignments into legal per-round assignments. The theorem does not state a running-time bound, although its decreasing-badness argument gives a finite constructive algorithm. The paper has no named NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity result. Theorem 6 establishes incentive compatibility, individual rationality, and Pareto optimality for an auction; Theorem 7 is an impossibility result about those properties. Neither gives a computational-complexity anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large batch of advertisers drawn from a small number \(\tau\) of campaign types. A type \(t\) records the advertiser’s value, budget, interest set, and per-round demand cap; advertisers of that type are identical in every respect used by the allocation problem. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of advertisers of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The batch contains \(N\) advertisers with \(N\gg\tau\), and slot supplies scale with \(N\), so mass allocations remain meaningful. Each advertiser still receives an integral bundle of slots; fractional quantities describe the share of a type receiving each bundle.

Call the resulting problem Mass-Slot Decomposition. An instance gives \(T,\mu\), slot classes \(J\), per-capita supplies \(s_j\), eligibility sets \(S_t\), caps \(\kappa_t\), and a proposed divisible allocation \(x_{t,j}\). The inputs are rational and satisfy \(0\le x_{t,j}\le\mu_t\), \(x_{t,j}=0\) for \(j\notin S_t\), \(\sum_j x_{t,j}\le\kappa_t\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{t,j}=s_j\). The question is to produce rational masses \(\lambda_{t,A}\) for admissible integral bundles \(A\subseteq S_t\), \(|A|\le\kappa_t\), such that \(\sum_A\lambda_{t,A}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{A:j\in A}\lambda_{t,A}=x_{t,j}\). Here \(\lambda_{t,A}\) is the mass of type \(t\) that receives bundle \(A\). The solution is an exact, sparse bundle-mass decomposition; there is no optimization objective. For multiple rounds, decompose each round’s marginals and couple the per-round bundle distributions over the persistent population.

I expect this problem to be tractable. For each type with \(\mu_t>0\), the normalized vector \(x_{t,j}/\mu_t\) lies in the independence polytope of a uniform matroid: its coordinates are between zero and one, and their sum is at most \(\kappa_t\). It therefore has a convex decomposition into integral bundles, with support of size at most \(|J|+1\). This gives a constructive algorithm polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the rational input bit length \(L\). If a per-type payment \(p_t\le b_t\) is carried over unchanged, the decomposition also preserves type-average utility under linear values: the average weighted capacity is \(\sum_j\alpha_jx_{t,j}/\mu_t\). This mirrors the assignment-realization step, not the full auction.

The scope is deliberately limited to implementing the divisible assignment while giving every unit of population an integral bundle. Further questions include whether a compact decomposition can preserve the paper’s ex-post Pareto guarantee and whether the full clinching mechanism has a high-multiplicity implementation retaining its incentive properties.

The weak point is that Theorem 4 concerns a finite matrix labelled by individual bidders; Mass-Slot Decomposition is a type-mass extension of that rounding step, not a mirror of the paper’s main mechanism theorem. I think the authors could recognize it as their assignment implementation problem in a large-market regime, especially with slot supplies scaled per capita. It is a credible but modest computational mirror, and should not be presented as a continuous version of Theorem 6 or Theorem 7.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the proposed anchor is outside ChoCo’s stated target. The paper’s named results establish mechanism properties, Pareto characterizations, and an impossibility theorem. Theorem 4 is the nearest computational anchor, but it asserts that a swapping algorithm exists; it gives no complexity bound or complexity classification. The proposed Mass-Slot Decomposition is therefore a new computational problem built from an implementation lemma, rather than a continuous version of a computational result whose complexity the paper studies.

A stronger version of the proposed scenario is plausible: many advertisers share campaign parameters, and the supply of ad impressions scales with the population. Types can include valuation, budget, interest set, and demand cap. Given a type-level fractional allocation, decomposing it into masses of integral bundles is coherent, and allocations across rounds can be coupled. I do not see a sound objection to that model. But it starts after the allocation has already been found. It does not compute the clinching auction’s outcome or mirror the paper’s incentive and Pareto guarantees. Extending the question to the full mechanism runs into a different issue: in a nonatomic population, one advertiser has zero mass, so individual deviations do not change the aggregate outcome. Restoring meaningful strategic behavior would require a different notion of incentive compatibility, such as group deviations.

That leaves a credible, narrow mirror of Theorem 4: a mass version of its assignment-realization step. It is modest and detached from the auction’s main results, but the paper’s authors could plausibly recognize it as their problem in a large-market setting. The negative case does not honestly defeat that anchor. It is strongest only if “worthwhile mirror” means a continuous complexity question corresponding to a complexity result in the paper. Under that reading, this paper has no qualifying anchor. Under the broader reading that includes constructive algorithmic lemmas, the proposed mirror survives; I would not reject it based on its familiar polyhedral structure or an expected polynomial-time solution.
