The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many household types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), demands \(d_t>0\), and per-capita supply \(s<D:=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t d_t\), with every household connected directly to the source, compute the largest \(r\) for which a feasible schedule exists. A schedule is a measurable function \(h_t:[0,1]^2\to\{0,1\}\), where \(z\) indexes households within type \(t\) and \(u\) is time; it must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t d_t\int_0^1h_t(z,u)\,dz\le s\) for almost every \(u\), and \(\int_0^1h_t(z,u)\,du\ge r\) for almost every household \(z\) of every type. Return \(r\) and a concise description of a schedule attaining it.

The model it lives in

The population is a distribution over complete types, each determined here by its demand \(d_t\), with mass \(\mu_t\). At each instant households still receive either their full demand or none, while any measurable fraction of each type can be served; total demand served is bounded by per-capita supply \(s\). Maximize the minimum connection duration across households. The exact optimum is \(r^*=s/D\), attained by rotating service through each type.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's geography-free MaxEED hardness result. It leaves the geographic tree results, including the Geographic Knapsack FPTAS and its transfer to leximin allocation, outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite populations with repeated demand types and total supply scaled per capita, what is the sharp gap between the finite MaxEED optimum and \(s/D\) as population size grows?
  • Which switching or geographic constraints prevent within-type rotation and change this atomless optimum?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is for a population mirror of Theorem 1, the paper’s hardness result for MaxEED. Theorem 1 is proved in this paper: MaxEED asks for the largest \(r\) such that every household can be connected for at least \(r\) of the unit time interval. The theorem says it is NP-hard to decide whether \(r \ge 1/2\), even when every household is directly connected to the power station and there are no geographic constraints. The proof reduces from Partition, setting supply to half the total demand. I would mirror this result alone; the paper’s tree-network algorithms would require extra assumptions about how network locations aggregate into types.

Here is a concrete continuous problem. Call it Atomless MaxEED. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of household types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a demand \(d_t>0\) for each type, and a per-capita supply \(s\), with \(s<D:=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t d_t\). All households are directly connected to the source, as in Theorem 1. Type \(t\) consists of a continuum of households of mass \(\mu_t\), labelled by \(z\in[0,1]\). A schedule is a measurable function \(h_t(z,u)\in\{0,1\}\), where \(h_t(z,u)=1\) means that household \(z\) of type \(t\) receives its full demand at time \(u\). It must satisfy \(\sum_t \mu_t d_t\int_0^1 h_t(z,u)\,dz\le s\) at almost every time. Each household’s utility is its connection duration, \(\int_0^1 h_t(z,u)\,du\). The task is to find the greatest \(r\) for which a feasible schedule gives almost every household utility at least \(r\), and to return such a schedule. The decision version asks whether this is possible for a specified threshold \(r\).

This mirror has an exact answer: \(r^*=s/D\). Any schedule giving every household at least \(r\) uses at least \(rD\) total demand-time, so \(rD\le s\). Conversely, set \(h_t(z,u)=1\) whenever the fractional part of \(z-u\) is less than \(r^*\). At each time, a fraction \(r^*\) of every type is served, and every household is served for fraction \(r^*\) of the interval. Thus the threshold decision reduces to checking \(r\le s/D\). The mirror is tractable.

The regime is a large population served through one effective bus or feeder, with repeated household demand profiles: for example, millions of households that need one of a modest number of common load levels during a scheduling period. A type is the complete description relevant to this problem—here, its demand and common network position. The masses are population fractions, and \(s=S/n\) is the paper’s total supply normalized by population size. This is a high-multiplicity regime: \(n\) can be very large while the number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct demand types is much smaller. Supply scaling is a necessary co-modification of the model; the object being continuized is the population. Time was already continuous in the paper, and each household still receives electricity all-or-nothing while connected.

The authors should recognize Atomless MaxEED as the high-multiplicity form of their own no-geography MaxEED problem: it retains the same shortage, full-demand-or-nothing service, connection-duration utility, and worst-off objective. It covers Theorem 1’s star-network case, not the paper’s geographic tree results. The result’s Partition hardness comes from choosing indivisible subsets of named households to fill the supply exactly. In the mirror, a positive fraction of each type can be served at once and service can rotate through its members. The hardness dissolves because it lives in finite household multiplicity—the Class A outcome this programme is meant to identify.

The further questions are whether finite populations with repeated types approach this answer at a useful rate, and when feeder, switching, or geographic constraints prevent within-type rotation. My weakest point is also the feature that makes the mirror tractable: a physical system may not let an operator select an arbitrary fraction of otherwise identical households at each instant. If households must be switched in fixed blocks, or their locations matter, this precise mirror no longer captures the deployment problem. But for the paper’s explicitly geography-free setting, that limitation does not undermine the continuous question; it marks the boundary of what Theorem 1’s mirror covers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection I can make is that Atomless MaxEED relies on splitting a demand type across households at each instant. On a shared feeder that switches everyone together, this may be impossible: the construction with \(h_t(z,u)\) assumes fine-grained control over otherwise identical households. But that is an operational limitation of some deployments, not a fundamental flaw in the mirror. The paper’s geography-free case permits selecting any feasible subset, and a large group of households with the same externally determined demand and individual switching is a plausible regime. Per-capita supply is an allowed co-modification when the population becomes a mass.

Nor does the move erase information MaxEED needs. The objective treats households anonymously, and households with the same demand are indistinguishable for this problem. The construction even gives every household the same utility, so replacing “almost every” with a pointwise guarantee would not undermine it.

Theorem 1 is a named computational result, and the paper’s continuous scheduling time is not already a continuous population. The mirror asks a legitimate high-multiplicity question. Its tractable answer may be simple, but simplicity of the answer is not a reason to reject the question under the programme’s criteria.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The control-granularity objection marks a boundary for the model, but a plausible scenario survives it; my case against a worthwhile continuous mirror is weak.
