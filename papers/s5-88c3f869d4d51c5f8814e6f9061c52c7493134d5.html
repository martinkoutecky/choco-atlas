The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported types \(t=(\succ_t,\rho_t)\), where \(\succ_t\) is a strict ranking and \(\rho_t\) identifies a constituency representative, and a fixed sequence \(\pi\) of \(m-1\) turns labelled by representatives, compute the unique SPNE winner \(c^\star\) and its weighted Borda welfare \(W_\mu(c^\star)=\sum_t \mu_t(m-r(t,c^\star))\). Each representative maximizes the ranking \(\succ_t\) over the final winner.

The model it lives in

Let each supported ranking-role type \(t\) represent mass \(\mu_t\) and designate one representative who acts on its assigned turns. The representatives play the paper’s finite sequential-elimination game; the output is its unique SPNE winner and that winner’s normalized, mass-weighted Borda score.

The objection that survived

The \(m-1\) turns are occupied by finitely many representatives, so constituency mass affects aggregate Borda welfare but not strategic choices; the mirror therefore covers delegate protocols.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 and its winner-computation result. It leaves the price-of-anarchy and sincerity-ratio bounds, and the experimental results, outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • For a fixed sequence \(\pi\), what are the exact worst-case weighted Borda price-of-anarchy and sincerity-ratio bounds as rational type masses vary?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror I see is Continuous Constituency Sequential-Elimination Winner. Let the society be a distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many strict rankings, with each \(\mu_t\) a binary rational fraction of a large electorate. In a plausible setting, preference-homogeneous constituencies each have a representative who acts for that bloc; the type records both its ranking and its role in the protocol. The fixed sequence lists which type’s representative eliminates a remaining candidate at each turn. Representatives maximize their own ordinal preference over the final winner. The population’s normalized Borda welfare is \(W_\mu(c)=\sum_t\mu_t(m-r(t,c))\).

Given \(C\), the supported types and their masses, and a type-labelled sequence of \(m-1\) turns, the problem asks for the unique SPNE winner \(c^\star\) and its welfare \(W_\mu(c^\star)\). A solution is that candidate and score. This keeps the paper’s discrete candidate-elimination actions and strategic objective; it makes the society a continuous population whose mass also gives a natural welfare measure.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It says that for any fixed elimination sequence and strict preference profile, a sequential-elimination game’s SPNE outcome is the same as sincere elimination with the sequence reversed. Since the paper also establishes that the outcome is polynomial-time computable, this is a credible in-P anchor: for the mirror, reverse the sequence and eliminate sincerely, then calculate the winner’s weighted Borda score. The running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(\tau\) is support size and \(L\) is the mass bit length. I consider this the strongest anchor; the paper offers no comparable named hardness result.

This covers Theorem 1 only. It does not claim a continuous version of the paper’s price-of-anarchy, sincerity-ratio, or experimental results. Those could motivate further questions: how do their worst-case welfare bounds change under arbitrary type masses, and do the discrete bounds survive in a weighted population?

The main weakness is substantial: the masses do not affect the SPNE winner. The strategic game is still a finite sequence, and its polynomial algorithm is the same reverse-order characterization as in the paper. The most plausible scenario is therefore a large electorate organized into preference-homogeneous blocs with representatives taking the turns. The mirror is a sensible weighted-population extension of the authors’ winner-computation problem, but it does not show that continuization removes a computational barrier.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about who acts. Theorem 1 characterizes a finite game whose input is a roster of voters and an \(m-1\)-slot sequence \(\pi\). A voter’s position in that sequence is part of what makes them strategically relevant. Replacing the roster with a distribution over rankings does not say who occupies those slots or how the sequence relates to the population.

The constituency version supplies an answer by giving each bloc a representative. That is coherent, but its strategic game is still played by finitely many delegates; the continuous society supplies their constituents’ welfare weights. If instead turns are selected from the population according to type masses, the natural question becomes the expected SPNE outcome under a random sequence. That may be worth studying, but it changes the fixed-sequence problem into a stochastic one. Weighted welfare ratios also face a limit problem: a type with vanishing mass can retain a pivotal representative, so the winner can persist while its per-capita Borda welfare approaches zero.

These points make the mirror less direct, but they do not defeat it. A large population divided into preference-homogeneous constituencies, each represented in the elimination protocol, is plausible. The resulting weighted-population winner problem is well posed, and Theorem 1 gives it a polynomial-time solution. The fact that masses may not affect the winner is not enough to reject it under the programme’s standard. I cannot honestly make a strong case that no worthwhile mirror exists; the negative case is weak at this anchor.
