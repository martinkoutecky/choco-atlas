Why no mirror

The proposed distributional Kemeny model is a sensible high-multiplicity reformulation over ranking types. However, Proposition 12 and Corollary 3 are axiomatic results, and the paper contains no numbered algorithmic, hardness, approximation, parameterized, or computational-formulation result to mirror. The eligibility condition therefore fails even though the continuous question itself is well posed.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the median/Kemeny aggregation rule and scoring-rule reinforcement over distributional societies, but leaves the paper's other rule definitions, inclusion relations, and axiomatic properties without computational mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Determine the complexity of computing a maximizing \(\rho\) from rational \(\mu\), parameterized by the number of alternatives \(m\) and the support size.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest answer is that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Its numbered results are structural or axiomatic: Proposition 1 classifies majority-preservation, Proposition 3 gives inclusion relations, and Propositions 4–19 concern unanimity, monotonicity, reinforcement, and homogeneity. None states an \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness result, a polynomial-time algorithm, a parameterized classification, an approximation guarantee, or an algorithmic LP/MILP formulation. Definition 3 gives a ranked-agenda procedure, but not a complexity theorem. The paper’s references to Kemeny, Young, or other known computational facts are background, not results proved here.

So, formally, there are zero eligible anchors and therefore no compliant positive case under the programme’s strict rule.

There is nevertheless a very natural substantive mirror. The closest named result is Proposition 12, proved in this paper: every scoring rule satisfies reinforcement. If two profiles \(P\) and \(Q\) over disjoint electorates have a common output, then the scoring rule applied to their concatenation returns exactly the intersection of the two separate output sets. Corollary 3 specializes this to the median rule \(\RMWA\) and the reversal-scoring rule \(\REVS\). This is not a computational anchor, but it identifies the cleanest rule to continuize.

My lead conditional question would be Continuous Kemeny Judgment Aggregation. Take a fixed preference agenda over alternatives \(C\), with transitivity as the integrity constraint. A type is a complete linear order \(\succ\) over \(C\), and the society is a finitely supported rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_\succ)\) over such orders. Here \(\mu_\succ\) is the fraction of the population having that ranking. Define the pairwise support \(w_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{\succ:x\succ y}\mu_\succ\).

The decision variable is a collective linear order \(\rho\). The problem is to compute every maximizer of

\[ K_\mu(\rho)=\sum_{x\succ_\rho y}w_\mu(x,y), \]

equivalently every order minimizing the expected Kendall distance

\[ \sum_{\succ}\mu_\succ d_K(\succ,\rho). \]

If the associated social choice function is wanted, the solution is the set of alternatives that are top-ranked in at least one maximizing order.

This is an author-recognizable mirror. The paper explicitly identifies the median rule with Kemeny aggregation on the preference agenda, and the continuous model changes only the population representation: many voters with the same complete ranking become one type with mass. A plausible regime is a large recurring consultation or institutional electorate with millions of participants but relatively few distinct ranking patterns. The candidates and collective order remain discrete; only the electorate is continuized.

I would expect this problem to be Class B rather than Class A. Given a finite profile with \(n_\succ\) voters of each ranking, set \(\mu_\succ=n_\succ/n\); the objective is merely scaled by \(1/n\), so the optimizing orders are identical. Conversely, rational masses can be denominator-cleared into a finite profile. Thus any hardness driven by selecting an order over \(C\) survives; it is not population-multiplicity hardness. The natural follow-ups are winner-membership, approximation, and parameterized complexity in \(m=|C|\), support size, and the number of ranking types.

The weakness is decisive: this is essentially a high-multiplicity reformulation of the paper’s Kemeny case, not a computational result contained in the paper. Proposition 12’s reinforcement statement follows from linearity of the score, and the continuous Kemeny objective likewise follows immediately from replacing counts by normalized counts. A referee could reasonably accept the model as a genuine, sensible continuous population question while rejecting it as a mirror of a named computational contribution. The correct overall verdict is therefore: natural continuous mirror, but no qualifying computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is decisive at the programme’s eligibility gate: this paper contains no qualifying computational result. Its numbered propositions classify axiomatic properties—majority preservation, inclusion, unanimity, monotonicity, reinforcement, and homogeneity. None gives a complexity theorem, algorithm, approximation guarantee, parameterized result, or computational formulation. Definition 3 supplies a rule procedure, but no running-time claim or computational theorem. The references to Kemeny, Young, and other voting rules are identifications or background, not results established by this paper.

Proposition 12 does not repair that omission. Its continuous analogue would be perfectly well-defined: replace a profile by a distribution \(\mu\) over rational judgment sets, define a scoring objective by expectation, and express profile concatenation as the mixture \(\lambda\mu+(1-\lambda)\nu\). Reinforcement then follows from linearity. But this is an axiomatic reformulation, not a computational question of the kind ChoCo is meant to chart. Calling it “Continuous Kemeny Judgment Aggregation” quietly changes the anchor from Proposition 12 to the paper’s identification of the median rule with Kemeny aggregation; neither is a numbered computational contribution here.

The proponent’s stronger modelling case should nevertheless be conceded. Judgment sets are complete agent types for this paper’s rules, identities do not matter, and a large population with masses \(\mu_J\) is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. One should not object that the continuum loses individual identity, nor that the resulting Kemeny problem is merely equivalent to the finite one: that equivalence is a legitimate Class B outcome, not a defect. Even allowing richer type descriptions does not change the paper’s status: either the added attributes are irrelevant to its rules, or one has defined a new problem.

Thus the strongest honest conclusion is narrower than the requested universal claim. It is not credible to say that no worthwhile continuous population model can be formed from this paper; the Kemeny distributional model is a sound benchmark. But it is equally clear that no qualifying continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper exists. Any genuinely computational version—winner computation, intervention, bribery, robustness, or high-multiplicity optimization—would be a new problem added by the ChoCo programme, not a mirror of one of the paper’s named contributions.
