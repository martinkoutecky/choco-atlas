The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) with rational masses on a line segment, compute the lottery \(F(\mu)=\tfrac12\delta_{\bar x}+\tfrac12\mu\), where \(\bar x=\int x\,d\mu(x)\), and establish its exact worst-case miniSOS ratio and its realization as the limit of anonymous, exactly strategyproof finite mechanisms.

The model it lives in

Types are ideal locations \(x_j\) with rational masses \(\mu_j\); the outcome is a lottery over facility locations, and the objective is expected population cost \(C(P,\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P}[\int (Y-x)^2\,d\mu(x)]\). The rule chooses the mean with probability \(1/2\) and samples a location from \(\mu\) with probability \(1/2\); its support and probabilities are computable from the \(\tau\) types.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 17's line mechanism and its \(1.5\)-approximation guarantee. It leaves aside Theorem 18's lower bound as a computational claim, the tree approximation results, and the randomized-mechanism characterization.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every admissible population-limit strategyproof rule have worst-case miniSOS ratio at least \(1.5\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is on the line, for the paper’s miniSOS objective. A plausible high multiplicity regime is a large institution choosing one shared location or temperature, where many people have the same ideal point and there are relatively few distinct preference types. A type is an ideal point \(x\); its mass \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of the population with that ideal point. For a lottery \(P\) over facility locations, the continuous social cost is
\[ C(P,\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P}\!\left[\int (Y-x)^2\,d\mu(x)\right]. \]
The optimal deterministic location is the mean \(\bar x=\int x\,d\mu(x)\), and the benchmark cost is the variance of \(\mu\).

Lead: the limit truthful miniSOS problem. Given finite-support distributions \(\mu\) with rational masses on a line segment, determine the best worst-case approximation ratio achievable by a rule \(F\) that maps \(\mu\) to a lottery over locations. To keep strategyproofness meaningful, require \(F\) to arise as the population limit of anonymous, exactly strategyproof randomized mechanisms \(F_n\) for \(n\) named agents: whenever empirical distributions converge to \(\mu\), the mechanisms’ outcome laws converge to \(F(\mu)\). Score the rule by its worst-case limiting expected miniSOS cost divided by the optimal cost, over nondegenerate populations.

This is the continuous mirror of Theorem 18 (proved here): *every randomized strategyproof mechanism on the line has approximation ratio at least \(1.5\) for miniSOS.* The theorem is a lower bound on what truthful mechanisms can achieve, not an NP-hardness result. In the limit problem, the question is whether that barrier persists for all admissible limit rules. The expected direction is hardness transfer in this approximation sense: every finite mechanism in an admissible family faces the paper’s lower bound. The continuous question should ask for the optimal ratio and an attaining rule, not claim a computational complexity classification the paper never gives.

Supporting upper-bound question. Given \(\mu\), return the lottery \(F(\mu)=\tfrac12\delta_{\bar x}+\tfrac12\mu\): choose the mean with probability \(1/2\), and otherwise sample a location from the population. Ask for its exact worst-case miniSOS ratio and whether it is realizable as a limit of truthful finite mechanisms. This mirrors Theorem 17 (proved here): on every finite location profile on the line, choosing the average with probability \(1/2\) and using random dictatorship with probability \(1/2\) gives a strategyproof \(1.5\)-approximation; the paper notes that this ratio is exact on every profile. In the continuous model, the mean costs \(\operatorname{Var}(\mu)\), while a sample from \(\mu\) costs \(2\operatorname{Var}(\mu)\) in expectation, so the mixture has ratio \(1.5\). Its finite version is exactly the paper’s mechanism. This upper-bound question has a closed-form solution and is tractable in the limited sense that the mean and lottery can be represented from the \(\tau\) support points.

Together, the questions ask whether the best truthful miniSOS ratio remains \(1.5\) as the population becomes a distribution. Theorem 17 supplies the matching rule; Theorem 18 supplies the barrier. The authors should recognize the model: it keeps their line facility-location problem, individual distance costs, miniSOS objective, and exact finite-population truthfulness, while replacing normalized counts with a population distribution. The mirror covers these line approximation results only; it does not attempt to continuize the paper’s tree mechanisms or randomized-mechanism characterization.

The weakest point is also central: direct strategyproofness for an individual of zero mass in a rule \(F(\mu)\) is vacuous, since their report does not change \(\mu\). Requiring exact truthful finite mechanisms before taking the limit preserves the paper’s strategic question, but makes this an asymptotic mechanism-design mirror rather than a direct continuum-agent incentive model. And although the population is genuinely continuous in the limit, the paper offers no named result about running time or computational hardness. So this is a credible, narrow positive case for a continuous population mirror—not a ChoCo-style complexity result. Further questions include whether another nonvacuous continuum incentive notion yields the same bound, and whether analogous limit results hold for trees.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper’s results are about incentive constraints, not computational complexity. Theorem 17’s “approximation” measures the social cost imposed by strategyproofness; Theorem 18 proves a lower bound over all strategyproof mechanisms. Neither asks how hard it is to compute an intervention, optimize over voter actions, or decide a property of an instance. The mechanisms are treated as mathematical functions, without a representation or runtime model. That distinction matters to ChoCo’s stated aim: continuizing a population does not, by itself, create a computational problem.

The proposed limit version of Theorem 18 is the strongest possible mirror, and it correctly avoids vacuous incentives by requiring limits of finite, exactly strategyproof mechanisms. But that defines an asymptotic mechanism-design question: which limits of those finite mechanisms are admissible, and what ratios can they attain? It preserves the paper’s incentive constraints; it does not add a complexity landscape. Direct strategyproofness for an individual of zero mass would be vacuous. Treating a positive-mass group’s deviation as the relevant test would change the paper’s unilateral incentive model. The finite-mechanism limit avoids both problems, but remains a question about mechanism existence and performance.

Theorem 17’s proposed mirror is even less computationally demanding: the rule takes a weighted mean and samples from the finite-support population. Those operations are straightforward in the support size \(\tau\). Theorem 9’s strategyproofness guarantee and the tree approximation results have the same basic character: they establish incentive and distortion properties, without studying computational cost. One could add a new question about efficiently finding an optimal strategyproof rule, but that would require introducing a computational model and optimization problem absent from the paper. It would be a new research direction, not a computational mirror of these results.

This is a fit objection, not a claim that the population model is unsensible. Large institutions with repeated ideal locations give a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the aggregate miniSOS objective retains the information the paper uses. The negative case is therefore weak against the broader claim that a continuous mechanism-design study could be worthwhile. It is stronger only against including this paper in ChoCo’s computational-complexity programme: none of the proposed anchors supplies the computational question that programme is designed to chart.
