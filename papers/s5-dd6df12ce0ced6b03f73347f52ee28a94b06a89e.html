The continuous mirror question

Given goods in classes \(S_1,S_2\), integer supply \(\omega\), bundle set \(\Omega=\{z\in\mathbb Z_+^n:z\le\omega\}\), and \(\tau\) explicitly listed integer-valued GM-concave valuation types \(u_t\) with rational masses \(\lambda_t>0\) summing to \(M\), does the type-level generalized double-track auction compute a Walrasian equilibrium from every integer starting price in finitely many rounds, and what round bound is achievable in \(n,\tau\), and the total input bit length? At price \(p\), type \(t\) reports \(D_t(p)=\arg\max_{z\in\Omega}\{u_t(z)-p^\top z\}\); use the weighted Lyapunov function \(\mathcal L_\lambda(p)=\sum_t\lambda_tV_t(p)+p^\top\omega\) and the corresponding weighted version of the paper’s update objective. The output is a price \(p\) and bundle-mass assignment \(q_{t,z}\ge0\) with \(\sum_zq_{t,z}=\lambda_t\), \(\sum_{t,z}q_{t,z}z=\omega\), and positive mass only on demanded bundles.

The model it lives in

A market with \(\tau\) complete valuation types carrying rational masses \(\lambda_t\), integral bundles for each unit of mass, and aggregate supply \(\omega\). Each type reports its demand set; the auctioneer updates prices using the weighted Lyapunov function \(\sum_t\lambda_tV_t(p)+p^\top\omega\). The goal is to compute equilibrium prices and a market-clearing assignment of type mass across demanded bundles.

The objection that survived

Theorem 5.2 proves finite convergence for a finite sum over bidders; it does not show that rational type weights preserve the descent argument or yield a bound polynomial in the support size and input bit length.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 5.2’s finite-convergence guarantee as a type-mass auction question. It leaves the characterizations in Theorems 3.3, 3.5, and 3.7, the equilibrium geometry in Theorems 4.5 and 4.6, and the extremal-price results in Theorems 5.3 and 5.4 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the type-weighted double-track process terminate from every integer starting price when type masses are rational?
  • Can its number of rounds be bounded polynomially in \(n\), \(\tau\), and the binary input length, including the mass denominators?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Theorem 5.2, proved in this paper. It says that when every bidder has an integer-valued GM-concave valuation, the generalized double-track auction, started from any integer price vector, reaches a Walrasian equilibrium price in finitely many rounds. The paper gives a convergence guarantee, not a polynomial-time bound or a standard complexity classification.

A natural regime is a large market with many repeated purchasing profiles: for example, a national procurement platform with many branches or firms that fall into a small number of exact valuation classes for standardized goods. A type is the full valuation function over feasible bundles, along with any other feature relevant to demand. If total population mass is \(M\), let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The mass of type \(t\) is \(M\mu_t\), and the total supply \(\omega\) scales with \(M\), keeping per-capita supply \(\omega/M\) meaningful. The regime of interest is \(M\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of positive-mass types. Each individual still receives an integral bundle; only the mass of a type assigned to each bundle can be fractional.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is Continuous GGSC Walrasian Equilibrium Search. An instance gives \(n\) goods in classes \(S_1,S_2\), integer supply \(\omega\), a rational total population mass \(M\ge 2\), and \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(M\mu_t\). Each type \(t\) has an explicitly listed, integer-valued GM-concave valuation \(u_t\) on the paper’s bundle set \(\Omega=\{z\in\mathbb Z_+^n:z\le\omega\}\). The task is to find a price vector \(p\in\mathbb Q^n\) and nonnegative masses \(q_{t,z}\) such that

\[ \sum_{z\in\Omega}q_{t,z}=M\mu_t \quad(\forall t),\qquad \sum_{t,z}q_{t,z}z=\omega, \]

and \(q_{t,z}>0\) only if \(z\in\arg\max_{y\in\Omega}\{u_t(y)-p^\top y\}\). Thus every member of each type receives an individually optimal integral bundle, while an atomless population lets the type’s mass divide across tied bundles to clear the market.

This is my lead mirror. I expect it to be tractable in the explicit-input model. A welfare-maximizing linear program over the type–bundle pairs has a dual price vector; complementary slackness makes every bundle used by positive mass a demand-maximizing bundle at those prices. The running time is polynomial in the explicit valuation-table size, \(\tau\), and the input bit lengths. That is a complexity result for the stated continuous problem; it is not a polynomial-time guarantee for the paper’s double-track auction. If valuations are given succinctly rather than by their tables, the pricing problem returns and needs separate analysis.

The mirror is plausible because it preserves the paper’s underlying equilibrium question: agents demand integral bundles of potentially complementary goods at anonymous linear prices, and the market must clear. What changes is the population: rather than a finite list of bidders, each exact bidder type has a mass. That is exactly the kind of high-multiplicity regime where a distribution over types is a sensible representation.

The weak point is that atomlessness convexifies aggregate allocation. It makes equilibrium computable by an LP even without the GGSC condition, so the mirror does not preserve the paper’s distinctive role for GGSC or its particular price-adjustment process. Theorem 5.2 itself supplies only finite convergence, not a complexity bound. I still consider the equilibrium-search question a credible mirror: it asks the same economic question with the population made continuous, while retaining individual indivisibility. The paper’s finite-round auction is then a further, sharper question: does a type-level double-track process retain a useful convergence or complexity guarantee when demand reports are aggregated by mass?

This case covers Theorem 5.2’s equilibrium-computation result. The mirror points toward Class A; the further question is whether its type-level price dynamics admit a round bound in \(n,\tau\), and the input bit length, without depending on the total population mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that the proposed LP does not mirror Theorem 5.2’s computation. The LP uses full valuation tables and divides each type’s mass among tied bundles. It is a centralized welfare calculation whose dual gives equilibrium prices; it works even without GGSC. The theorem, by contrast, is about a finite-round price process driven by bidders’ demand reports. So the LP’s polynomial solvability does not establish that the paper’s auction has a meaningful continuous counterpart.

A stronger mirror would keep the price process and replace the bidder sum in its Lyapunov function by a type-mass sum, \(\mathcal L_\mu(p)=\sum_t M\mu_t V_t(p)+p^\top\omega\). That is a coherent question for markets with many buyers of a few exact valuation types. Rational masses can be scaled to integer coefficients, and the paper’s generalized convexity structure appears to carry over. The likely result may be a close extension of the existing finite-convergence proof; the paper itself supplies no round-complexity bound that would give the mirror a sharper target.

That makes the LP a poor anchor and leaves the proposed dynamics open to a novelty challenge. But it does not defeat the repaired mirror: a round bound in \(n,\tau\), and the input bit length would be a well-posed computational question, and identical valuation types are sensible in some markets. I can make the negative case against the proponent’s formulation; I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
