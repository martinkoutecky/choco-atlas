Why no mirror

Theorem 4 supplies a genuine computational anchor, and the replicated-market configuration formulation is a meaningful continuous-population question. However, the nonatomic limit makes individual bidders unable to affect prices, changing the equilibrium concept and requiring new supply, coupling, and type-splitting semantics. The cases do not settle whether this is an authors-recognisable analogue or a different competitive-equilibrium problem.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorem 4's polynomial-time construction of a \(2\)-approximate pure equilibrium, but leaves the Bayesian price-of-anarchy result, the general fractionally-subadditive Potential Procedure, and its exponential convergence bound untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a standard \(N\)-bidder, \(Nq_j\)-copy limit of the finite second-price games converge to the stated nonatomic equilibrium?
  • Can the \(2\)-approximate equilibrium or allocation be computed in polynomial time for the chosen representation of submodular valuations?
  • Does type splitting preserve implementability by item-level second-price bids and guarantee pure equilibrium existence?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a replicated-market mirror of Theorem 4, which I would make the lead anchor.

The paper’s Theorem 4, proved in the paper, states:

If the valuation functions are submodular, then a pure Nash equilibrium of the complete-information game—where items are sold in independent second-price auctions—can be computed in polynomial time, and the resulting equilibrium achieves a \(2\)-approximation to optimal social welfare.

The theorem is implemented by the Marginal-Value Procedure: process the \(m\) items in order, give each item to the bidder with the greatest current marginal value, and use that marginal value as the winner’s bid.

The continuous mirror I would propose is Continuous Submodular Second-Price Equilibrium.

An instance consists of:

  • item classes \(M=\{1,\ldots,m\}\);
  • a finite set \(T\) of bidder types;
  • for each \(t\in T\), a normalized monotone submodular valuation \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\);
  • a rational population distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the mass of type \(t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\);
  • a per-capita supply \(q_j\) of each item class \(j\).

The supply scaling is essential. A finite set of \(m\) indivisible items cannot support a meaningful continuum of bidders: almost all mass would receive nothing. The intended high-multiplicity regime is a replicated market with \(N\mu_t\) bidders of type \(t\) and \(Nq_j\) copies of item class \(j\), followed by normalization as \(N\) grows. Each infinitesimal bidder receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\), although the aggregate allocation is fractional.

An allocation is described by \(x_{t,S}\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\), subject to

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \]

and

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le q_j \qquad\text{for every }j\in M. \]

Its welfare is

\[ W_\mu(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}v_t(S), \]

and \(\operatorname{OPT}_\infty(\mu,q)\) is the maximum of this expression over feasible allocations.

Each type chooses a common bid vector \(b_t\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^m\). Every item class is sold by a continuum version of the second-price rule: the highest-bidding mass receives up to \(q_j\), ties are split measurably, and winners pay the cutoff bid \(p_j\). The allocation \(x\), including the coupling of item-level winners into bundles, is part of the auction outcome. A type-symmetric pure nonatomic equilibrium is a bid profile \(b=(b_t)_{t\in T}\) for which no individual of any positive-mass type can change its bid vector and increase its utility

\[ v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j. \]

The continuous problem is:

Given \((M,T,(v_t),\mu,q)\), compute a type-dependent bid profile, induced allocation, and prices forming a pure nonatomic second-price equilibrium such that
\[ > W_\mu(x)\ge \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{OPT}_\infty(\mu,q). > \]

This is recognisably the authors’ problem: the agents still bid separately on items, valuations still express preferences over bundles, the mechanism is still a product of second-price auctions, and the objective is still social welfare. The only substantive co-modification is the per-capita supply needed to make a large population meaningful. The scenario could be a large procurement or retail market with many buyers, a small number of recurring preference segments, and many interchangeable copies of each product or service class. Here \(N\gg m,\tau\), while agents of the same type share the same valuation function.

I would expect this mirror to be a Class A candidate for the requested \(2\)-approximation. The finite proof’s central idea is marginal submodularity, not the identities of the \(n\) bidders. In the continuum, the marginal procedure should become a mass-allocation or configuration-LP procedure, with \(x_{t,S}\) as the columns and type-level marginal pricing as the structure behind separation.

That expectation needs to be tested carefully. Aggregating identical bidders may introduce exponentially many partial-bundle states, and a configuration formulation may require pricing queries of the form

\[ \max_{S\subseteq M}\left(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\right). \]

If those queries are hard in the chosen valuation representation, the continuous problem may be hard even though the finite-\(n\) Marginal-Value Procedure is polynomial. That would not invalidate the mirror; it would identify the relevant boundary between population multiplicity and bundle combinatorics. Further questions include whether the finite and continuous equilibria admit a rounding dictionary, whether exact pure equilibria always exist under continuum tie-splitting, and whether the running time can depend on \((m,\tau)\) rather than on the population denominator.

I would not use the paper’s cited NP-hardness of submodular welfare as an anchor: it is attributed to reference [13], not stated as a numbered result of this paper. Nor would I promote Theorem 3—the \(\Omega(2^m)\) lower bound for the Potential Procedure with two bidders—to hardness of the equilibrium problem, since it is only a lower bound for one procedure. Thus Theorem 4 is the cleanest and strongest computational anchor available.

The weakest point is that the continuous version is not literally the paper’s one-copy, \(m\)-item game: it is its high-multiplicity replicated-market limit, with item classes and a nonatomic equilibrium notion. I think that modification is forced by the population continuization rather than an evasion of the problem, but an author could reasonably argue that the equilibrium concept has changed too much. The underlying bundle valuations, bidding actions, second-price competition, and welfare objective nevertheless remain intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is against the proponent’s *particular formulation*, not against every possible mirror.

Theorem 4 is a genuine computational anchor. Its high-multiplicity regime is also entirely natural: many bidders may share one of finitely many valuation profiles, and welfare depends only on the resulting allocation and values, not on bidder identities. Thus neither identity dependence nor lack of multiplicity defeats it.

There is, however, a serious modelling discontinuity. The paper sells one copy of each of \(m\) named items. Keeping those items while taking the bidder population to a continuum makes almost every bidder receive nothing. Replicating the items repairs this, but changes the mechanism into a multi-unit market. One must then decide whether bidders may receive several copies of a class, how their valuations extend to multisets, and how item-level winners are coupled into bundles. These are not forced by the paper.

More seriously, the proposed “nonatomic second-price Nash equilibrium” is not the limit of the paper’s strategic equilibrium in any straightforward sense. An individual of measure zero cannot affect a cutoff price or the allocation of positive mass. The equilibrium condition consequently becomes price-taking demand optimality, essentially a competitive or Walrasian equilibrium. The natural formulation is the configuration LP

\[ \max \sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S) \]

subject to

\[ \sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j, \]

with prices \(p_j\) satisfying

\[ x_{t,S}>0 \;\Longrightarrow\; S\in\arg\max_R\left(v_t(R)-\sum_{j\in R}p_j\right). \]

That is a meaningful continuous market problem, but it is no longer the paper’s finite second-price game: the strategic content has collapsed into dual prices and demand queries. Requiring type-symmetric bids makes matters worse, since a single valuation type may need to split across several bundles and therefore across several supporting bid vectors. Allowing such splitting repairs the model, but moves still further toward a configuration-market formulation.

This is the best available negative argument: the literal one-copy model degenerates, while the natural nondegenerate repair changes the equilibrium object and requires new supply and valuation semantics. It does not establish the requested universal conclusion, however. The repaired configuration-market version is precisely a sensible high-multiplicity problem, and its pricing question is computationally substantive. Since the programme explicitly permits per-capita supplies and type-level fractional allocations, Theorem 4’s anchor survives the negative case.
