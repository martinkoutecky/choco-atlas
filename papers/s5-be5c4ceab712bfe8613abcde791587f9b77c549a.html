The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), and a complete weak preference order over types for each \(i\in T\), does there exist a stable pairing of the atomless population? Represent a pairing by type-mass variables \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\mu_i\) and \(x_{ij}=x_{ji}\) for \(i\ne j\); for \(q_i(j)=\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\), require \(q_i(i)=0\) and, for every distinct \(i,j\), \(q_i(j)=0\) or \(q_j(i)=0\).

The model it lives in

An atomless society of preference types with rational masses; \(x_{ij}\) records the mass of type \(i\) paired with type \(j\), and the question is feasibility under mass-balance and no-positive-mass-blocking-pair constraints.

The objection that survived

The opponent’s limit argument appears to imply universal feasibility for rational cohort profiles, so the decision version has no nontrivial complexity landscape; this limits the mirror’s algorithmic interest, though it does not invalidate the question.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3.11, proved in this paper. It says that, when a preference system contains ties, deciding whether it admits a \((r_1,r_2,r_3)\)-bundle is NP-complete; minimizing \(r_3\) is NP-hard, with no multiplicative approximation unless P=NP; and the bundle found by Algorithm 1 after any tie-break gives an additive \(\lfloor n/3\rfloor\) approximation for the minimum \(r_3\). The hardness proof reduces from weakly stable roommates, whose NP-completeness is cited from Ronn [37]. The cleanest mirror is the theorem’s \((0,n/2,0)\) case: as the paper observes in its proof, such a bundle is exactly a weakly stable matching covering every agent.

I would call the mirror Fractional Stable Pairing over Cohorts. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of agent types, a complete weak preference order \(\succeq_i\) over types for each \(i\in T\), and positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to one. There is an atomless population of type \(i\) with mass \(\mu_i\). All members of a type have the same preferences over types, and members of a type are tied with one another. The question is whether the whole population can be paired so that no two agents both strictly prefer each other to their current partners.

A solution can be represented by \(x_{ij}\), the mass of type-\(i\) agents paired with type-\(j\) agents. Require \(x_{ij}\geq 0\), \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\mu_i\), and \(x_{ij}=x_{ji}\) for \(i\ne j\). A type’s mass may be divided across partner types, but each infinitesimal agent has one partner: the mass matrix is realized by pairing corresponding atomless subpopulations. For \(j\in T\), let \(q_i(j)=\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\), the mass of type \(i\) paired with someone strictly worse than type \(j\). Stability requires, for every distinct \(i,j\), that \(q_i(j)=0\) or \(q_j(i)=0\); also \(q_i(i)=0\), so two members of one type cannot both prefer each other to their partners. These conditions say exactly that no positive-mass groups on both sides can form a blocking pair.

The intended regime is a large event or professional network with many participants but few preference cohorts: for example, \(n\) attendees whose partner rankings are determined by a small set of fields, career stages, and languages, with ties within each cohort. Thus \(n\gg\tau\), where \(\tau=|T|\). This is a specific block-preference regime, not a claim that arbitrary individualized rankings are interchangeable. It is plausible for the paper’s problem because weak preferences and ties are already allowed, and the all-\(P_2\) bundle is its own stable-pairing special case.

I expect this mirror to be tractable in the bounded-\(\tau\) regime. For construction, enumerate for each type pair which side has zero mass assigned below the other; each choice gives a linear feasibility problem in the \(x_{ij}\). This yields an \(2^{\binom{\tau}{2}}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L)\) algorithm, hence polynomial time for fixed \(\tau\). There is also a structural reason to expect feasibility: replicate rational type masses into finite cohorts, apply the stable-partition construction used in the paper, and split each odd cycle’s mass across its adjacent pairs. The at-most-one singleton contributes vanishing mass as the replication grows; a limit gives a full stable mass pairing. This is a Class A case for bounded support, with polynomial dependence on variable \(\tau\) left as a further question. Clearing denominators in a rational solution gives a finite block-type instance with a stable matching, so the bridge also runs back up to suitably scaled discrete populations.

This is a close mirror of the theorem’s all-pairs subproblem: the stability condition is unchanged, and each unit of population still receives an integral pair. What becomes fractional is how much mass of a type is paired with each other type. The expected difference from the discrete NP-hard result has a clear source: an atomless cohort can split across the pair patterns that an individual, indivisible matching cannot use fractionally.

The scope is deliberately narrow. It mirrors the all-\(P_2\) decision slice of Theorem 3.11, not the theorem’s minimum-\(r_3\) objective, the paper’s graph-placement hardness in Theorem 1.1, or its seating and team-formation algorithms.

The weakest point is that Theorem 3.11’s hardness reduction may use a number of identity-specific preference distinctions that grows with \(n\). The paper does not establish hardness—or tractability—for its problem under a bounded number of preference types. The cohort regime therefore motivates a genuine mirror, but the discrete hardness itself is not evidence that the variable-\(\tau\) continuous problem is hard. The useful next question is whether the pair-orientation choices can be handled in time polynomial in \(\tau\), or whether growing type diversity creates continuum-specific hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.11 is a real computational anchor, and cohort preferences are a legitimate population regime. The strongest case against its mirror is that atomless mass removes the combinatorial obstruction the theorem measures.

Take the proposed finite set of types and rational masses, replicate each type into many named copies, and break ties to get strict preferences. The paper’s stable-partition result gives a stable partition of those copies. Keep each even cycle as pairs; on each odd cycle, pair half the mass along each cycle edge. Every agent’s partner mass is then split between its predecessor and successor. A positive-mass blocking pair would mean that both types receive positive mass with partners below the other type in their rankings. The corresponding copies would prefer each other to their predecessors, contradicting the stable-partition condition. At most one singleton remains in the reduced partition, so its mass vanishes as the replication grows. With finitely many types, a convergent subsequence yields a full stable mass pairing.

This fractionalization is realizable in an atomless cohort: each infinitesimal agent still has one partner; the cohort is divided among partner types. Thus the natural continuous version of the all-\(P_2\) question is feasible for every rational type profile. The natural mass version of minimizing \(r_3\) likewise has optimum zero: odd cycles can be represented by fractional pairs, with no positive mass left in \(P_3\) components. The issue is not simply that the continuous answer is easy. The discrete objective—whether whole, named agents can be partitioned into stable pairs, or how many whole \(P_3\) components remain—has disappeared under the mass formulation. Preserving that granularity would require keeping the integral population constraint that continuization was meant to relax.

The proposed orientation enumeration may be a valid algorithm for its stated model, but it gives no meaningful complexity landscape once feasibility is guaranteed by this structural argument. Clearing denominators may also show that some replicated cohort instances have finite stable matchings. That is a potentially useful high-multiplicity consequence, but it is a replication statement, not a resolution of the original instance’s hardness.

This case against the anchor is not airtight. The paper has no continuous treatment already answering the question, and type-level preferences could be plausible in large matching markets. Added costs or constraints might preserve a nontrivial optimization problem, though they would need independent motivation from the paper. I would reject this particular all-\(P_2\)/minimum-\(r_3\) mirror as a computational target, while conceding that the argument does not rule out every worthwhile continuous agent-arrangement problem inspired by the paper.
