The continuous mirror question

Given a prior log odds λ_π, a population of size \(N\), and a finite set of forecaster types Τ with rational masses μ_r satisfying \(Nμ_r ∈ ℤ\), type \(r\) has residual-signal precision \(q_r\) and observed mean residual log-likelihood ψ̄_r. If each member’s residual signal is conditionally independent with law \(ψ_{r,i}∣Y ∼ ℕ(±κ q_r,2κ q_r)\), compute the aggregate posterior log odds from the observed type means and its ex-ante conditional distribution: γ=λ_π+N∑_rμ_rψ̄_r\), and \(γ∣Y ∼ ℕ(λ_π±Nκ∑_rμ_rq_r,2Nκ∑_rμ_rq_r)\).

The model it lives in

Types encode ordered wave, overlap or rationale profile, and residual precision \(q_r\); mass μ_r represents each type’s share of the forecasting population, and ψ̄_r is its aggregate observed residual evidence. The task is to compute the weighted posterior and its conditional law; it requires no optimization.

The case FOR (proponent)

The only defensible positive anchor is a narrow one: Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that when experts provide rationales, their residual log-likelihood signals \(\psi_1,\ldots,\psi_t\) are conditionally independent, with means \(\pm \tau_{\mathrm{sig}}f_{\alpha,j}\) and variances \(2\tau_{\mathrm{sig}}f_{\alpha,j}\). The posterior log odds are
\[ \gamma_t=\lambda_\pi+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\psi_j, \]
and, conditional on \(Y\), have distribution \(\mathcal N(\lambda_\pi\pm\tau_{\mathrm{sig}}F_{\alpha,t},\,2\tau_{\mathrm{sig}}F_{\alpha,t})\), where
\[ F_{\alpha,t}=1+(1-\rho)\frac{1-(1-\rho)^{\alpha(t-1)}}{1-(1-\rho)^\alpha}. \]
The theorem does not state a complexity classification. Its explicit formula does, however, give a linear-time aggregation algorithm. The paper’s Theorem 8 establishes a truthful, effortful PBE, but it is an equilibrium result rather than a result about the complexity of computing equilibria. I would not present either theorem as a hardness anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large forecasting service that runs a finite number of ordered review waves. Millions of forecasters may fall into a modest number of types, distinguished by their wave, evidence-source profile, residual-signal precision, and rationale behavior. Mass is the fraction of forecasters in each type. Members can have different signal realizations: for aggregation, the instance supplies each type’s mean residual log-likelihood, which is sufficient because the posterior uses the sum of those contributions. This keeps the outcome binary and makes the population, rather than the outcome, continuous.

Call the mirror Residual Evidence Aggregation\(_\infty\). Its instance consists of a population size \(N\), a prior log odds \(\lambda_\pi\), a finite type set \(\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_r\) summing to one, and, for each type \(r\), a precision \(q_r\) and observed mean residual log-likelihood \(\bar\psi_r\). Here \(N\mu_r\) is the number of experts of type \(r\), and each expert’s residual signal has conditional law \(\mathcal N(\pm\kappa q_r,2\kappa q_r)\), independently given \(Y\). Types include the overlap and rationale profile needed to identify residual evidence; the \(q_r\) values generalize the paper’s position-dependent \(f_{\alpha,t}\) across finite arrival waves. The task is to compute the posterior log odds and its ex-ante conditional distribution:
\[ \gamma=\lambda_\pi+N\sum_{r\in\mathcal T}\mu_r\bar\psi_r, \qquad \gamma\mid Y\sim\mathcal N\!\left( \lambda_\pi\pm N\kappa\sum_r\mu_rq_r,\; 2N\kappa\sum_r\mu_rq_r \right). \]
A solution is those exact outputs. The expected direction is tractable: the type-level sufficient statistics give an \(O(|\mathcal T|)\) computation. This mirrors Theorem 2’s aggregation problem, not the paper’s entire mechanism.

The strongest point is the fit between rationales and the mirror: they expose residual evidence so shared information can be discounted, and the posterior is then computed from type-level mass and averages. The weakest point is that the paper’s residual precisions depend on experts’ sequential positions; grouping experts into finite waves changes that part of the model. Also, this mirror is a straightforward continuation of a formula the paper already gives. A continuous incentive-design version of Theorem 8 would be more central, but it would need a careful per-mass incentive model: an infinitesimal expert’s contribution to a market score may vanish. That is a worthwhile follow-up question, rather than an anchor I can honestly claim this paper has already established.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper states no computational-complexity result. Theorem 2 gives a Bayesian aggregation identity: once the residual log-likelihoods \(\psi_j\) are known, the posterior is \(\gamma_t=\lambda_\pi+\sum_{j\le t}\psi_j\). The proposed mirror takes those already-computed residuals, replaces repeated inputs by type averages, and sums them with multiplicities. That is a valid calculation, but it is not a complexity problem the theorem poses; the masses re-encode sufficient statistics in an explicit formula. I’m not objecting that the answer is easy or that continuity fails to help. The gap is that the paper’s result is an inference formula, not a computational task whose complexity it classifies.

There is also a fidelity problem with the proposed inputs. In the paper, \(\psi_t\) is the “new” evidence at arrival \(t\), defined relative to the overlaps revealed by earlier experts; its distribution depends on position through \(f_{\alpha,t}\). Supplying each type’s mean residual signal assumes that this sequential overlap analysis has already been done. It can recover an endpoint posterior, but it does not preserve the paper’s sequence of intermediate beliefs or the individual incentives in the mechanism. Grouping experts into waves could make a coherent model, but simultaneous cohorts or wave-level reporting change the sequential setup that gives rationales their role.

A better extension could retain source profiles and costs, then ask how to allocate effort or incentives across a large forecasting crowd. That may be a worthwhile project. But if the profiles and overlaps are restricted enough for mass aggregation to stay compact, posterior computation reduces to Gaussian sufficient-statistic arithmetic. If the model retains the detailed overlap relations and arrival order that make rationales informative, those relations must be represented in the types, and the support may grow with the number of experts. Optimizing incentives would add a new objective and equilibrium model; Theorem 8 supplies no complexity question for it, and an atomless version would need a carefully specified per-person payoff.

So the proponent has a plausible large-crowd application and a mathematically well-posed aggregation exercise. That is the strongest counterexample to a categorical “no scenario” claim, and I can’t rule out a worthwhile new forecasting-computation project. But I don’t think it establishes a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper’s computational results: the proposed anchor is too thin, and making it richer requires adding the computational problem the paper never studied.
