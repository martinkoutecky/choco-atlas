What the mirror covers

Theorem 4.1 is the paper’s algorithmic guarantee; Theorem 3.1 is a mechanism-performance impossibility, and the other results provide mechanism guarantees rather than computational complexity results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I find no named computational complexity result in this paper that can serve as an anchor. Its theorems give welfare, fairness, and incentive guarantees for mechanisms; they do not classify the complexity of a computational problem as in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The ranking algorithm is easy to execute, but its theorem does not make a complexity claim of that kind.

The closest results are Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper: for \(n=2\), for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there is a valid distribution \(D\) under which no DSIC mechanism achieves a welfare approximation greater than \(\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}+\varepsilon\). And Theorem 4.1, also proved here: for every valid \(D\), the ranking algorithm is BIC, envy-free with high probability, and achieves a \(1-o(1)\) welfare approximation. These are meaningful mechanism-design results, but neither asserts a computational complexity classification.

The continuous-population case is also a poor fit for those results as written. Their stochastic model is a distribution over agents’ valuations, while the main asymptotic parameter is the number of items \(m\); it does not make the population continuous. With continuous i.i.d. values, complete valuation vectors will typically be distinct, so they do not naturally form a small set of repeated types. A finite-type, large-population fair-division model could be proposed, but it would change the regime and would not be anchored by a named computational result here. Under the requested standard, there are zero anchors and therefore no valid per-anchor continuous problems to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper gives no named computational-complexity result to mirror. Theorem 3.1 bounds the welfare achievable by DSIC mechanisms; Theorem 4.1 gives welfare, fairness, and incentive guarantees for a mechanism. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem by complexity or studies an algorithmic bottleneck. The ranking algorithm itself sorts each agent’s bids and assigns items, so its computation is straightforward. The paper’s randomness is over valuations while the society remains finite; it does not make the population continuous.

Theorem 3.1 is the strongest candidate to rescue. A continuous version might ask for the best DSIC welfare guarantee when valuation types have specified masses. But the theorem concerns exactly two strategic agents. Replacing each with a positive-mass cohort changes the strategic model: treating a cohort as one strategic player makes it a coalition, while treating its members as agents gives a many-agent mechanism problem the theorem does not address. Letting the number of agents grow changes the problem too. One could formulate a new mechanism-synthesis problem over type masses, but the paper supplies neither that computational question nor a representation of mechanisms and deviations. With unrestricted reports, DSIC constraints cover bids beyond the realized type support; restricting reports to a finite grid would define a different problem.

Theorem 4.1 fares no better as an anchor. Its guarantee comes from letting the number of items grow and using concentration across item values for each agent. Under continuous valuations, agents’ complete valuation vectors are almost surely distinct, so their support grows with the population rather than giving a compact high-multiplicity description. Replacing each realized vector with a distribution over values would discard private information relevant to incentives. Restricting to repeated finite valuation vectors could define a sensible new regime, but it would not make this theorem a computational result about that regime.

A separately motivated project on computation in continuum mechanism design could be worthwhile. That possibility is the honest weakness in a universal negative: the paper does not rule out such a project. But it supplies no computational anchor for one, and neither named candidate survives as a continuous computational mirror of its result.
