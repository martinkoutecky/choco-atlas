The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror I can defend is the ARD apportionment problem on a continuous electorate. Let the types be the \(n\) party choices, and let \(\mu_i\) be the fraction of voters supporting party \(i\), with rational \(\mu_i\ge 0\) and \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). Given an integer seat total \(M\), choose \(x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^n\) with \(\sum_i x_i=M\) to minimize
\[ 100\sum_{i=1}^n\left|\frac{x_i}{M}-\mu_i\right|. \]
A plausible regime is a large electorate with millions of voters and relatively few party-choice types. Each type groups voters who are indistinguishable for this problem; its mass is their share of the electorate. The decision remains an integer apportionment, so the mirror preserves the paper’s central choice rather than making seat outcomes divisible. It is also recognizable from the paper’s own use of vote shares \(v_i=V_i/V\). Every discrete vote profile gives such a \(\mu\), and every rational \(\mu\) can be represented by a finite profile.

There is a serious limit to this case: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result that qualifies as an anchor under your criteria. Its numbered Statements concern quota compliance, paradoxes, and other method properties; its simulations compare those properties. The integer-programming formulation in equations (1)–(2) is not a named complexity result either. The cited Balinski–Young impossibility theorem is background, not a numbered computational result of this paper. So I cannot honestly present the mirror above as anchored to a theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a problem is hard, tractable, or parameterized in some way.

The candidate problem is likely tractable: with the ARD objective, Hamilton’s largest-remainder procedure supplies an allocation by rounding quotas and sorting remainders. But that is an assessment of the mirror problem, not an eligible anchor from this paper. Its weakest point is therefore substantial: although a continuous-population formulation is plausible and precise, it does not connect to a named computational result in the paper, and it appears to preserve an already straightforward apportionment computation rather than expose a new complexity question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed mirror has a sensible population story: many voters can be grouped by party choice, and their vote shares are natural masses. I would not reject it on high-multiplicity grounds. The problem is that it does not anchor to a computational result in this paper. The paper’s numbered Statements concern apportionment properties; its main quantitative findings come from simulations. Equations (1)–(2) formulate an integer optimization problem, but the paper gives no named complexity result about solving it. The proponent acknowledges this, and it is decisive for the proposed anchor.

The strongest version of their mirror keeps the \(M\) seats integral and gives party-choice types rational masses \(\mu_i\). But its objective is exactly the paper’s ARD objective after normalizing vote counts: \(V_i/V=\mu_i\), so \(I=100\sum_i |x_i/M-\mu_i|\). The paper already expresses its inputs as vote percentages. Replacing vote counts with rational shares gives a clean continuous-population formulation, but it does not turn any of the paper’s findings into a computational question about complexity or algorithms.

The other plausible routes do not supply a better anchor. Turning the quota, favoritism, or paradox findings into claims over real-valued shares would continuize properties of apportionment rules, not mirror computational results. Recasting the simulations as probabilities over continuous vote-share profiles would make uncertainty over profiles the focus. One could formulate new algorithmic questions around apportionment, but those would be new questions prompted by the topic, not continuous mirrors of results this paper establishes.

So the negative case is strong against every anchor offered: the ARD formulation is recognizable and well-posed, but no named computational result in the paper supports it. That does not show that apportionment has no worthwhile continuous computational questions. It does mean this paper supplies no paper-anchored computational mirror for ChoCo.
