The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive premise is a large hiring market: worker types share complete rankings over employer types and unemployment; firm types share responsive preferences over worker types and quotas. The masses could be rational fractions of a regional or annual market, and an allocation could be represented by a distribution over feasible firm bundles. That makes the population a continuous object, while allowing the capacity scaling the model requires.

But this paper has no named computational result to anchor that mirror to. Theorem 1, proved here, says \(S\subseteq C\) in many-to-one markets with indifferences. Theorem 2, proved here, says \(C^S=SS\); Theorem 3, proved here, says \(C^{SS}=SSS\). Propositions 2 and 3 characterize the super core and the one-to-one core through stable matchings of strict tie-breakings. These are structural relationships, not claims about complexity, algorithms, or a computed formulation. The algorithmic results mentioned in the introduction are prior work cited by the authors, not results established in this paper.

A follow-up could ask whether a finite-type, rational-mass hiring market admits a stable, strongly stable, or super-stable allocation. But that would be a new computational question, not a continuous mirror of a qualifying result here, so I can’t honestly name an anchor or classify its expected complexity under the requested standard. The main weakness is that blocking and coalitional domination need careful definitions for an atomless population; the hiring scenario is plausible, but the paper’s results do not establish that those continuum concepts preserve its relationships.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has not supplied a qualifying anchor, and that is the strongest case against a mirror of this paper. Theorem 1 proves \(S\subseteq C\); Theorems 2 and 3 prove \(C^S=SS\) and \(C^{SS}=SSS\). Propositions 2 and 3 relate solution sets to stable matchings under strict tie-breakings. These are structural statements, not computational results. The algorithms mentioned in the introduction are prior work, not results of this paper. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, there is therefore no named computational result here to continuize.

A stronger proposal would use a plausible high-multiplicity hiring market and ask for algorithms to find or test stable, strongly stable, or super-stable allocations when worker and firm types have rational masses. That may be a worthwhile computational project. But its results would answer new existence or algorithmic questions; they would not mirror the paper’s inclusions and equalities. Recasting those relationships for type-level mass allocations would remain a structural question, outside the programme’s computational focus. The fact that continuous blocking and coalitional domination need careful definitions is a modelling task, not by itself a reason to reject the mirror.

So the negative case is strong against this article as an anchor, but weak as the universal claim that no continuous matching question could be worthwhile. A continuous hiring model is sensible, and the paper’s subject could inspire useful computation. The defensible conclusion is narrower: this paper offers no qualifying computational result for ChoCo to mirror.
