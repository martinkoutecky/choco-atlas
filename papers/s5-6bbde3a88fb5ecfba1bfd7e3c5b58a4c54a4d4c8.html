The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is an extension of the paper’s robustness question to a high-multiplicity population. But under your anchor rule, this paper has no eligible named computational result: its named theorems concern allocation guarantees, not whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

The closest anchors are Theorem 2 and Corollary 2, both proved in this paper. Theorem 2 says that if \(g(y)=yf'(y)\) is non-increasing and \(f\) is \(\delta\)-scaled, then the allocation rule satisfies \(q\)-NNE for \(q=\delta/(\delta+3)\). Corollary 2 specializes this to Nash Welfare: when one agent adds a general diversity constraint, every other agent retains at least one quarter of their prior value. These are substantial results, but neither asserts a computational complexity bound, so I cannot honestly use them as formal anchors.

A plausible population regime is a large allocation platform serving many clients with the same per-item values and the same diversity requirements—for example, standardized clients requesting resources from several data-center regions. The clients form a small number \(\tau\) of complete types, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population in type \(t\). This makes the population continuous while keeping the paper’s divisible items as outcomes; those fractional outcomes alone are not the continuization.

As a concrete candidate, call the problem Continuous-Mass Diversity Robustness. An instance gives \(m\) item categories, finite types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-type values \(v_{tj}\), and convex diversity sets \(P_t\) containing the zero allocation. Each type receives a per-person allocation \(z_t\), subject to \(\sum_t \mu_t z_{tj}\le 1\) for every item \(j\). A specified positive-mass cohort \(S\) is initially unconstrained and then adopts its type’s diversity set. Compute the Nash Welfare allocation before and after that change, maximizing \(\sum_t \mu_t\log(\sum_j v_{tj}z_{tj})\) in each case, and report
\[ \rho=\min_{t\notin S} \frac{\sum_j v_{tj}y_{tj}}{\sum_j v_{tj}x_{tj}}, \]
where \(x\) and \(y\) are the before and after allocations. Given \(q\), the decision question is whether \(\rho\ge q\). Under ordinary convex-program representations of the constraints, I would expect allocation computation to be tractable; the externality guarantee for a positive-mass cohort is a further mathematical question, not a result established by this paper.

The scenario is plausible because clients in a standardized platform can share values and constraints, and the paper itself studies diversity requirements in large-scale allocation settings. The authors could recognize the model as a population-level version of their question. Still, this is an extension: a single agent has zero mass in a continuum, so the constraint change must apply to a cohort. The paper’s one-agent guarantee does not directly give a constant bound for such a cohort; indeed, its multi-agent bound in Corollary 3 weakens as the number of constrained agents grows.

That cohort limit is the weakest point in the positive case. The model is worth asking, but it cannot be presented as a continuous mirror of a named computational-complexity result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that Theorem 2 and Corollary 2 are not computational results. Theorem 2 proves an analytic bound on negative externality under conditions on \(f\); Corollary 2 specializes that bound to Nash Welfare. The other numbered results likewise establish guarantees or impossibilities about allocation rules. None classifies a computational problem by its complexity or gives an algorithmic result of the kind ChoCo is set up to continuize. The proponent acknowledges this, so neither theorem can serve as an eligible anchor.

The proposed Continuous-Mass Diversity Robustness model is coherent. Repeated clients with identical values and constraints can form types with positive mass, and a cohort-level constraint is a sensible population question. Divisible items and the weighted objective fit the model. I would not reject it on the grounds that its outcomes are fractional or that a single agent has zero mass.

But the proposal introduces a new computational task: compute two Nash Welfare allocations and decide whether their value ratio meets a threshold. The paper studies universal guarantees about that ratio across instances; it does not study the complexity of computing the allocations or checking a ratio on a given instance. One could define the computational task precisely by specifying how each \(P_t\) is represented and how numerical precision is handled. That would make a follow-on question. It would not supply a computational result from this paper to mirror.

There is a related possible repair: ask whether the paper’s universal robustness guarantee extends to a positive-mass constrained cohort. That is close to the authors’ subject and may be worth investigating. But it is an analytic extension of Theorem 2, not a computational continuization. Turning it into a search problem for counterexamples or worst-case instances would add a computational layer the paper itself does not pose.

So the strongest negative case is a scope judgment: this paper may inspire a plausible high-multiplicity research question, but it supplies no named computational result for a continuous mirror to carry forward. I cannot honestly prove the broader claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile research; the cohort model is a real weakness in that universal claim. What I can defend is that the proposed model does not meet this programme’s computational-mirror criterion.
