The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Its named results are economic characterizations: Theorem 1, proved here, says that every empirical equilibrium of the direct revelation game yields the truthful outcome for every common prior exactly when the social choice function is strategy-proof and non-bossy in welfare-outcome. Theorem 2, also proved here, gives the corresponding full-support characterization using essentially unique dominant strategies. Theorem 3 characterizes robust Nash implementation; Theorem 4 gives a revelation principle for empirical equilibrium. None classifies a computational problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise. The paper discusses calculating empirical equilibria, but supplies no named algorithm or complexity result. So there are no eligible anchors, and I would not present either characterization theorem as a complexity result.

The best positive lead is a follow-on question inspired by Theorems 1 and 2: represent a society by rational masses \(\mu_t\) over finitely many complete payoff types, and ask whether every equilibrium that is a limit of weakly payoff-monotone behavior produces an outcome equivalent to truthful reporting. A large school-choice cohort with a modest number of preference and payoff types is a plausible regime for asking it. The paper’s discussion of continuum school-choice work provides some support for that setting, though it does not establish a computational mirror of this paper’s results.

The weak point is substantial: in a nonatomic population, one person’s report cannot affect the aggregate outcome. That can erase the incentive differences on which the paper’s empirical-equilibrium characterizations rely. Restoring those effects may require a different model, so this is a research lead rather than a continuous problem the authors’ named results already anchor. I cannot responsibly predict its complexity class from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes when empirical equilibria implement the truthful outcome; Theorem 2 gives a related characterization for full support. Theorem 3 concerns robust Nash implementation, and Theorem 4 a revelation principle. None gives a complexity bound, algorithm, or hardness result. That is the strongest negative: the proposed mirror would not continue a computational result from this paper.

The school-choice lead is the strongest way to try to build one anyway: take a large cohort with finitely many complete payoff types, scale school capacities with population, and ask whether a report game has an empirical equilibrium producing a non-truthful allocation. But the paper’s characterizations turn on unilateral report changes and their effects on the outcome. In an atomless population, one student’s report does not change the aggregate type distribution. If outcomes are aggregate allocations, profiles differing in one student’s report become indistinguishable in the limit; non-bossiness becomes automatic at that level, and the paper’s outcome comparison loses its content. With reports yielding no personal payoff difference either, weak payoff monotonicity instead selects equal frequencies among indifferent reports. Neither behavior captures the paper’s mechanism-specific distinction between TTC and SPDA.

Keeping that distinction requires tracking an individual’s allocation when they report differently, or retaining finite cohorts where one person can affect other assignments. The first needs a pointwise, identity-sensitive outcome model that a mass distribution alone does not encode; the second preserves the finite game rather than giving its atomless mirror. And the paper’s prior is a joint distribution over payoff-type profiles. A population share vector does not specify agents’ beliefs or correlations. Imposing an independent prior would give a workable special case, but would drop the paper’s characterizations over arbitrary common priors.

So a computational study of an explicitly redesigned mean-field reporting game is possible in principle. It would be a new problem, with its own equilibrium and outcome definitions, rather than a continuous version of a computational result in this paper. That leaves a small honest weakness in the negative case: one could judge such a newly defined problem worthwhile on its own. But the paper itself supplies no computational anchor, and its central results depend on individual-level effects that aggregate continuization erases.
