The continuous mirror question

Given a path \(P=(v_1,\ldots,v_m)\), three complete monotone valuation types \(t\in T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_v\), decide whether there exists \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) assigning mass of each type to integral connected intervals \(B\), satisfying \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B\ni v}x_{t,B}=q_v\), and \(u_t(B)\ge u_t^-(B')\) whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(B'\) has positive occupied mass; construct such an allocation when one exists.

The model it lives in

A support-constrained high-multiplicity allocation problem: valuation types carry mass \(\mu\), individuals receive integral connected intervals, \(x_{t,B}\) records assigned mass, \(q_v\) supplies item locations, and the objective is EF1 feasibility or construction.

The objection that survived

Theorem 4.3 relies essentially on exactly three agents, whereas a positive-mass realization of three types may contain \(K\) copies of each type and therefore many agents; the resulting EF1 support-selection problem has no supplied algorithm or existence guarantee.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.3's path, monotone-valuation, connected-EF1 allocation problem and opens the corresponding high-multiplicity complexity questions. It leaves the graph characterization, EF2 and four-agent existence theorems, identical-valuation algorithm, cited external hardness result, and appendix results untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the fixed-\(|T|=3\) path problem always admit a continuous EF1 allocation, and can one be found in \(O(m)\) or polynomial time?
  • For arbitrary \(\tau\), what is the complexity in \(m\), \(\tau\), and valuation encoding length, and when can rational mass solutions be rounded to finite high-multiplicity allocations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a two-anchor case. The lead is the paper’s cited hardness anchor; the paper’s strongest in-house computational anchor is Theorem 4.3.

The continuous model I would use throughout is this. Let \(G=(V,E)\), let \(\mathcal C(G)\) be its connected vertex sets, and let \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\) be complete valuation types. Type \(t\) has a monotone valuation \(u_t:\mathcal C(G)\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), and occupies mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). There is also a per-capita supply \(q_v\) of copies of each item-location \(v\). An allocation is a family \(x_{t,B}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,B}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral connected bundle \(B\), satisfying

\[ \sum_{B\in\mathcal C(G)}x_{t,B}=\mu_t \]

and

\[ \sum_t\sum_{B\ni v}x_{t,B}=q_v \]

for every \(v\). Thus only population mass is fractional: every individual still receives an integral connected bundle. With rational data, multiplying by a common denominator gives a finite high-multiplicity instance with repeated agents and repeated item copies.

For a bundle \(B\), write \(u_t^-(B)\) for its value after removing the best admissible outer item, as in the paper. The natural continuous EF1 condition is that, whenever \(x_{t,B}>0\) and \(B'\) is occupied by positive mass,

\[ u_t(B)\ge u_t^-(B'). \]

This says that every individual of type \(t\) is EF1 relative to every bundle actually received by someone. It is the direct mass version of the paper’s underlying problem, not cake-cutting or fractional allocation of an individual bundle.

My lead anchor is the result cited in the conclusion as “Theorem 2” of Deligkas, Eiben, Ganian, Hamm, and Ordyniak. The paper states that deciding whether an EF1 allocation exists on a given graph is NP-hard; the hardness already holds when the graph is a star and the agents have binary additive valuations. This result is cited from elsewhere, not proved in the paper.

The corresponding problem is Continuous Connected-EF1 Feasibility:

Given a graph \(G\), binary additive valuation types \(u_t(B)=\sum_{v\in B}a_{t,v}\) with \(a_{t,v}\in\{0,1\}\), rational type masses \(\mu\), and rational per-capita item supplies \(q\), decide whether there exists an \(x\) satisfying the supply equations and the continuous EF1 condition above.

The regime is plausible: a large population consists of recurring cohorts with identical approval patterns for connected facilities, plots, or time blocks on a hub-and-spoke network. The number of people can be enormous while the number of valuation types remains modest. A finite instance with \(n\) agents and one copy of each item is recovered by setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) and \(q_v=1/n\).

I expect the unrestricted version to be Class B: hardness should largely survive because the cited reduction’s combinatorics live in the graph and the connected-bundle choices, not in the names of individual agents. The genuinely interesting question is whether hardness survives when \(\tau\ll n\). The cited theorem does not establish that compressed-type claim, so this is a research conjecture rather than a transferred theorem.

My second anchor is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper. It states that, on a path with three agents and arbitrary monotone valuations, the discrete moving-knife protocol finds a connected EF1 allocation in \(O(m)\) time. The result includes the full algorithmic claim: the procedure terminates, returns EF1, and runs linearly in the number \(m\) of items, using lumpy ties and median lumpy ties.

Its continuous mirror is Continuous Three-Type Path-EF1:

Given a path \(P=(v_1,\ldots,v_m)\), three monotone valuation types \(u_1,u_2,u_3\) over intervals, rational masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3\), and rational per-capita item supplies \(q_v\), find a mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\) in which every individual receives an integral interval and the continuous EF1 inequalities hold.

The authors should recognise this as their problem: the path, connected bundles, monotone valuations, EF1 notion, and moving-knife order are unchanged. Only the population is replaced by repeated valuation types, with aggregate assignment mass recorded by \(x\). I would expect this fixed-three-type path problem to be Class A, probably via a finite support lemma followed by a sweep or flow computation. The ordered path and the paper’s lumpy-tie machinery provide exactly the structure one would hope to exploit. That expectation is not proved by Theorem 4.3: once a type may split across several intervals, the support condition becomes a new issue.

This mirror generates several worthwhile questions: whether the \(O(m)\) bound survives; whether arbitrary \(\tau\) admits an algorithm polynomial in \((m,\tau,L)\); whether the paper’s EF2 Theorem 5.4 has a continuum analogue for arbitrary \(\tau\); and whether finite high-multiplicity solutions can be rounded with controlled loss.

I would not anchor on Theorems 5.4 or 6.2, despite their importance, because they are existence theorems rather than named complexity classifications. Nor would I claim that the paper already contains a continuous algorithm. Its contribution is discrete, although it explicitly imports ideas from cake-cutting.

The weakest point is the population-copy construction itself. Splitting a type’s mass among several bundles can create feasible continuous allocations with no exact finite EF1 counterpart, and the cited NP-hardness reduction may depend on having many distinct valuation types. Thus hardness transfer is secure only as an expectation in the unrestricted model. The positive case nevertheless survives: the model is a genuine high-multiplicity version of the paper’s connected EF1 problem, preserves integral bundles, has a realistic repeated-cohort interpretation, and exposes two concrete computational questions rather than merely replacing \(n\) by a probability distribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proponent’s two anchors do not preserve the computational statements they claim to mirror.

The first anchor is not actually a result of this paper: the NP-hardness theorem is merely cited in the conclusion. More importantly, the proposed continuous model quietly changes two objects, not one. To make a large population compatible with indivisible bundles, it introduces per-capita copies of every graph item. After clearing denominators, this is a finite allocation problem on many replicated graphs, not the paper’s allocation of one connected graph among many agents. That modification may be legitimate, but it needs independent justification; it is not a direct continuous version of the cited hardness result.

There is also a deeper problem with the proposed EF1 condition. If \(x_{t,B}>0\), then every bundle \(B'\) with positive mass imposes the same EF1 constraint, regardless of how small its mass is. Thus a bundle receiving mass \(10^{-100}\) matters exactly as much as one receiving half the population. The mass variables do not soften or average fairness; they merely select a support of integral bundles. The resulting feasibility problem is a mixed discrete support-selection problem disguised by continuous notation. If one instead averages envy over mass, that is a new fairness notion, not the paper’s EF1.

The second anchor fails more sharply. Theorem 4.3 is a theorem for exactly three agents, not for three valuation types. Its proof depends on taking the median of exactly three lumpy ties, having at most one left and one right agent, and terminating when two of the three agents shout. If each of three types is repeated \(K\) times, the finite realization has \(3K\) agents, not three. The median-lumpy-tie argument no longer applies. Indeed, for \(K\) large this lands precisely in the paper’s unresolved territory: EF1 existence for five or more agents on a path is open.

The alternatives do not repair this. Keeping only three aggregate agents destroys the high-multiplicity interpretation. Forcing each type’s entire mass into one bundle turns the problem into a capacitated three-bundle problem, where the masses affect supply but not EF1. Allowing a type to split across bundles restores the genuine high-multiplicity problem, but then every supported bundle must satisfy the pairwise EF1 constraints and Theorem 4.3 provides no algorithmic basis for it. Calling this a “continuous three-agent mirror” therefore conflates three agents with three types.

A better positive anchor might be Theorem 7.1, since it permits any number of agents under identical valuations. But its continuous version has only one valuation type: the population distribution disappears, and the new data are capacities for replicated item locations. That is a capacitated repeated-goods problem, not a meaningful continuization of the paper’s population. The two-agent graph characterization has the same defect: preserving two agents leaves no multiplicity axis, while repeating the types produces a different many-agent problem.

This is not an airtight universal negative. A rational mass solution can indeed be expanded into a finite high-multiplicity instance, so the proposed model is mathematically sensible, and its compressed complexity could be worth studying. But that is evidence for a new high-multiplicity fair-division programme, not for a successful mirror of the paper’s named results. The proponent has not shown a continuous population version of Theorem 4.3; their model crosses the exact agent-count boundary on which the theorem depends.
