The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a valid positive case under ChoCo’s criteria: this paper contains no named computational result. Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Theorem 2, and Corollary 2 are axiomatic characterizations. In particular, Theorem 2 says that on the domain with a strong Condorcet winner, a voting rule is unanimous, neutral, anonymous, and binary self-selective exactly when it is the Condorcet rule; Corollary 2 gives the corresponding result for universal self-selectivity. None asserts a complexity classification or presents an algorithmic formulation the paper computes with.

One could define a continuous-population version of binary self-selectivity, with ranking types carrying masses and compatible tie-breaks split across indifferent mass. But the resulting question would be whether the paper’s axiomatic characterizations persist for a continuum electorate. That is a new axiomatic question, not a continuous computational mirror of a named result. It also needs extra choices about extending voting rules—especially nonanonymous ones such as dictatorships—to distributions.

So there is no qualifying anchor, and therefore no continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover. The paper’s relevance is to axiomatic voting theory, not the computational complexity landscape ChoCo is charting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 and Corollary 2 equate axioms; Corollaries 1 and 2 characterize which voting rules satisfy them. None defines an algorithmic problem, gives an algorithm, or establishes a complexity bound.

The best population mirror is still easy to imagine: represent voters by masses over rankings, extend majority voting to those masses, and ask whether a rule remains self-selective when indifferent mass can be split between compatible rankings. The strongest version would use the anonymous, unanimous, neutral setting with a strong Condorcet winner, avoiding the difficulty of representing a named dictator in a nonatomic population. That could be a sensible continuous axiomatic question. It would not mirror a computational result from this paper.

One could invent a computational task—say, deciding whether an encoded voting rule is self-selective over all rational mass distributions. But the paper supplies no encoding of rules, input class, or finite decision problem to determine that task. Choices about those ingredients would define a new complexity project, rather than continue one in the paper. A large election is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here; the objection is not that mass is meaningless, but that the paper’s contribution is outside ChoCo’s computational scope. That leaves the negative case strong as a programme-fit judgment, though it does not rule out an interesting continuous axiomatic follow-up.
