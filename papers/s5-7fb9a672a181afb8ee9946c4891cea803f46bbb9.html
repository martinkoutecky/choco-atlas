The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper describes a very plausible high-multiplicity population: honest stakeholders share a cost type, malicious stakeholders form another class, and mass represents their share of the ecosystem. Pool formation, delegation, and abstention are meaningful population-level choices. But this is already the paper’s model: its agents are explicitly a continuum, with costs distributed according to \(F\). So presenting that as a new continuous mirror would restate the paper’s starting point.

The required computational anchors are missing. Theorem 1, proved here, gives existence and uniqueness of a threshold equilibrium exactly when \(\lambda > M/(H+M)\). Proposition 1, proved here, says honest pool participation is maximized at \(\lambda=1\). Theorem 2, proved here, characterizes the welfare-maximizing threshold under a concavity condition. These are substantial analytic results, but none classifies a problem’s computational complexity or gives an algorithm with a defined input representation and running time. The paper’s discrete-case discussion in Section 8.3 is also not a named computational result.

The nearest follow-on question would be: given an explicit encoding of the cost distribution and rational parameters \(H,M,R,\lambda\), compute the equilibrium threshold and resulting pool mass, or compute the welfare-maximizing design. That could be a worthwhile computational study of this model. But it is not anchored by a named complexity result in the paper, and its complexity would depend on how the distribution is encoded. I therefore cannot present it as a valid mirror under the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The three named anchors all fail as mirrors for the same reason: the paper already studies a continuous population, and none of its results is a computational complexity result. The authors model honest agents with a cost distribution \(F\), malicious agents with mass \(M\), and pool participation with population masses. That makes the high-multiplicity regime entirely sensible, but it means a continuous-population restatement would repeat the paper’s model.

For Theorem 1, the strongest computational follow-up is to ask how to compute the equilibrium threshold \(c^*\) from an encoded cost distribution and parameters. The theorem already gives the continuous equilibrium characterization and proves uniqueness through a one-dimensional monotone equation. The paper gives no finite encoding for arbitrary \(F\), so its computational complexity cannot be stated without choosing a representation. With a finite list of cost types, a natural approach is to sort the costs and inspect possible cutoffs, with a rule for agents indifferent at a cutoff. Any substantial complexity question would depend on extra structure beyond the theorem’s continuous threshold model.

For Proposition 1, the corresponding design question is to find the \(\lambda\) that maximizes honest pool participation. The proposition already answers it: \(\lambda=1\). A finite-population version with exact random delegation could be studied, but that would be a new finite stochastic game. Section 8.3 already sketches a discrete recasting of the threshold calculation; neither it nor Proposition 1 states a computational problem whose continuous complexity remains open.

For Theorem 2, one could ask for an algorithm to compute the welfare-maximizing threshold or reward share. Again, the result is already about the continuous society and reduces the design choice to a scalar optimization over \(c^*\). For an arbitrary distribution, welfare evaluation depends on how \(F\), its density, and the relevant integrals are represented. For a restricted encoding, the question becomes computational work on that encoding. The paper supplies no complexity anchor for it.

There is no sound objection based on agents lacking multiplicity: large communities with repeated cost types are plausible here, and the authors explicitly use a continuum to represent them. The negative case is narrower. This paper offers no discrete computational result to continuize; its named results are already continuous equilibrium and design results. A richer model with multidimensional costs, stake sizes, or pool constraints could produce worthwhile computational questions, but those would be new results in an expanded model, not mirrors of these anchors. So the case against a paper-anchored mirror is strong; the broader claim that no related continuous computational project could be worthwhile is not established.
