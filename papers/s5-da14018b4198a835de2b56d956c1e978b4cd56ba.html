The continuous mirror question

Given types \(\mathcal T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), post categories \(\mathcal P\) with rational capacities \(\kappa_p\), and an acceptable-pair graph \(E\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=\sum_p\kappa_p=1\) and each type ties all its acceptable categories at rank \(1\), count the vertices of \(X=\{x\ge 0: x_{tp}=0\text{ for }(t,p)\notin E,\ \sum_p x_{tp}=\mu_t,\ \sum_t x_{tp}=\kappa_p\}\) that are popular assignments.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population of types carrying rational masses \(\mu_t\), assigned by mass variables \(x_{tp}\) to post categories with per-capita capacities \(\kappa_p\). Each individual receives one post, while a type's mass may split among acceptable categories; the computational task counts extreme feasible assignments that are popular.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3 counts labeled matchings, while the mirror counts only extreme mass assignments and excludes feasible split assignments; the proponent acknowledges this and defends vertices as natural aggregate outcomes, but does not establish that this restriction captures the full high-multiplicity analogue.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's counting hardness on the all-top-tied instances used in its reduction. It leaves the cheating-strategy, equilibrium, popular-pair, and other results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does counting vertices remain #P-hard when the support size \(\lvert\mathcal T\rvert\) is fixed?
  • What is the complexity of deciding which type–post pairs receive positive mass in some popular population assignment?

The case FOR (proponent)

Lead mirror — Counting Extreme Popular Population Matchings. The type set \(\mathcal T\) describes agents who have identical preferences and all other attributes used by the model. A type has rational mass \(\mu_t\), and each post category \(p\in\mathcal P\) has rational capacity \(\kappa_p\), with both totals equal to \(1\). Each type lists its acceptable post categories, all tied at rank 1. The population is nonatomic: each individual gets one whole post, while a type’s mass can be divided among its acceptable categories.

The instance asks for the number of vertices of
\[ X=\left\{x\ge 0: x_{tp}=0\text{ if }(t,p)\notin E,\quad \sum_p x_{tp}=\mu_t,\quad \sum_t x_{tp}=\kappa_p \right\}. \]
Here \(x_{tp}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to category \(p\). A vertex is one aggregate assignment pattern, and the answer is the number of such patterns. Every feasible assignment is popular: every assigned post is tied for rank 1, so no reassignment produces a strict majority of agents who prefer it. Thus this asks, in a continuous population, how many extreme popular assignment patterns the society admits.

My anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that counting the total number of popular matchings in a bipartite instance with ties is #P-complete. The paper reduces from counting perfect matchings in 3-regular bipartite graphs, a #P-complete problem cited to Dagum and Luby [2]. Given such a graph, the reduction makes it the matching instance and ranks every edge first; its perfect matchings are exactly its popular matchings.

That reduction carries directly to the mirror. For a 3-regular bipartite graph with \(N\) vertices on each side, make each left vertex an agent type of mass \(1/N\), each right vertex a post category of capacity \(1/N\), and retain the graph edges as the acceptable pairs, tied at rank 1. Scaling \(x\) by \(N\) gives the bipartite perfect-matching polytope. Its vertices are exactly the graph’s perfect matchings, so the mirror’s answer equals the count in Theorem 3’s reduction. This establishes #P-hardness by a direct, parsimonious reduction; on that restricted family it is #P-complete.

The regime can represent \(K\) interchangeable applicants of each type and \(K\) slots of each post category. Then the physical population has \(KN\) agents but only \(N\) preference types, and \(K\) can be large. The input records masses and per-capita capacities, while each person still receives an integral post. The reduction uses many types, so it does not show that hardness survives with fixed support size. It does give a high-multiplicity scenario in which the continuous aggregate question remains hard.

I think the paper’s authors could recognize this as a continuous analogue of their counting result: it keeps the popularity criterion, the acceptable-pair graph, and the count of assignment patterns, while replacing individual counts with mass and scaling post supply with population. The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Theorem 3’s all-top-tied hardness construction, not the cheating-strategy, equilibrium, or popular-pair results.

The main weakness is that the paper counts matchings of named agents and posts, whereas this mirror counts vertices after aggregating interchangeable people and grouping slots into post categories. In a replicated finite market, many labeled matchings can collapse to one aggregate pattern. That changes the counting object. I think the mirror remains defensible because aggregate assignment patterns are the natural objects in a continuum population, and the reduction preserves the count exactly at that level.

The expected outcome is hardness transfers: the graph structure carries the hardness into the mass-allocation model. It also raises two useful questions: does this counting problem remain hard when \(|\mathcal T|\) is fixed, and what is the complexity of computing all type–post pairs that occur with positive mass in some popular population matching?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this mirror changes what gets counted. Theorem 3 counts matchings of named agents and posts. In a population model, the direct analogue is a mass assignment \(x\); in the proposer’s all-top-tied instances, every feasible \(x\) is popular. There are then uncountably many popular assignments. Counting only the vertices of the feasible polytope makes the problem finite, but adds an extremality condition that Theorem 3 does not impose.

That choice has a concrete cost in the high-multiplicity story. If each type is replicated \(K\) times, finite markets allow clones of a type to split across post categories. The proposed count keeps only extreme aggregate patterns, which in the reduction send each entire type to one category. Those patterns match the original graph’s perfect matchings, but they omit the population assignments created by replication. Counting assignments at finite resolution instead would depend on \(K\); counting all continuum assignments would not be a finite counting problem. The vertex count is a sensible alternative question, but it is not a direct continuation of the paper’s count.

There is also little popularity structure left in the proposed hard instances: with every acceptable post tied at rank 1, popularity imposes no restriction. The question reduces to counting vertices of a supported transportation polytope, and total unimodularity makes those vertices exactly the graph’s perfect matchings. That makes the mirror a clean transfer of the reduction, but leaves the continuous population itself with little work to do.

I cannot make this a decisive case against the anchor. Vertices are a natural finite set of aggregate outcomes, and the paper’s own hardness proof uses instances where popularity is similarly permissive. A reader could reasonably accept the mirror as a new, well-posed question about extreme popular population assignments. The negative case rests on the gap between that question and counting all popular matchings; it does not show that the vertex-count formulation is one the authors would reject.
