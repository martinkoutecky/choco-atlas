The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, finite rational step-function own-utility curves \(g_t\), externality curves \(h_{r,s}\), and a rational threshold \(K\), decide whether there are action distributions \(\nu_t\) over the finitely many action regions such that \(U_t(a;\nu)=g_t(a)+\sum_{s\in T}\mu_s\int h_{t,s}(z)\,d\nu_s(z)\ge 0\) for \(\nu_t\)-almost every assigned action \(a\), and average welfare \(W(\nu)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\int U_t(a;\nu)\,d\nu_t(a)\ge K\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic concessions population with type masses \(\mu_t\); each type's mass may be assigned across finitely many action regions, represented by fractions \(z_{tq}\). Per-capita externalities are weighted by source mass, participation applies to every region receiving positive mass, and the objective is average social welfare. For fixed regions, welfare is generally quadratic in the assigned fractions, while participation depends on which regions are used.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 7's welfare-threshold hardness setting and Theorem 9's concave-utility welfare optimization setting, with type masses and per-capita externalities. It leaves the feasibility results, the maximal-concession algorithm, and mechanism-design questions aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity in the number of types \(\tau\), action regions, and input bit length \(L\)?
  • Does the threshold problem remain hard under mass splitting, or is there a polynomial-time or parameterized algorithm, particularly for fixed \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror here is negotiation over abatement by a very large population of industrial sites. Sites share exact types defined by their abatement-cost curve and their externality effects on other types—for example, technology and location classes. The population may have millions of sites but only \(\tau\) distinct types. Each site still chooses a real-valued concession, as in the paper; the change is that the population is represented by type masses.

For a precise model, let \(T\) be the finite set of types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. Let \(g_t(a)\) be a type-\(t\) agent’s utility contribution from its own concession \(a\), and let \(h_{r,s}(a)\) be the effect of a type-\(s\) agent’s concession on an agent of type \(r\). A type includes the complete cost and externality profile needed to make its members interchangeable. Allow members of the same type to receive different concessions: \(\nu_t\) is the distribution of actions assigned within type \(t\), so the population mass assigned actions in a set \(A\) is \(\mu_t\nu_t(A)\).

In the nonatomic model, a type-\(t\) agent assigned action \(a\) receives \(U_t(a;\nu)=g_t(a)+\sum_{s\in T}\mu_s\int h_{t,s}(z)\,d\nu_s(z)\). The mass weights give the per-capita externality effect; the agent’s own concession cost remains in \(g_t\). Feasibility requires \(U_t(a;\nu)\ge 0\) for \(\nu_t\)-almost every assigned action, matching the paper’s participation constraint. The objective is average social welfare, \(W(\nu)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\int U_t(a;\nu)\,d\nu_t(a)\). This is a population limit of the paper’s decomposed utilities, not a continuization of its action space or of uncertainty.

My lead anchor is Theorem 7, which the paper establishes here, although its proof is omitted from the supplied text. It states that the decision version of SW-MAXIMIZING-CONCESSIONS—whether a feasible solution with social welfare at least \(K\) exists—is NP-complete even when there are only negative externalities, utilities decompose into step-function components, and each agent controls one variable.

Its continuous mirror is Step-Mass Concessions Welfare Threshold. The input is a finite type list with rational masses, finite rational step-function curves \(g_t\) and \(h_{r,s}\) satisfying the concessions and negative-externality conditions, and a rational threshold \(K\). The question is whether there are action distributions \((\nu_t)_{t\in T}\) that satisfy participation and have \(W(\nu)\ge K\). A solution is such a family of distributions; because the curves have finitely many steps, action mass can be recorded by the finitely many step intervals.

I expect this mirror to be Class C, hard for continuum-specific reasons, but that is a research hypothesis rather than a consequence of Theorem 7. The discrete NP-completeness result does not automatically transfer: in the mirror, a type’s mass can split across actions, which can create feasible population plans unavailable to one discrete agent. Step functions also leave the participation condition dependent on which action intervals receive positive mass, creating a natural support-selection problem. A new reduction would be needed to establish hardness. The mirror itself remains close to the paper’s problem: it keeps individual concessions, decomposed utilities, externalities, participation, and welfare maximization.

The second anchor, and the cleanest tractability result, is Theorem 9, also established in this paper. It states that SW-MAXIMIZING-CONCESSIONS is solvable in polynomial time by linear programming when utilities decompose and every component is piecewise linear and concave. Its mirror is Concave-Mass Concessions Welfare Maximization: use the same type, mass, action-distribution, and participation model, but require \(g_t\) and every \(h_{r,s}\) to be piecewise linear and concave. Find feasible distributions maximizing \(W\); a solution is an optimal family \((\nu_t)\).

This one has a strong Class A case. For any feasible distributions, replace each type’s action distribution with a point mass at its mean action. Jensen’s inequality says that each concave contribution at the mean is at least its average under the original distribution. So every type’s mean-action assignment remains feasible and weakly improves welfare. There is therefore an optimum with one action \(x_t\) per type. The resulting utilities are concave piecewise-linear functions of the type actions; their nonnegative superlevel constraints and the welfare objective have a linear-program representation. This gives a compact LP in the support size, curve descriptions, and bit length.

I would make Theorem 7 the lead because it opens the most valuable question for the programme: do step-utility welfare hardness results persist when action mass can split across a continuous population? Theorem 9 provides a useful boundary case where concavity removes that difficulty. Together they cover the paper’s decomposed concessions welfare problem in these two utility regimes; they do not attempt to mirror its separate feasibility results, maximal-concession algorithm, or mechanism-design questions.

The weakest point is the step-utility hardness forecast. Theorem 7 is about a finite set of individually controlled variables, and its hardness proof does not establish hardness after type masses can split their actions. The concave mirror avoids this problem through the Jensen reduction; the step mirror does not. Its central follow-up is whether the threshold problem is hard for fixed \(\tau\), admits an algorithm parameterized by \(\tau\), or becomes tractable in the genuinely high-multiplicity regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirrors is about their likely research value, not whether they are well-posed.

Theorem 9’s mirror is mathematically sound, but it looks like a short corollary of the paper’s existing LP result. In the concave setting, replace each type’s action distribution by its mean action. Jensen’s inequality preserves participation and weakly improves welfare: it improves both the type’s own concave utility and the concave externality contributions from other types. The resulting problem has one action per type and an LP formulation. That gives an efficient algorithm in the continuous-population input size, but it adds little new optimization structure. It is a weak reason to prioritize this paper for the programme. The paper’s continuous action variables do not themselves constitute a continuous population, though, so this is not an existing continuous treatment or a novelty collision.

Theorem 7’s mirror is harder to dismiss. For finite step functions, divide each type’s actions into their finitely many value regions and let \(z_{tq}\) be the fraction of type \(t\) assigned to region \(q\). A receiver type’s externality then depends on the mass in each region. Participation requires that every region used by that type give nonnegative utility; welfare includes interactions between the masses assigned to regions. This yields a finite, generally nonconvex problem with support choices and bilinear terms. The population size disappears, so any hardness would have to grow with the number of types, utility regions, or their descriptions. That limits the mirror’s appeal as a story about enormous populations of only a few kinds of agent.

But this does not defeat the mirror. Those parameters are precisely relevant to the proposed computational question, and a population split across action regions is a natural continuum version of the paper’s individually controlled variables. Whether splitting makes the problem tractable, or leaves a continuum-specific hardness result, is open on the evidence given. The SAT reduction for Theorem 7 cannot settle it; the mass model may admit plans unavailable to individual agents, but that is the question rather than an objection. A plausible setting—many sites with repeated abatement-cost and externality profiles—also survives the high-multiplicity test.

So I cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case. Theorem 9 is probably too immediate to be a compelling standalone anchor; Theorem 7 remains a worthwhile mirror. The strongest defensible recommendation is to treat the step-utility question as the real research opportunity and the concave result as a quick baseline.
