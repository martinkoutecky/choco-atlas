The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a committee size \(k\), a finite support \(T\subseteq 2^C\) of approval types with rational masses \(\mu_S\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), a required retained fraction \(q\), and a score threshold \(\theta\), does there exist an integral committee \(X\subseteq C\), \(|X|=k\), and retained masses \(0\le y_S\le\mu_S\) such that \(\sum_{S\in T}y_S\ge q\) and \(\sum_{S\in T}y_S s(X,S)\le\theta\)? Consider \(s(X,S)=|X\triangle S|\), \(s(X,S)=|X\setminus S|\), and \(s(X,S)=|X\setminus S|-|X\cap S|\).

The model it lives in

A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval-ballot types \(S\in T\subseteq 2^C\). Choose an integral \(k\)-candidate committee \(X\) and retained mass \(y_S\in[0,\mu_S]\) to minimize \(\sum_{S\in T} y_S s(X,S)\), subject to retaining at least fraction \(q\) of the society.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is \(s\)-Outliers\(_\infty\), the population-continuous version of the paper’s outlier committee problem.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for each \(s\in\{\text{Minisum},\text{Disapproval},\text{Net-Disapproval}\}\), \(s\)-Outliers is NP-complete even when every vote approves exactly three candidates and every candidate is approved by exactly three votes. The theorem reduces from 3-Vertex-Cover. I expect the continuous mirror of these three cases to remain NP-hard; the Minisum case is the clearest lead.

Here is the problem. An instance has candidates \(C\), a committee size \(k\), a finite support \(T\subseteq 2^C\) of approval ballot types, and a society distribution \(\mu\) with rational masses \(\mu_S\) summing to \(1\). It also has a required retained fraction \(q\) and a score threshold \(\theta\). A solution is a committee \(X\subseteq C\), \(|X|=k\), and retained masses \(y_S\) satisfying \(0\le y_S\le\mu_S\), \(\sum_S y_S\ge q\), and
\[ \sum_{S\in T} y_S\,s(X,S)\le\theta. \]
The rest, \(\mu_S-y_S\), is outlier mass. For Minisum, \(s(X,S)=|X\triangle S|\); for Disapproval, \(s(X,S)=|X\setminus S|\); and for Net-Disapproval, \(s(X,S)=|X\setminus S|-|X\cap S|\). Equivalently, minimize the retained population’s total score subject to retaining at least \(q\) of society.

The regime is a large public consultation or election over a fixed shortlist: many residents submit approval ballots, and people who submit the same ballot are identical for this problem. A type is precisely that approved subset; mass is the fraction of society submitting it. With a moderate candidate set and recurring ballot patterns, the number of people \(N\) can be much larger than the support size \(\tau\). The committee stays an integral \(k\)-set; only the population and the retained portion of each ballot type become continuous. This is a direct translation of the paper’s question—choose a committee that scores well for at least a prescribed part of the electorate—and the authors should recognize it as their problem.

The hardness survives fractional retention on the paper’s reduction. For a 3-regular graph on \(N\) vertices, use one candidate per vertex and the ballot \(S_u\) containing the three neighbors of vertex \(u\), with mass \(1/N\) per vertex ballot. Set \(q=(N-k)/N\). For Minisum, set \(\theta=(N-k)(k-3)/N\); for Disapproval use the same threshold; for Net-Disapproval use \(\theta=(N-k)(k-6)/N\). A committee of \(k\) candidates gives every ballot score at least the corresponding per-ballot threshold: \(k-3\) for the first two rules, \(k-6\) for the third. Thus feasibility at the stated threshold requires enough retained mass on ballots attaining that minimum, meaning ballots whose three approved candidates all lie in the committee. Since each vertex contributes at most \(1/N\) mass, at least \(N-k\) such vertex ballots must be available. The paper’s vertex-cover argument then applies to those vertices and the committee. For Net-Disapproval, one can restrict the source instances to \(k\ge6\) by adding fixed disjoint \(K_4\) components, so the minimum score is nonnegative and extra retained mass does not undermine the tight-threshold argument. Conversely, a vertex cover of size \(k\) gives a committee and enough retained ballots attaining the threshold. Fractional \(y_S\) therefore does not erase the reduction’s combinatorics. I expect an NP-completeness result for this continuous decision problem: the reduction gives hardness, and for any fixed committee the retained-mass feasibility test is a rational linear program.

This is a Class B case in the programme’s sense: the hardness rests on the incidence structure among candidates and ballots, not on needing indivisible voters. I would limit this mirror to Theorem 1. The paper’s parameterized and approximation results suggest further questions about the continuous problem, especially its complexity in \(m,\tau,L\) and whether it admits useful algorithms when \(\tau\) is moderate; I am not claiming those results transfer automatically.

My weakest point is that Theorem 1’s bounded-degree construction does not establish hardness specifically in the strongest high-multiplicity regime: each candidate appears in only three votes, so its support may be comparable to the population size. The mirror itself is still natural for large electorates with repeated ballot types, and the reduction shows that allowing partial retained mass does not make the stated problem easy in general. But a separate result would be needed to show hardness, or an algorithmic boundary, when \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is narrower than the prompt’s universal claim. The model is well posed: an approval set is a complete type for this problem, and large consultations over a fixed shortlist can plausibly have many people sharing each ballot. That makes the high-multiplicity regime credible. I cannot honestly argue that no scenario makes the mirror worthwhile.

The proponent’s hardness argument does have a concrete gap. Their reduction from 3-Vertex-Cover does not establish the claimed equivalence, because a size-\(k\) committee can supply enough perfectly scoring ballots without encoding a vertex cover. For example, take three disjoint \(K_4\) components, so \(N=12\), and set \(k=8\). The graph has no vertex cover of size \(8\): each \(K_4\) needs three vertices, for a minimum of \(9\). Yet choose all four candidates from one component, three from a second, and one from the third. This committee has five ballots whose three approved candidates all lie in it. The continuous instance needs only \(q=(N-k)/N=1/3\), so four such ballots’ mass suffices. Their scores meet the proposed thresholds: \(5\) for Minisum and Disapproval, and \(2\) for Net-Disapproval. Thus the proposed instance is feasible despite the source graph being a no-instance. This undermines the claimed hardness transfer for all three variants; it does not show that a correct reduction cannot be found.

There is also a limit to what that reduction would establish even if repaired. Its construction has up to \(N\) ballot types, each of mass \(1/N\). It therefore does not demonstrate hardness in the regime that most distinguishes a continuous population: a large electorate represented by a much smaller support \(\tau\). The proponent acknowledges this limitation, and their suggested \(K_4\) padding does not fix it.

The best argument against investing in this particular mirror is about what the continuum adds computationally. For a fixed committee \(X\), the retained-mass problem is a small linear program over the \(\tau\) ballot types. For Minisum and Disapproval, sort types by \(s(X,S)\) and take the cheapest mass needed to reach \(q\). Net-Disapproval has negative scores, but it too reduces to sorting and selecting type masses. Searching over committees then gives a direct algorithm with a factor of \(\binom{m}{k}\) and polynomial work in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. This model offers no evident column-generation or separation problem of the kind that makes some continuizations especially compelling.

That is a reason to rate this as a less promising showcase for continuous-optimization methods, not a reason to dismiss the question. The committee search could still have a worthwhile complexity landscape in \(m,\tau,L\), and the continuous model captures a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The discrete-to-continuous embedding also remains natural. So the against case is weak against the existence of a good mirror; it is stronger against the proponent’s specific claim that this reduction establishes its hardness, and against presenting this example as a particularly rich use of continuous optimization.
