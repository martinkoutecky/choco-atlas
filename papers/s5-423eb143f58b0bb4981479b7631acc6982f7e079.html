The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no qualifying named computational result: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity classification. Equations (3)–(5) give variational updates, and Section 4 reports empirical comparisons; neither is a named complexity result. So there is no honest theorem anchor to restate. The strongest positive case I can make is an anchorless mirror of the paper’s central inference problem in Sections 2.1–2.5.

Call it Continuous Ordinal Crowd Inference. Imagine a large annotation service with \(N\) workers but only \(\tau\) recurring, standardized worker profiles, where \(\tau \ll N\). A profile is a group whose members share the same expertise and spam-response parameters; profiles might arise from common training and qualification regimes. This is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, though the paper’s own datasets—51 and 762 annotators—do not themselves exhibit it.

An instance consists of \(M\) items, ordinal labels \(1,\ldots,K\), categories \(c(m)\), rational profile masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and, for each profile \(t\) and item \(m\), the mass \(\rho_{mt}\) assigned to that item and the observed label proportions \(q_{mtk}\). The unknowns are the item truths \(z_m\), profile expertise \(\tau_t\), profile informativeness \(\epsilon_t\), and category difficulty \(\delta_c\), with the paper’s priors and ordinal cutpoints. The predicted probability of label \(k\) is the paper’s spam mixture:
\[ p_{mtk}=(1-\epsilon_t)\pi_k+\epsilon_t\left[\Phi\!\left((b_k-z_m)\sqrt{\tau_t\delta_{c(m)}}\right)-\Phi\!\left((b_{k-1}-z_m)\sqrt{\tau_t\delta_{c(m)}}\right)\right], \]
using end bins that absorb the Gaussian tails. Define the aggregate log likelihood by
\[ \sum_{m,t}\rho_{mt}\sum_k q_{mtk}\log p_{mtk}, \]
together with the specified priors. The task is to return each posterior mean \(\mathbb E[z_m\mid q]\), to a requested additive precision. The output remains the inferred ground truth for each item; the continuum is the annotator population. The paper’s real-valued latent truths and its probabilistic noise model are not themselves the continuization.

The mirror preserves the authors’ central question: infer item truths from ordinal ratings while accounting for annotator expertise, spam, and item difficulty. It changes the finite rating records into exact type-level rating proportions, the natural aggregate description when each profile contains many workers. I would tentatively expect tractable approximate inference: the variational updates in the paper suggest type-level computations whose sums run over items, profiles, and labels. But the paper gives no polynomial-time guarantee, and its iterative variational method does not establish exact posterior-mean computation or global convergence. Exact complexity remains open.

The main scope is ground-truth inference. It does not mirror the paper’s empirical claim that one model outperforms baselines, nor its downstream ranking use. It also does not continuize an outcome space: item truths are already real-valued in the paper.

The weak point is the pooling assumption. The paper gives each annotator individual expertise and spam parameters, and its observed data are sparse individual records. A platform with millions of workers but only a small number of genuinely shared profiles is plausible, yet it is not the regime tested in the paper. That makes this a credible continuous version of the inference problem, but it cannot turn the paper’s algorithm or experiments into a named complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying computational result to mirror. The paper gives a probabilistic model, variational update equations, and empirical comparisons; it does not state a theorem or classify the complexity of a computational problem. That is decisive for the proposed anchor: it cannot support the kind of result-to-question mirror the analysts were asked to identify.

The strongest substitute is the proposed profile-level inference problem. It is coherent, but it changes a defining feature of this paper’s model. The paper gives each annotator individual expertise and spam parameters, learned from that annotator’s ratings across items. Under its continuous gamma priors, annotators almost surely have distinct parameter values. Grouping them into a small number of profiles therefore imposes shared parameters the paper did not assume; keeping the parameter values exact leaves no small set of recurring types. A large workforce alone does not supply high multiplicity in the required sense.

One could build a new regime where many workers share standardized profiles and each item receives substantial ratings from each profile. Then aggregate rating proportions could support a mass-based inference problem. But that would be a new statistical model and a new inference-complexity question, not a continuization of a named result in this paper. It may be worth studying on its own; the paper gives no computational classification for it whose continuous mirror ChoCo could chart.

This negative case has a limit: the profile model is plausible, and the central goal—inferring item truths from noisy ordinal reports—survives aggregation. I cannot rule out that the resulting problem would be useful to ChoCo. The case against rests on the narrower point that the proposed anchor is an attractive extension of the paper, not a continuous mirror of a computational result it established.
