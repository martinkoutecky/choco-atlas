The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest negative case is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes an equilibrium of a two-agent subsidy game; Theorem 2 and its corollary compare equilibrium contributions; Theorem 3 characterizes equilibria of an \(n\)-agent mechanism. None studies an algorithmic problem or makes a complexity claim. The equilibrium quantities are already continuous. So a computational mirror would have to add a new task—such as computing equilibria from encoded utility functions—rather than extend a result in the paper.

The strongest candidate is the one the other analyst sketches: represent consumers by preference-and-wealth types, give each type a rational population share, and ask for equilibrium contributions or subsidies. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But it runs into a structural problem: these theorems rely on agents’ individual choices affecting the public good or other agents’ prices. In an atomless population, one consumer has zero mass and cannot affect either. The voluntary-contribution game loses its individual strategic force; the sequential commitment and subsidy-setting effects that distinguish Theorems 1–3 disappear as well. That is a continuum degeneration, not an objection about what answer a surviving theorem would produce.

One could preserve those effects by making each type a coordinated bloc whose members choose together. That is the strongest repair, but it changes the strategic actor from an individual to a coalition. The two-agent results then concern a small number of blocs, while Theorem 3’s subsidy mechanism assigns rates among identified agents in a cycle. Extending it to many blocs or to a continuum requires specifying a new mechanism and new equilibrium question. The paper supplies no computational result for that model to mirror.

The remaining obstacle is computational formulation. The paper allows broad classes of utility functions and preferences but gives no finite encoding of them. A question about exact or approximate equilibrium computation needs such an encoding and a defined output. Choosing a restricted representation—say, piecewise-linear concave utilities—could make a convex-optimization problem, but that would be a new computational-economics project, not a computational mirror anchored in this paper.

So the case against a worthwhile mirror of this paper is strong: its results concern finite strategic agents, and the natural atomless version removes the strategic effects they analyze. The universal claim is less airtight. A carefully designed model of coordinated population blocs might be worthwhile in its own right, but it would need substantial new modeling and would no longer be a mirror of the paper’s named results.
