The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is an application-shaped one, but it has no qualifying anchor in this paper. The paper’s named results characterize existence through topological conditions; they do not establish that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. For example, Theorem 3.11, proved here, says that for an acyclic relation \(R\), the set of \(R\)-maximal elements is nonempty and stable under \(R\) if and only if there is a compact topology on \(X\) with the stated \(T_1\)-order separation property and \(R\) is Nachbin closed. Theorem 3.15, Theorem 3.16, Theorem 3.19, and Theorem 3.23 likewise give topological characterizations of existence for variants of stable sets. None is a computational-complexity result, so none can serve as the required anchor.

A plausible *unanchored* population problem is Majority-Stable-Set\(_\infty\). An instance has a finite alternative set \(C\) and a support of \(\tau\) complete rankings \(T\), each with rational mass \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). Define \(xR_\mu y\) when the mass ranking \(x\) above \(y\) exceeds the mass ranking \(y\) above \(x\). The task is to return a set \(V\subseteq C\) such that no alternative in \(V\) dominates another under \(R_\mu\), and every alternative outside \(V\) is dominated by some alternative in \(V\); if no such set exists, report that. There is no objective to minimize: the output is a stable choice set.

The regime would be a large electorate with millions of members but relatively few recurring, complete preference types—for example, voters represented by a small support of ballot patterns. Mass is population share, and \(\mu_t\) replaces counts. The underlying stable-set question is recognizable as a collective choice problem, and keeping \(C\) finite makes the population the continuous object. But this is only a plausible application of the paper’s solution concept, not a mirror of one of its computational findings.

I would not assign this candidate a tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific classification from the paper. Theorem 3.11 applies only when \(R\) is acyclic and gives an existence characterization, not an algorithm. With finite alternatives, the topological conditions do not provide the computational contribution this programme is looking for; with genuinely infinite alternatives, the mirror would also be continuizing the alternative space, which is outside the programme’s scope. The weakest point is therefore decisive: the paper studies topological existence over infinite sets of alternatives, not computational questions about a continuous population. No anchor-based continuous problem can honestly be stated for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.11 is not a computational anchor: it characterizes when maximal elements form a stable set, using compactness and order-separation conditions. It gives no complexity result or algorithm to continuize. Its setting also has no voters, population, or type distribution; the input is an arbitrary relation \(R\) on alternatives.

The strongest repair is the proposed majority model: take a rational distribution over complete ranking types, derive pairwise majority \(R_\mu\), and ask for a stable set. That is a coherent population problem, but it adds the key machinery the paper does not supply. The paper never derives \(R\) from individual preferences or specifies majority aggregation. So this would be a new computational problem using the paper’s solution concept, not a continuous mirror of its result.

Keeping alternatives finite makes the mismatch especially clear. For an acyclic majority relation, Theorem 3.11 covers only a case where the maximal alternatives already give the stable set; on a finite domain, the discrete topology is compact and satisfies the relevant separation and closedness conditions. For cyclic majority relations, the theorem does not apply. One could turn to the paper’s other stable-set variants, but their theorems are also existence characterizations, not computational results. Asking how hard it is to find such sets may be worthwhile independently; it is not a complexity question answered by this paper.

The paper does use topology on possibly infinite alternative spaces. That is continuity of the alternative space, which the programme explicitly places outside its target unless population continuity is also present. Adding a voter distribution would not turn those topological characterizations into complexity results.

So the strongest case against a mirror is the missing anchor: none of the paper’s named results makes a computational claim about a population problem. That rules out an anchored mirror under this programme’s criteria. It does not establish that Majority-Stable-Set\(_\infty\) lacks independent research value; it establishes that proposing it would start a different project.
