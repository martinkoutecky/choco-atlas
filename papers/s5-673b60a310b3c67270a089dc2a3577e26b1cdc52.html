The strongest positive lead, though unanchored, is a continuum version of the paper’s pricing-rule design problem. Consider a large combinatorial exchange with \(\tau\) complete participant types and population distribution \(\mu\): a type specifies an agent’s endowment, valuation over trades, and information about potential profit. A plausible regime is many small storage or computing-resource operators with a modest number of recurring profiles. Resource supplies scale with the population, while each realized participant still receives an integral bundle. The mechanism chooses an efficient allocation at reported values and applies a common payment rule \(r(\psi)\), where \(\psi\) is the participant’s potential VCG profit. The design question is to find \(r\) and consistent type-dependent equilibrium reporting strategies that minimize the \(\mu\)-weighted blinded deviation incentive, subject to individual rationality and aggregate budget balance. A solution is the payment rule and those equilibrium strategies.

For fixed type-conditional profit distributions and strategies, the center’s rule-optimization subproblem has a credible Class A path: with finite piecewise-rational input, its discretization is an LP in the style of equation (7). The full equilibrium problem may be harder; the paper gives numerical methods, not a complexity classification. Its population-based version could be a useful object for the authors, but it is not established by a named result in this paper.

The weakest point is that an atomless participant may have no effect on the allocation, which could change or erase the critical-value and deviation incentives that drive the paper’s problem. Also, the paper’s distribution \(f(\psi)\) is over potential profits, not over participants. The mirror would need to show that introducing population mass preserves the paper’s strategic question rather than replacing individual deviations with coordinated cohort deviations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a paper-anchored mirror is that the paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Observations 1–3 are structural identities about VCG. Equations (4) and (6) formulate optimization problems; equation (7) gives a discretized LP for the center’s subproblem; the paper then reports numerical solutions. It proves no complexity classification or algorithmic guarantee for the full equilibrium design problem. The proponent correctly identifies no eligible anchor. Their proposed population pricing problem is therefore an extension, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

The extension also changes what the paper’s distributions mean. \(f(\psi)\) describes potential VCG profit for a focal participant across counterfactual market instances, conditional on winning; the blinding kernel models that participant’s uncertainty about \(\psi\). Neither is a distribution over participants. Giving each population type its own \(f_t\), then weighting incentives and budget balance by type mass, is a coherent possible model, but it adds a new layer of heterogeneity and a new design objective. The paper’s discretized LP does not establish the complexity of that larger problem, and its equilibrium computation is numerical rather than a complexity result.

There is a further obstacle to a faithful atomless limit. The paper’s incentive measure concerns one bidder’s unilateral misreport and the resulting change in that bidder’s outcome relative to a critical value. In a continuum market, a single agent has zero mass and cannot affect aggregate winner determination. A positive-mass deviation can affect it, but measures a cohort’s coordinated deviation rather than an individual bidder’s regret. A tagged bidder against a continuum market could preserve an individual decision, but then the population describes the bidder’s environment; the paper’s strategic object remains a focal-agent problem rather than a computation over population mass.

That case rules out the proposed extension as a *result-level mirror*, but it does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. A large exchange with recurring, fully specified participant types is plausible, and the paper itself discusses large combinatorial markets. Such a model could be worth studying even though this paper supplies no theorem for it. So the honest negative verdict is narrow: no eligible anchor survives, and the offered lead is unanchored. The broader “no worthwhile continuous mirror” claim is weak; the material does not rule out a useful new population-based pricing problem.
