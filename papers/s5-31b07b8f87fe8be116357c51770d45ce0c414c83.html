The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly give a continuous mirror anchored to a named computational result: the paper contains no named algorithmic or complexity result. Its numbered results classify strategic manipulability, not the complexity of deciding it or finding a manipulation.

The strongest nearby result is Theorem 15, proved in this paper: if there are at least three alternatives and four individuals, every unanimous positional social choice correspondence is \(\Omega_F\)-\(K\)-manipulable. In full, some individual can misreport so that, among all profiles of others’ preferences consistent with knowing the truthful outcome, the misreport is weakly preferable under that individual’s Kelly extension for every profile, and strictly preferable for at least one. Theorems 16–19 give more specific manipulability and strategy-proofness classifications for Borda, plurality, negative plurality, and Copeland. These are substantial results, but none asserts a running time, a complexity class, or a hardness reduction. So Theorem 15 is not an eligible anchor under your rule.

The best positive direction I can offer is a new, unanchored extension: *Winner-Informed Mass-Cohort Kelly Manipulation*. Let types be complete rankings \(T=L(A)\), and extend a positional rule \(F_w\) to a society distribution by scoring each alternative with its mass-weighted score. An instance gives a rational distribution \(\mu_0\), a ranking \(q\) with \(\mu_0(q)\ge\alpha>0\), and a rational cohort mass \(\alpha\). Write \(\nu_0=\mu_0-\alpha e_q\), the other voters’ distribution, and \(X=F_w(\mu_0)\). The cohort knows its common ranking \(q\) and the winner set \(X\), but not the other voters’ distribution. Its possible worlds are
\[ \mathcal W=\{\nu\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{T}:\ \sum_t\nu_t=1-\alpha,\ F_w(\nu+\alpha e_q)=X\}. \]
Ask whether there is a report \(q'\in T\) such that \(F_w(\nu+\alpha e_{q'})\succeq_{K(q)}F_w(\nu+\alpha e_q)\) for every \(\nu\in\mathcal W\), with strict Kelly preference for at least one \(\nu\). A solution is such a report \(q'\); the decision problem asks whether one exists.

A plausible regime is a large electorate with a small support of repeated, complete rankings, where a sizable organized constituency shares a ranking and can coordinate its ballot. That gives a continuous society with types and rational masses. But the strategic actor is a positive-mass cohort, not the individual whose unilateral deviation Theorem 15 studies. In an atomless model, a single voter has zero influence on the outcome; preserving influence by making the actor a bloc is a material change. This is the proposed mirror’s weakest point and prevents me from presenting it as a direct mirror the paper’s authors would necessarily accept. Its complexity is open: the score regions are polyhedral, but the search over reports and the universal condition over the winner-consistent set are new computational questions. The paper’s theorems do not tell us whether this extension is tractable or hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 15 is the strongest candidate, but it proves a strategic manipulability property of unanimous positional correspondences; Theorems 14 and 16–19 likewise give sufficient conditions or classifications of when manipulation exists. None studies the complexity of deciding or finding a manipulation, or gives an algorithm or hardness result. One can pose such computational questions for a continuous society, but they would be new problems, not continuous versions of the paper’s stated results.

The proposed mass-cohort version is coherent, but it changes the strategic actor. In an atomless society, a single voter has mass zero, so changing their report leaves the aggregate distribution—and therefore a positional or Copeland outcome—unchanged. To retain influence, the model must give the actor positive mass. A fixed \(\alpha\) then describes a bloc’s deviation, whereas taking \(\alpha\) to zero removes its influence except under special tie or margin conditions. Those are possible research questions, but they concern coalition manipulation or limiting pivotality, not the paper’s unilateral manipulation results. The continuum of possible worlds based on a shared winner set is also a sensible extension; it does not restore that lost correspondence.

So the strongest negative conclusion is that this paper supplies no direct computational anchor, and its natural population limit erases the individual deviation at the heart of its results. The universal claim is weaker: the cohort formulation may itself support worthwhile algorithmic work, perhaps through its polyhedral outcome regions. I cannot rule that out. Its value would have to be established as a new computational problem, not inherited from the paper’s theorems.
