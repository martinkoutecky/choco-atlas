The continuous mirror question

Given a finite resource catalogue \(R\), a finite support \(T\) of represented utility functions \(u_t:2^R\to\mathbb{Q}\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, and rational per-capita supplies \(q_r\), decide whether every feasible state \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\), satisfying \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:r\in S}x_{t,S}=q_r\), that admits no rational mass 1-deal maximizes \(W(x)=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}u_t(S)\). A rational mass 1-deal moves some positive mass of type-\(t\) depots from bundle \(S\) to \(S\setminus\{r\}\) and the same mass of type-\(t'\) depots from \(S'\) to \(S'\cup\{r\}\), where \(r\in S\) and \(r\notin S'\), and is rational when \(u_t(S\setminus\{r\})-u_t(S)+u_{t'}(S'\cup\{r\})-u_{t'}(S')>0\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of depots represented by type-and-bundle masses \(x_{t,S}\), with each unit of mass holding an integral bundle, fixed per-capita resource supplies, welfare \(W(x)\), and local moves transferring one resource between equal masses of two depot types.

The objection that survived

Cohort splitting creates stable mixed states absent from the two-agent reduction, so hardness transfer needs a preservation lemma that has not been proved.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4 and the representation-specific cases in Corollary 1 for one-resource negotiation with side-payments; it leaves the sufficiency and maximality results for modular utilities, and the other negotiation results, aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the co-NP-hardness reduction be lifted while ruling out new stable states created by splitting a type across bundles?
  • What is the exact complexity of deciding the local-to-global property for rational type masses and per-capita supplies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest computational anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It says that, under three conditions on a utility representation \(\mathrm{REP}\), deciding whether a set of utilities permits 1-deal negotiation is co-NP-hard even with only two agents. The conditions are: \(\mathrm{REP}\) is an \(r\)-composition over some representation \(\mathrm{REP}'\); utility improvement for \(\mathrm{REP}'\)—given \(u\) and \(Q\), whether some \(Q'\subseteq R\) has \(u(Q')>u(Q)\)—is NP-hard; and \(\mathrm{REP}\) contains the zero utility function. Here, being an \(r\)-composition means that the representations can express, with polynomially bounded size, the functions that equal \(u(S)-k\) when \(r\in S\) and zero otherwise, or equal \(u(S)-k\) when \(r\notin S\) and zero otherwise. The paper’s Corollary 1 gives concrete cases: the decision problem is co-NP-hard for \(k\)-additive utilities for \(k\ge3\), posiforms of degree \(k\ge3\), straight-line programs, and bundle-form utilities. The paper proves the reduction; it relies on utility-improvement hardness results, some cited from earlier work.

A natural high-multiplicity setting is a large network of local service depots that exchange indivisible equipment. Many depots have the same mission and hence the same utility function over equipment bundles; the number of depots is large while the support of utility types is small. Each depot still holds an integral bundle. To make a population limit meaningful, equipment stocks scale with the population: the continuum describes the mass of identical copies of each equipment category. That is a co-modification of the resource model, but it keeps the paper’s central question intact: can rational transfers of one item at a time get the society to maximum total utility?

Here is the continuous decision problem I would give a prover, as the mirror of Theorem 4. Call it Type-Mass 1-Deal Exactness. An instance consists of a finite catalogue \(R\), a finite support \(T\) of represented utility functions \(u_t:2^R\to\mathbb Q\), rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, and rational per-capita stocks \(q_r\in[0,1]\). Let \(x_{t,S}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) depots holding bundle \(S\subseteq R\). Feasible states satisfy
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq R}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni r}x_{t,S}=q_r. \]
Their welfare is \(W(x)=\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}u_t(S)\).

A mass 1-deal chooses \(r\in S\), \(r\notin S'\), and positive mass \(\varepsilon\) of type-\(t\) depots in state \(S\) and type-\(t'\) depots in state \(S'\). It transfers one copy of \(r\) per matched pair, changing their bundles to \(S\setminus\{r\}\) and \(S'\cup\{r\}\). With side-payments, the deal is rational exactly when
\[ u_t(S\setminus\{r\})-u_t(S) +u_{t'}(S'\cup\{r\})-u_{t'}(S')>0. \]
The question is whether every feasible state that admits no rational mass 1-deal is a global welfare maximum. A “no” answer has a concrete witness: a deal-stable state with lower welfare than the optimum over all feasible states. This is the continuous local-to-global question underlying the paper’s negotiation guarantee; it does not ask about an LP encoding.

I expect this problem to be co-NP-hard, with hardness transferring from Theorem 4. The target regime is already strikingly high-multiplicity: \(\tau=2\) utility types can each represent a large cohort, with rational masses such as \((1/2,1/2)\). The theorem’s two-agent constructions suggest a route: replicate the two utility types as cohorts and scale each resource’s stock with the population. A strict suboptimal trap in the finite construction should remain a trap at the corresponding block-constant mass state. The concrete representations in Corollary 1 make this a real target for expressive utility languages, rather than an abstract possibility.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors the paper’s hardness result about checking whether one-item rational negotiation is guaranteed to reach social optimum. It does not claim to continuize every result in the paper. The authors should recognize the question: it retains their resource bundles, utility types, welfare objective, side-payments, and one-resource deals, while making the population the continuous object.

The weak point is the yes direction of the reduction. The continuous model allows a type cohort to split across bundle states; the finite theorem only has two named agents. A full hardness proof must show that these additional mixed states do not create new counterexamples in source instances that should answer “yes.” There is also a separate operational question about arbitrary sequences of ever-smaller mass deals: the static local-to-global problem above avoids confusing that scheduling issue with the paper’s finite-state termination theorem. I would present co-NP-hardness as the expected outcome, with preservation under type splitting as the key lemma still to prove.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 and Corollary 1 are the strongest possible anchors here: they are genuine computational results, and the proposed depot scenario makes repeated utility types plausible. So the case against cannot dismiss them as axiomatic or argue that their hardness makes a continuous question pointless.

The serious objection is to the claimed bridge. The reduction in Theorem 4 concerns two agents holding whole bundles of a fixed set of indivisible resources. In the proposed mirror, a type cohort can split across many bundle states, and feasibility constrains only aggregate resource masses. A discrete trap embeds as a pure mass state, but that does not establish the converse needed for hardness: in source instances where every discrete state is safe, the continuous model may have additional, fractionally split states that are stable yet suboptimal. Corollary 1’s hardness for utility improvement therefore does not by itself transfer to the continuous exactness problem. The proponent identifies this missing preservation lemma, and it is central, not cosmetic.

A more faithful lift could try to prevent that splitting, but making each cohort an indivisible bloc would remove the population continuum. Keeping the cohorts divisible yields a well-defined, potentially worthwhile problem, but it asks about a new state space and needs its own complexity analysis. Keeping the resource catalogue fixed also makes each item’s per-capita supply vanish as the population grows; scaling supplies avoids that degeneration, but changes the setting to repeated resource categories. The programme permits that co-modification, so it is a modeling cost rather than a disqualifier.

That leaves a real negative case against the proposed hardness transfer, but not against the mirror’s value. The type-mass local-to-global question is recognizable and computational, and the depot scenario is plausible. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; this anchor survives the strongest objection available from the paper.
