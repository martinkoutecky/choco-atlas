The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of parties \(P\), a binary-rational voter mass \(\mu_i>0\) for each party-choice type \(i\in P\), with \(\sum_{i\in P}\mu_i=1\), an integer house size \(H\), and a fixed tie priority, compute the seat allocation obtained by applying DCEL to claims \(d_i=\lfloor H\mu_i\rfloor+1\) and estate \(H\). Equivalently, give each party \(\lfloor H\mu_i\rfloor\) seats and assign the remaining seats by descending fractional remainder of \(H\mu_i\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over voter types identified by their party choice. The decision is an integer seat vector \(a\ge0\) with \(\sum_i a_i=H\), selected by the DCEL rule on \(d_i=\lfloor H\mu_i\rfloor+1\), with a fixed tie priority; the paper specifies no separate optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s DCEL–greatest-remainder equivalence; it leaves Theorem 5, the governability properties, and the Spanish election case study aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. For an apportionment problem \((N,v,h)\), it sets \(q_i=h v_i/\sum_j v_j\) and defines the associated discrete bankruptcy claims \(d_i=\lfloor q_i\rfloor+1\) with estate \(E=h\). It proves that applying the discrete constrained equal losses rule to those claims gives the same allocation as the greatest remainder method, when both use the same tie-breaking rule; without tie-breaking, their allocation sets coincide. This is a constructive allocation result, though it is not stated as a complexity-class theorem. If an eligible anchor must explicitly assert “in P,” “NP-hard,” or similar, the paper has no such named result.

My lead mirror is Mass-Hamilton/DCEL Apportionment. An instance consists of a finite set \(P\) of parties, a rational mass \(\mu_i\) for each party with \(\sum_{i\in P}\mu_i=1\), and an integer seat supply \(H\). Each voter’s type is the party they vote for; \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of valid voters casting that vote. Set \(q_i=H\mu_i\) and \(d_i=\lfloor q_i\rfloor+1\). The question is to return the seat allocation obtained by applying DCEL to \(d\) with estate \(H\). A solution is an integer vector \(a\ge0\) with \(\sum_i a_i=H\); with a fixed tie priority, it is exactly the allocation that gives each party \(\lfloor q_i\rfloor\) seats and assigns the remaining seats to the largest fractional remainders. Equivalently, among allocations with \(a_i\in\{\lfloor q_i\rfloor,\lceil q_i\rceil\}\), it minimizes \(\sum_i(a_i-q_i)^2\).

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a national election may have millions of valid voters but only tens of distinct party-vote types. Voters in a type are indistinguishable for this apportionment problem, since only their party’s vote total matters. The society is the distribution \(\mu\); the seat supply remains fixed and the seats remain indivisible. That is a direct analogue of the paper’s problem, not continuity introduced only in the outcome space. The authors should recognize the model: it is their vote vector expressed as proportions, with the same quota and allocation rule. Any rational \(\mu\) can be cleared to integer vote counts, and scale invariance makes the allocation identical.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: sort the fractional parts of \(H\mu_i\), then assign the residual seats. This takes \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) comparisons for \(\tau\) positive-mass party types, with polynomial bit complexity in the input length and \(\log H\). The result carries over directly from Theorem 2 after clearing denominators. Further questions include how small a change in vote mass can alter the allocation, and how the result extends to coupled apportionment across constituencies.

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s DCEL–greatest-remainder equivalence, not the paper’s full governability discussion or its 2015 case study. Its weakest point is that the paper gives no complexity classification, and normalization may look like a change of notation rather than a new computational problem: the main algorithm is just sorting remainders. That limits the mirror’s novelty, but does not make the population-continuous question incoherent. It is a modest, exact high-multiplicity mirror whose answer is already easy to derive.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The voters-as-types story is credible: in a party-list election, a voter’s party choice is enough to determine their contribution to this apportionment problem. The difficulty for the proponent is the anchor. Theorem 2 proves that two allocation rules coincide; it does not analyze the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 5 has the same character. The paper’s other contributions are rule properties and case-study comparisons. So Mass-Hamilton is a reasonable way to *state* a computational task, but computing the allocation is an extra question posed after the paper’s result, not a computational result the paper establishes.

For that task, writing votes as masses adds no new instances. Given rational \(\mu_i\) and house size \(H\), choose a common denominator \(D\) and set \(v_i=D\mu_i\). Then \(\sum_i v_i=D\), and the quota in the original model is \(H v_i/\sum_j v_j=H\mu_i\). Thus Theorem 2 applies directly. With a fixed tie priority, computing the allocation amounts to sorting the fractional remainders and assigning the residual seats. This is an exact change of units, enabled by the paper’s scale invariance. A large electorate is a sensible setting, but its size has already been compressed into the vote vector; replacing those counts with proportions removes no computational structure.

The best extensions do not supply a stronger anchor. Applying the same translation to the governability rules or the constituency case studies still asks for the output of finite allocation procedures. A model where voters continuously switch party and one minimizes the mass moved to change a seat outcome could be a distinct computational problem. But the paper neither formulates nor proves a result about such manipulation; it would be a new project around the paper’s rules, not a mirror of a result in it. The governability axioms themselves are outside the programme’s computational scope.

The honest weakness in this negative case is that the continuous-population model is coherent and recognizable to the authors. If simply asking for the complexity of computing a named rule counts as a worthwhile mirror, the proponent’s example survives. The stronger case against it is that the paper offers no named computational result to mirror, and its one proposed task is just a normalized input to an allocation rule whose output the paper already characterizes.
