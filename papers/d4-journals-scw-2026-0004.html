What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a cohort-level mirror of the paper’s obvious-manipulation result. In a large centralized placement or hiring market, many doctors and positions can share the same complete preferences over a finite set of contract types. Their masses represent population shares, and a mass assignment records how much of each doctor type receives each contract type. The number of agents can be very large while the number of distinct types stays moderate.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that a \(q\)-quantile stable rule \(\phi_q\) is not obviously manipulable if and only if \(q=0\), the doctor-proposing DA rule. The proof uses doctor-proposing DA’s known strategy-proofness for the \(q=0\) case. For every \(q>0\), it constructs a market with two doctors and two hospitals, including \(k\) contracts \(x_1,\ldots,x_k\) between \(d_1\) and \(h_1\), where \(\lceil kq\rceil=2\). Doctor \(d_1\)’s true order is \(x_1 \succ \cdots \succ x_k\), while \(h_1\)’s order is reversed. Truthful reporting gives \(d_1\) \(x_2\); reporting only \(x_1\) as acceptable gives \(x_1\), regardless of the other doctor’s preferences. Since \(d_1\) prefers \(x_1\) to \(x_2\), the deviation is obvious. The theorem classifies the whole family of quantile rules, though its manipulation witness is deliberately small.

I would name the mirror Cohort Obvious Manipulation in Quantile Stable Flows. An instance has finite doctor and hospital type sets; rational mass distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\); contract templates linking types; and strict rankings over those templates and the outside option. A type records every relevant preference and contract attribute. A feasible outcome is a flow \(x\) of mass over contracts, with each type’s total assigned mass no greater than its population mass. It is stable if it is individually rational and no contract can be formed by positive masses of both sides that prefer it to their current assignments.

The mechanism \(Q_q\) selects a stable flow as a continuous extension of \(\phi_q\). A concrete way to specify that extension is by replica limits: expand rational masses into finite markets of identical agents, apply \(\phi_q\), aggregate the assignments, and take the large-market limit. The extension or limit-selection rule must be specified as part of the model, since the paper does not define a quantile selector over a continuum of fractional stable flows.

The question is whether there is a true doctor type \(t\), an alternative reported type \(r\), and a positive mass \(\alpha\) of type-\(t\) doctors such that having that cohort report \(r\) is obvious manipulation. Compare the cohort’s option sets under truthful and deviating reports as the other doctors’ reports vary. A witness is \((t,r,\alpha)\) together with the option sets showing that either the cohort’s worst possible contract improves or its best possible contract improves, according to its true ranking. This mirrors Definitions 2 and 3 at the level of mass. In the two-cohort market with opposed rankings over \(x_1,\ldots,x_k\), the paper’s construction suggests a witness for every \(q>0\): choose \(k\) with \(\lceil kq\rceil=2\), then have the doctor cohort report that only \(x_1\) is acceptable.

The expected qualitative direction is that non-extreme quantiles remain vulnerable to obvious cohort deviations. The continuous model also raises new questions: does the result hold for every replica-limit selector, does doctor-proposing DA remain safe against same-type cohort deviations, and how does fractional mixing among stable flows change the option sets? I would not assign this problem to ChoCo’s tractable / hardness-transfer / continuum-specific-hard trichotomy yet. Theorem 1 is an incentive-property theorem, not a complexity result; any complexity classification of this mirror would be new work. The mirror covers Theorem 1, not the paper’s broader discussion of stable mechanisms.

The weak point is that the paper studies one doctor’s report, while a positive-mass cohort deviation is coordinated behavior. A unilateral report has vanishing mass in a nonatomic society, so a direct population-distribution model cannot preserve its effect without adding individual-level state or changing the strategic unit. Cohort deviation is a plausible mass-scale companion question, and the theorem’s construction motivates it, but the authors might regard it as a neighboring problem rather than the continuous form of their own.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is not a computational result. It classifies which quantile stable mechanisms are obviously manipulable; it gives no complexity claim or algorithmic problem. The proof’s positive case is an explicit two-doctor witness. The paper therefore supplies no result in ChoCo’s target landscape to continuize.

The proponent’s cohort mirror is the best candidate, but it changes the strategic unit that makes the theorem meaningful. In a nonatomic society, one doctor’s report changes a set of measure zero, so it cannot alter the outcome flow. Giving a positive-mass cohort a coordinated false report restores an effect, but asks whether a coalition can manipulate. The theorem concerns one doctor’s options as other doctors’ reports vary. A cohort’s flow can split its members across contracts, so even its “worst” and “best” outcomes need a new definition: must every member improve, or is improvement for some enough? An ordinal ranking of contracts does not settle how to compare the cohort’s mixed assignment. Those choices define a different incentive property.

A hybrid model with a few individually strategic doctors and a continuous background preserves unilateral deviations. But the strategic agents remain distinguished atoms; the theorem’s individual incentive question still depends on those identities. The continuous background could affect their options, yet the paper gives no computational question about such a hybrid market. Studying it might be useful, but it would be a new model motivated by the paper, not a continuous mirror of Theorem 1.

There is also a substantive obstacle in extending \(\phi_q\). Its definition selects the \(\lceil kq\rceil\)-th outcome from a finite set of \(k\) stable allocations. A continuous flow model needs a rule for selecting among its stable flows. Replica limits do not specify that rule by themselves: the finite quantile and its option sets can depend on the replica sequence, and any choice of selector can affect the manipulation predicate. Since the theorem is precisely about that predicate, the selector is part of the new question’s substance.

The matching setting can certainly support high multiplicity; many doctors or applicants may share a complete type, and that makes a continuous market sensible. That supports the cohort regime, but it does not preserve this paper’s individual-manipulation result. The negative case is strongest as a scope judgment: the proposed cohort question could be worth studying, yet it is a new collective-incentive problem with a new mechanism and a new computational target. This does not prove that no such project could be worthwhile; it does leave the universal “no scenario” claim weaker than the objection to this specific mirror.
