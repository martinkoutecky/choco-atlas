The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible population-continuum direction here, but no eligible computational anchor. The numbered results concern information-theoretic gain regions and bounds, equilibrium, or optimal spying strategies. For example, Theorem 2 characterizes an asymptotic rate-gain region, and Theorem 4 establishes an equilibrium result. Neither asks for the computational complexity of a social-choice problem. The repeated-game limit \(n\to\infty\) continuizes the number of rounds, while the divisible cake and bundles continuize outcomes; neither by itself makes the population continuous.

The closest match is Theorem 7, proved in the paper. It says that if a finite society’s cluster \(P_1\), of size \(n_1\), is refined to form \(P'\), then
\[ W_P \le W_{P'} \le W_P\,2^{n_1 I(V_1;E)}. \]
This is a welfare bound for a given refinement, not a runtime or complexity result.

The strongest *prospective* mirror would be a large public agency allocating resources among residents who share a relatively small set of complete valuation types. Let \(\mu\) be the rational mass distribution over valuation types \(v\); choose a refinement label \(\phi(v)\) and a bundle \(b_e\) for each resulting cluster, subject to the aggregate resource constraint \(\int b_{\phi(v)}\,d\mu(v)=\mathbf 1\). The objective is per-capita log Nash welfare, \(\int \log(v\cdot b_{\phi(v)})\,d\mu(v)\). One could ask for the maximum welfare gain from refining a specified cluster into at most \(r\) subclusters. This preserves the paper’s clustering and Nash-CUF question while making the population the continuous object. Taking logs of Theorem 7 gives the corresponding finite-society bound on normalized gain, \(\frac{1}{n}\log_2(W_{P'}/W_P)\le (n_1/n)I(V_1;E)\).

That is a credible research extension, and the high-multiplicity regime is sensible for residents grouped by shared valuations. But Theorem 7 only bounds a specified refinement; it does not solve the optimization problem over refinements or classify its complexity. So I cannot honestly present this as a mirror anchored in a computational result from the paper, or predict whether the resulting optimization is tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorems 1–3 characterize information and gains in two-player games; Theorem 4 is an equilibrium result; Theorems 5–6 bound the value of spying questions. Their asymptotic variable is repeated games, and their distributions describe uncertainty about valuations. Turning either into a population would change the object: a mass of cutters and choosers needs a new interaction model, while a distribution over repeated instances is uncertainty, not a continuous society.

Theorem 7 is the proponent’s best anchor, but it is a welfare bound for a specified clustering refinement, not a complexity or algorithmic result. Its quantities already come from the finite society’s empirical distributions. Normalizing the bound gives \(\frac{1}{n}\log_2(W_{P'}/W_P)\le (n_1/n)I(V_1;E)\). Replacing those empirical frequencies with rational masses carries the formula over directly; it does not uncover a computational question that the theorem leaves open.

The proposed stronger mirror—optimizing welfare over refinements into at most \(r\) clusters—is coherent. A finite list of complete valuation types with rational masses is a sensible high-multiplicity model, and identical types need not be treated as named individuals. But optimizing over refinements changes the question: Theorem 7 bounds each chosen refinement; it neither asks for the best one nor makes a computational claim about finding it. That optimization could be worth studying, but on the evidence here it is a new algorithmic problem built from the paper’s model, not a mirror of one of its computational results.

So I can reject the proposed anchor as a *computational-result mirror*. I cannot honestly establish the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario: the clustering optimization remains plausible, and the brief’s criteria rule out dismissing it just because high multiplicity or fractional bundles are involved. The negative case is therefore strongest on the paper’s lack of eligible results, and weak on the universal claim.
