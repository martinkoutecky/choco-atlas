What the mirror covers

The proposed aggregation covers outcomes and utilitarian welfare under fixed voting responses; it leaves aside endogenous equilibrium analysis, finite-sample bootstrap frequencies, and experimental inference. No named computational result in the paper anchors this question.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is strongest at the level of the paper’s model. It explicitly studies a large electorate whose voters draw multi-issue valuations from a common distribution \(F\). For a statewide ballot, a continuous society is natural: a type records a voter’s direction and intensity on each of the four propositions, plus any relevant voting behavior; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the electorate with that complete type. The paper’s SV and QV rules then specify how voters allocate their bonus vote or voting power, and welfare is the mass-weighted sum of the intensities on the winning sides. The authors would likely recognize this distributional electorate as a natural representation of their setting.

An unanchored candidate question is: given a finite, rational-support \(\mu\) over those types and a specified response rule for each type under majority voting, SV, and QV, compute the winning side on each proposition and each rule’s utilitarian welfare. A solution reports those outcomes and welfare values, including the paper’s random tie rule where needed. With the response rules fixed, this is tractable by aggregating vote margins and welfare over the \(\tau\) supported types. It also leads naturally to questions about welfare comparisons over uncertain \(\mu\), and about finite-electorate deviations from the population result.

That candidate mirrors the paper’s mechanism-performance comparison, but the paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its own results are experimental and bootstrap findings. The formal welfare result summarized in the appendix is attributed to Casella and Gelman (2008); the cited QV results are also external, and the paper says the multi-election QV theory has not been worked out. None is a qualifying anchor from this paper. So there are zero anchor-specific continuous problems to report under the prompt’s rule.

The weak point is substantial: the candidate fixes behavior, making computation simple, while the paper’s central evidence concerns finite samples, simulated elections, and observed choices. Making behavior endogenous would be closer to strategic voting, but the paper does not supply a solved multi-issue QV equilibrium to mirror. This is a plausible population-level extension of the setting, not a continuous mirror anchored by a named computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its findings are experimental comparisons and bootstrap estimates; the formal results it discusses are attributed to earlier work, and the multi-election QV theory is explicitly left undeveloped. That is the strongest objection to the proposed mirror: there is no algorithmic claim here whose continuous analogue could chart a complexity boundary.

The proponent’s fixed-response question is well-defined, and California’s electorate is a convincing high-multiplicity setting. Those facts support the population model, but the proposed computation is just aggregation: for each proposition, sum type masses times votes to get the margin, then sum masses times intensities on the winning side to get welfare. With \(K\) propositions and support size \(\tau\), this takes \(O(K\tau)\) arithmetic operations. It provides a population-level way to report outcomes, but no substantial computational question.

A stronger version would make voters’ choices endogenous and ask for equilibria of multi-proposal SV or QV under a distribution of valuation types, then compute outcomes and welfare. That could be a worthwhile project. It is also the paper’s clearest research gap: the authors say the multi-election QV theory has not been worked out. But solving equilibrium properties is an extension of the paper’s experimental study, not a computational result it establishes; the paper’s QV benchmark is explicitly heuristic. The resulting question may be interesting, but its interest does not arise from continuizing a computational problem in this paper.

The paper’s bootstrap findings also depend on variation across finite electorates. For a fixed continuous society, aggregate vote shares and welfare are deterministic; the repeated-sample frequencies that drive much of the comparison disappear. Keeping those frequencies requires modeling finite sampling or uncertainty over societies, which changes the object being computed.

So the negative case is strong against a *paper-specific computational mirror*, not against the naturalness of a continuous electorate. Its weak point is the authors’ acknowledged gap in multi-election QV theory: a new computational equilibrium project might be valuable, even though this paper supplies no named computational result to anchor it.
