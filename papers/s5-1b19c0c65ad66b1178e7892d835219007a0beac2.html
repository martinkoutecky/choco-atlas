The continuous mirror question

Given rational potential levels \(z_j\) with conditional masses \(q_j\), disadvantaged share \(p\), bias factor \(\beta\), and voucher budget \(\hat c\), choose voucher masses \(x_j\) with \(0 \le x_j \le p q_j\) and \(\sum_j x_j \le p\hat c\). Advantaged students at level \(z_j\) are ranked by \(z_j\); disadvantaged students are ranked by \(z_j\) if vouchered and \(\beta z_j\) otherwise. Using expected ranks under uniform tie-breaking, find an allocation minimizing the maximum positive rank displacement of any disadvantaged type from its unbiased rank.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic student population with finite types \((g,z_j)\), rational masses, and common school rankings. The planner chooses voucher mass \(x_j\) at each disadvantaged potential level; the objective is to minimize the largest positive displacement from the all-unbiased matching, with ties assigned their expected rank under a uniform lottery.

What the mirror covers

It extends Theorem 4’s deterministic minimax voucher allocation to listed finite-support potential distributions. It leaves the PAUC result in Theorem 5, randomized and stochastic-effect vouchers, incentive compatibility results, and empirical matching with heterogeneous preferences aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For arbitrary listed distributions \((q_j,z_j)\), when does an optimal voucher allocation have interval or cutoff structure, and when must it use separated potential levels?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is Continuous Minimax Voucher Allocation. The types are \((g,z_j)\), where \(g\) is the student’s group and \(z_j\) their true potential. A society is a list of rational masses: a fraction \(p\) is disadvantaged, and both groups share a distribution \(q_j\) over potential levels. Disadvantaged students without vouchers are ranked by \(\beta z_j\); with vouchers, they are ranked by \(z_j\). Advantaged students are ranked by \(z_j\). The planner chooses how much mass \(x_j\) of each disadvantaged potential type to voucher, subject to \(\sum_j x_j\le p\hat c\). Schools have unit mass and rank students by perceived potential; for ties, use the expected rank under a uniform lottery. The objective is to minimize the largest positive change in school rank for any disadvantaged student, compared with the unbiased matching. A solution is an optimal allocation vector \(x\) and its maximum mistreatment.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large annual admissions cohort in which many students share a group label and a test-score level, with the same school preferences assumed in the paper’s theory. The paper reports roughly 29,000 SHSAT applicants in the cohort it studies; under this stylized model, types are group-and-score pairs, so there can be many more students than distinct types. The type definition matters: this model treats students with the same group and potential as identical for ranking and intervention purposes, just as the paper does.

My anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It is an exact optimization result rather than a conventional complexity-class theorem; the paper gives no named NP-hardness, \(P\), or parameterized-complexity result. Theorem 4 considers a Pareto potential distribution with parameter \(\alpha\), multiplicative bias \(\beta\), disadvantaged fraction \(p\), and voucher budget \(\hat c\). Assuming \(p<1-\beta^\alpha\), it proves that the unique optimal debiasing set up to measure zero is an interval \(T=[Z_1^*,Z_2^*]\). If \(\hat c\ge \frac{(1-p)(1-\beta^\alpha)}{1-p+1-\beta^\alpha}\), its endpoints are \(Z_1^*=\bigl(\frac{(1-p)+(\beta^{-\alpha}-1)\hat c}{\beta^{-\alpha}-p}\bigr)^{-1/\alpha}\) and \(Z_2^*=\bigl(\frac{(1-p)(1-\hat c)}{\beta^{-\alpha}-p}\bigr)^{-1/\alpha}\), and the resulting maximum mistreatment is \((1-p)(1-\beta^\alpha)\frac{1-\hat c}{1-p\beta^\alpha}\), down from \((1-p)(1-\beta^\alpha)\) without vouchers. If \(\hat c\le\frac{(1-p)(1-\beta^\alpha)}{1-p+1-\beta^\alpha}\), the optimal value is \((1-p-\hat c)(1-\beta^\alpha)+p\hat c\), with \(Z_1^*=\bigl(\frac{(1-p-\hat c)\beta^\alpha}{1-p}+\hat c\bigr)^{-1/\alpha}\) and \(Z_2^*=\bigl(\frac{(1-p-\hat c)\beta^\alpha}{1-p}\bigr)^{-1/\alpha}\).

The finite-support problem above asks the same underlying question: how should limited voucher mass be allocated across potential levels to minimize the worst mistreatment? The proposed computation generalizes the paper’s Pareto case to an explicitly listed society distribution. I expect a Class A result for the one-dimensional, common-ranking model: Theorem 4’s interval structure suggests that a useful structural characterization may reduce the search to a small number of cutoffs. That is a conjecture for arbitrary finite support, not a consequence already proved by Theorem 4; establishing whether optimal allocations retain that structure is one of the natural questions this mirror generates.

The mirror’s strongest point is that it preserves the paper’s actual policy choice, ranking rule, budget, and objective. It does not replace voucher allocation with an easier proxy. It covers the deterministic minimax intervention result, not the paper’s incentive-compatible randomized policies, stochastic voucher effects, or empirical stable matchings with heterogeneous preferences. The continuous school interval is part of the paper’s matching model; the proposed computational question is about the masses of student types.

The soft spot is also substantial: the paper already calls its model a continuous matching market, makes the student population continuous, and proves Theorem 4 directly for that model. So this cannot honestly be presented as the paper’s first population continuization. The positive case is narrower: its theorem gives a closed-form answer for a parametric Pareto society, while an algorithmic problem with an explicit finite list of type masses asks how the same intervention behaves across input societies. The authors would readily recognize the objective as theirs, but the reviewer could reasonably judge that as a computational generalization of an existing continuous model, rather than a new continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the only anchor, and its population is already continuous: students form a nonatomic population with a Pareto distribution of potentials, the planner chooses a measurable mass of disadvantaged students to voucher, and the theorem gives the exact minimax allocation and value. That is a same-paper continuous treatment of the proposed intervention question, not an outside answer or a fractional-outcome model. A continuous mirror of this result would repeat work the paper has already done.

The finite-support version is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. A large cohort can have many students sharing a group and score level, and arbitrary listed masses extend the paper’s Pareto assumption. But that extension changes the distributional input; it does not supply a missing continuization of Theorem 4. Under the proponent’s stated worst-case objective, it also appears to reduce directly to an LP: sort the possible perceived-score levels, express each level’s mass and each resulting rank as an affine function of the voucher masses, then minimize a bound on mistreatment subject to the budget and mass constraints. That yields a polynomial-time algorithm in the support size and input bit length. It could be a useful generalization, but it offers little of the computational landscape the programme is meant to uncover.

A richer model could include students’ preference lists and the paper’s heterogeneous-preference stable matching. That has a plausible high-multiplicity interpretation, with types including group, score, and preference list. It would, however, move away from Theorem 4’s common-ranking model and would be a new computational study of the empirical setting. It is not evidence that the anchored result needs a continuous mirror.

The negative case is strongest for the anchor and weaker for the universal claim. The paper itself leaves analysis beyond Pareto potentials open, and a finite-support study could be worthwhile as that generalization. I cannot honestly rule out every useful mirror; I can say that Theorem 4 already supplies the continuous result, while the clearest proposed extension looks like a straightforward optimization over its input distribution.
