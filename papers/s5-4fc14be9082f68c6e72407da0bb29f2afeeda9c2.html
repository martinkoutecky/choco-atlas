What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror names Proposition 3.3 and Proposition 4.3, but neither is a computational result; the paper’s remaining equilibrium characterizations and examples likewise do not state computational guarantees or hardness.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case under the anchor rule: this paper has no named computational-complexity result. It does not classify an underlying problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar, and it gives no algorithmic analysis.

The closest results are proved in the paper, but they characterize equilibria. Proposition 3.3 says that, generically, for all sufficiently high \(\delta\), an outcome \(r\) can arise in a semi-stationary perfect equilibrium exactly when \(r\in SG(\Gamma)\). Proposition 4.3 says that when opponents can make positive pre-commitments, the corresponding condition is \(r\in QC(\Gamma)\). These are substantive equilibrium-set results, but not computational anchors.

One could imagine a continuum version with a large legislature whose members have a small number of complete payoff types: mass records the share of each type, and the formateur chooses preconditions and a coalition composition. The question would be which outcomes can arise in equilibrium. But that would extend the paper’s game-theoretic characterization; it would not mirror a computational result in the paper. The paper’s actual bargaining actors are parties, and replacing them with divisible masses of legislators also needs a convincing regime and model. I would not present this as a ChoCo case for a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper gives ChoCo no computational result to mirror. Proposition 3.3 characterizes outcomes sustainable in semi-stationary perfect equilibrium; Proposition 4.3 characterizes them when positive pre-commitments are possible. Neither states an algorithmic problem or proves a complexity bound. Asking how to compute or enumerate \(SG(\Gamma)\) or \(QC(\Gamma)\) could be a useful new question, but it would add a computational result to the paper’s equilibrium analysis rather than continuize one of its results.

The best population-based reinterpretation still has a problem at the level of the strategic actors. The paper’s players are parties, each with a distinct place in the coalition options and its own bargaining position. A party is not a collection of interchangeable legislators: its members do not each separately accept the formateur’s proposal. Aggregating MPs by complete preference and payoff type therefore does not preserve the party-level vetoes and coalition choices the model studies. Treating a mass of identical parties as the actors would require a new account of how a coalition forms from fractions of a type—and what happens to a party’s pivotal role when each individual party has measure zero. That may be a research direction, but it is a redesigned bargaining model, not a convincing mirror of these propositions.

One could instead make voter opinions or constituencies continuous inputs to the payoff functions while keeping the finite set of parties. That gives a plausible continuous population behind the game, but leaves the paper’s equilibrium-characterization results as they are; computing outcomes for the resulting payoff data would again be a new computational question. So the negative case is strong for excluding this paper as a ChoCo mirror. It is weaker as a literal claim that no worthwhile continuous computational study could grow out of it: that broader claim cannot be established from the paper, and a redesigned model might be interesting on its own.
