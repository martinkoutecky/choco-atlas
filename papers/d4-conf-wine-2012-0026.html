What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the plurality rule illustrated in Example 11; it does not cover Theorem 8's finite-sample privacy and incentive guarantees or the paper's other privacy, social-welfare, and deterrence results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case for a continuous mirror is that this paper already studies mechanisms whose decisions depend on aggregates of many agents’ reports. But it has no eligible computational anchor under your rule: its numbered theorems establish privacy and approximate truthfulness, not that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

The closest result is Theorem 8, proved in this paper. It says that a histogram mechanism over a partition whose bins all have positive probability is \((k,\epsilon,\delta)\)-Bayesian differentially private when \(0\le k\le n-2\), \(4/[p_{\min}(n-k-1)]\le\epsilon\le1\), and \(\delta=e^{-\Omega((n-k)p_{\min}\epsilon^2)}\). With utilities bounded by \(\alpha\), it is also \((k-r+1,r)\)-persistent \((r\epsilon+2r\delta)(2\alpha)\)-Bayes-Nash truthful for \(1\le r\le k+1\). The paper’s Example 11 applies this to plurality by grouping rankings according to their top choice. That gives a credible large-electorate regime: many voters, relatively few recurring ballot types, and decisions based on the mass in each group.

A concrete nearby continuous problem is Continuous Plurality Winner. An instance gives candidates \(C\), a finite support of ranking types \(\mathcal T\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\). Find a candidate maximizing
\[ p_\mu(c)=\sum_{t:\operatorname{top}(t)=c}\mu_t. \]
For a large election with many voters and comparatively few supported ranking types, this is a natural mass-based model. It is tractable by summing the masses and comparing candidates, in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). The authors would recognize the plurality rule and histogram aggregation here, since they give that example themselves.

This is a candidate mirror, not a per-anchor question: Continuous Plurality Winner has no named computational result in the paper to mirror. The paper’s theorem is about the privacy and incentive properties of a finite-sample mechanism. Its guarantees rely on randomness from the other agents’ independently drawn types; replacing those draws with a fixed mass distribution removes that source of output randomness. So the positive case is strongest for the *population regime and aggregate model*, and weak as a claim that this paper contributes a computational-complexity question. The mirror covers Theorem 8’s histogram-mechanism result and Example 11’s plurality application, not the paper’s full privacy and truthfulness programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central problem is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 8 proves privacy and approximate-truthfulness guarantees for a finite-\(n\) mechanism whose input consists of individual reports. It gives no algorithm or complexity classification, and its histogram function \(f\) is arbitrary. The other main theorems likewise concern privacy, incentives, and deterrence rather than computational complexity.

The proposed Continuous Plurality Winner is a sensible high-multiplicity problem: summing rational masses by top choice is a natural task. Example 11 supports that population model. But the task is a new rule-evaluation problem, not a continuous version of Theorem 8’s result about privacy and truthfulness. A richer version—computing or optimizing a plurality mechanism subject to those guarantees—has no specified computational object in the paper. With arbitrary \(f\), its complexity would depend on how \(f\) is represented.

The obstacle to that richer mirror is structural. Theorem 8 gets privacy from the randomness of the other \(n-k-1\) truthful players’ reports. In an exact nonatomic society, changing one person’s type changes the mass distribution by zero. A deterministic mechanism’s output therefore stays unchanged, making individual privacy and incentives vacuous. If a change can affect the output, the output distributions have disjoint support, which cannot satisfy finite-\(\epsilon\) privacy with \(\delta=0\). Defining privacy for positive-mass cohorts could avoid this degeneracy, but it changes the privacy unit and asks a new question. Sampling finitely many agents from a type distribution preserves the paper’s smoothing argument by retaining its finite-agent model.

The same issue applies to the other privacy theorems, which rely on independent finite samples; the deterrent-payment result also tracks named players and individual audits. The paper’s plurality example is good evidence that high multiplicity is a sensible regime. It does not supply the missing computational anchor. I would argue against a ChoCo mirror of this paper, while conceding that a separately motivated computational study of mass-based mechanism design could be worthwhile.
