The continuous mirror question

Given a population divided into τ response types with rational masses μ, a current yes-mass profile x, and explicit rational piecewise-affine response maps for each type and current opinion, compute the exact closure of all next yes-mass profiles attainable with positive probability as the cohort sizes grow. Output the transition relation as a finite symbolic representation.

The model it lives in

Each type t has mass μ_t and current yes-mass x_t, with 0 ≤ x_t ≤ μ_t. For current opinion b ∈ {0,1}, let a_{t,1}(x)=x_t and a_{t,0}(x)=μ_t-x_t; let p_{t,b}(x) be a rational piecewise-affine, coordinatewise nondecreasing response probability. Assume members update independently, as in the paper. Define ℓ_{t,b}(x)=a_{t,b}(x) when p_{t,b}(x)=1 and 0 otherwise; define u_{t,b}(x)=a_{t,b}(x) when p_{t,b}(x)>0 and 0 otherwise. The next-mass relation is ℛ(x)=∏_t[∑_b ℓ_{t,b}(x), ∑_b u_{t,b}(x)]; the task is to compute this relation exactly over all x in a finite symbolic form.

The objection that survived

The paper supplies no canonical scaling from its finite-player aggregation functions to the proposed piecewise-affine response maps over masses, so the mirror requires a modeling choice beyond the stated theorem.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of Theorem 1, which reconstructs the qualitative one-step transition relation from influence information. The paper has no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or otherwise; its procedures for eliciting influence are presented without numbered complexity theorems. Theorem 1 is the nearest named computational anchor, but it is a structural characterization, not a complexity classification.

As printed, Theorem 1 says that for an influence process based on aggregation functions, \(b_{S,T}=1\) exactly when both conditions hold: for every \(i\in T\), some nonempty yes-influential coalition for \(i\) is contained in \(S\); and for every \(i\notin T\), some nonempty no-influential coalition for \(i\) is disjoint from \(S\). Otherwise \(b_{S,T}=0\), with the all-no and all-yes states absorbing. The paper recalls this result from Grabisch and Rusinowska [27] rather than proving it anew, then uses it to calculate transitions in its example. It is a useful anchor because it identifies the computational object the paper cares about: the support of possible transitions, not their probabilities.

My lead mirror is Typed Qualitative Transition Reconstruction\(_\infty\). Consider a large online community discussing a yes/no issue. Members fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of cohorts—perhaps by role or participation category—and everyone in a cohort has the same complete update rule. The community may have millions of members, while \(\tau\) stays small. Let \(\mu_t\) be cohort \(t\)’s rational share of the population, and let \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) be the mass of that cohort currently saying yes. Cohort members with the same current opinion \(b\in\{0,1\}\) respond alike: \(p_{t,b}(x)\in[0,1]\) is their probability of saying yes next, given the current mass profile \(x\). For a precise finite-input version, supply these response maps as rational piecewise-affine functions.

The instance is \((T,\mu,(p_{t,b})_{t\in T,b\in\{0,1\}})\). The question is to compute, in a finite symbolic description, the exact support-limit transition relation between current and next mass profiles. For each \(t,b\), let \(a_{t,1}(x)=x_t\) and \(a_{t,0}(x)=\mu_t-x_t\). Define
\[ \ell_{t,b}(x)= \begin{cases} a_{t,b}(x),&p_{t,b}(x)=1,\\ 0,&p_{t,b}(x)<1, \end{cases} \qquad u_{t,b}(x)= \begin{cases} 0,&p_{t,b}(x)=0,\\ a_{t,b}(x),&p_{t,b}(x)>0. \end{cases} \]
The possible next yes-masses for cohort \(t\) form
\[ \mathcal R_t(x)= \left[\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}\ell_{t,b}(x),\ \sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}u_{t,b}(x)\right], \]
and \(\mathcal R(x)=\prod_t\mathcal R_t(x)\). A solution gives this relation exactly as a finite union of rational polyhedral cells or an equivalent symbolic representation.

This is a population continuization: \(x_t\) and the next mass \(y_t\) may be fractional because they describe fractions of a large cohort. The response rates are part of the update model, not a replacement of the population by a probability space. The construction also has a direct high-multiplicity interpretation. In a finite cohort, if every member has a probability strictly between zero and one, every possible number of next-period yes-voters has positive probability; as cohort size grows, those attainable fractions become dense in the interval above. The cases \(p=0\) and \(p=1\) preserve the forced endpoints. This makes the proposed relation the support-limit analogue of the reduced matrix in Theorem 1.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable when \(\tau\) is fixed and the response maps have explicit polyhedral descriptions: the task is to partition the input region according to whether each response is zero, one, or interior, then describe the resulting intervals. I would not claim a polynomial-time bound uniformly in \(\tau\) without controlling the size of that partition. The natural follow-up is to classify the terminal communicating classes of \(\mathcal R\), and to compare that classification with the paper’s finite terminal classes. This mirror covers Theorem 1’s one-step qualitative reconstruction; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s entire convergence analysis or to supply a hardness anchor.

The weak point is that this is a support-limit model, not the only canonical continuum dynamics. Under a standard atomless law-of-large-numbers interpretation, next-period mass may be fixed by expected response rates rather than ranging over every fraction with finite-population positive support. That distinction matters. The case for this mirror is that the paper deliberately discards transition probabilities and retains every positive-probability transition, so support-limit semantics preserve the paper’s chosen object. It is a plausible, limited mirror for that theorem—not evidence that the full model’s convergence analysis has already been continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the proponent’s only anchor, and it supplies no complexity result: it gives an exact Boolean test for whether a finite transition is possible, with no running-time, query-complexity, or approximation claim. Given the influence coalitions, testing one transition just checks the stated conditions. The paper’s practical contribution is its finite-agent elicitation procedure, not a complexity classification that Theorem 1 establishes.

There is also a structural mismatch. The theorem represents influence through inclusion-minimal coalitions of named agents. In an atomless population, any positive-mass coalition can be split into smaller positive-mass coalitions, while every finite set of individuals has zero mass. The theorem’s minimality condition and individual influence arcs therefore disappear in the limit. A mass-based transition relation can replace them, but it asks a different question.

The proposed replacement needs response maps \(p_{t,b}(x)\) as input. The paper gives aggregation functions on finite binary profiles; it does not specify how those functions extend to fractional mass profiles. In the anonymous OWA case, many identical agents are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but constructing a continuum response map still requires choosing a sequence or interpolation of finite models. If the maps are supplied as piecewise-affine input, the proposed task largely compiles their zero, one, and interior regions into interval endpoints. If they must be learned from behavior, arbitrary maps cannot be identified exactly from finitely many queries without extra assumptions.

The support-limit choice also needs defending. At every finite population size, interior response probabilities make every attainable yes-count possible, even when a particular count has vanishing probability as the population grows. Keeping all those transitions yields the proposed intervals. A law-of-large-numbers interpretation instead gives a deterministic next mass, \(y_t=x_t p_{t,1}(x)+(\mu_t-x_t)p_{t,0}(x)\). Both are coherent continuations, but they preserve different aspects of the finite model. The interval relation is a deliberate support semantics, not a forced limit.

That is the strongest case against this anchor: the source theorem concerns a finite identity-sensitive object, and the proposed computational task needs new continuous response data and a chosen limit semantics. Still, I cannot make the universal negative claim convincingly. A large anonymous community with shared response rules is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and classifying terminal sets for a specified mass transition model could be a genuine computational question. The objections weaken the proposed mirror’s connection to Theorem 1; they do not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists.
