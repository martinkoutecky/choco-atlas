The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper that qualifies as an anchor for the requested case. Theorems 1 and 5, and Propositions 2, 3, 6, and 7, establish stationary distributions, vote share limits, and accuracy bounds. For example, Theorem 5(c), proved here, gives lower bounds on limiting plurality accuracy. None classifies a computational problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise.

The paper does give a natural continuous population limit: as the number \(n\) of free voters grows, their state shares converge to a Dirichlet random vector. A possible follow-on question would take rational \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m\) and a threshold \(q\), and ask whether
\[ \Pr\!\left[\Theta_1>\max_{j\ne1}\Theta_j\right]\ge q, \qquad \Theta\sim\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m). \]
That has a plausible large, exchangeable population behind it, but it is a new computational question, not a result anchored by this paper. Also, the states \(i\) are alternatives voters adopt; they are not voter types in the programme’s sense.

So the honest positive case is limited: the paper studies a genuine large population and derives a continuous vote share limit, which could motivate later complexity work. It supplies no eligible named computational anchor, so I cannot give the required anchored mirror problem without overstating what the authors proved.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 5, with their propositions, establish stationary distributions, continuous vote-share limits, and accuracy guarantees. Even Theorem 5(c), the closest candidate, proves lower bounds on plurality accuracy; it says nothing about computational complexity or an algorithm. The paper’s main contribution is analytical.

The proposed question—deciding whether
\[ \Pr\!\left[\Theta_1>\max_{j\ne1}\Theta_j\right]\ge q \]
for a Dirichlet vote-share vector—is a possible follow-on computation, but it does not rescue an anchor. Its input is the zealot parameters \(\alpha_i\), and its output is a probability about stationary vote shares. It does not ask how to change a society given as a mass distribution over voter types. One could treat agents’ current states as types, but the paper already studies those aggregate state counts; turning the remaining probability calculation into an algorithmic problem would still be a new computational question, not a result established here.

A stronger proposed mirror might introduce voter types for signal quality or influence exposure, then ask how to reallocate mass, or spend a budget on zealots, to maximize the chance of choosing correctly. Those are coherent research questions. But the paper supplies neither such choices nor costs: zealot counts are fixed, and the free voters are homogeneous. Adding an intervention objective creates a new model; adding heterogeneity or structured networks also moves beyond the exchangeable setting that yields the paper’s closed forms. The nontrivial continuum the paper does derive is already an analytical limit of the kind the programme distinguishes from its computational gap.

I would not claim that no related continuous computation could ever be worthwhile. Exact algorithms for plurality accuracy or a costed zealot-allocation problem might make good projects. The negative case is strongest, and honest, at the narrower point: those would be new extensions, not continuous mirrors of this paper’s named results.
