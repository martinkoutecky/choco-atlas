The continuous mirror question

Given a finite activity set \(A\), a finite set of complete participant types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\), rational activity interests \(v_r(a)\) with \(v_r(\theta)=0\), and per-capita capacities \(\kappa_a\), compute a mass assignment \(x_{r,a}\geq 0\) such that \(\sum_{a\in A\cup\{\theta\}}x_{r,a}=\mu_r\) for every \(r\), and \(\sum_{r\in T}x_{r,a}\leq\kappa_a\) for every real activity \(a\). Require that whenever \(x_{r,a}>0\), every activity \(b\neq a\) with \(v_r(b)\geq 0\) and \(v_r(b)>v_r(a)\) is full: \(\sum_{r\in T}x_{r,b}=\kappa_b\). What is the computational complexity of finding such an assignment in terms of \(|T|\), \(|A|\), and the input bit length?

The model it lives in

A continuous-population IA matching with finite support \(T\): each type records the preferences and peer-affinity information relevant to the original problem, and \(x_{r,a}\) is the mass of type \(r\) assigned to activity \(a\) or inactivity \(\theta\). Each participant still receives one activity; fractional values describe proportions of a type, while capacities scale with population as per-capita bounds \(\kappa_a\). The anchored cohesion condition depends on activity interests and whether preferred activities are full.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Property 4’s social-cohesion guarantee, with soundness as a feasibility constraint. It leaves the utilitarian and egalitarian welfare mechanisms, coalition decisions based on peer affinities, stability properties, decentralization, and the experimental comparisons unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a socially cohesive mass assignment be found in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|A|\), and the input bit length?
  • Can the inclusive mechanism’s coalition decisions using peer affinities be lifted to mass assignments while retaining its cohesion guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no anchor that meets your stated complexity-result rule: it gives no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its closest named computational guarantee is Property 4 (Social cohesion), proved here: for every IA instance, the inclusive algorithm returns a socially cohesive matching. Together with Property 3, this means the algorithm terminates and returns a sound matching; Property 4 says that whenever an individual is assigned to an activity while preferring another nonnegative-interest activity, that preferred activity is full. The guarantee applies with either social decision rule. These are algorithmic guarantees, but the paper gives no complexity bound. So I cannot honestly make a claim about discrete hardness dissolving in the continuum.

If Property 4 is accepted as a weaker, algorithmic anchor, my lead candidate is Continuous Social-Cohesion Matching. A plausible regime is a regional or citywide senior activity programme with thousands of participants and a modest number of recurring tours. A complete type records a participant’s tour interests and affinity toward each other type; participants of a type are identical in every respect the matching uses. The paper itself describes computing preferences from user features, with thousands of users and ten tours as its intended scale. Such a regime is plausible if the feature and preference profiles are shared by many people; it is much less plausible if affinities are individually idiosyncratic.

The instance is a finite activity set \(A\), a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_r>0\) summing to \(1\), rational interests \(v_r(a)\) with \(v_r(\theta)=0\), type-to-type affinities \(w_{rs}\), and per-capita capacities \(\kappa_a\). The decision variable is \(x_{r,a}\geq 0\), the mass of type \(r\) assigned to activity \(a\) (or to \(\theta\), inactivity). It must satisfy \(\sum_a x_{r,a}=\mu_r\) for every type and \(\sum_r x_{r,a}\leq\kappa_a\) for every real activity. The requested solution is any such assignment where, whenever \(x_{r,a}>0\), every activity \(b\) with \(v_r(b)\geq0\) and \(v_r(b)>v_r(a)\) is full: \(\sum_r x_{r,b}=\kappa_b\). This is the paper’s social-cohesion condition applied to type mass. The affinity table retains the peer information of the IA problem; for the paper’s additive specialization, peer utility can be represented by \(\frac12\bigl(v_r(a)+\sum_s w_{rs}x_{s,a}\bigr)\), though Property 4’s condition itself depends only on interests and capacity.

I would expect this problem to be tractable for finite support: the condition is a non-wastefulness requirement on divisible assignments, and an algorithm based on activity preferences and capacity filling seems plausible. That is a conjecture about the mirror, not a result in this paper. The follow-up questions are whether one can prove a polynomial bound in \(|T|\), \(|A|\), and the input bit length; whether a continuous inclusive mechanism can preserve the paper’s coalition decision rule as well as cohesion; and how its mass solutions relate to finite high-multiplicity executions.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as a continuous form of the matching property they prove: participants choose activities, capacities scale with the cohort, and mass records proportions of otherwise identical participants receiving integral assignments. The weakest point is also substantial: this mirrors only Property 4’s cohesion guarantee. It does not carry over the paper’s harder feature—choosing coalitions using peer affinities to pursue welfare—and the mirror lacks a qualifying complexity anchor under your rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no result in the programme’s target category. Properties 2–4 prove termination, soundness, and social cohesion for the proposed algorithms; none gives a worst-case complexity bound or an exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithm. The runtime evidence is experimental. So Property 4 is a correctness guarantee, not a complexity result for a computational problem.

The proposed continuous social-cohesion question is recognizable, but it preserves only the condition that a more-preferred, nonnegative-interest activity must be full. That condition does not use peer affinities or the coalition decision rule, which are central to the paper’s matching model. Its mass formulation is a capacitated fractional assignment problem. It asks for an assignment satisfying a qualitative property, rather than analyzing the complexity of a result the paper establishes about its algorithms. The mirror may be sensible, but it is a thin anchor for ChoCo’s complexity programme.

A more faithful mirror could keep complete preference types, type-to-type affinities, per-capita capacities, and mass assignments, then ask about maximizing utilitarian or egalitarian welfare. That would retain the paper’s coalition composition effects. But it would be a new computational study of the optimization problems the paper formulates and solves heuristically; the paper proves no complexity result about them either. In the generic model, arbitrary pairwise affinities may also make most individuals distinct types. Feature-based affinities and recurring cohorts could avoid that, so this is a concern about the regime, not a decisive objection.

I would not claim the universal negative has been established. A cohort model with shared preference profiles and capacities that scale with population is plausible, and the authors could recognize its mass version. The case against is strongest on the programme’s anchor requirement: the paper’s named guarantee does not carry a complexity claim. It is weaker against the existence of a worthwhile continuous question built from the paper’s broader model.
