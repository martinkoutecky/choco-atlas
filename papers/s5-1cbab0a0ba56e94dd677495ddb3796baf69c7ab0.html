The continuous mirror question

Given finite patient-type and provider-type sets \(T\) and \(R\), rational patient masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational provider supplies \(\kappa_r\), match qualities \(\theta_{t,r}\in[0,1]\), and a common acceptance probability \(p\in(0,1]\), assign each patient type fractions \(x_{t,S}\) to upfront menus \(S\subseteq R\), with \(\sum_{S\subseteq R}x_{t,S}=1\). Patients of each type arrive uniformly interleaved over \([0,1]\), accept with probability \(p\), and, if accepting, choose their highest-quality available provider type in their menu or abstain; a match consumes one unit of provider supply. Is there a polynomial-time algorithm in the type counts and input bit length that returns a menu policy with expected normalized match quality at least \(p\) times the optimum over all such policies?

The model it lives in

Model patients and providers as finite types carrying masses \(\mu_t\) and \(\kappa_r\), with type-level qualities \(\theta_{t,r}\). Menu fractions \(x_{t,S}\) define the upfront policy and the objective is expected match quality under fluid sequential responses. A transportation LP over patient-provider mass \(z_{t,r}\) upper-bounds the optimum; assigning its matched mass singleton menus gives the \(p\)-approximation.

The objection that survived

The opponent’s residual concern is that this is a straightforward fluid lift of the same matching bound and may add little new algorithmic structure; that limits novelty, not whether the analogue is a well-posed computational question.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is the paper’s pairwise approximation result. The supplied text contains no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time complexity classification; its best computational anchor is an approximation guarantee.

Anchor: Theorem 3.6, proved in this paper (Appendix F). Under the uniform choice model, where each patient accepts with probability \(p\) and otherwise abstains, the pairwise policy achieves match quality at least \(p\) times that of the optimal assortment policy. The pairwise policy finds a maximum-weight bipartite matching between patients and providers, using match qualities \(\theta_{i,j}\) as edge weights, and offers each matched patient only their paired provider. The comparison is with \(\pi^*\), the policy maximizing expected match quality over all assortments under the same model.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large health system reassigning patients after provider turnover. Patients could share types defined by catchment area, care needs, preferences over provider classes, and willingness to accept a match; providers could share types defined by clinic location and specialty. In such a system, the number of patients may be hundreds of thousands while the number of distinct patient and provider types is in the hundreds or low thousands. The type must capture every feature used by the model, including the patient’s match-quality vector and choice probability. Provider supply then scales with the population, as the continuization programme allows.

The continuous problem: Fluid Pairwise-Menu Approximation. An instance consists of finite patient and provider type sets \(T\) and \(R\), rational patient masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), rational provider supply masses \(\kappa_r\), rational match qualities \(\theta_{t,r}\in[0,1]\), and a common rational acceptance probability \(p\in(0,1]\). Each provider unit has capacity one. Before responses begin, the administrator assigns each patient a menu of provider types. Formally, \(x_{t,S}\) is the fraction of type-\(t\) mass offered menu \(S\subseteq R\), with \(\sum_{S\subseteq R}x_{t,S}=1\).

Interpret responses as the fluid limit of a uniform random order: arrivals of each type are spread uniformly over \(u\in[0,1]\). An arriving patient accepts with probability \(p\); if they accept, they choose their most preferred provider type in their menu that still has supply. Each match consumes one unit of provider mass and contributes its \(\theta_{t,r}\) to total match quality. The question is: given the instance, return a fixed menu policy whose expected match quality is at least \(p\) times the optimum over all such policies. A solution is the menu fractions \(x_{t,S}\); for support sizes \(\tau_P=|T|\) and \(\tau_R=|R|\), the target is an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau_P,\tau_R\), and the input bit length.

The expected answer is tractable, in the approximation sense. Solve the transportation LP
\[ \max \sum_{t\in T,r\in R}\theta_{t,r}z_{t,r} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_r z_{t,r}\leq\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t z_{t,r}\leq\kappa_r,\qquad z_{t,r}\geq0. \]
Assign mass \(z_{t,r}\) of patient type \(t\) the singleton menu \(\{r\}\), and give the remaining mass an empty menu. Because total mass offered provider type \(r\) is at most \(\kappa_r\), those patients cannot exhaust its supply before they respond. The policy therefore achieves \(p\sum_{t,r}\theta_{t,r}z_{t,r}\). Conversely, the realized matches of any policy obey the LP’s patient-mass and provider-capacity constraints, so the LP objective upper-bounds the optimal policy’s quality. This gives the claimed \(p\)-approximation by a polynomial-time flow computation.

I think the authors would recognize this as their problem: the menus are fixed upfront, patients respond in random order, choice follows the same acceptance-and-best-available rule, and the administrator still maximizes system-wide match quality. The population and provider supply become masses; type-level menu fractions express what happens to many otherwise identical patients. The mirror covers Theorem 3.6’s pairwise guarantee, not the paper’s fairness, empirical, or other choice-model results. Its natural follow-up questions are whether the \(p\) factor is tight, whether exact optimal menu design is tractable, and which guarantees extend to threshold or MNL choice.

The weakest point is the type assumption. Real patients may have nearly unique quality vectors, and rounding or grouping them can change their rankings. The fluid response model also relies on a genuine large-population regime. Those are meaningful limits on where the mirror applies; in a system with repeated preference and care-need profiles and many independently timed responses, they do not erase the continuous approximation question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.6 is a real computational anchor: it gives a \(p\)-approximation for an optimization problem over assortment policies. The strongest negative case is about the mirror’s likely payoff. Under exact patient and provider types, the proposed fluid version of that guarantee follows from the same matching bound: a transportation LP upper-bounds the quality of any policy, and assigning singleton menus according to its solution achieves \(p\) times that bound. That is a clean continuous result, but it appears to transfer the paper’s proof directly rather than expose a new computational bottleneck.

There is a practical limit to the simple lift. A type must preserve the full quality vector and choice behavior; in a geometric health system, exact patient types may therefore be nearly unique. Coarser location or specialty classes could change qualities and preferences. This makes the Connecticut data a weak high-multiplicity example. It does not rule out a different system with repeated cohorts and provider classes, though, and the programme explicitly permits such a regime.

A stronger mirror would optimize the exact expected match quality over type-level menu fractions \(x_{t,S}\), with provider availability evolving through the random response stream. That preserves the paper’s central menu-design objective and creates a genuine continuous optimization problem. Theorem 3.6’s pairwise LP would give a benchmark, not solve the exact problem. Existing work on sequential assortment and matching is nearby, but studies different response protocols and does not establish that this fluid problem is already solved ([Torrico et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04313)).

So the best case against is that the proponent’s particular \(p\)-approximation mirror may offer little beyond a routine fluid lift, while exact type compression may fit only selected regimes. Those points lower the expected payoff; they do not defeat the anchor or the stronger exact-menu version. I cannot make an honest universal negative case here.
