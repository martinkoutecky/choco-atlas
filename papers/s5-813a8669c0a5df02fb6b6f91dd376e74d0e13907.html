The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying computational-mirror case from this paper: it contains no named result asserting a complexity classification such as polynomial-time solvability or hardness.

The paper’s own main result is the unnumbered Proposition in Section 3. Under the paper’s standing assumptions, for an even electorate of size \(2n\), with \(n>1\), and a singleton core \(x_0\), there is a finite sequence of majority dominations from any other feasible policy to \(x_0\). That is an existence theorem, not a result about deciding accessibility, finding a sequence efficiently, or the complexity of either task. Proposition 0 is attributed to Cox (1987), and Proposition \(0^\prime\) to Bhattacharya and Ciardiello (2024); neither is a computational result either. The numbered lemmas support the geometric proof, but do not classify a computational problem. So there are no qualifying anchors, and I can’t assign a Class A, B, or C expectation to an anchored mirror.

The strongest nearby population analogue would give ideal points a distribution \(\mu\) over a compact convex policy set \(Z\), with type \(q\)’s utility \(u_q(x)=-\|x-q\|^2\). A policy \(x'\) majority-dominates \(x\) when \(\mu(\{q:u_q(x')>u_q(x)\})>1/2\); the question would be whether a singleton interior core point can be reached from every policy by a finite chain of such moves. That genuinely continuizes the population, and its solution would be a finite sequence of policies. But it is an unanchored existence question, not a computational mirror established by this paper. The main weakness is also structural: the finite proof uses an even electorate and pairings of voters’ gradients at the core, whose analogue for a population distribution is not supplied here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its main proposition says that, under the stated geometric assumptions, some finite chain of majority dominations reaches the core. It gives no complexity classification for deciding accessibility or finding a chain. Proposition \(0\) and Proposition \(0^\prime\) are also existence results, and Lemmas 1 and 2 are geometric steps in the proof. So there is no paper-anchored computational question here.

The proponent’s population version is a sensible mathematical question, but it does not fill that gap. It replaces the finite electorate with a measure over ideal points and asks whether the core remains reachable. The paper’s theorem does not supply a computational task for that model. To get one, someone would need to choose an encoding for the policy set and population, and specify what output counts: a path of bounded length, a shortest path, or a finite path with no length bound. Those are new algorithmic problems, not mirrors of a result in this paper.

There is also a real risk that the natural atomless version loses the paper’s central even-electorate phenomenon. Put \(x_0=0\), write a policy as \(h\), and let \(q\) be an ideal point. The voter prefers \(x_0\) to \(h\) exactly when \(q\cdot h<\|h\|^2/2\). If \(x_0\) is an interior core point, no sufficiently small move from it can be preferred by a strict majority. For a smooth, full-dimensional distribution with positive mass near the relevant bisectors, this leaves \(x_0\) with a strict majority over every other feasible policy: voters with \(q\cdot h<0\) already favor \(x_0\), and the positive threshold adds more. Accessibility then reduces to one move. The finite proof’s difficulty comes from exact ties among paired voters; a regular atomless population can wash out those ties.

One can preserve the ties with an atomic distribution whose masses balance across opposing ideal points. That is the strongest rescue: it can make a mass version of accessibility nontrivial and may be a worthwhile question in its own right. But the paper assumes distinct ideal points, so retaining its precise setting gives each voter a separate type; and even in a grouped version, the paper supplies no algorithmic claim for that mass problem. The negative case is therefore strongest as a scope judgment, not as a claim that no useful population model exists. A computational study of mass accessibility could be valuable, but it would be a new target rather than a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
