The continuous mirror question

Given two equally sized populations, finite sets of preference types ranking counterpart types, and an explicit finite list \(\mathcal E\) of balanced rational mass profiles closed under role swap, find a randomized type-report mechanism minimizing \(\max_{e\in\mathcal E} V_e\), and determine whether an optimal mechanism can be role-symmetric. For each profile \(e\), truthful type lotteries must induce feasible flows \(\mu_a^e q^S_{eab}=\nu_b^e q^C_{eba}=x^e_{ab}\), and satisfy first-order stochastic-dominance truthfulness when an individual report changes while the population masses stay fixed. Define \(z^e_{ab}=\max\{1-\sum_{b'\succeq_a b}q^S_{eab'}-\sum_{a'\succeq_b a}q^C_{eba'},0\}\) and \(V_e=\sum_{a,b}\mu_a^e\nu_b^e z^e_{ab}\).

The model it lives in

Two balanced nonatomic populations with finite preference types and masses \(\mu,\nu\); decision variables are type-report lotteries and feasible matching flows. Individuals have fixed-mass stochastic-dominance incentives, instability is averaged over type pairs, and the objective minimizes worst-case violation over the supplied profiles.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers role symmetry and worst-case instability for randomized mechanisms in balanced type-mass markets; it leaves the deterministic \(3\times3\) bound and algorithms, the small-market numerical comparisons, and the larger-market simulations unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do exact finite-market strategy-proofness constraints converge to the fixed-mass type-level constraints, with a controlled error in instability?
  • Can the paper’s objective over all preference profiles be represented with finite input when the mass profiles range over all rational distributions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. My strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in the paper: when the two sides have equal size, every two-sided strategy-proof randomized matching mechanism has a two-sided strategy-proof, symmetric randomized counterpart whose objective is no worse under either the average-case or worst-case stability-violation objective. The proof averages the mechanism with its role-swapped, transposed version. This is a computational-optimization result used to reduce the paper’s LP, though it is not a complexity classification.

A plausible mirror is Type-Mass Minimum-Instability Matching. Take two large, balanced populations—say, applicants and vacancies in a recurring labor market—with \(N\) agents on each side but only \(\tau\) distinct preference types, where \(N\gg\tau\). A type is a complete ranking over the other side’s finitely many types and the outside option. Agents of one type are interchangeable for the model’s purposes; \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) are the fractions of each type on the two sides. The rankings over types, and hence indifference among individuals of a given type, are part of the mass model.

An instance consists of the two finite type sets, a finite list \(\mathcal E\) of balanced population profiles with binary-rational masses, and a role-swap correspondence between the type sets. The decision is a randomized mass-matching mechanism for those profiles: for each profile \(e\), it assigns each reported type a lottery over counterpart types or being unmatched. The lotteries must induce a feasible matching flow \(x^e_{ab}\), so \(\mu^e_a q^S_{e,a,b}=\nu^e_b q^C_{e,b,a}=x^e_{ab}\). For a population-continuum agent, a unilateral report leaves the population masses unchanged. Require strategy-proofness at fixed \(e\) by first-order stochastic-dominance constraints comparing that type’s truthful lottery with its lottery under every alternative report.

For type pair \((a,b)\), use the limiting fractional-stability slack
\[ z^e_{ab}=\max\!\left\{1-\sum_{b'\succeq_a b}q^S_{e,a,b'}-\sum_{a'\succeq_b a}q^C_{e,b,a'},\,0\right\}. \]
The profile’s normalized violation is \(V_e=\sum_{a,b}\mu^e_a\nu^e_b z^e_{ab}\). The problem is to minimize \(\max_{e\in\mathcal E}V_e\), and return an optimal mechanism. The objective averages over potential type pairs; the exact probability that two particular named agents match vanishes in the high-multiplicity limit.

This is a tractable-side candidate: with the profile list explicit, feasibility, stochastic-dominance truthfulness, role symmetry, and the linearized slack objective form a rational LP of size polynomial in the number of types, profiles, and input bits. Theorem 2’s averaging argument predicts that restricting to role-symmetric mechanisms does not change the optimum when \(\mathcal E\) is closed under role swap. That prediction is not a complexity result proved in the paper. The mirror covers its balanced, randomized, worst-case optimization and symmetry reduction; it does not claim to mirror the deterministic \(3\times3\) result or the larger-market simulations. Natural next questions are whether the explicit-profile LP extends to all rational mass profiles without enumeration, and whether finite anonymous mechanisms converge to this model with a controlled incentive and stability error.

The weakest point is the incentive notion. In the continuum, an individual report has zero effect on population masses; in the paper, a finite agent’s misreport changes the reported profile. A referee could reasonably ask whether fixed-mass truthfulness captures the limit of the paper’s exact finite strategy-proofness. The paired-type preference model and limiting stability objective also need a formal high-multiplicity bridge. Those are substantial gaps, but they are explicit mathematical questions about the paper’s mechanism-design problem, rather than a mirror based only on fractional outcomes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proponent’s anchor, Theorem 2, is a symmetry lemma, not a complexity result. Its proof averages a mechanism with its role-swapped version; linear incentive constraints survive averaging, and convex instability cannot increase. The same argument applies pointwise to a finite-type mass model whenever the two type spaces and population profiles have a role-swap correspondence. Continuization supplies no new algorithmic insight to that theorem.

The proposed finite-profile LP does not strengthen the anchor much. With an explicit list \(\mathcal E\), it is a generic rational LP, and the symmetry reduction follows from the same averaging argument. But \(\mathcal E\) is a selected list, not the paper’s optimization over all preference profiles. The stronger version would optimize over every rational population distribution. That requires a mechanism schedule indexed by the continuum of population states, plus a finite-to-continuum result explaining how its incentive constraints arise from the paper’s finite mechanisms. The paper establishes neither a tractable representation of that schedule nor a complexity question about it.

There is a legitimate stronger mirror, though, and it blocks a confident universal rejection. In a large market with finitely many preference types, one can define an individual’s lottery by both their report and the distribution of the other agents. A unilateral report then need not be vacuous: it can change the individual’s lottery while leaving the aggregate distribution fixed. Type-level preferences with indifference within classes and instability weighted by type-pair mass also give a plausible matching model. That would be a real population-continuum mechanism-design problem, not merely a fractional-outcome model.

So the negative case is strongest against Theorem 2 as the anchor: its result transfers by generic symmetrization, and the finite LP formulation does not make it a complexity contribution. But I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile mirror. The stronger large-market problem is plausible; the paper simply gives little reason to expect its continuous version to have a useful finite optimization form. That is a serious gap in the case for this paper, but not a decisive objection to the problem itself.
