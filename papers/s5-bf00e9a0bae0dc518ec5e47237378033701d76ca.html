What the mirror covers

The proposed extension targets the implementation claims in Theorem 1 and Corollary 1; no computational result of the paper is mirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a new mass-type extension suggested by the paper, not a mirror supported by one of its computational results.

A candidate society would be an atomless population of residents with payoff types \(\theta\) from a finite set \(\Theta\), where \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of the population of type \(\theta\). A social choice rule would take the population composition as input, \(F:\Delta(\Theta)\rightrightarrows\Delta(X)\). A mechanism would give each resident a finite message set and map the resulting aggregate report distribution to a lottery over outcomes. The question would be whether a bounded mechanism can virtually implement \(F\): for each \(\varepsilon>0\) and each \(\mu\), every equilibrium outcome is within \(\varepsilon\) of \(F(\mu)\), while every recommendation in \(F(\mu)\) is approximated by some equilibrium outcome. A closer analogue of this paper would also require robustness to nearby information structures.

The paper’s nearest anchor is Theorem 1, proved here. It says that, under Assumption 1 and with at least three players, every compact-valued social choice rule \(F:\Theta\rightrightarrows Y\) is continuously virtually implementable in Bayesian Nash equilibrium and in interim correlated rationalizable strategies, with respect to the uniform-weak topology, by a bounded mechanism. Corollary 1, also proved here, adds that if \(F\) has finite range, such implementation is possible by a finite mechanism.

Neither result qualifies as the requested computational anchor: neither classifies a problem as tractable or hard, nor gives an algorithm or running-time bound. The paper’s “continuity” is continuity in players’ hierarchies of beliefs around a fixed, finite-player complete-information model. It does not make the population continuous. Its mechanism-existence results could motivate a mass-population implementation problem, but do not establish its answer.

The weakest point is substantial: replacing the paper’s finite strategic players with atomless residents changes the incentives. If the outcome depends only on the aggregate report distribution, one person’s deviation has zero effect on that distribution. The proposed mass-type question is therefore an extension that would need its own game model, information topology, and results; I would not claim these authors would accept it as the continuous form of Theorem 1. Under the stated anchor requirement, this paper has no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 establish mechanism existence: under their assumptions, every SCR is continuously virtually implementable, and a finite-range SCR has a finite implementing mechanism. They give no complexity classification, running-time bound, algorithm, or bound on the size of that finite mechanism. The paper’s continuity concerns players’ hierarchies of beliefs around a finite-player complete-information model. It does not make the society continuous.

The proposed \(\mu\)-model changes the object being studied. In the paper, a state is a joint profile \(\theta\in\prod_i\Theta_i\), and named players have utilities \(u_i(y,\theta_i)\) over a common lottery \(y\). Replacing that profile with a distribution over individual payoff types can discard which players have which types and how their types are jointly related. The paper offers no population-composition input or computational task that would resolve this change.

There is also a basic incentive problem for the proposed aggregate-report mechanism. If a resident is atomless and the outcome depends on the distribution of reports, one resident’s deviation does not change that distribution. The paper’s construction relies on each named player having a positive chance of being dictator or audited; those chances create strict incentives to report truthfully. In an atomless population, choosing one resident at random gives any particular resident probability zero. Treating each positive-mass type group as a single strategic reporter could restore influence, but it assumes coordinated blocs and changes the strategic players. Giving residents personalized allocations could create individual incentives too, but that changes the paper’s common-lottery outcome model.

A stronger proposed mirror would ask for an algorithm that synthesizes a bounded mechanism for an SCR on the population simplex, perhaps with robustness to nearby information structures. That may be a worthwhile new mechanism-design problem. But the theorem allows arbitrary SCRs and supplies no canonical representation for such a function, no encoding of candidate mechanisms, and no computational objective such as minimizing mechanism size. Those choices would define a new programme; they do not extract a computational question from this paper’s result.

So the negative case is strong against treating Theorem 1 or Corollary 1 as a continuous computational mirror. The broader claim that no related atomless mechanism-design problem could ever be worthwhile is weaker: a carefully specified new model might be. The paper itself, however, provides neither the computational anchor nor a population model that makes that extension a natural mirror.
