The proponent’s strongest point is also the strongest case against a mirror: this paper proves no named computational result. Theorem 4.4 and Corollary 4.5 are axiomatic characterizations; Theorem 5.1 gives an equilibrium construction for a two-player game; and Theorem 6.1 characterizes dynamics by their equilibrium properties. None states a complexity bound or gives an algorithm for an encoded input. “Continuous” in Theorem 4.4 refers to continuity in demand \(q\), and the games’ continuous time is not a continuous population.

The best rescue for Theorem 4.4 would be to group repeated users into cohorts and ask how to compute their Moulin–Shenker cost shares. But in this model, \(N\) indexes both agents and their personalized outputs. Each \(q_i\) and marginal cost \(D_i c\) belongs to its own coordinate, with cross-effects through \(c\). Grouping people with similar demands does not by itself preserve that structure. A cohort model would need to define shared or per-capita output, its effect on the technology, and how cohort cost shares translate back to members. That could be a sensible new model, but it would not mirror the theorem’s computational content, because there is none. And the paper permits arbitrary differentiable cost functions and utility functions without specifying a finite input representation, so it does not provide the basis for an exact, approximation, or parameterized complexity question.

The strongest rescue for Theorems 5.1 and 6.1 is a many-agent equilibrium problem with repeated types.
