The continuous mirror question

Given \(k\ge 3\) and positive rational type masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_k\) summing to \(1\), construct a game on atomless cohorts of those masses whose measurable coalitions win exactly when they include the full mass of at least \(k-1\) types; verify its axioms and that its Nakamura number is \(k\).

The model it lives in

There are \(k\) agent types with atomless masses \(\mu_j\). A coalition chooses mass \(x_j\in[0,\mu_j]\) from each type and wins iff \(x_j=\mu_j\) for at least \(k-1\) types. Construct the finite rule description and certify that the game is monotonic, proper, nonstrong, nonweak, and has Nakamura number \(k\).

The objection that survived

The task constructs a threshold rule from a desired \(k\), rather than computing an outcome on an input society, so it yields no broader complexity landscape. This limits the mirror to explicit game realization, but Proposition 11 itself is a uniform constructive existence result.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 11's realization of every \(k\ge 3\) by a monotonic, proper, nonstrong, nonweak game. It leaves the paper's full classification, computability results for general games, and other propositions unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror I can defend is narrow, and it exposes a gap in the paper for this programme: the supplied text has no numbered result asserting a complexity class such as \(P\), NP-hardness, or FPT. Its closest computational results concern Turing computability. For example, Proposition 3 gives a sufficient condition for a simple game to be \(\delta\)-computable; it is attributed to Kumabe and Mihara (2007), rather than presented as a complexity bound. So there is no qualifying complexity anchor here.

A useful structural anchor is Proposition 11, proved in the paper: for every \(k\ge3\), there is a finite, computable type-3 simple game—monotonic, proper, nonstrong, and nonweak—with Nakamura number \(\nu=k\). Its construction partitions a finite carrier into \(k\) blocks and declares a coalition winning when it contains all members of at least \(k-1\) blocks.

A continuous counterpart is the Atomless Type-3 Nakamura Realization problem. Given \(k\ge3\) and positive rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_k\) summing to one, construct a game on \(k\) voter types, with an atomless cohort of mass \(\mu_j\) for each type \(j\). A coalition selects measurable portions of those cohorts; write \(x_j\in[0,\mu_j]\) for the mass it selects from type \(j\). The proposed winning rule is: the coalition wins exactly when \(x_j=\mu_j\) for at least \(k-1\) types. A solution is this finite description of the rule, together with a verification of its axioms and Nakamura number.

The rule is monotonic. A winning coalition’s complement can contain all of at most one type, so for \(k\ge3\) the game is proper. It is nonstrong: split one cohort into two positive-mass parts, include \(k-2\) whole cohorts and one part in a coalition, and its complement also loses. The \(k\) coalitions that each omit a different whole cohort are winning and have empty intersection. Fewer than \(k\) winning coalitions must all contain at least one same whole cohort, so their intersection has positive mass. Thus \(\nu=k\).

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: each type is a large constituency whose members share the relevant preferences and act together, while the number of types is \(k\). The authors could recognize it as the continuous-population version of Proposition 11’s block game. If forced into the programme’s trichotomy, this *realization task* is tractable by direct construction. It mirrors only Proposition 11; it says nothing about the paper’s full classification or about the difficulty of computing Nakamura numbers for general games.

The weak point is decisive for the ChoCo brief: Proposition 11 is an existence and structural-classification result, not a complexity result. The continuous construction is also immediate once proposed. It gives a credible continuous analogue of one theorem in the paper, but it does not supply the requested continuous computational question anchored in a named complexity result. The paper’s recursion-theoretic computability results do not fill that gap: they ask whether a program can decide coalition membership, not how its running time scales with population mass, support size, or input length.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The Proposition 11 construction has a coherent atomless analogue. With type masses \(\mu_j>0\), let a coalition choose mass \(x_j\in[0,\mu_j]\) from each type and win when \(x_j=\mu_j\) for at least \(k-1\) types. The \(k\) coalitions that each omit a different type have empty intersection; any fewer winning coalitions share a type in full. So the resulting game has Nakamura number \(k\).

That does not give ChoCo a computational result to mirror. Proposition 11 proves that games with certain axioms can realize each \(k\); the proposed task takes \(k\) and the masses, prints the rule, and verifies a short intersection argument. Its running time measures the size of that construction, not the difficulty of a computational social choice problem on an input society. The paper has numbered results about \(\delta\)-computability, but those ask whether an algorithm can decide winning status from an index for a recursive coalition. They give no finite-input complexity landscape for bribery, control, robustness, or a comparable task.

A stronger candidate would take a finitely encoded continuous simple game as input and ask for its Nakamura number or whether every preference profile has a nonempty core. That would need a new representation of the winning rule over measurable coalitions. In the paper, the game is given through a decision procedure on programs describing coalitions of named players. A finite mass vector does not retain those coalition descriptions or their identity-sensitive membership patterns; the paper notes that computable games violate anonymity. Restricting the input to threshold formulas or another finite language could produce an interesting complexity problem, but its answers would depend on that new language and encoding. They would not mirror Proposition 11’s result.

The honest negative case is therefore strong within ChoCo’s stated scope: the one anchor is a structural existence theorem, and its atomless version supplies no computational question of the programme’s kind. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous theory could grow from this paper is weaker. Proposition 11’s block construction makes a structural continuum extension plausible; it simply does not establish a worthwhile computational mirror.
