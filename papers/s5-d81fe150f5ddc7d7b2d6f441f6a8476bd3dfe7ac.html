What the mirror covers

The proposed mirrors cover Theorem 2.1’s hardness boundary and Theorem 6.1’s profit approximation; they leave Theorem 4.1’s surplus algorithm and the queueing results outside the anchors. Both covered results already apply to the paper’s fluid models.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite cohorts sampled from a fixed distribution over decision-relevant types, what guarantees relate the cohort’s achievable outcomes to the paper’s fluid expected-flow guarantees as cohort size grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest affirmative case is a high-volume marketplace cohort: over a service period, many buyers and sellers arrive at a modest number of locations or feature classes, with only \(\tau\) distinct decision-relevant types among \(N\) agents, where \(N\gg\tau\). A type records everything the policy uses: side of the market, location, value or cost class, and, when relevant, deadline. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the cohort. This is plausible for repeated marketplace transactions or deadline classes; it would be much less convincing for a small, individually tailored market.

My lead is High-Multiplicity Two-Sided Facility Location. An instance has a finite metric over locations, rational type masses, a service radius \(R\), and a minimum facility flow \(\Lambda\). Choose prices and wages by location, open facilities, and route participating mass fractionally. A buyer type participates when its value meets the posted price; a seller type participates when its cost is covered by the wage. Every open facility must receive equal buyer and seller mass, with at least \(\Lambda\) of each, and total revenue must cover total wages. The objective is total buyer value minus seller cost. The requested solution is a policy meeting these conditions; a bicriteria solution may route mass as far as \(\alpha R\) and achieve at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/\gamma\).

The anchor is Theorem 2.1, proved in this paper. It states that finding an \((\alpha,\gamma)\)-approximation for \(\mathrm{Two\text{-}Sided\ Fac\text{-}Loc}(L,R)\) is NP-hard unless \(\alpha\ge 2\) or \(\gamma\ge L^c\) for some constant \(c>0\). The reduction is from maximum independent set in regular graphs: graph vertices become supply locations and edge midpoints become demand locations, so feasible open facilities encode an independent set. In the cohort mirror, I would ask the same bicriteria complexity question for rational masses over complete finite types. I expect hardness to transfer: the reduction’s combinatorics are in the metric and facility choices, not in the number of agents. In ChoCo’s terms, this looks like Class B. It covers the paper’s surplus maximization result and its hardness boundary, not every marketplace policy.

A second, separately plausible mirror is High-Multiplicity Envy-Free Facility Location. Here types also include a deadline class and its weight. For each location, buyer and seller subtypes have envy relations represented by a DAG. Choose facilities, route expected type mass, and offer lotteries over monotone price and wage ladders, with the facility choice made independently of which subtype an agent reports. Each facility must balance expected buyer and seller flow and receive total type weight at least \(\Lambda\). Maximize expected profit. A solution is a pricing and routing policy satisfying those conditions, with distance allowed up to \(4R\).

Its anchor, Theorem 6.1, is proved in the paper: there is a polynomial-time \((4,1)\)-approximation for \(\mathrm{Envy\text{-}Free\ FL}(L,R)\), meaning the policy earns optimal expected profit while relaxing the distance bound by a factor of four. I expect the finite-support cohort version to be tractable as well, through its LP and rounding structure: the input types and price-ladder constraints are explicit, and the algorithm’s key facility operations act on their aggregate flows. This is a Class A question. It mirrors the paper’s envy-free profit result, not its full dynamic control problem. Further questions include whether the fourfold distance relaxation can be improved, and how closely a finite cohort implements the expected-flow guarantee as \(N\) grows.

The authors would recognize both models as their problem: the paper explicitly uses fluid demand and supply at locations, and it introduces deadline subtypes, weights, and expected flows in the envy-free variant. Its dynamic marketplace motivation, with atomic Poisson arrivals, makes the cohort interpretation especially natural. The paper’s queueing analysis supports the link between individual arrivals and aggregate flow, though that analysis is not itself a computational-complexity anchor.

My weakest point is also fundamental: the static problems in these anchors are already population-continuous. The paper does not merely suggest a fluid model; it computes on one. So these are strong mirrors in the sense of sensible, recognizable continuous questions, but poor candidates for claiming a new continuization gap. The positive case is that this paper gives unusually clear evidence that high-multiplicity marketplace questions are meaningful and computationally rich. A genuinely new ChoCo contribution would need to formalize the finite-agent counterpart and establish the dictionary to these fluid results, rather than present the fluid problems themselves as newly discovered mirrors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that both anchors are already computational results about continuous populations. In Section 2, the authors explicitly make buyers and sellers fluid: locations generate demand and supply volumes from value and cost distributions, and the platform routes fractional flows. Theorem 2.1’s hardness result—and the approximation algorithm in Theorem 4.1—apply to that model. Normalizing those volumes as type masses and rescaling the facility threshold gives the proposed cohort formulation. The best version of this mirror therefore asks the same bicriteria question the paper already answers. A finite cohort might motivate new bounds on rounding or finite-size error, but those would be questions about recovering a finite instance from the fluid solution, not missing complexity results for continuous societies.

The same collision is even clearer for the second anchor. Section 6 formulates expected subtype flows, lottery pricing, facility balance, and a weight lower bound; Theorem 6.1 gives the polynomial-time \((4,1)\)-approximation. Treating location, deadline, value, and cost classes as types gives the proposed high-multiplicity story, but the relevant aggregate quantities and computational result are already there. Adding finite-cohort randomness or asking about the full dynamic control problem could lead to worthwhile research. That would change the object of study, though: the paper’s full dynamic problem is not the continuous static facility-location problem in Theorem 6.1.

The plausible salvage is the finite-agent-to-fluid dictionary. That could be valuable as a separate bridge result, and Section 5 already supplies queueing arguments connecting atomic Poisson arrivals to flow conditions. But it does not rescue either anchor as a new continuous computational question: their continuous sides are the paper’s own models and theorems. The negative case is strongest for these two proposed mirrors; it cannot rule out every substantially different marketplace problem one might formulate from the paper.
