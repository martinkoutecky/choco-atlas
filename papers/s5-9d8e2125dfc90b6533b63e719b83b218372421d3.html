The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a strong, properly anchored case for a continuous mirror here. The paper’s only numbered theorem is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: if a scoring rule gives the population a strict social order, then the social order in i.i.d. samples converges in probability to that population order as sample size grows. That is a statistical consistency result, not a named result about computational complexity. The paper has no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing that a computational problem is tractable or hard, so it supplies no valid anchor under the requested standard.

The most plausible computational question would be: given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(m\) candidates, determine whether Condorcet, Borda, and plurality select the same winner. A type would be a ranking, its mass the population fraction with that ranking, and the output the agreement decision. In a regime such as a large electorate with five candidates and relatively few distinct rankings, this is a sensible high-multiplicity input: the mass representation replaces voter-by-voter counts. The question is directly relevant to the paper’s comparison of voting rules, but computing these winners from \(\mu\) is straightforward. More importantly, the paper already models cultures as population distributions over preference types and studies population social orders. That proposed mirror would largely restate its model, rather than continuize a discrete problem.

Theorem 4.1 does not rescue the case: it already reasons about that continuous population distribution and its samples. Its convergence claim is proved using the weak law of large numbers; it does not identify a computational problem whose complexity a continuous mirror could chart.

So the strongest honest positive case is limited to a computational extension of the paper’s agreement analyses. It is not an anchored continuous mirror of a named computational result in the paper. The weakness is decisive: the paper’s population is already continuous, and the required complexity anchors are absent.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.1 is a consistency theorem: when a culture’s scoring order has no ties, the order in an i.i.d. sample converges in probability to it. The culture is already a distribution over preference types. The theorem specifies no computational problem or resource measure. The paper’s other results are statistical analyses, simulations, and proposals for further empirical work.

The proposed question—whether Condorcet, Borda, and plurality choose the same winner for a given rational distribution \(\mu\)—does not mirror Theorem 4.1 or the paper’s sample-agreement analysis. It replaces the latter’s probabilities over samples with a deterministic comparison of population winners. That may be a useful query, but it is a new problem posed on a population model the paper already uses.

A stronger candidate would preserve the paper’s focus: given \(\mu\) and sample size \(n\), compute or approximate the probability that the rules agree on a sample winner, including ties. But the paper already estimates precisely this kind of probability by bootstrapping from fitted cultures. Studying its complexity could be worthwhile as a computational extension; it would not continuize a discrete problem from this paper. The population distribution is already the input, and no intervention or other discrete computational task is being relaxed.

So the negative case is strong against calling any of these a *continuous mirror* of a result in this paper. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile research could grow out of the paper: a complexity study of sample-agreement probabilities is plausible. Its weakness is that such a study would be adjacent new work, not an anchored mirror.
