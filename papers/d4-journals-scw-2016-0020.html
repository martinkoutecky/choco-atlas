The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here: a large school system with many pupils but relatively few complete pupil profiles. A type could be a pupil’s profile of initial achievement and background characteristics; mass would be each type’s share of a school’s cohort. That makes pupil composition a natural continuous object.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a mirror to. Proposition 1 proves an axiomatic incompatibility between rewarding administration and eliminating selection incentives for arbitrary production functions. Proposition 2 characterizes when both principles can hold through a separability condition. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem, and the paper’s empirical tests and simulations do not supply such a result either.

One could pose a new computational problem about designing subsidy schemes from school-level type distributions, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper. The paper’s own questions concern axioms and incentive trade-offs; continuizing those would be axiomatic continuization, outside ChoCo’s stated scope. So the population makes a continuous model conceivable, but there is no honest anchor-specific continuous problem to offer under the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only positive foothold is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large school system may have many pupils but relatively few relevant profiles. I would concede that. But it supplies a possible continuous population, not a computational result from this paper to mirror.

The paper’s two named propositions are axiomatic. Proposition 1 proves incompatibility between two subsidy principles for arbitrary production functions; Proposition 2 characterizes exactly when both principles can be satisfied. Neither poses a computational problem or studies its complexity. The empirical section estimates production functions, tests separability, and compares compromise schemes, but does not define an algorithmic policy-design problem. In particular, the paper explicitly leaves a complete ordering of schemes for future work.

The strongest proposed mirror would give each school a distribution over pupil types and ask an algorithm to choose subsidies that balance rewards for administration against selection incentives. But to make that an optimization problem, someone must specify how to value the trade-off. That is the normative question the authors leave open; different added objectives could produce different problems and answers. Alternatively, one could ask for mass transfers of pupils between schools, but the paper models no such feasible transfers, costs, or capacity constraints. It treats school behavior through a fixed reduced-form production function. Those additions would create a new allocation or policy-design problem, rather than continuize a result in this paper.

So the population-continuum setting is sensible, but the paper offers no computational anchor for it. A computational extension might be worthwhile on its own; its value would depend on new modeling choices the paper does not make. The negative case is strongest on that distinction, and weakest if one is willing to treat a newly specified policy-design problem as a mirror despite the missing anchor.
