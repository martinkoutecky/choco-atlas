There is no eligible computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are allocation formulas and mathematical properties, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. For example, Proposition 3, proved here, gives the universal consistent share of each of \(n\) sequentially positioned individuals:
\[ s_i=\frac{(s/(1-s))^{n-i}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}(s/(1-s))^{k-i}}, \]
where \(s\) is the two-person universal share from equation (1). Corollary 1 supplies a closed form for \(s\) under the binomial/Poisson assumptions. Neither result analyzes the complexity of a computational problem.

The nearest positive lead would be a high-multiplicity version of the sequential sharing rule, with types describing position and behavioral parameters, and mass representing the fraction of a large population in each type. But the paper does not say how to extend its rule when many agents share a position, nor pose an optimization or decision problem over such masses. Defining that version would require adding choices the paper does not settle.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 and Corollary 1 give allocation formulas; Proposition 3 extends the formula by consistency; the remaining results establish properties of that rule. None defines a computational problem or classifies its complexity. By the programme’s own criteria, that is decisive: there is no anchor to classify as tractable or hard.

The best rescue is to ask for the rule given a distribution of people over sequential positions and behavioural parameters, perhaps \((\alpha,\lambda)\). But the paper already reduces its stated model to an aggregate share \(s\), then gives the sequential shares by a closed formula. With the binomial–Poisson assumptions, evaluating it is direct; with finite rational support, it is a sum over the listed types. If the challenge instead comes from encoding an arbitrary density, estimating behavioural parameters, or adding an acceptance constraint, the complexity comes from that newly added problem, not from a computational question in this paper. The paper’s distributions over proposals and valuations are part of an analytical model, not a complexity result.

The sequential structure also makes a high-multiplicity version difficult to ground in the rule. Each rank affects an individual’s share, so rank is part of the complete type: with \(n\) positions, the support can grow with \(n\). Collapsing many people into a shared position would require a new rule for how they share that position’s allocation. And under the natural scaling of ranks to \([0,1]\), the rule’s generic limit concentrates the resource at the top. Writing \(q=s/(1-s)>1\), the shares are \(s_i^{(n)}=q^{n-i}/\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}q^j\); the allocation measure \(\sum_i s_i^{(n)}\delta_{i/n}\) converges to \(\delta_0\). The equal-share case \(q=1\) avoids that collapse, but it is a boundary case, not the generic behavioural rule.

So the proponent’s nearest lead—Proposition 3—does not survive as a computational mirror. It may inspire a new study of group sharing or continuum limits, but that would be a different question. This is a strong negative about the paper as a source for ChoCo; it cannot prove that no adjacent computational project could ever be worthwhile.
