The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(C\), and a proposed zone \(Z\subseteq C\), decide whether every candidate set \(K\subseteq C\) with \(K\cap Z\ne\emptyset\) has its fractional-mass IRV winner in \(Z\). In each round, each type's mass supports its highest-ranked remaining candidate, and the paper's fixed deterministic tie-breaking resolves elimination ties.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types \(t\in T\subseteq\mathcal{L}(C)\), with rational masses summing to \(1\). IRV totals type mass at each round and transfers it according to the rankings; the decision variable is the candidate set \(K\), and the question tests whether its winner lies in the proposed zone \(Z\).

The objection that survived

The hardness reductions use arbitrary preference profiles and do not show hardness for rankings induced by shortest-path distances; this limits the mirror to the paper's profile-based problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers profile-based exclusion verification and minimum-zone computation under rational distributions over ranking types. It does not establish a continuous analogue of the tree dynamic program or settle the graph-distance-restricted problem.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper's hardness reductions be realized by rankings induced by shortest-path distances on graphs?
  • How does the rational-mass version of the tree dynamic program behave when its vote totals are fractional?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is the paper’s profile-based general-graph hardness, not its tree algorithm. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of the candidate locations: each ranking type \(t\) has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and the input lists only the \(\tau\) types with positive mass. In a large election or organizational decision with many repeated preference orders, the number of people can be far larger than \(\tau\). IRV counts mass in each round, transfers it according to the rankings, and uses the paper’s fixed deterministic tie-breaking.

My lead question is Continuous Profile Exclusion. Given the candidate universe \(C\), the paper’s graph and location data, a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings of \(C\), and a proposed zone \(Z\), decide whether every candidate set \(K\subseteq C\) with \(K\cap Z\ne\emptyset\) has its fractional-mass IRV winner in \(Z\). A solution is yes or no; a no instance has a candidate set \(K\) witnessing an outside winner. This is precisely the exclusion question in the paper’s profile formulation, with the society represented by masses over ballot types. I expect Class B hardness: an ordinary finite profile with \(n_t\) voters of each type is represented by \(\mu_t=n_t/\sum_j n_j\), and scaling every round’s scores by the same positive denominator preserves all eliminations and ties.

The anchor is Theorem 5, proved here: for any deterministic rank-based elimination rule satisfying SFE, \(\mathcal R\)-Exclusion is co-NP-complete. The paper’s Proposition 1, proved here, says deterministic rank-based elimination rules satisfy SFE, so the theorem applies to IRV. Its proof adapts the cited RX3C construction; the reduction idea is not presented as originating in this paper.

A second, related question is Continuous Minimum Profile Exclusion. Given the same kind of candidate and weighted-profile instance, output a smallest-cardinality exclusion zone \(Z\subseteq C\), meaning one for which every candidate set meeting \(Z\) has its IRV winner in \(Z\). The solution is such a zone. I expect Class B hardness here too. Its anchor is Theorem 6, proved here: for every fixed deterministic rank-based elimination rule satisfying SFE, computing a minimum-cardinality exclusion zone is NP-hard. As with Theorem 5, the paper adapts an RX3C-based construction whose antecedent is cited.

The common high-multiplicity regime is concrete: many voters share one of a modest number of full rankings, with every voter of a type treated identically by the election. The mass model preserves exactly the information the profile-based problem uses. This case covers the paper’s profile-based general-graph hardness results; it does not claim that their hardness reduction is restricted to rankings induced by graph distance.

That restriction is my weakest point. The paper’s central graph-voting model ties rankings to shortest-path distances, while its Appendix F.2 formulates the hardness problem using a preference profile. The weighted-profile mirror is a strong match for the latter and for Theorems 5 and 6 as stated, but those theorems alone do not establish the same complexity for a continuous model restricted to graph-induced rankings. The tree results, including Theorem 4, are outside this case: their dynamic program tracks integer vote totals, so its polynomial bound does not automatically extend to arbitrary rational masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorems 5 and 6 do not clearly establish hardness for the paper’s graph-induced voting model. In the main model, vertices are both voter locations and candidate sites, and rankings come from shortest-path distances. The RX3C construction in Appendix F instead uses arbitrary preference profiles. Its problem definition also takes \(Z\subseteq V\) while giving the profile over \(C\), without clearly identifying the two sets. No reduction is shown to realize those profiles as graph-distance rankings. So Continuous Profile Exclusion and Continuous Minimum Profile Exclusion are coherent questions about weighted ordinal profiles, but the cited theorems do not establish their complexity for the paper’s spatial graph problem. Proposition 1 does not close that gap.

That objection applies to both proposed anchors: Theorem 5’s co-NP-hardness and Theorem 6’s NP-hardness. Their reductions may transfer to rational ballot masses by scaling scores, but that supports the unrestricted profile variant. It does not show that the same hardness survives when rankings must come from graph distances. The proponent appropriately identifies this limitation.

A stronger spatial mirror remains available, though. Fix a tree or graph and its candidate sites, and let \(\mu_v\) be the rational fraction of residents at each vertex \(v\); residents at \(v\) rank sites by the paper’s distance and tie-breaking rule. Then ask whether a proposed zone is valid, or compute a minimum zone. This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: many residents can share a location and hence a type, while the candidate geography stays fixed. The tree algorithm tracks integer vote totals; with rational masses, its state space and exact running time become genuine questions in \((n,\tau,L)\). That seems worth studying, even though the paper’s hardness reduction does not answer it.

So the profile anchors are weaker than the proponent’s case suggests, but they do not support the broader claim that no continuous mirror is worthwhile. The honest negative case is weak at that point: the weighted tree version is a clear, computationally meaningful mirror, and the paper’s results leave its complexity open.
