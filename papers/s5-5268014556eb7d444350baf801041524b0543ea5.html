The continuous mirror question

Given finite resident types \(T\), apartment classes \(O\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational supplies \(q_o\), and valuations \(v_t(o)\), choose a transport \(x_{t,o}\ge 0\) with row sums \(\mu_t\) and column sums \(q_o\) that minimizes the supremum average envy weight over finite directed cycles of distinct residents. Each unit of mass receives one indivisible apartment; \(x_{t,o}\) records the share of type \(t\) assigned to class \(o\), and an arc from resident \(i\) to resident \(j\) has weight \(v_{\theta(i)}(a(j))-v_{\theta(i)}(a(i))\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of residents grouped by complete valuation type, with apartment classes supplied in proportions \(q_o\). The decision variable is the type-to-class mass transport \(x\); the objective is the maximum average envy weight over finite cycles among the individual residents represented by its positive-mass cells. The minimizing transports are maximum-value transports, computable by a rational transportation LP.

The objection that survived

The worst-cycle value depends on whether a type-apartment cell has positive mass, not on its size, so a rare cohort can determine the objective; this limits its interpretation but does not invalidate the mirror.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is the paper’s clean-slate result, not its open question about arbitrary preexisting envy.

Lead anchor — Corollary 3.3, proved in this note: given the agents’ valuations, one can find in polynomial time an allocation \(A\) minimizing \(\operatorname{MACW}(G_A)\), the maximum average weight of a directed cycle in the resulting envy graph. The proof uses Propositions 3.1 and 3.2: an allocation has \(\operatorname{MACW}(G_A)\le 0\) exactly when it maximizes total value, and the allocations minimizing \(\operatorname{MACW}(G_A)\) are exactly the maximum-value allocations. The authors say these background results are probably already known, but prove them here and use them to derive the corollary.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large housing project with many residents but a small menu of standardized apartment types. Residents who value every apartment type identically share a type; the type records their complete valuation vector. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of residents of type \(t\). Let \(q_o\) be the fraction of apartments of type \(o\). Both sum to one. In this clean-slate version, there is no preexisting envy graph: \(G_O=0\), the case the paper identifies as tractable. The regime of interest has many more residents than resident or apartment types. Apartment types are interchangeable within a class, and each resident still receives one whole apartment.

Here is the continuous problem. Its input is finite sets of resident types \(T\) and apartment types \(O\); rational masses \(\mu_t\) and supplies \(q_o\); and positive rational valuations \(v_t(o)\), all encoded in binary. A feasible solution is a nonnegative matrix \(x=(x_{t,o})\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{o\in O}x_{t,o}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}x_{t,o}=q_o. \]
Each \(x_{t,o}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) residents receiving apartment type \(o\). It describes a measurable assignment in which every individual gets one apartment; the fractional entries represent population shares, not fractional apartments.

For any such assignment, consider its continuum envy graph: its vertices are individual residents, and the arc from resident \(i\) to resident \(j\) has weight \(v_{\theta(i)}(a(j))-v_{\theta(i)}(a(i))\), where \(\theta(i)\) is \(i\)’s type and \(a(i)\) their apartment type. Let \(M(x)\) be the supremum of average weights over finite directed cycles of distinct residents. The problem is to find a feasible \(x\) minimizing \(M(x)\); a solution consists of the matrix \(x\), or any measurable assignment realizing it.

This is a genuine population mirror of Corollary 3.3: it asks for the same envy-cycle objective when society and apartment supply are represented by type masses. I expect it to be tractable. In fact, the paper’s exchange-cycle argument extends: a total-value-maximizing transport has no positive envy cycle, while if a transport is not maximum-value, an improving alternative can be decomposed into exchanges, at least one of which yields a positive envy cycle. In the continuum, any positive-mass type–apartment cell contains enough residents to form a zero-weight two-cycle with another resident in that cell. Thus the minimum cycle value is \(0\), and the minimizers are exactly the maximum-value transports. A maximum-value transportation problem is a linear program with \(|T||O|\) variables, solvable in time polynomial in the support sizes and input bit length.

The continuous optimum being \(0\), even when a finite optimum is negative, is a real change in objective value: repeated residents of the same type receiving the same apartment type create zero-envy cycles. The optimizing allocations nevertheless coincide with the maximum-value transports, just as the paper’s result says in the finite setting.

The scope is deliberately narrow. It covers the paper’s zero-baseline case and does not settle Open Question 4.1, which permits an arbitrary nonzero \(G_O\). A natural further question is the type-homogeneous baseline version: given a matrix \(g_{t,s}\) of baseline envy from type \(t\) toward type \(s\), minimize the maximum average cycle weight where an arc from a resident in cell \((t,o)\) to one in cell \((s,p)\) has weight
\[ v_t(p)-v_t(o)-g_{t,s}. \]
This is a restricted continuous analogue of the open question: arbitrary individual baseline graphs need not admit such a type-level description. Its complexity would need separate study.

My weakest point is that this anchor is already polynomial-time solvable discretely, and the continuous version reduces directly to optimal transport. It therefore offers a credible, author-recognizable mirror, but little evidence that continuization dissolves hardness or resolves the paper’s central open problem. The case rests on the clean-slate result only.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that the maximum-cycle objective degenerates in a non-atomic population. In the clean-slate model, represent residents by types \(t\), apartments by classes \(o\), and an assignment by masses \(x_{t,o}\). Every positive-mass cell contains distinct residents with the same type and apartment class. The envy arc in either direction between two such residents has weight \(v_t(o)-v_t(o)=0\), so the continuum maximum average cycle weight is always at least \(0\).

That floor determines the entire clean-slate optimization. If \(x\) maximizes total value, every envy cycle has weight at most \(0\), by the paper’s exchange-cycle argument; hence its maximum average cycle weight is \(0\). If \(x\) is not value-maximizing, an improving cycle in the transportation problem gives a positive envy cycle among residents in the corresponding positive-mass cells. Thus the minimizers are exactly the maximum-value transports, and the minimum is \(0\). Corollary 3.3’s mirror reduces directly to the transportation LP. More fundamentally, the cycle objective depends on which cells have positive mass, not how much mass they contain: an arbitrarily small positive cell can witness a cycle just as a large one can. A mass-weighted or typical-cycle criterion might retain prevalence, but it would change the paper’s worst-cycle objective.

This is a real continuum degeneration, not a complaint that the answer is easy: the finite problem’s negative optimum can disappear, and the cycle statistic stops distinguishing among efficient allocations. The type-and-apartment scenario itself is sensible, so I cannot reject it on high-multiplicity grounds. The objection is that this particular result contributes little computational content beyond finding a maximum-value transport.

That argument does not dispose of the stronger baseline question. A large housing project with cohorts sharing valuation vectors and old-apartment classes could yield a plausible type-level baseline \(g_{t,s}\). Its cycle objective may remain nontrivial, and the paper’s Open Question 4.1 gives it a clear computational target. I cannot honestly rule out that mirror as artificial, already solved, or out of scope. So the negative case is limited: the clean-slate Corollary 3.3 mirror is weak, but the paper’s structured-baseline problem survives, and I do not have a convincing case against every worthwhile continuous mirror.
