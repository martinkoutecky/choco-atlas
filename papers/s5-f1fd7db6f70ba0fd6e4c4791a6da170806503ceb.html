The continuous mirror question

Given hospitals \(H\), applicant types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, per-capita capacities \(\kappa_h\), utilities \(v_{th}\), type rankings \(\succ_t\), and hospital priorities \(\succ_h\) over types, choose feasible assignment masses \(x_{th}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_h x_{th}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{th}\le\kappa_h\). For each type–hospital pair define \(D_{th}(x)=\mu_t-\sum_{j\succeq_t h}x_{tj}\) and \(L_{th}(x)=\sum_{s:\,t\succ_h s}x_{sh}\), and let \(B(x)=\sum_{t,h}D_{th}(x)\mathbf{1}[\sum_s x_{sh}<\kappa_h\text{ or }L_{th}(x)>0]\). Given a rational allowance \(\varepsilon\), compute an assignment maximizing \(\sum_{t,h}v_{th}x_{th}\) subject to \(B(x)\le\varepsilon\), or report infeasibility; varying \(\varepsilon\) yields the utility–blocking frontier.

The model it lives in

Use interchangeable applicant types with mass \(\mu_t\), tied copies within a type, strict hospital priority across types, and capacities scaled per capita. Assignment variables \(x_{th}\) allocate type mass; the objective maximizes total utility under a bound on normalized blocking-pair count, with vacancy or any lower-priority mass activating blocks.

The objection that survived

A vanishingly small positive mass of lower-priority assignees can trigger a nonvanishing blocking mass, so the objective is discontinuous as type masses approach zero; this bounds the model’s limit behavior but does not undo its exact correspondence on finite replicated cohorts.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, which the authors prove here while crediting Baïou and Balinski [3] for the characterization. It says: for any feasible binary matching \(x\), \(x\) is stable if and only if, for every resident \(r\) and hospital \(h\),
\[ q_h x_{rh}+q_h\sum_{j\succ_r h}x_{rj}+\sum_{i\succ_h r}x_{ih}\ge q_h. \]
The authors use this characterization to encode blocking pairs in their utility-versus-stability optimization model. The paper gives no named theorem classifying an optimization problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard; Theorem 1 is a structural anchor used in computation, not a complexity result.

A plausible mirror is a regional placement market with a large annual cohort of applicants and a fixed set of hospitals. Applicants fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of complete profile types, each sharing the same hospital preferences, utility scores, and hospital-priority tier. The applicant count \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\); hospital capacities scale with \(N\). This is credible where applications and priorities are organized around recurring specialties, training backgrounds, or other standardized cohorts. It is a scenario, not a claim that every resident-matching market has this structure.

I would call the problem the Continuous Utility–Blocking Frontier. An instance has hospitals \(H\), applicant types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, per-capita capacities \(\kappa_h\), utilities \(v_{th}\), and rankings \(\succ_t\) and \(\succ_h\) over hospitals and types. The utilities can be the paper’s combined resident-and-hospital utility. A solution is a mass assignment \(x_{th}\ge0\), with \(\sum_h x_{th}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{th}\le\kappa_h\).

For each type–hospital pair, let
\[ D_{th}(x)=\mu_t-\sum_{j\succeq_t h}x_{tj} \quad\text{and}\quad L_{th}(x)=\sum_{s:\,t\succ_h s}x_{sh}. \]
Here \(D_{th}\) is the mass of type \(t\) that is unmatched or assigned somewhere it likes less than \(h\); \(L_{th}\) is the mass at \(h\) that \(h\) ranks below \(t\). Define the blocking mass
\[ B(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{h\in H} D_{th}(x)\, \mathbf{1}\!\left[\sum_s x_{sh}<\kappa_h\ \text{or}\ L_{th}(x)>0\right]. \]
Thus every member of a type who would prefer \(h\) contributes to the blocking mass when \(h\) has room or would accept that type over some current assignee. For a finite cohort of these types, \(B(x)\) is the paper’s number of blocking pairs divided by \(N\). Given a blocking-mass allowance \(\varepsilon\), the problem is to maximize \(\sum_{t,h}v_{th}x_{th}\) subject to feasibility and \(B(x)\le\varepsilon\), returning an optimal assignment and value, or certifying infeasibility. Varying \(\varepsilon\) gives the utility–stability frontier; \(\varepsilon=0\) is the utility-maximizing stable-matching case.

I expect the exact positive-\(\varepsilon\) frontier to be continuum-specifically hard, as a conjecture rather than a conclusion from this paper. Fractional assignments remove the need to choose among individual copies, but the penalty still depends on whether any lower-priority mass is present at a hospital. Those activation decisions remain across type–hospital pairs. The \(\varepsilon=0\) case may be tractable through a continuous stable-admissions formulation; establishing that, and locating the boundary as \(\varepsilon\) grows, would be useful follow-up questions.

This mirrors the paper’s bi-objective utility/blocking-pair problem in Section 2.2 and the stability characterization in Theorem 1. I would not stretch it to the matching-in-groups model: its predetermined groups and their small-subgroup penalty depend on identity links that do not carry over naturally to a population of interchangeable type mass.

The weak point is hospital priority. The paper assumes strict rankings among named residents, while a high-multiplicity type makes its members indistinguishable and therefore tied in hospital priority. The mirror needs strict priority across types, with indifference among copies of the same type. If meaningful priorities are genuinely individual-specific, the type count can grow with \(N\) and the continuous regime loses its appeal. That is a real restriction, but in markets with cohort-based priorities it still leaves a question the authors could recognize: the same utility-versus-blocking objective, now for a large society represented by type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is not a computational result. It characterizes stable matchings by inequalities; it proves no complexity bound or algorithmic guarantee. The paper’s computational contribution is to formulate mixed integer models and compare their outputs in simulations. So the proposed Continuous Utility–Blocking Frontier is a reasonable new optimization problem, but it does not mirror a complexity result the paper established.

There is also a problem with the proposed blocking mass \(B\) as a high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s blocking-pair count. Suppose a hospital is full, with one resident of a lower-priority type among \(N\) assignees, while \(\Theta(N)\) higher-priority residents would prefer that hospital to their assignments. Each of those residents forms a blocking pair with the one lower-priority resident, so the normalized count can remain bounded away from zero. Yet that lower-priority type has mass \(1/N\), which tends to zero; at zero mass, the hospital may have no blocking pairs. Thus \(B\) jumps when an arbitrarily small type disappears. It preserves the finite count, but it does not behave robustly as an aggregate measure.

A more natural continuum quantity might count how much mass could actually block: for each type–hospital pair, cap dissatisfied mass by the hospital’s vacancy plus the lower-priority mass it could replace. That avoids the one-resident trigger. But it changes the paper’s objective from counting blocking pairs to measuring feasible blocking mass. It may be a sensible question the authors would recognize; it is not the direct continuous counterpart the proponent claims, and it has no complexity result in this paper to mirror.

That leaves a real weakness in the negative case. Large applicant cohorts with shared preference profiles and priority tiers are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The paper’s utility-versus-stability trade-off can be expressed over such type masses, and the authors might regard a mass-based blocking objective as a natural extension. The objections establish that the chosen anchor is structural rather than computational, and that its proposed objective has a poor limit behavior. They do not establish that no worthwhile continuous optimization question exists in any scenario.
