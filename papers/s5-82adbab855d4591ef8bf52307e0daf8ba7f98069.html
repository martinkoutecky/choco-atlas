The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive opening is that the paper explicitly leaves frequency differences for future research. A mass-weighted version is therefore a natural extension of its model: take \(n\) binary attributes, a finite support \(S\subseteq\{0,1\}^n\), rational masses \(\mu_x\) summing to one, and a shared initial attitude \(a_{x,0}\) for each type \(x\). Define each attribute’s salience from the mass-weighted mean attitudes of people who possess and lack it. Then let an agent of type \(x\) give type \(y\) weight proportional to
\[ \mu_y\sum_{i:x_i=y_i}\Delta_t[i], \]
normalized across \(y\), and update by averaging those attitudes. With equal mass on every type in \(\{0,1\}^n\), this recovers the paper’s transition rule. With many agents but small support \(\tau=|S|\), it is a credible high-multiplicity regime. A precise question would be: given this rational instance and a promise that the process converges, approximate the limiting difference in mean attitudes between the two groups for the initially most salient attribute. The authors could recognize this as the frequency-heterogeneous version of their dynamics that they set aside.

The natural anchor for that direction is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says eventual attitudes always exist; disagreement occurs exactly when attribute \(1\) is initially the unique most salient attribute, \(\Delta_0[1]>\Delta_0[2]\). In that case every type \(A\) has \(\lvert a^A_\infty\rvert=\frac12\tau(W^\infty)\Delta_0[1]\), with \(\tau(W^\infty)\in(0,1]\), and its eventual attitude is positive exactly when \(1\in A\). This is a strong analytical result to motivate the extension, but it is not a computational-complexity result of the kind required for an anchor.

That distinction is the weak point, and it is decisive under the requested standard: the paper has no numbered result asserting that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar, and it presents no optimization or decision encoding that it computes with. Theorem 2 and the propositions also characterize dynamics, segregation, and convergence speed; they do not classify computation. Moreover, Theorem 1’s proof uses the paper’s equal-weight, one-representative-per-type structure, so its salience criterion cannot simply be carried over to arbitrary masses. I can make a concrete case for a future mass-weighted problem, but I cannot honestly give it a valid named computational anchor or predict its complexity class from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is a theorem about the limit of an attitude process: it characterizes when disagreement persists and what the limiting attitudes look like. The other numbered results concern segregation and convergence speed. None defines a computational problem or classifies its complexity. Theorem 1 is therefore a sound analytical anchor for studying the model, but it is not a named computational result of the kind this programme asks us to continuize.

The proposed mass-weighted extension is coherent and close to the authors’ stated interests. The paper explicitly leaves unequal frequencies of types for future research, so grouping many otherwise identical people by type is not a strained scenario. I would not reject it on grounds of identity, multiplicity, or a vacuous continuum. But the extension changes the dynamics: unequal masses change the group averages and the normalization of each type’s attention weights. The symmetry behind Theorem 1’s spectral argument is then lost. Its condition \(\Delta_0[1]>\Delta_0[2]\) does not establish what happens in the weighted model, much less the complexity of computing it.

One could ask whether, given rational masses and initial attitudes, the limiting difference in mean attitudes can be approximated. That is a well-defined computational question, but it adds the output, precision requirement, and convergence promise; none is a computational problem formulated or analyzed in this paper. A stronger alternative would ask whether disagreement persists in the weighted model, or how many steps it takes to get within \(\varepsilon\) of the limit, drawing on Theorem 1 or Proposition 2. Those questions might be worthwhile. Their difficulty would come from analyzing a new weighted nonlinear dynamic, not from continuizing a computational result in the paper.

Turning that work into a computational social choice problem would likely require adding a collective-choice rule or an intervention, such as a costed campaign to change opinions or attention. The paper supplies no such problem, so the resulting algorithmic question would be a new construction rather than a mirror of one of its results. The analytic work on opinion dynamics mentioned in the paper is not a substitute for computational complexity theory, but neither does this paper itself provide that missing complexity question.

So the best negative case is narrow: the mass-based model is plausible, but this paper does not give us a computational result to anchor it to, and the proponent’s proposed limit-computation task must supply its own computational target. That weakens the case for choosing this paper as a ChoCo mirror; it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror can be built.
