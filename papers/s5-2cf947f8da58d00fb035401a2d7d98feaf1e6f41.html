The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give this paper a qualifying computational anchor. Its named results concern cooperative-game structure and profit allocation; none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. In particular, Theorem 6.1, proved in the paper, says that the unique RS-game solution satisfying efficiency, retailer stability, retailer reduction, and preservation of differences is the mgpc solution: \(\xi_0=n\beta\), \(\xi_i=v(\{0,i\})-\beta\), where \(\beta=\min_{\emptyset\ne S\subseteq N}(v(S_0)-v(S))/|S|\). Theorem 5.2, also proved here, says every RS-game is balanced and gives a core allocation. These are substantive results, but neither analyzes computational complexity; the paper also gives no finite encoding of its arbitrary continuous price functions or running-time claim.

There is a plausible population mirror behind Theorem 6.1. Imagine a large chain of small retailers supplied by one wholesaler, with many retailers sharing the same local demand profile. Let a type \(t\) specify a retailer’s complete demand function and other relevant parameters, and let \(\mu_t\) be its population fraction. A coalition is a mass vector \(\alpha\) with \(0\le\alpha_t\le\mu_t\); its size is \(|\alpha|=\sum_t\alpha_t\). Let \(q_t\) be the order per retailer of type \(t\) in that coalition, and let \(Q=\sum_t\alpha_tq_t\) be its order per unit of population. The wholesale price \(w(Q)\) is then based on per-capita order volume, a scale adjustment of the kind the programme permits.

Write \(V_-(\alpha)\) for the coalition’s maximum profit without the supplier, optimizing its type-level orders under the common wholesale price. Write \(r_t=\max_q (p_t(q)-c)q\), so that \(V_+(\alpha)=\sum_t\alpha_t r_t\) is its value when it cooperates with the supplier. The natural continuous counterpart of the paper’s mgpc quantity is \(\beta_\infty=\inf_{\alpha\ne0}(V_+(\alpha)-V_-(\alpha))/|\alpha|\). A concrete new problem would take rational type masses and bounded rational piecewise-linear demand and wholesale-price functions as input, and ask for \(\beta_\infty\) and a stable allocation. With total population normalized to one, the proposed allocation gives the supplier \(\beta_\infty\) and each retailer of type \(t\) the per-retailer share \(r_t-\beta_\infty\). The defining infimum ensures stability against retailer-only subpopulations; the supplier’s share also supports the supplier-containing coalition constraints.

This is a credible high-multiplicity scenario: a large franchise or retail network can have many locations but relatively few demand and cost profiles. The proposed question preserves the paper’s central economic feature—the shared quantity discount among retailers and the supplier’s role in removing the wholesale layer. But it is a new computational question suggested by the mgpc formula, not a computational result established by this paper. Its complexity is open; continuum-specific hardness is plausible given the nonlinear optimization over masses and orders, but the paper supplies no basis for claiming it.

That is the strongest honest positive lead I see. Its weakest point is decisive for the requested anchors: the paper’s contribution is analytical cooperative-game theory, and a complexity study of computing a continuum mgpc allocation would extend that contribution rather than mirror a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper gives no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 6.1 characterizes the unique mgpc allocation, and Theorem 5.2 proves that the core is nonempty. Neither states an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a computational problem with an input representation. The mgpc formula does take a minimum over coalitions, but the paper presents that minimum as part of a game-theoretic characterization.

The proponent’s best version is coherent: model a large retail network with finitely many demand types, let a coalition be a mass vector \(\alpha\), and define \(\beta_\infty\) as the infimum of the supplier’s cooperation gain per unit of coalition mass. A franchise network with many outlets sharing a few demand profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The allocation formula also has a natural mass interpretation. I would not reject this on the grounds that retailers are individuated, that fractional coalitions are illegitimate, or that the mirror changes the answer.

But the proposed computation problem is added by the mirror’s author. The paper permits arbitrary continuous demand and wholesale-price functions, with no finite encoding, and does not ask how to compute an mgpc allocation. If coalition values are supplied explicitly, computing the minimum is just a scan of the supplied table. If the supply-chain primitives are the input, then choosing rational piecewise-linear functions and asking for the complexity of optimizing over orders and coalition masses creates a new computational problem. That may be worthwhile research, but it is not the continuous-population counterpart of a computational problem this paper formulated or analyzed.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s computational remit, but weak as a claim that the paper admits no worthwhile continuous extension at all. The nonatomic retailer model is a plausible adjacent project; what it lacks is a computational result here to mirror.
