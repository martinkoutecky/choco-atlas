The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational dual pseudometrics \(d_m,d_c\), a finite center set \(M\), and \(k\), compute centers \(x_j\in M\) and rational allocations \(q_{tj}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_j q_{tj}=\mu_t\). For each positive allocation in cluster \(j\), define its loss as \(d_c(t,x_j)+\max_{u:q_{uj}>0}d_m(t,u)\). Does there always exist, and can one compute in time polynomial in \(\tau, |M|, k\), and input bit length \(L\), an allocation with no deviation consisting of \(z_{tj}\leq q_{tj}\), total selected mass at least \(1/k\), and a center \(y\in M\), such that every selected cohort \((t,j)\) has new loss \(d_c(t,y)+\max_{u:\sum_j z_{uj}>0}d_m(t,u)\) strictly less than its current loss divided by \(3+2\sqrt{3}\)?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population of \(\tau\) client types with rational masses, where each type has fixed distances to all types and feasible centers. Each person receives one cluster assignment, while type mass may split across clusters; cluster and coalition peer losses use the maximum distance over types with positive mass.

The objection that survived

A positive mass, however small, of a distant type can change every member’s peer loss, so the aggregate loss is sensitive to rare types; the proponent retains this worst-peer semantics but does not address that sensitivity.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s polynomial-time dual-metric core approximation, with related mirrors for Theorem 4’s weighted single-metric core guarantee and Theorem 6’s dual-metric FJR guarantee. It leaves the paper’s existential bounds, lower bounds, simultaneous-guarantee impossibility results, and experiments outside the main question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a mass-weighted cohesive-cluster and capture procedure compute the stated \((3+2\sqrt{3})\)-core guarantee in time polynomial in \(\tau, |M|, k\), and \(L\)?
  • Can the continuous guarantee be improved, or can the exact core be computed efficiently for this mass-input model?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a high multiplicity version of proportional fairness in clustering. The paper has no numbered hardness result of its own: its numbered computational results are approximation guarantees and existence theorems. It mentions, citing Caragiannis et al. (2024), that finding an exact most cohesive cluster is NP-hard; that is useful context, but it is not a numbered hardness anchor in this paper.

A plausible regime is a large federated learning service with many clients drawn from a relatively small set of recurring data profiles. A client’s type must capture its complete loss signature: its distance to every other type under the peer-cohesion metric, and its distance to every feasible cluster model or center under the centroid metric. Clients with the same signature are indistinguishable for the clustering problem. The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with \(\tau\) supported types and \(\tau\ll n\), the number of clients. This fits the paper’s own motivation: clients care both about the model their cluster produces and about the other clients with whom they train.

Here is the common continuous instance for the mirrors. Give the algorithm a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), a finite listed center set \(M\), the two rational pseudometrics \(d_m\) and \(d_c\), and \(k\). A solution chooses up to \(k\) centers \(x_j\in M\) and rational masses \(q_{tj}\geq0\) such that \(\sum_j q_{tj}=\mu_t\). Each unit of population is assigned wholly to one cluster; \(q_{tj}\) records the aggregate mass of type \(t\) assigned there. Thus the cluster allocation is integral for each person even though a type’s mass can be divided across clusters.

For a cluster \(j\) containing type \(t\), its loss is
\[ \ell_{t,j}=d_c(t,x_j)+\max_{u:q_{uj}>0}d_m(t,u). \]
A deviation chooses mass \(z_{tj}\leq q_{tj}\) from the current type-and-cluster cohorts, totaling at least \(1/k\), and a feasible center \(y\in M\). Its members’ new loss is
\[ \ell_t(z,y)=d_c(t,y)+\max_{u:\sum_j z_{uj}>0}d_m(t,u). \]
The continuous \(\alpha\)-core condition forbids a deviation for which every selected cohort satisfies \(\alpha\ell_t(z,y)<\ell_{t,j}\). For FJR, the right side is instead the minimum current loss among all selected cohorts. These definitions preserve the paper’s coalition tests, including the maximum-distance peer loss.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that, under dual metric loss, a clustering in the \((3+2\sqrt{3})\)-core can be computed in polynomial time. The corresponding mirror, Dual-Metric Core Clustering\(_\infty\), asks: given the finite rational instance above, compute a rational allocation and centers with no violating coalition at factor \(3+2\sqrt{3}\). The natural complexity target is time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|M|\), \(k\), and the input bit length \(L\), rather than in a population count encoded through the masses. I expect this approximation problem to be tractable: the paper’s construction works through cohesive clusters, a capture phase, and controlled reassignment, all of which have plausible mass-weighted versions over the listed support. The continuous question is whether those versions retain the guarantee with exact binary-rational bookkeeping. Further questions include improving the factor and determining whether the exact core can be found efficiently.

Theorem 4, also proved here, is a worthwhile, more specific anchor because it tracks the tradeoff between the two losses. For weighted single metric loss with \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), it establishes that a \(\min\{2/\lambda,3\}\)-core clustering always exists and that a \(\min\{2/\lambda,f_\lambda\}\)-core clustering can be computed in polynomial time, where
\[ f_\lambda=\frac{\sqrt{2\lambda-11\lambda^2+13}+3-\lambda}{2-2\lambda}. \]
At endpoints, the expressions with zero denominators are read by their limiting branches: \(2/\lambda=+\infty\) at \(\lambda=0\), and \(f_1=+\infty\). Its mirror, Weighted-Metric Core Clustering\(_\infty\), uses one rational metric \(d\) and rational \(\lambda\), with loss
\[ \ell_t^\lambda(z,y)=\lambda\max_{u:\sum_j z_{uj}>0}d(t,u)+(1-\lambda)d(t,y). \]
The question is to compute an allocation satisfying the theorem’s \(\lambda\)-dependent core factor. I again expect tractability over support-size input. This version is particularly recognizable as the paper’s own model: it keeps its single metric and varies only the weight between peer cohesion and center quality. A useful follow-up is whether the continuous approximation bounds meet the paper’s lower bounds for this parameterized family.

A third, distinct anchor is Theorem 6, proved here. It says that, for arbitrary losses and any \(\alpha\geq1\), iteratively finding an \(\alpha\)-approximate most cohesive cluster returns an \(\alpha\)-FJR clustering; hence a \(1\)-FJR clustering exists for arbitrary losses and, using the paper’s polynomial-time \(4\)-MCC routine, a \(4\)-FJR clustering can be computed in polynomial time for dual metric loss. The mirror, Dual-Metric FJR Clustering\(_\infty\), asks for a rational allocation and centers satisfying the mass-based FJR condition at factor \(4\). The expected direction is tractability, again via a weighted cohesive-cluster procedure. The result also poses a separate question: can the existence guarantee of \(1\)-FJR be made efficiently computable in this mass-input regime?

These mirrors leave the paper’s main object intact: agents receive clusters and centers, and groups assess whether they would improve by forming a cluster of their own. The population becomes continuous; the outcome space does not. In particular, the proposed mirror retains both loss components as the paper defines them. The paper’s Theorem 1 shows why it would be a mistake to replace that objective with a demand for simultaneous guarantees under centroid and non-centroid losses separately: the authors prove that such guarantees can be incompatible.

The weakest point is the high multiplicity assumption. Many datasets may have almost as many distinct loss signatures as data points, leaving little support-size advantage. And the paper’s finite-agent polynomial algorithms do not by themselves prove polynomial time in \(\tau\) and \(L\): fractional mass can be split at capture thresholds, and the core tests quantify over fractional coalitions. A prover would need to establish that the mass-weighted procedures preserve the stated guarantees and have polynomial bit complexity. That is a real gap, but it is a focused continuation of the authors’ clustering and fairness questions, rather than a change of subject.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make a strong universal case against a mirror here. Theorem 3 gives a named polynomial-time approximation result, and Theorems 4 and 6 give further algorithmic results. The absence of a hardness classification does not erase those computational anchors.

The best version of the Theorem 3 mirror is a fixed library of client profiles, each with the same distances to every profile and feasible center. Give profile \(t\) rational mass \(\mu_t\), and assign fractions \(q_{tj}\) of it to clusters, with \(\sum_j q_{tj}=\mu_t\). A coalition can draw mass at least \(1/k\) from those clusters and compare its current loss with the loss of forming a cluster at a feasible center. That is a coherent high-multiplicity model for federated clients with recurring data profiles.

The strongest objection is that the peer loss is a maximum. If a cluster contains any positive mass \(\epsilon\) of a distant type \(u\), then \(u\) can determine the peer loss of every member; at \(\epsilon=0\), it has no effect. Thus the loss can jump when a vanishingly small cohort appears or disappears. This weakens the interpretation of the model as a stable aggregate limit. It does not invalidate the question: it preserves the paper’s worst-peer semantics, and a deployment may care about even rare incompatible clients. Moreover, the paper’s capture procedure looks amenable to a direct weighted adaptation: take the required mass from types, splitting a boundary type, and operate over the finite support. The remaining work—especially proving polynomial bit complexity—may be useful, but this looks more like a focused lifting than a new use of continuous-optimization machinery.

Theorem 4’s best mirror uses the same mass model and asks for its \(\lambda\)-dependent core guarantee. The objection is similar: for every \(\lambda>0\), an arbitrarily small positive amount of a type can activate the peer-distance term. The transition at \(\lambda=0\), where that term disappears, is therefore singular. Also, this theorem’s mirror largely repeats the dual-metric core question on a narrower family of inputs. That makes its independent research payoff modest; it does not make the question incoherent.

Theorem 6 is the least convincing separate anchor. Its arbitrary-loss version does not yield a compact type model: a complete type would need to encode losses for every possible coalition and center, potentially an enormous set of subsets. Restricting it to the paper’s dual-metric losses gives a finite support model, but then the \(4\)-FJR computation follows the same most-cohesive-cluster machinery as the core mirror. The \(1\)-FJR existence statement is structurally simple and may transfer almost verbatim; the computational question is the weighted most-cohesive-cluster procedure.

I also cannot use prior art as a collision. The nearby foundational papers I checked formulate clustering over finite sets of \(n\) data points or agents; they do not establish that these population-distribution questions have already been answered ([Chen et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/chen19d.html), [Caragiannis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.23273)). Under the programme’s rule, that finite-agent work supports the plausibility of the regime.

So the strongest negative case is about priority, not validity: the three anchors compress to one plausible weighted core question, with FJR as a close variant, and the paper’s greedy methods may lift with limited new machinery. That could make the project less distinctive than the proponent suggests. It does not honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large federated service with many clients drawn from a small, stable set of profiles remains a credible scenario, so this negative case is weak against the universal claim.
