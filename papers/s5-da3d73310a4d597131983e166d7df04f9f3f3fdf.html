The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is weak: this paper has no named computational result of the kind the programme requires as an anchor. Theorem 1 characterizes the uniform rule; Theorems 2 and 5 characterize the equal-split rule; Theorem 3 and Theorem 6 characterize concede-and-divide; and Theorem 4 gives another characterization of the uniform rule. Corollary 1 also characterizes equal-split. These are axiomatic uniqueness results, proved here—not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. So there is no honest per-anchor computational mirror or A/B/C prediction to give.

The nearest plausible mirror would treat a large federation of clubs as the population. A type would specify everything relevant to a club’s audience interactions; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of clubs of type \(t\); and an audience kernel \(a(t,u)\) would give the broadcast audience generated between types. A continuum version of equal-split would assign each type-\(t\) club the per-club amount
\[ r(t)=\frac12\int \bigl(a(t,u)+a(u,t)\bigr)\,d\mu(u). \]
The corresponding question would be whether continuum versions of the paper’s axioms characterize this allocation rule. That is a concrete population-continuum extension of the revenue-sharing model, but it remains an axiomatic characterization question. Computing \(r\) is just integration; the paper supplies no computational result whose complexity this mirror could test.

The regime is plausible only for something like a very large federation with many clubs grouped into relatively few genuinely indistinguishable audience-interaction types. It is much less convincing for a conventional top-flight league with few teams. The weakness is deeper than that regime choice: the paper allocates revenue to teams, while the viewers whose numbers populate its audience matrix are not the recipients or agents in its model. Continuizing audience quantities alone would not continuize the population. Thus the club-continuum idea is the best nearby positive angle, but it does not meet ChoCo’s computational-question requirement or anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorems 1–6 and Corollary 1 characterize allocation rules from axioms; none analyzes an algorithm, a complexity class, or a computational problem. That leaves no anchor for ChoCo’s complexity landscape.

The strongest proposed mirror is still coherent: imagine a large federation with recurring team types, and let \(a(t,u)\) describe the audience generated between types. Equal-split then becomes
\[ r(t)=\frac12\int \bigl(a(t,u)+a(u,t)\bigr)\,d\mu(u). \]
The fact that teams receive the revenue while viewers generate it does not defeat this model: the continuous population can be the teams. But asking whether continuum versions of the paper’s axioms characterize this allocation remains an axiomatic question, outside the programme’s computational scope.

An algorithmic recasting fares little better. With finitely many supported types and a rational audience table, the allocation follows by summing the relevant entries, in time polynomial in the table size. If the input is an arbitrary continuous audience kernel, computational complexity depends on how that function is represented; any difficulty may come from numerical integration rather than the allocation problem. Optimizing schedules or designing audience patterns could pose harder questions, but those would be new problems, not mirrors of this paper’s results.

So I would reject this paper as a source of a credible ChoCo complexity anchor. The negative case is weaker against the user’s universal claim: a continuum of teams in a large federation is a plausible regime, and this paper alone cannot establish that no worthwhile computational question could ever be built around it.
