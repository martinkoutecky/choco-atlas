The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that voter control has a natural population mirror, but this paper supplies no qualifying anchor for the programme’s complexity question. Its named results establish equalities, separations, and incomparabilities between control problems, or characterize those relationships using voting-rule axioms. For example, the universal-separations theorem says that any compatible constructive and destructive control types differ under every voting rule; the weak-resoluteness theorem characterizes when constructive and destructive UW/NUW variants collapse. Neither says that a control problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The NP-hardness mentioned in the paper’s background belongs to cited work, not to a named result asserted here. The supplied text also omits visible theorem numbers, showing cross-reference labels instead.

A plausible *new* mirror would be continuous voter-deletion control for Copeland. Let \(C\) be the candidates and \(T\) the ranking types. The input is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), a target \(p\), and a deletion budget \(\beta\). Choose deleted masses \(d_t\) with \(0\le d_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t d_t\le\beta\), leaving positive total mass. The remaining weighted pairwise margins determine Copeland scores; ask whether some choice of \(d\) makes \(p\) a winner. The witness is the vector \(d\). This has a credible regime: a large electorate with millions of voters but relatively few distinct ballots, where each type’s mass is its population share. Voter rankings are already the paper’s type information, and weighted pairwise margins give a direct way to evaluate the rule on rational masses.

That is a concrete population-continuous version of one of the paper’s underlying control problems. But the paper’s theorems compare control languages; they do not classify this weighted-profile problem’s complexity. Turning their relationship results into complexity anchors would overstate what they prove, so I can’t honestly supply a qualifying “one continuous problem per anchor” case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper gives no result of the kind the programme is set up to continuize. Its universal-separation theorem compares two control languages; its weak-resoluteness theorem characterizes when pairs of languages coincide. The majority-criterion results and the tables likewise establish relationships between control types. None classifies the complexity of a control problem or gives an algorithm for it. The NP-hardness results mentioned in the introduction belong to cited work, not to this paper. So there is no named computational result here to anchor a continuous complexity question.

A continuous restatement of the universal-separation or weak-resoluteness results could be formulated over mass profiles, but it would still ask whether control languages differ or coincide. It would not supply the computational question this programme targets. The paper’s many separations do not become complexity results just because the voter collection is represented by a distribution.

The proposed Copeland voter-deletion model is the strongest counterexample to a broader negative claim. A large electorate with a small support of ranking types is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and deleting fractions of those types gives a coherent continuous control problem. I cannot honestly dismiss that model as meaningless or unworthy of study. But it is a new complexity question about Copeland voter deletion, not a mirror of a complexity result asserted in this paper.

So the negative case is strong as a reason to reject this paper as an anchored ChoCo case study. It is weak as a claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario: the proposed voter-deletion problem is plausible, but its value would have to be argued as new work rather than as a continuization of one of this paper’s results.
