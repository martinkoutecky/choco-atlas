The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies no qualifying anchor, and the paper supplies none: it has no named theorem or other result classifying the complexity of an allocation, control, or optimization problem. Its nearest candidate is §5.1, which compares the cost of evaluating two metrics on a finite episode trace: \(\Theta(\nu+n)\) for RP and \(\Theta(\nu n)\) for ALT. That is a useful implementation result, but it is not a complexity landscape for a computational social choice problem. A continuous version could repeat the cost comparison over weighted histories; it would not turn the paper’s contribution into the kind of algorithmic question ChoCo is built to chart.

The stronger proposed mirror—large fleets grouped into policy or priority types, with a distribution of access histories—runs into a problem the paper’s metric is designed to avoid: it tracks individual histories over time. Grouping by policy does not tell us which agents won in successive episodes, so it loses the inter-win gaps that RP measures. Grouping by complete trajectory preserves those gaps, but then the “types” are the histories the evaluation is meant to summarize. For an explicit finite trace, calculating a weighted average of the metrics over those histories is a direct scan; if trajectories are given through a compressed stochastic process, the new difficulty comes from evaluating that process, not from making the population continuous.

There is also a scaling obstacle in the game itself. Each episode has at most one exclusive winner. In an atomless population, an individual has zero mass, so that win does not give a positive fraction of the population access; PA’s one-win-per-agent-per-\(n\)-episode meaning disappears. Scaling capacity with population would change exclusive access into service to a positive mass, while rescaling time to preserve individual win rates would require a new dynamic model. Either might motivate a separate study, but neither supplies a continuous mirror of the paper’s metric comparison or experiments.

So this is a strong case against a direct, worthwhile ChoCo mirror, not a proof that no related continuous research question could ever be worthwhile. A mean-field coordination or control model inspired by HJG might be interesting. The paper itself, though, offers metric definitions, finite-trace evaluation costs, and empirical findings—not a named computational result whose continuous population version opens the kind of complexity question the programme targets.
