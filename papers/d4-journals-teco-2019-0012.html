What the mirror covers

No named result yields a computational population mirror; the paper’s truthfulness, focality, and relabeling results remain unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. It has numbered theorems, but none classifies an underlying problem as tractable or hard, or gives an algorithmic result of the kind this programme needs. Its results concern incentives and expected payments.

The closest candidates illustrate the gap. Theorem 6.3, proved here, says that with infinitely many similar questions, the \(f\)-mutual-information mechanism is detail-free, minimal, and (under the stated conditions) dominantly truthful and truth-monotone; its effort extension gives further incentive guarantees. Theorem 7.8, attributed to Prelec [22] and analyzed further here, gives truthfulness and focality guarantees for Bayesian Truth Serum under an infinite-agent assumption. Proposition 8.8, proved here, is an impossibility result about equilibrium and payment comparisons under prior relabeling. These are not computational complexity results.

The most plausible population regime would be a very large survey or peer-review workforce with many workers sharing the same complete signal model and effort cost. One could describe the workforce by masses of those types and ask whether truthful reporting maximizes expected payment. But that would recast an incentive question, not supply the required continuous computational problem. Moreover, Theorem 7.8 already assumes infinitely many agents, so that particular population mirror would largely restate a model already in the paper.

So the positive case is weak for a specific reason: the paper’s central contribution is incentive design, not a computational problem whose complexity changes when the population becomes continuous. The numbered results do not provide an honest anchor for the requested mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is about the results the paper actually gives, not whether a large workforce is plausible. A crowd of many workers doing similar reviews is a sensible high-multiplicity setting; Section 7 even assumes infinitely many agents. That supports the population regime. But the paper’s named results establish incentive properties, not the computational complexity of a problem whose input is that population.

Theorem 6.3 is the best candidate for a stronger mirror: represent workers by masses of complete types, then ask how a colluding mass can change expected payments. Yet the theorem already gives a pointwise guarantee: a truthful agent’s nontruthful deviation lowers every agent’s expected payment, regardless of the other agents’ strategies. For payment-only coalition objectives, applying that guarantee as coalition members deviate in turn already settles the natural question. To get a new optimization problem, one must add an objective such as side payments, coalition-specific costs, or a constraint on who can coordinate. Those additions may be worth studying, but they are new strategic questions rather than computational mirrors of Theorem 6.3.

Theorem 7.8 is the strongest evidence for a continuous population: its large-group assumption makes the aggregate report distribution central. But the theorem analyzes truthfulness and focality in that limit; it does not ask an algorithm to find or optimize anything. With a finite signal alphabet \(\Sigma\), the aggregate is a distribution over \(\Sigma\), and the theorem supplies its incentive conclusion. A computational task such as finding equilibria under added constraints would need a separate input and output specification. The fact that the paper already treats an infinite population is not itself a collision under ChoCo’s rules; it simply leaves no named computational result here to mirror.

Proposition 8.8 likewise proves an impossibility about relabeling signals and comparing payments across priors. A continuous version could preserve the relabeling argument, but that would extend a strategic impossibility, not yield a computational problem. Searching for a mechanism that breaks the impossibility by adding prior information or other structure would be a new mechanism-design project, with no population-complexity question supplied by the proposition.

So I would reject these as ChoCo anchors, while keeping the negative case narrow: plausible high-multiplicity scenarios exist, but each proposed computational mirror needs a new objective or problem definition that the cited result does not provide. This is not proof that no useful project can be built from the paper; it is a case that the paper, as written, offers no worthwhile computational-continuization anchor.
