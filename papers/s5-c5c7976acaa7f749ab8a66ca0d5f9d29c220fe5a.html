The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_a>0\), rational incentives \(\alpha_a\), a common rational conformity parameter \(\omega>0\), a rational type-level peer matrix \(\bar{\mathbf G}\), and a specified long-run preference or action target, compute a typewise policy vector \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\) that implements the target under the paper's dynamics. For action targets, apply Proposition 5's action formula with \(\mathbf G\) replaced by \(\bar{\mathbf G}\); for preference targets, use its preference formula.

The model it lives in

A continuum cohort represented by finitely many complete types, where type \(a\) has mass \(\mu_a\), shared incentives and peer exposure, and one policy \(\sigma_a\). A nonnegative peer kernel \(K\) defines the row-stochastic matrix \(\bar G_{ab}=\mu_bK_{ab}\), with \(\sum_b\mu_bK_{ab}=1\). The decision variable is the typewise policy vector \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\); the objective is to implement the specified long-run profile.

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition 5's policy synthesis for homogeneous targets and heterogeneous steady-state preference or action targets; it leaves Propositions 1–4 as convergence and equilibrium characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strict anchor test exposes a real gap: this paper has no named result classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or otherwise. Propositions 1–4 characterize convergence and steady states. The closest computational foothold is Proposition 5, proved in this paper, which gives an exact policy for reaching a specified long-run integration profile. It is a constructive result, but it does not state a complexity bound. If only formal complexity results count as anchors, this paper has none.

Proposition 5 says that in the authors’ finite-agent model: (1) to reach homogeneous long-run choices and preferences, the planner sets \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^*=\mathbf{x}^*-\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{s}^*-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\); (2) to reach heterogeneous long-run preferences \(\mathbf{s}^*\), the planner sets \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^*=\mathbf{s}^*-\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\omega(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{G})\mathbf{s}^*\); and (3) to reach heterogeneous long-run actions \(\mathbf{x}^*\), the planner sets
\[ \boldsymbol{\sigma}^*=(2+\omega)\left[\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{1+\omega}(\mathbf{I}-\theta\mathbf{G})^{-1}\right]^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\theta'\mathbf{G})\mathbf{x}^*-\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \]
where \(\theta=\omega/(1+\omega)\) and \(\theta'=\omega/(2+\omega)\). Here \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^*\) is the tax or subsidy vector. The result is an exact target-implementation rule, not a policy-cost minimization theorem.

The strongest mirror I can make from it is Continuous Targeted Integration-Policy Synthesis. Consider a large regional integration programme with many participants in recurring language-training, school, or employment cohorts. A type records everything this problem uses: the participant’s material return to integration, taste for conformity, and mix of peer types. For example, thousands of participants might fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of recurring combinations of prior language proficiency, labour-market returns, and cohort exposure. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime; an arbitrary friendship network need not have it.

An instance consists of a finite set of types \(T\), their rational population masses \(\mu_a>0\) summing to \(1\), rational material incentives \(\alpha_a\), a common rational conformity parameter \(\omega>0\), and a rational target profile. The type-level mixing matrix \(\bar{\mathbf G}\) is row-stochastic: \(\bar G_{ab}\) is the share of a type-\(a\) participant’s peer weight placed on type \(b\). To make the population masses explicit, one can specify a nonnegative kernel \(K\) with \(\bar G_{ab}=\mu_bK_{ab}\) and \(\sum_b\mu_bK_{ab}=1\). A policy assigns one tax or subsidy \(\sigma_a\) to every member of type \(a\).

The question is: compute a type-level policy vector that implements the specified long-run profile. Its solution is the vector \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\); the objective is implementation of the input target, not selection of the target or minimization of expenditure. For a target of heterogeneous long-run actions \(\mathbf{x}^*\), the mirror uses Proposition 5’s third formula with \(\mathbf{G}\) replaced by \(\bar{\mathbf G}\). For a preference target it uses the second formula; for a homogeneous target \(q\), it uses \(\boldsymbol{\sigma}=q\mathbf{1}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\). The aggregate dynamics retain the paper’s substantive ingredients: type \(a\)’s action responds to its material incentive, preference, and peer average, and its preference updates toward its action. The population is what becomes continuous; the individual action was already continuous in the paper.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: the proposed algorithm is exact rational matrix arithmetic on \(\tau\times\tau\) matrices, polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). That is a complexity conclusion for the mirror, not one stated by the authors. The finite-population bridge is also concrete: a discrete society with many participants in these types induces rational masses \(\mu_a=n_a/n\), while type-level policy and exposure data remain represented by \(\tau\) types.

This covers Proposition 5’s targeted policy synthesis in a finite-type, population-continuum regime. It does not mirror Propositions 1–4 as computational results, nor does it claim that continuous populations resolve a hardness result: the paper supplies no such hardness anchor. Natural next questions include whether minimum-cost policy under subsidy bounds remains an LP when the target is a population-wide integration threshold, and what happens when the finite type matrix is replaced by a continuous distribution of peer exposures.

The weak point is substantial. The paper studies named individuals on an arbitrary finite network; the mirror needs repeated agents with the same incentives and type-level exposure profile. That is plausible in standardized cohorts, but restrictive for personal networks. More basically, Proposition 5 is a formula, not a complexity theorem, so the proposed tractable mirror is a modest computational extension rather than evidence that this paper contains the sort of discrete hardness whose fate in the continuum is the programme’s central question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest negative point is that the paper states no computational complexity result. Propositions 1–4 characterize convergence and steady states; Proposition 5 gives a policy formula. So there is no algorithmic theorem here whose discrete complexity the mirror could continue. Proposition 5 is the only plausible anchor, and it needs to be treated as such rather than waved away.

Its strongest proposed mirror—a large cohort population with finitely many types and a type-level peer matrix—is coherent. If agents of a type truly share incentives and peer exposure, the system closes on a \(\tau\times\tau\) matrix. But then the proposed task is just to evaluate Proposition 5’s map on that matrix: given a target, calculate the corresponding subsidy vector. The masses enter only through the peer-mixing matrix; the proposition imposes no budget, subsidy bounds, target selection, or population-level feasibility constraint. Its policy vector is determined directly by the formula. This is a computational reformulation, but the paper supplies no search or optimization problem whose complexity the reformulation could illuminate.

The better version would ask for a least-cost policy that gets everyone above an integration threshold, with mass-weighted costs and realistic subsidy bounds. That would make population masses matter directly. But those constraints and that objective are new modeling choices, not part of Proposition 5’s result. With finite types and linear costs, the natural formulation is an LP over the types. It may be a sensible policy-design question, but it is no longer the mirror of a computational result in this paper. Adding sparsity or fixed costs could create more difficult optimization, but those additions would need an independent substantive justification.

The type reduction also carries a strong condition: agents grouped together must match in all problem-relevant respects, including their peer exposure. In an arbitrary network, positions differ, and the paper’s results make those differences central. A block-network cohort can meet the condition, so this is not a decisive objection; it does mean that the attractive small-\(\tau\) instance regime is an imposed special case. In that regime the finite model already has continuous actions and preferences, and aggregation yields the same linear system on representative types. Population continuity removes headcount from the calculation, but there is no integrality of individual actions or subsidy choices here for a high-multiplicity relaxation to resolve.

A richer continuum of network and incentive types could lead to an operator-control problem. But computational complexity would require a finite encoding of the kernel and a specified accuracy model. Without those, the mirror is an analytic or numerical question, not yet a computational problem with a defined input size.

That leaves a real weakness in the negative case: standardized cohorts with repeated peer profiles are plausible, and a constrained policy-design problem could be worth studying. I would not claim to have ruled out every worthwhile extension. The stronger conclusion is narrower: the paper itself offers no computational social-choice result to mirror, and its best anchor yields a direct type-level policy formula unless substantial new optimization structure is added.
