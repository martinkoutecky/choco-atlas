The continuous mirror question

Given \(r\) object categories and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) types \((u_t,e_t)\), with each \(u_t(j)\) drawn from at most three common nonnegative rational values, set \(q_j=\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}[j\in e_t]\). Does there exist a rational mass allocation \(x_{tB}\geq 0\) over integral bundles \(B\subseteq[r]\) satisfying \(\sum_B x_{tB}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B:j\in B}x_{tB}=q_j\), and \(x_{tB}>0\Rightarrow u_t(B)\geq u_t(e_t)\), with \(\sum_{t,B}x_{tB}(u_t(B)-u_t(e_t))>0\); can this be decided in time polynomial in \(r,\tau,L\)?

The model it lives in

Types \(t\) share utility vector \(u_t\) and initial integral bundle \(e_t\), with mass \(\mu_t\). Category \(j\) has per-capita supply \(q_j=\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf{1}[j\in e_t]\). Variables \(x_{tB}\) assign mass to integral bundles \(B\), preserving type masses and supplies while ensuring each assigned bundle is individually rational; the objective is positive aggregate utility gain.

What the mirror covers

The proposed model covers the three-value cardinal testing result in Theorem 3 and describes a two-object-endowment variant corresponding to Theorem 2. It leaves the paper's other cardinal cases and its ordinal possible- and necessary-optimality results unmodeled.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the proposed three-level pricing procedure be formalized into a polynomial-bit-complexity algorithm for the mass reallocation test in \(r,\tau,L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Three-Value Continuous Pareto-Improvement Existence, anchored in Theorem 3. The paper proves Theorem 3 here: testing Pareto optimality is strongly coNP-complete even when utilities take only three values. Its reduction is from \((3,B2)\)-SAT, which the paper cites as NP-complete. The result is a good anchor because the hardness survives a sharp restriction on preferences, while the corresponding continuous problem has a clear route to an algorithm.

Take a mass-one population with \(\tau\) types. A type \(t\) specifies both its complete additive utility vector \(u_t\) and its initial bundle \(e_t\); its rational mass is \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). These are the attributes that make agents indistinguishable for this problem. Let \(r\) be the number of object categories. Each agent receives a discrete bundle \(B\subseteq[r]\), and the supply of category \(j\) per unit of population is \(q_j=\sum_t\mu_t\mathbf 1[j\in e_t]\). Supplies scale with the population; bundles remain indivisible for every individual.

A reallocation is a finite rational table of masses \(x_{tB}\geq0\): \(x_{tB}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(B\). It must satisfy \(\sum_Bx_{tB}=\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,B:j\in B}x_{tB}=q_j\) for each object category. Every bundle assigned positive mass must satisfy \(u_t(B)\geq u_t(e_t)\), and a Pareto improvement exists exactly when \(\sum_{t,B}x_{tB}(u_t(B)-u_t(e_t))>0\). Thus a cohort may split across different integral bundles, but no individual receives a fractional bundle or is allowed to lose utility. A basic feasible solution has finite support, so the table is a concrete solution certificate.

For the named Three-Value problem, require all utility values to come from a common set of at most three nonnegative rationals. I expect this mirror to be Class A: tractable. Its improvement test is a rational LP with up to \(\tau 2^r\) bundle variables. For separation, fix a type and dual prices \(\pi_j\) for object categories. The pricing problem is to choose a bundle with \(u_t(B)\geq u_t(e_t)\) maximizing \(\sum_{j\in B}(u_t(j)-\pi_j)\). When utilities have three possible levels, enumerate the number selected at each level; for each triple of counts, choose the highest adjusted-price items within each level and check the utility threshold. There are only \(O(r^3)\) count triples, so pricing is polynomial in \(r\). With rational input, this gives a polynomial-time separation route for the LP. The question for a prover is whether the mirror can be solved in time polynomial in \(r,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), with this pricing argument made formal.

A plausible regime is a large institution reallocating indivisible resources, such as course seats. Many students may share both a valuation profile over a common catalog and the same initial schedule. Then the student count \(N\) can be far larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct preference-and-endowment types. The paper’s authors should recognize the question: it is still whether the initial allocation admits a reallocation that weakly benefits everyone and strictly benefits someone. Only the population representation has changed.

My second anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here by reduction, from the strongly NP-complete 2-numerical matching with target sums problem cited as [26]. It states that testing Pareto optimality is strongly coNP-complete even when every agent initially owns exactly two objects. Its mirror, Two-Object-Endowment Continuous Pareto-Improvement Existence, uses the same allocation model above, with the restriction \(|e_t|=2\) for every positive-mass type and arbitrary nonnegative rational additive utilities. The question and solution certificate are exactly the weakly improving mass reallocation and positive total gain defined above.

For this second mirror, I expect hardness, probably Class C unless a discrete embedding can be established. Arbitrary utilities remove the three-level pricing shortcut: pricing eligible bundles is a threshold-constrained subset problem, which looks knapsack-like. That is a reason to investigate hardness, not a proof of it. The theorem’s discrete hardness does not automatically transfer, because a continuous cohort can split among bundles in ways a single named agent cannot. Further questions are whether that splitting dissolves the two-object hardness, whether bundle pricing is actually hard enough to imply hardness of the full test, and whether restrictions on the number of utility levels recover tractability.

The mirror covers the paper’s cardinal additive Pareto-optimality testing results in Theorems 2 and 3. It does not try to continuize the paper’s ordinal possible- or necessary-optimality results, or to replace the population question with fractional outcomes.

The weakest point is that the reductions themselves use many agents with distinct, purpose-built utility and endowment data; they do not demonstrate a regime with many agents per type. Replicating those agents creates repeated cohorts, but also gives the continuum room to split each cohort and possibly eliminate the reduction’s obstruction. That makes direct hardness transfer uncertain. It does not make the mirror artificial: repeated preference-and-endowment cohorts are a natural high-multiplicity allocation regime, and the three-value anchor gives a concrete reason to expect that continuization could change the complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about the change of model needed to get repeated types. In the paper, an allocation partitions a set of individually identified objects. A type includes its initial bundle as well as its complete utility function. With named objects and disjoint endowments, two agents cannot have the same nonempty initial bundle. So a large population with few repeated types requires treating objects as interchangeable categories and specifying per-capita supplies. That describes a plausible cohort market, but it is a meaningful shift from reallocating a particular stock of goods.

That shift weakens Theorems 2 and 3 as evidence for a mirror. Their reductions build agents and objects around individual logical roles; they do not exhibit a large population with a small number of complete types. In those instances, the support size \(\tau\) would grow with the construction. Theorem 2’s two-object condition survives as an initial-endowment restriction, but it does not preserve the structure of the reduction once agents can split across bundles. The best version of its mirror is still coherent: study mass reallocation with two-category endowments and arbitrary additive utilities. But a knapsack-like pricing problem is a reason that question could be difficult, not a reason it is unworthy. The paper itself supplies little evidence for which repeated-type regime would make this particular restriction compelling.

Theorem 3 is harder to argue against. For the three-value mirror, mass can be assigned across integral bundles while every bundle used by a type is individually rational. The aggregate supply and gain conditions are linear. And the proposed pricing method appears sound: for each type, enumerate the three counts of objects chosen at each utility level, then choose the highest reduced-price objects within each level. That gives polynomially many cases in the number of object categories. This is a strong, recognizable computational question about a continuous population; changing to category supplies does not by itself make it artificial. It is also exactly the kind of case where continuous optimization might expose a tractable mirror.

I cannot make outcome-space continuity, random allocation, or prior high-multiplicity work carry the negative case. None, on the evidence here, establishes that this ex-post, type-level mass-reallocation question has already been answered. And the paper’s finite reductions having many distinct agents is not decisive: the proposed mirror need not reproduce those reductions to be worth studying.

So the honest negative case is narrow: the paper’s own instances do not establish a natural repeated-type regime, and the category-level supply model should be made explicit before claiming these are close mirrors of its results. That is a real qualification, especially for Theorem 2. It does not defeat the stronger three-value mirror. On the supplied material, the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile continuous mirror is weak.
