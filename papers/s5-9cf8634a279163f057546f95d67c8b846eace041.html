The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of applicant types \(T\), post categories \(P\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_p\), ranked eligibility edges \(E\), and a feasible rank-maximal allocation \(x\), compute a rank-maximal allocation after one rational type-mass, capacity, or preference-edge update. An allocation satisfies \(\sum_{p:(t,p)\in E}x_{tp}\leq\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t:(t,p)\in E}x_{tp}\leq\kappa_p\), and \(x_{tp}\geq0\); it is rank-maximal when its rank totals \((\rho_1(x),\ldots,\rho_r(x))\) are lexicographically maximal.

The model it lives in

Applicant types carry rational mass \(\mu_t\), post categories have rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_p\), and decision variables \(x_{tp}\) assign mass along ranked eligibility edges. The objective is lexicographic maximization of rank totals; a dynamic update changes type mass, capacity, or a rational slice's preference edges, and asks for a new optimum.

The objection that survived

DCRMU asks for the updated optimum without requiring an update-time bound, so it does not preserve the paper's \(O(r(m+n))\) maintenance guarantee; the proponent acknowledges this gap but supplies no continuous bound.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the rank-maximal allocation update result for changes to applicant-type mass, post-category supply, or preference data. It does not establish the paper's \(O(r(m+n))\) update bound for the continuous model.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the updated allocation be computed in time polynomial in the type, category, edge, and input bit-length parameters while using the previous allocation?
  • How do rational per-capita supplies and normalized population changes correspond to finite high-multiplicity update sequences?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror is the paper’s exact dynamic rank-maximal matching result. There is one hitch with the requested anchor citation: the supplied text omits the theorem counters, so I can locate the result in §1.1 and §§4.1–4.3, but cannot faithfully quote a printed theorem number. The main theorem in §1.1 says that, given a rank-maximal matching instance with \(n\) vertices and \(m\) edges, an existing rank-maximal matching can be updated after adding or deleting a vertex or edge in \(O(r(m+n))\) time, where \(r\) is the maximum rank used by a rank-maximal matching. The paper proves the vertex-update claims here; it reduces edge updates to vertex deletion and reinsertion. The construction builds on Irving et al.’s earlier algorithm. This is an algorithmic anchor; the supplied paper contains no comparable named hardness result.

A credible regime is a large, centralized assignment of graduates to training positions, an application the paper itself mentions. Consider a programme where applicants use a limited set of standardized preference lists for training-post categories. An applicant’s type is their complete ranked list and eligibility profile. If there are \(N\) applicants but only \(\tau\) such profiles, with \(\tau\ll N\), the high-multiplicity premise is plausible. It would not describe every hiring process: if almost every applicant has a unique list, this regime is a poor fit. Posts can be grouped into interchangeable categories with rational per-capita supplies; that capacity co-modification is part of the mirror.

Here is the concrete question I would hand to a prover: Dynamic Continuous Rank-Maximal Matching Update (DCRMU). An instance has a finite set \(T\) of applicant types, a finite set \(P\) of post categories, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), rational per-capita post capacities \(\kappa_p\), and a ranked eligibility relation \(E\subseteq T\times P\). Each edge \((t,p)\) has a rank \(\ell(t,p)\). The input also gives a feasible rank-maximal allocation \(x\) for this instance, where \(x_{tp}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to category \(p\), subject to
\[ \sum_{p:(t,p)\in E}x_{tp}\leq\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t:(t,p)\in E}x_{tp}\leq\kappa_p,\qquad x_{tp}\geq0. \]
For each rank \(i\), define \(\rho_i(x)=\sum_{(t,p)\in E:\ell(t,p)=i}x_{tp}\). Rank-maximal means that \((\rho_1(x),\ldots,\rho_r(x))\) is lexicographically maximal among feasible allocations.

An update is one rational cohort or post-supply insertion or deletion, or a preference-edge change affecting a rational slice of a type. For the latter, split that slice into a new type whose complete list differs by the changed edge. The updated rational masses, capacities and preference data are part of the input. The question is to return any rank-maximal allocation for the updated instance; there is no extra objective to minimize how much the allocation changes. This keeps the paper’s underlying matching objective intact while replacing single agents by mass. The type-level flow \(x\) may be fractional, while each person still receives at most one whole post; in a nonatomic population, such a flow can be realized by assigning portions of each type to categories. For rational data, the bipartite matching polytope also gives the expected exact bridge to sufficiently scaled finite populations.

I expect DCRMU to be tractable—Class A—because it is a capacitated bipartite-flow problem with \(r\) lexicographic objectives. Sequentially optimizing the rank totals gives polynomial-time exact optimization in the type, post, edge and bit-length parameters. That is a reasonable mirror of the paper’s problem: it still asks for exact rank-maximal matching after population or eligibility changes, rather than substituting a different allocation criterion. It does not claim that the paper’s \(O(r(m+n))\) update bound transfers unchanged. Natural follow-up questions are whether updates can be processed faster than recomputing the flow, and whether that speedup can depend on the changed mass or affected types rather than the total edge set.

My weakest point is that this is an algorithmic anchor, not a discrete hardness result whose difficulty might dissolve in a continuum. The aggregate problem may be straightforward enough that the continuous version adds little complexity-theoretic insight, and the paper’s central contribution—fast maintenance after a small graph edit—does not automatically become a fast update after changing an entire cohort’s mass. The scenario also depends on genuinely repeated complete preference profiles. Still, the underlying objective and update question remain recognizable; on this anchor, I would make a modest positive case for a mirror, not claim a major new tractability phenomenon.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The dynamic rank-maximal matching theorem is the paper’s one strong computational anchor. Its contribution is the update guarantee: after a single vertex or edge change, the algorithm uses the stored stage structure to return a rank-maximal matching in \(O(r(m+n))\) time. The proposed DCRMU formulation gives an updated instance and asks for any rank-maximal allocation, but sets no required update-time bound. As written, it asks for reoptimization after an edit; the supplied feasible allocation \(x\) need not matter. That misses the theorem’s central computational question.

A stronger mirror would require maintaining a type-level allocation through a sequence of cohort, capacity, or preference changes, with a bound on the work per update. The paper gives no reason to expect its key structural guarantee—at most one augmenting path per rank—to extend to fractional cohort changes. Such a change may alter a continuum of mass across many edges. That is a real obstacle to transferring this particular algorithm and its bound.

It does not defeat the stronger question. Large assignment programmes with repeated preference and eligibility profiles make a credible high-multiplicity setting; fractional type-level flows can represent assignments, and the paper’s rank-maximal objective carries over naturally. Whether the resulting dynamic problem is routine or yields useful update bounds is a question about its answer, which is not grounds for rejecting the mirror. I can make a case that DCRMU, as stated, is too weak to mirror the paper’s main result. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The negative case is weak, and this anchor survives in a properly specified dynamic version.
