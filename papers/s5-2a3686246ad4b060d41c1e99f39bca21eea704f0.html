The continuous mirror question

Given \(m=2^n\) candidates, a list of \(\tau\) complete rankings \(t\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a target \(c^\star\), with no pairwise ties under \(\mu\), decide whether some bracket \(\pi\) makes \(c^\star\) the winner. A bracket is a permutation of the candidates paired in order over successive knockout rounds; each match is won by the candidate preferred by more than half the society. Describe the input and running time in \(m,\tau,L\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings of the \(m\) candidates, each carrying population mass \(\mu_t\). For each pair, the mass preferring one candidate determines the majority winner; the decision variable is the bracket \(\pi\), and the question is whether \(c^\star\) wins.

The objection that survived

The proposed decision problem is the established tournament-fixing problem, already known NP-hard, with a best-known general algorithm taking \(2^m\operatorname{poly}(m)\) time; this limits novelty but is not fatal under the programme's rule for outside-literature answers. [Kim, Suksompong, and Vassilevska Williams](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~mpkim/pubs/siam2017-tournaments.pdf)

The case FOR (proponent)

Under the requested anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying anchor: none of its named results classifies a general computational problem as tractable, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise. Theorem 1, proved here, gives an explicit majority graph \(G_n\) and, for every candidate \(j\), a bracket \(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(n;j)}\) that makes \(j\) win. Theorem 3, also proved here, constructs profiles \(R_n\) and \(\tilde R_n\) generating \(G_n\), with \(4n-2\) and \(4n-3\) preference lists respectively. Theorem 4 proves that a Poisson sample reproduces \(G_n\) with high probability above its stated growth threshold. These are structural construction and probability results, not complexity classifications.

The strongest positive case I can make is therefore a follow-on computational question, not a compliant mirror of a complexity result in the paper:

Continuous Possible-Bracket Winner. Given \(m=2^n\) candidates, a finite list of \(\tau\) complete rankings with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a target candidate \(c^\star\), decide whether there is a bracket—a permutation of the candidates, paired in order through successive knockout rounds—in which \(c^\star\) wins. Require strict majority in every pairwise comparison, so the winner of each match is unambiguous. A solution is a bracket witnessing the target’s victory. The input size is described by \(m,\tau\), and the binary encoding length \(L\).

A plausible regime is a large elimination-style contest whose participants’ rankings fall into a relatively small set of shared types: \(N\) is much larger than \(\tau\), and \(\tau\) is far smaller than the number of possible rankings. Mass \(\mu_t\) represents the fraction of participants with ranking \(t\). This is a natural high-multiplicity interpretation because the paper’s match outcomes depend only on pairwise majorities, and its definition of \(w_n\) already makes bracket choice central. Its Theorem 1 supplies explicit yes-instances for every target on the constructed \(G_n\); Theorem 3 shows that such a graph can come from a rational mass distribution, by assigning the rows of \(\tilde R_n\) equal masses and combining any repeated rankings. Neither theorem, however, settles the proposed problem for arbitrary inputs.

My expectation for this follow-on is hardness transfers: the choices concern the bracket and the candidate agenda, and any discrete profile embeds as rational masses \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). Continuization does not obviously remove that combinatorics. This is a prediction about the proposed problem, not a result established by this paper. Natural extensions include deciding whether *each* candidate can win under some bracket, adding the paper’s seeded constraints, and finding the smallest mass change that makes a target bracket-feasible.

The weakness is substantial: the paper’s headline question about the size of a commission does not carry over directly, since normalizing a society to total mass \(1\) removes population size as the objective. And for possible-winner testing, the masses can be aggregated into the majority graph before bracket search; continuous optimization offers no evident advantage. Theorem 4 is about random counts of discrete rankings, so it is a noise result rather than a continuous-population result. Thus the positive case is that the paper’s bracket-control question has a sensible high-multiplicity formulation. It is not a case that this paper already provides a computational-complexity anchor for ChoCo.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper has no named result classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1 constructs a majority graph and brackets; Theorem 3 constructs profiles realizing that graph; Theorem 4 bounds the probability that random voter counts reproduce it. None gives an algorithmic complexity result to mirror. Under ChoCo’s stated standard, that is a decisive reason not to treat this paper as an anchor.

The proposed possible-bracket question is well-posed, but it does not rescue the anchor. With strict majorities, its answer depends only on the pairwise majority graph: once that graph is computed from \(\mu\), the exact masses disappear. The same bracket question is the established tournament-fixing problem, formulated directly from pairwise match outcomes; Kim, Suksompong, and Vassilevska Williams study that problem and give an exact algorithm.[SIAM paper](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~mpkim/pubs/siam2017-tournaments.pdf) That prior work is not, by itself, a reason to reject a continuous mirror. The concern is more basic: this particular formulation puts a distribution at the input, but its computational object is the induced tournament. Theorem 1 supplies explicit yes-instances for that object; it does not classify the general decision problem.

Theorem 3’s \(4n-3\) figure is a count of preference lists, not a population fraction. Normalizing a society to total mass \(1\) removes the quantity the theorem bounds. Asking instead for a minimum number of supported ranking types would be a different ballot-representation problem: support size is \(\tau\), not population size. Theorem 4 does not supply a continuous-population result either. It samples discrete voters from fixed rankings, so it is a noise result of the kind the programme explicitly distinguishes from continuization.

A richer mirror could make masses matter—for example, minimize the mass of voters whose rankings must change before some bracket lets a target win, or maximize the target’s margin across its bracket. Those may be worthwhile questions, but they add a preference-change cost or a robustness objective absent from the paper’s results. They are new problems inspired by its setting, not continuous versions of Theorems 1–4. Likewise, the plausible large-commission scenario means I would not argue that high multiplicity is inherently unnatural here. The negative case is narrower: this paper offers no computational-complexity anchor, and its most natural bracket-feasibility follow-on discards the population masses after computing pairwise majorities. The case is weaker against the broader claim that some new mass-sensitive problem in this setting could be worth studying.
