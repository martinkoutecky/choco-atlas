The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses of buyer and seller types, where each type fixes its value, per-capita budget or supply, and compatibility, along with a price increment \(\varepsilon\), can one compute a type-level polyhedral clinching allocation and transfers in time polynomial in the support size and binary input length, with \(\mathrm{LW} \ge \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{LW}_{\mathrm{OPT}}\) whenever the paper’s price-increment condition holds?

The model it lives in

A rational-mass two-sided market with complete buyer and seller types. Type-level budgets and supplies scale with mass; variables \(w_{bs}\) represent goods transferred between compatible types, and liquid welfare is computed from aggregate type allocations and budgets.

The objection that survived

The type market can be represented as an ordinary finite PCA instance with rescaled budgets and supplies, so the mirror exposes no distinct population-specific optimization structure.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the liquid-welfare guarantee in Theorem 4.3 and the clinching computation stated in Theorem 3.1. It leaves the single-sample guarantee in Theorem 5.5 and individual incentive properties outside the proposed mass-level question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the divisible type-level auction be computed in time polynomial in the type support size and binary input length, despite the paper’s lack of a nontrivial bound on its \(\varepsilon\)-step iteration count?
  • Does the type-level clinching procedure preserve the stated welfare guarantee for rational masses and mass-scaled budgets and supplies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a type-compressed polyhedral clinching auction for a high-volume display-ad exchange. Think of many advertisers running repeated campaign templates and many publishers supplying standardized inventory. A buyer type records its per-unit value, budget, and eligible publisher types; a seller type records its reservation value, per-capita supply, and eligible buyer types. Members of a type share all those attributes. The number of participants can be much larger than the number of types, \(\tau_B+\tau_S\).

Let \(\mu_b\) and \(\nu_s\) be rational type shares on the buyer and seller sides, with population scales \(N_B\) and \(N_S\). Pooling a buyer type gives value \(v_b\) and budget \(N_B\mu_b B_b\); pooling a seller type gives value \(\rho_s\) and supply \(N_S\nu_s q_s\). The decision variable is the flow \(w_{bs}\) of goods from seller type \(s\) to buyer type \(b\). If \(x_b=\sum_s w_{bs}\), the liquid welfare is

\[ \mathrm{LW}(w)= \sum_b \min(v_bx_b,N_B\mu_bB_b) +\sum_s \rho_s\left(N_S\nu_sq_s-\sum_b w_{bs}\right). \]

This is population continuization: the masses represent people, and budgets and supplies scale with their type masses. Fractional goods are already part of the paper’s model.

Lead anchor — Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper. For the polyhedral clinching auction with divisible goods and truthful seller values, if the price increment satisfies \(\varepsilon\le v_{\min}^2/(v_{\max}-v_{\min})\), then its liquid welfare is at least half the optimal liquid welfare. Here \(v_{\min}\) and \(v_{\max}\) include the virtual buyers’ values, and the auction assumes values are multiples of \(\varepsilon\).

The corresponding continuous problem is Mass-PCA-LW. Its instance is the rational-mass type market above, together with \(\varepsilon\) and the values, supplies, budgets, and type graph. Its solution is a type-level PCA allocation and transfer profile. The question is whether one can compute that outcome from \(\tau_B+\tau_S\) and the binary input length, while obtaining \(\mathrm{LW}\ge \tfrac12\mathrm{LW}_{\mathrm{OPT}}\) under the same price-increment condition. The optimal benchmark is the maximum of the displayed welfare expression over feasible type flows.

There is a direct reason to expect the welfare guarantee to survive: pooling identical agents gives exactly the same aggregate liquid-welfare term, and the pooled budget and supply scale linearly with mass. That makes the problem recognizable to the authors as their auction over type cohorts. I expect a Class A answer: the population count has been removed, leaving a type graph and a polyhedral clinching computation. The theorem itself does not establish the requested bit-complexity bound, however. Its auction advances prices in \(\varepsilon\)-steps, and the paper notes that the divisible version has no nontrivial bound on the number of iterations. A type-compressed, exact event-driven implementation is a real part of the question.

Supporting anchor — Theorem 3.1, cited from Hirai and Sato (2022). For the clinching polytope \(P^i_{w,d}\), a vector \(\xi_i\in P^i_{w,d}\) can be computed in polynomial time. For any such vector, its total clinch satisfies
\[ \xi_i(E_i)=f_{x,d}(N)-f_{x,d}(N\setminus\{i\}), \]
where \(f_{x,d}\) is monotone submodular and is given by equation (1) in the paper. Finally, if the virtual buyer for seller \(j\) is priced below \(\rho_j\), no real buyer clinches goods from that seller in that step.

Its continuous mirror is Mass-Clinch. Given rational type masses, a buyer-type/seller-type graph, current aggregate allocations and residual demands, and residual seller capacities, compute a maximal vector \(z_b\) for a selected buyer type \(b\). The vector must leave unchanged the set of feasible future allocations to all other buyer types—the same “do not affect other buyers’ possibilities” condition used in the paper. The question is whether this can be done in time polynomial in the number of types and the input bit length, with the corresponding submodular formula and reserve-price guard. I expect Class A: this is the paper’s flow-based clinching subproblem on a smaller graph. This anchor is cited rather than a new result of the paper, but it is the computational step used inside the auction.

The limit of this case is incentive semantics. Pooling identical agents preserves aggregate feasibility and liquid welfare, but a pooled buyer behaves like a coalition; it does not by itself preserve the paper’s individual DSIC claim. So these mirrors cover the clinching computation and Theorem 4.3’s welfare guarantee, not every strategic property of the mechanism. I would also leave Theorem 5.5’s single-sample extension outside this case: one sample per named seller needs a separate, defensible mass-level sampling model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this mirror has little population-specific computation to expose. In the proposed cohort model, a buyer type of mass \(\mu_b\), per-person budget \(B_b\), and value \(v_b\) can be represented as one buyer with budget \(N_B\mu_b B_b\) and value \(v_b\). A seller type similarly becomes one seller with supply \(N_S\nu_s q_s\). With divisible goods and uniform compatibility within each type pair, the aggregate allocation is feasible, and splitting it evenly among cohort members gives the same total liquid welfare. The type-level market is therefore a finite market with \(\tau_B+\tau_S\) participants and rescaled parameters.

That weakens the proposed mirror of Theorem 4.3: its welfare guarantee applies directly to that finite type-level instance. The population masses add a representation and an interpretation, but no new optimization structure. There is a real strategic gap. If each cohort is one pooled buyer, that buyer can report and spend as a coalition; if each person remains a strategic agent in an atomless continuum, one person’s report changes no mass and DSIC becomes vacuous. Keeping finite, individually meaningful deviations returns to a finite population. Thus the welfare comparison can be mirrored cleanly, while the mechanism’s individual incentive semantics do not pass through the same aggregation.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 3.1 has the same limitation. Its clinching step is a flow computation on the market graph. A type quotient gives a smaller graph when neighborhoods and other relevant attributes are shared; applying the cited result then uses the same flow machinery. If neighborhoods differ by person, they belong in the type description and the support may grow to the number of participants. Either way, the mirror introduces no new pricing problem or population-driven algorithmic structure.

The best remaining opening is the exact running time of the divisible auction. The paper gives no polynomial bound in the binary input length for its \(\varepsilon\)-step price process, and a type-level event-driven implementation could be worthwhile. That question, though, concerns the finite-market auction routine just as much as its population interpretation. I cannot honestly rule out a useful algorithmic result for standardized cohorts; the negative case is strongest against the claim that these anchors reveal a distinct, population-continuous complexity landscape.
