The case FOR (proponent)

There is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition in the paper asserting a computational result. It does describe PMA and CCE algorithms and gives complexity claims in prose, including an \(O(n!)\) full search for CCE; PMA is presented as an existing method cited to [27,28]. Under your anchor rule, there are no eligible anchors, so I can’t honestly present a per-anchor question. The strongest positive case I can make is therefore an unanchored candidate based on the paper’s CCE algorithm.

A natural mirror is Continuous Compromise Choice Exploration. Let \(X\) be a finite agenda of \(n\) alternatives. Its voter types are complete strict rankings \(\succ_t\) over \(X\), and the instance gives the \(\tau\) ranking types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\), summing to \(1\). For a proposed common ranking \(\pi\), let \(d_t(\pi)\) be the number of inversions between \(\succ_t\) and \(\pi\). Set
\[ \bar d_\mu(\pi)=\sum_t\mu_t d_t(\pi),\qquad \sigma_\mu(\pi)=\sqrt{\sum_t\mu_t\bigl(d_t(\pi)-\bar d_\mu(\pi)\bigr)^2}. \]
The problem is to find every \(\pi\) minimizing \(\bar d_\mu(\pi)+\sigma_\mu(\pi)\), and return its first-ranked alternative as a consensus choice. This preserves CCE’s sorting-count measure, average-plus-standard-deviation objective, and output. It changes the input from a list of participants to the distribution of their rankings.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large digital-democracy or municipal consultation on a fixed, small agenda. For seven alternatives there are at most \(7!\) ranking types, so a consultation with many thousands of participants could have far more people than distinct types. People with the same complete ranking are indistinguishable to CCE, which uses no other personal attributes. The paper’s own trial has only five participants, so this regime is suggested by its intended applications rather than demonstrated by its experiment. I would limit this mirror to CCE; the paper’s later sublated-choice step relies on discussion and does not specify a computational optimization problem.

The paper enumerates \(n!\) candidate rankings. With type masses, each score can instead be computed from the \(\tau\) types, giving an exhaustive bound of \(n!\,\mathrm{poly}(n,\tau,L)\), where \(L\) is the mass encoding length. Thus the problem is tractable for a fixed agenda, but the mirror does not remove the permutation search when \(n\) grows. I would expect any general hardness to come from the agenda and to transfer from the discrete version, rather than to arise specifically from continuous mass. The paper itself establishes neither that hardness nor a polynomial-time result.

I think the authors would recognize this as their CCE objective applied to a large society. The weak point is substantial: the paper’s score already depends only on the empirical distribution of individual rankings, so the mirror is a high-multiplicity reformulation of its finite-participant problem, and its main search bottleneck remains. That makes it a plausible continuous-population question, but it does not supply the numbered computational anchor your standard requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The requested case has a formal opening: the paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. PMA is an existing method the authors describe, and CCE’s \(O(n!)\) search bound appears in prose. So the proponent raised no eligible result to mirror; their CCE proposal is unanchored under the stated standard.

The strongest substantive objection is that Continuous CCE adds no new decision model. Its input is a list of rankings, and its score depends only on the distances from those rankings to a candidate ranking. Grouping identical rankings and recording their frequencies therefore preserves the objective exactly. Conversely, any rational distribution over ranking types can be represented by replicating voters in the corresponding proportions. The continuous formulation is a lossless frequency encoding of the same CCE problem, and it keeps the \(n!\) search over candidate rankings. For the paper’s intended small agendas, that makes the gain look like input compression rather than a new optimization structure.

That is a limited case against the mirror’s research value, not a decisive objection under ChoCo’s standards: high multiplicity is expressly in scope, and a digital consultation with many participants and a fixed small agenda is a plausible setting. The same frequency compression also makes a mass-weighted PMA variant easy to define. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The negative case is strongest on the lack of an eligible anchor and weakest on the underlying model: CCE’s continuous version is simple, but it is a sensible population question.
