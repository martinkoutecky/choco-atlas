The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of objects and facts, and a finite support of complete claim-pattern types \(t\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), define \(r_1(t)=1\), \(b_1(f)=\sum_{t:f\in A_t}\mu_t\), \(r_{k+1}(t)=\sum_{f\in A_t}b_k(f)\), and \(b_{k+1}(f)=\sum_{t:f\in A_t}\mu_t r_{k+1}(t)\). Compute the eventual total preorders of types by \(r_k\) and facts by \(b_k\), or determine whether those preorders fail to stabilize.

The model it lives in

A crowdsourcing population labels a fixed finite set of objects. Each type \(t\) is a complete pattern \(A_t\) of claims and abstentions, with at most one claimed fact per object, and \(\mu_t\) is its population share. The iteration assigns per-worker trust scores to types and mass-weighted belief scores to facts; the output is the pair of eventual preorders, with no optimization objective.

The objection that survived

Theorem 5.4 supplies no effective bound or stopping test for \(J_N\), so it does not itself show how to compute the stable rankings in finite time from an instance.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 5.4's ordinal stabilization result for UnboundedSums. It leaves the paper's axiomatic results and its analyses of Voting, Sums, and other operators untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do the mass-weighted rankings always stabilize for every rational-mass instance?
  • Can the stable preorders be computed exactly, and with what complexity in the support size, number of facts, and mass bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. The paper proves no result placing a problem in \(P\), proving it hard, or giving another complexity classification. Its strongest algorithmic anchor is Theorem 5.4, proved in this paper: for every finite truth-discovery network, the rankings produced by UnboundedSums eventually stop changing. More precisely, there is a limiting ordinal operator \(T^*\), and for each network \(N\) there is a finite \(J_N\) such that every iteration \(n\ge J_N\) induces the same rankings of sources and facts as \(T^*\). This is an algorithmic convergence result, not a running-time bound. Since the paper offers no comparable hardness result, it is the best anchor available; under a rule requiring a complexity-class result, the paper has no qualifying anchor.

My lead mirror is Mass-UnboundedSums Stabilization. Consider a large crowdsourcing service where many workers label the same fixed, finite set of objects. A type \(t\) is a worker’s complete pattern of claims and abstentions: a set \(A_t\) of facts, with at most one fact per object. The instance is a finite support of \(\tau\) such types, each with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The mass is the fraction of workers with that pattern. A plausible regime is hundreds of thousands of workers answering a common questionnaire or labeling a stable catalog, with far fewer distinct response patterns than workers.

For fact scores \(b_k(f)\) and per-worker trust scores \(r_k(t)\), use the mass-weighted UnboundedSums updates
\[ r_1(t)=1,\qquad b_1(f)=\sum_{t:f\in A_t}\mu_t, \]
\[ r_{k+1}(t)=\sum_{f\in A_t}b_k(f),\qquad b_{k+1}(f)=\sum_{t:f\in A_t}\mu_t r_{k+1}(t). \]
The problem is: given the objects, facts, type patterns, and masses, compute the eventual total preorder of the supported types by trust and of the facts by belief—or determine that the rankings do not stabilize. The solution is the pair of exact preorders; there is no optimization objective in the paper’s underlying task. This preserves its question of how a truth-discovery procedure ranks sources and facts. It groups workers only when the claims the model uses are identical, and ranks a type by the trust of each worker in it.

The high-multiplicity connection is exact for rational populations: if \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), the mass-weighted recurrence gives the same rankings as the discrete UnboundedSums recurrence on \(n_t\) cloned workers of each type, up to a common positive scale at each iteration. The continuous question therefore asks whether Theorem 5.4’s ordinal stabilization persists when that duplicated electorate is represented by type masses. I expect stabilization and a tractable algorithm, using the finite weighted update matrices. Further questions include bounding the stabilization time in \(\tau\), \(|F|\), and the mass bit length \(L\), and computing the stable rankings in polynomial bit complexity. The paper proves none of these weighted claims.

The weakness is substantial: this mirror extends an algorithmic convergence theorem, not a hardness result, and Theorem 5.4 gives no bound on \(J_N\). Also, identical full response patterns are credible in repeated common-task settings but not in every crowdsourcing dataset. If the requested anchors must establish standard complexity classifications, that weakness is decisive: this paper supplies no such anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed anchor is not a complexity result of the kind ChoCo is set up to mirror. Theorem 5.4 proves eventual stabilization of UnboundedSums on each fixed finite network. It gives no complexity classification or bound on \(J_N\), and the paper’s main results characterize and evaluate axioms for truth discovery operators. The proponent’s question—compute the eventual rankings for a society of weighted claim-pattern types—is a new algorithmic problem, not a continuous version of a computational problem whose complexity this paper establishes.

The proposed setting is otherwise credible. Many workers answering the same fixed questionnaire could share complete claim patterns, and rational masses faithfully represent cloned discrete sources. I would not reject it on multiplicity grounds. Nor does switching to rankings of types and facts break the model’s output. The difficulty for the negative case is that this leaves a well-posed continuous question, even if it is outside the paper’s main contribution.

A stronger intervention mirror, such as finding the least mass of source types to change to reverse a fact ranking, has no footing in the paper: it specifies no edit costs or permitted interventions, and the authors explicitly set manipulation and collusion aside. Adding those choices would create a new problem rather than mirror a result here.

So the best case against is a scope case: the paper offers no named complexity result to anchor, and its most promising computational extension moves beyond what its theorems study. That does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. If ChoCo counts exact computation of an operator’s rankings as an eligible computational question, the proponent’s mirror survives and my negative case is weak.
