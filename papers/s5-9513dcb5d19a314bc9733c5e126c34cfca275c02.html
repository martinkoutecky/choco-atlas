The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no eligible named anchor under your rule. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition stating a complexity result. Its nearest internal results are the worked calculations: equation (10) expresses the total Shapley–Shubik index as a weighted count of critical coalition pairs, and equations (11)–(13) compute the EEC members’ indices. The paper cites Matsui and Matsui for NP-completeness, but that is an external result, not a result proved or numbered here. So I cannot honestly present an anchored positive case.

The strongest plausible mirror of the paper’s actual computational task is Limit-SSI by type. Take a finite set of voter types \(T\), each with rational mass \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a positive integer vote weight \(w_t\). Let \(W=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t w_t\), and let \(\theta\in(0,1)\) be the quota as a fraction of total weight. For each sufficiently divisible replication factor \(r\), form a weighted voting game with \(r\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\), each carrying weight \(w_t\), and quota \(\theta rW\). Let \(\phi_t(r)\) be the sum of the individual Shapley–Shubik indices of type-\(t\) voters in that game. The problem is: given \((T,\mu,w,\theta)\), determine whether \(\pi_t=\lim_{r\to\infty}\phi_t(r)\) exists for every type, and, if so, compute the vector \((\pi_t)_{t\in T}\). A solution is that vector; there is no optimization variable, because the underlying task is to compute how pivotal power is distributed.

A plausible regime is a very large federation or cooperative whose members vote yes or no and fall into a few charter classes with identical voting weights. The number of members grows while the number of types stays fixed. Mass is the fraction of members in each class, and members of a type are indistinguishable in every respect this model uses. That seems recognizable to the authors: it preserves their weighted yes–no game and their question about pivotality, while aggregating power at the type level. I would expect this limit problem to be tractable; a natural conjectured answer for an interior quota is \(\pi_t=\mu_t w_t/W\). The work would be to establish the limit and its conditions, including the effects of ties and arithmetic properties of the weights. It would also prompt questions about convergence rates and extensions to abstentions or other voting rules.

This is a candidate mirror of the paper’s SSI computation, not a mirror anchored in a named complexity result. It covers neither a claimed complexity classification nor the paper’s Karnaugh-map technique, and the EEC’s six-member example itself is not a convincing high-multiplicity regime. The weakest point is that the continuous question is a limit of discrete SSI values: the paper does not formulate this limit, and the tractability might come from the limit discarding much of the coalition-level detail that makes its finite calculation interesting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no eligible named complexity result to mirror. Equation (10) is a counting identity; equations (11)–(13) work out the indices for one six-member EEC game. The NP-completeness result is cited from elsewhere. That makes the paper a weak ChoCo target on the requested grounds: its main contribution is a tutorial calculation, not a complexity classification or algorithmic result.

The proposed Limit-SSI mirror also has a real modeling gap. In the paper, a voter is pivotal when that individual joins a coalition in a permutation. In an atomless society, a single individual has zero mass, so changing that person leaves the aggregate coalition unchanged. The continuous model therefore has no direct pivotal-voter interpretation or canonical random ordering. A finite replication sequence can define an aggregate limit, but then the sequence is part of the new problem: the same distribution \(\mu\) represents many finite populations, and their exact SSI values need not agree. The limit may be a worthwhile asymptotic question, yet it is a new statistic whose connection to the paper’s finite computation would need to be established.

That argument does not defeat the strongest mirror. Large voting bodies with many members in a few identical weight classes are plausible high-multiplicity settings, and a type-level limit of their power indices could be a sensible research question. The paper’s symmetry calculations even make such an extension recognizable to its authors. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario. The strongest negative case is narrower: this paper itself supplies no named computational result to anchor one, and its proposed limit requires a substantive new definition before it becomes a continuous SSI problem.
