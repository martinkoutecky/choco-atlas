The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no qualifying named computational result. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity classification or algorithmic result. Its numbered equations and reported findings are empirical estimates of SISU’s effects on student composition. The deferred-acceptance procedure in Appendix A is described as institutional context, not presented as a numbered computational result. So there is no honest anchor to restate, and no continuous problem I can supply *per anchor* under your criteria.

There is an adjacent continuous question one could study: take a finite set of programs with seat capacities, and a distribution \(\mu\) over complete student types, where each type includes its ranked program choices, ENEM-based priorities, and quota eligibility. Scale capacities with the cohort and ask for the mass assignment produced by student-proposing deferred acceptance. A regime with millions of applicants and repeated type profiles is plausible, though the paper does not establish that the support size \(\tau\) is small. This would mirror the assignment procedure the paper describes; it would not mirror a named computational result or the paper’s empirical causal estimates.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no named computational result to continuize. Equations (1) and (2) estimate causal effects of SISU adoption; the tables report changes in student composition. Appendix A describes deferred acceptance as part of the institutional setting, but gives no computational claim about it. A continuous deferred-acceptance problem would therefore be a new question inspired by the setting, not a mirror of a result in this paper.

The most favorable version is coherent: put students into types defined by their ranked program choices, ENEM priorities, and quota eligibility; represent them by masses; scale program capacities to the cohort; and compute the mass assignment under student-proposing deferred acceptance. Large admissions cohorts make that population regime plausible, and composition outcomes do not require tracking student identities. So the objection is not that mass is meaningless or that the mechanism cannot be restated.

But that assignment answers a different question from the paper’s. It takes applications, scores, and capacities as inputs, while the paper asks how adopting SISU changes who applies and enrolls. The authors identify reduced frictions, changing competition, exam retaking, and migration as parts of that response, and they lack application data to model those channels directly. A closer computational analogue would need to model applicant behavior under both centralized and decentralized admissions, then compare the resulting compositions. That requires new behavioral primitives and a new computational target; it does not continue the paper’s empirical estimates. The proposed mass assignment may be worthwhile on its own, but this paper supplies no computational result for it to mirror.
