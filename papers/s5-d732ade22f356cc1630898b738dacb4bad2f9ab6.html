The continuous mirror question

Given a graph \(G\) with candidate set \(C=V(G)\), a nonempty zone \(Z\subseteq C\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(C\), decide whether weighted IRV elects a member of \(Z\) for every nonempty slate \(K\subseteq C\) with \(K\cap Z\neq\emptyset\).

The model it lives in

Use ranking types as complete voter descriptions and their rational masses as population shares. For each slate, assign each type’s mass to its highest-ranked remaining candidate, eliminate the lowest-scoring candidate using fixed deterministic tie-breaking, and test whether the winner always lies in \(Z\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the paper’s profile-based exclusion hardness in Theorems 5 and 6. It does not establish hardness for the shortest-path tree model or cover the tree algorithms.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does bounded ranking support size \(\tau\) make continuous exclusion verification tractable?
  • Does Theorem 5’s hardness persist when rankings are induced by shortest-path distances on a fixed tree with rational mass at each vertex?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s profile-based general-graph hardness: a large electorate is a distribution over complete rankings, while the candidates and the exclusion-zone question stay discrete.

Let \(C\) be the finite candidate set, with a fixed deterministic tie-breaking order. A type \(t\) is a complete ranking of \(C\), and the society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) ranking types with positive mass, where each \(\mu_t\) is a binary rational and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). In each IRV round, a candidate receives the mass of voters whose highest-ranked remaining candidate is that candidate; the lowest-scoring candidate is eliminated, with the paper’s tie-breaking rule. This type is complete for these questions: voters with the same ranking are indistinguishable to IRV.

A plausible regime is a large national or organizational election with \(N\) voters but far fewer distinct ballots: for example, hundreds of thousands or millions of voters represented by hundreds or thousands of ranking types. Cohorts may share rankings because of party alignment, region, or repeated ballot patterns. Mass means the fraction of the electorate in each cohort.

Lead: Continuous IRV-Exclusion. An instance is \((C,\mu,Z)\), where \(Z\subseteq C\) is nonempty. Ask whether, for every nonempty candidate slate \(K\subseteq C\) with \(K\cap Z\neq\emptyset\), the weighted IRV winner belongs to \(Z\). The output is yes or no; a no answer has a concise witness slate \(K\) and its IRV outcome. This is the paper’s exclusion-zone question over a continuous society: the universal quantification over candidate slates remains exactly the same.

The anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper (with proof details deferred to the appendix; the IRV-only hardness it builds on is cited from earlier work). It states that, for every deterministic rank-based elimination rule satisfying Strong Forced Elimination, \(\mathcal R\)-Exclusion is co-NP-complete. The paper says IRV is among those rules. The continuous question is therefore whether a proposed zone has the winner guarantee for every candidate slate, now when each ranking contributes rational mass.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. For any finite discrete profile with \(n_t\) voters of ranking type \(t\), set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), where \(N=\sum_t n_t\). Every round’s weighted scores are the discrete scores divided by \(N\); hence every slate has exactly the same elimination sequence and winner. The discrete instance maps directly to the continuous one, preserving whether \(Z\) is an exclusion zone. This makes co-NP-hardness transfer, while a slate witnessing failure can be checked by simulating weighted IRV with polynomial-bit rational arithmetic.

A second, distinct question is Continuous Min-IRV-Exclusion. The instance is \((C,\mu)\); output a nonempty exclusion zone \(Z^\star\) of minimum cardinality. Its anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper: for any deterministic rank-based elimination rule satisfying SFE, computing a minimum-cardinality \(\mathcal R\)-exclusion zone is NP-hard. The continuous problem uses the same feasible zones and objective, with the electorate represented by ranking masses. The same discrete-to-rational-mass embedding preserves every slate’s winner and therefore the set of valid zones and its minimum size. I expect hardness to transfer here as well. This is a useful separate mirror because it concerns finding a smallest stability certificate, rather than verifying a proposed one.

These questions cover the paper’s profile-based exclusion hardness results, not its polynomial-time tree results. They generate natural follow-ups: does bounded support size \(\tau\) help, and what happens when rankings must be realizable as shortest-path preferences on a fixed tree with rational mass at each voter location?

My weakest point is the boundary of that scope. The paper’s central graph-voting model constrains rankings by shortest-path distances, while the SFE hardness section works with preference profiles and its reductions do not establish hardness under that metric restriction. The mirror is strongest for the paper’s explicitly profile-based IRV exclusion problem; it should not be presented as a continuation of the tree-metric theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to what Theorems 5 and 6 actually anchor. In Appendix F, the exclusion problems take an arbitrary preference profile \(P\) over candidates; the graph is not required to generate those rankings by shortest-path distance. The RX3C reductions are not shown to produce graph-metric profiles. So these theorems do not establish hardness for the paper’s graph-voting model. A distribution over ranking types would be a sensible mirror of the profile-based problems, but presenting it as a mirror of the paper’s spatial results overstates the connection.

That objection does not defeat either mirror on its own merits. For Theorem 5, replacing ballot counts by rational masses over rankings preserves each slate’s elimination sequence under the discrete-to-continuous embedding. A losing slate remains a certificate that a proposed zone fails. For Theorem 6, the same embedding preserves the winner for every slate, hence preserves the valid zones and their minimum cardinality. Both are well-posed high-multiplicity questions, and their hardness would be a meaningful Class B result under this programme’s criteria.

The stronger version for the paper’s central model also looks viable: keep the tree and its candidate locations fixed, but assign rational population mass to each vertex, with rankings still induced by distance. That represents cohorts living at the same network locations. Weighted IRV is well-defined, and exclusion zones retain their meaning. The paper’s integer-count dynamic program may not carry over directly to rational vote totals, but that creates a computational question; it does not make the mirror degenerate.

I therefore can’t make the requested universal negative case honestly. The profile-based anchors are not evidence for the specific graph-metric results, and the existing interval treatment mentioned in the paper is analytic rather than a computational collision. But the weighted-ranking mirrors and the mass-on-tree mirror survive the available objections. The negative case is weak precisely where the better mirrors begin.
