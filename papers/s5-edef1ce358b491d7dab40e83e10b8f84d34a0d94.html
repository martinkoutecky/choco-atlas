What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest point for a continuous mirror is that large weighted assemblies can have many interchangeable voters in a few voting classes. That suggests a population distribution over voter types, with class mass representing the fraction of members in each class. But the paper has no named computational result to anchor that mirror to.

The closest results are Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: if a reasonable quarrel conception is disposed to induce non-monotonicity over quarrellers, then every measure of voting power violates the standard quarrel postulate based on it; and Theorem 10.2, also proved here: the Penrose–Banzhaf measure satisfies that postulate for a symmetric, weak quarrel. Theorem 10.1 gives the corresponding result for the Shapley–Shubik index. These are structural and normative claims, not claims about an algorithm, running time, hardness, or parameterized complexity. They therefore cannot serve as the required anchors.

A mass model exposes a further difficulty. The paper’s quarrel is between two individual players. In an atomless population, two marked players have zero mass; making the quarrel operate between two positive-mass classes would change that primitive. And replacing equiprobable divisions with a distribution over voting behavior would switch to the behavioral approach, which the paper explicitly sets aside for a priori voting power.

So there is a plausible new research direction here, but no per-anchor continuous problem that can honestly be presented as a continuous form of this paper’s computational result. Under the stated criteria, inventing one would overstate the positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s main concession is decisive under ChoCo’s test: this paper proves no running time, hardness result, or algorithmic classification. Theorem 4.1 is a universal impossibility claim about a normative postulate; Theorems 10.1 and 10.2 say that two measures satisfy it under a specified quarrel. Turning any of these into a computational problem would require adding one the paper does not pose. A continuous study of voting power might be interesting, but it would not mirror a computational result here.

The most promising anchor is Theorem 4.1, whose proof turns a dummy into a non-dummy after a quarrel. But that jump relies on an individual having positive pivotal force. In an atomless population whose outcome depends on aggregate vote mass, changing one person’s vote changes no mass. Every individual is therefore a dummy; a quarrel between two individuals also changes no mass. The theorem’s mechanism disappears. Giving the pair positive mass can restore influence, but then the actors are blocs, not the paper’s individual players.

Theorem 10.2 (Penrose–Banzhaf) and Theorem 10.1 (Shapley–Shubik) face the same mismatch in their definitions. Banzhaf power counts pivotal divisions under independent, equiprobable individual votes; Shapley–Shubik power counts pivotal positions in a random ordering of individuals. With many interchangeable voters, an individual’s influence vanishes in the population limit. A type-level substitute—one fair coin or one position per type—makes each type a collective player and assigns probability over types rather than over individual divisions or orderings. Rescaling individual influence could preserve finer asymptotic information, but it would require specifying a finite-population sequence and scaling regime; the mass distribution alone does not supply that.

Large weighted assemblies are the strongest plausible setting, and repeated voter classes make high multiplicity sensible there. That supports studying a related model, such as power for collective blocs or asymptotic influence in growing assemblies. The obstacle is specific to this paper: its results concern the power of identified players under structural quarrels, while its measures derive from finite individual pivotality. The available rescues either make individual power collapse or change the unit of agency and the probability model. The negative case is not a proof that no adjacent project could be worthwhile; it is that none of the paper’s named results yields a worthwhile continuous *computational mirror* without replacing what the paper studies.
