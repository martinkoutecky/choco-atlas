The continuous mirror question

For each \(N\ge2\), let \(D_N\) contain finite-support distributions \(\mu\) on rational locations in \([0,1]\), with \(N\mu_x\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}\) and total mass \(1\). A randomized mechanism \(M_N\) maps \(\mu\) to a facility location \(Y\in[0,1]\); require that, whenever \(N\mu_x\ge1\), for every report \(y\in[0,1]\), \(\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim M_N(\mu)}|x-Y|\le\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim M_N(\mu-\frac1N\delta_x+\frac1N\delta_y)}|x-Y|\). Determine the minimum, over truthful mechanism families \((M_N)_{N\ge2}\), of \(\sup_{N\ge2,\,\mu\in D_N}\left[\sup_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim M_N(\mu)}|x-Y|-\frac{\max\operatorname{supp}(\mu)-\min\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}2\right]\), and give an attaining rule computable from the support.

The model it lives in

A society is a finite-support mass distribution over ideal-location types in \([0,1]\), with \(N\) unit agents represented by mass \(1/N\). The mechanism returns a lottery over facility locations, and strategyproofness compares the true type’s expected distance before and after one unit of mass reports another location. BLRC selects \(1/2\), the support minimum, the support maximum, or their midpoint with probabilities \(1/3,1/6,1/6,1/3\), respectively.

What the mirror covers

Theorems 5 and 12 establish the tight randomized, one-facility maximum-cost additive error of \(1/6\). The mirror leaves the deterministic, average-cost, and multiple-facility results outside its scope.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: continuous high-multiplicity mechanism design for locating one facility under the maximum-cost objective.

A location type is an ideal point \(x\in[0,1]\), and \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of residents with that ideal point. To keep truthfulness meaningful, include a population granularity \(N\), with \(N\mu_x\) integral: one agent’s misreport moves mass \(1/N\) from \(x\) to another point \(y\). A randomized mechanism maps \((N,\mu)\) to a lottery over facility locations. Its maximum cost is \(\sup_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mathbb{E}|x-Y|\); the optimal maximum cost is half the distance between the support’s endpoints. The objective is to minimize the worst-case additive gap, subject to truthful individual reports.

A plausible regime is a municipal service for a very large population whose residents fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of shared ideal locations—for example, neighborhoods or common preferred sites. The mass at each location records its share of residents. This matches the paper’s facility-location setting and its public-library motivation; the continuous description is especially natural when the question is about population shares rather than headcounts.

The best anchors are Theorem 5 and Theorem 12, both proved in this paper. Theorem 5 says that every randomized truthful-in-expectation mechanism for one facility has additive error at least \(1/6\) for maximum cost. Theorem 12 says that BLRC is truthful in expectation and has additive error at most \(1/6\): it places the facility at \(1/2\) with probability \(1/3\), at the leftmost and rightmost reports with probability \(1/6\) each, and at their midpoint with probability \(1/3\).

Their continuous mirror is the problem of finding the smallest worst-case additive error achievable by a truthful randomized mechanism on these mass distributions, and a mechanism attaining it. Theorem 5 supplies the lower bound; Theorem 12 supplies the matching construction. Since every such rational distribution with granularity \(N\) is a finite profile, the paper’s results give the exact answer \(1/6\) for this high-multiplicity version. Evaluating BLRC from the support takes a scan for its endpoints and midpoint, so its rule is directly computable from \(\tau\) types.

There is an important limit to this case. Under the task’s strict anchor requirement, the paper has no named computational-complexity result: Theorems 5 and 12 bound mechanism performance and establish truthfulness, but do not classify a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or otherwise. The runtime observation about evaluating BLRC follows from its formula; it is not a complexity result claimed by the paper. So this is a credible continuous-population re-expression of a result in the paper, but not a strong ChoCo complexity anchor. It does not expose hardness that might dissolve under continuization.

The weakest point is strategic: in a genuinely atomless population, a single agent has zero mass, so changing their report does not change \(\mu\) and truthfulness becomes vacuous. Keeping \(N\) and the \(1/N\) deviation makes the question precise and preserves the paper’s individual-incentive condition, but it also makes the mirror a high-multiplicity family of finite strategic problems rather than a pure atomless limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against rests on ChoCo’s computational gate. Theorem 5 and Theorem 12 are the proponent’s best anchors, and both are real results about truthful mechanisms. They establish an additive-error lower bound and a matching mechanism; neither states the complexity of a computational problem. The paper studies which mechanisms are truthful and how well they perform, rather than the complexity of computing an outcome or solving an instance.

The most favorable repair is the proposed mass-distribution model with granularity \(N\), where a report moves mass \(1/N\). That gives a sensible high-multiplicity interpretation and keeps individual truthfulness meaningful. But it still yields no complexity landscape: for one facility, evaluating BLRC from a finite support takes a scan for its endpoints and midpoint, while the paper’s theorems already establish the \(1/6\) guarantee. A pure atomless version removes the individual incentive constraint, since one person’s report changes no mass. A harder question about synthesizing mechanisms would need a new mechanism class and an encoding for its constraints; that would be a new research problem, not a computational mirror of these theorems.

So the mirror is mathematically coherent, and the population interpretation is plausible. The negative case is that it offers little for this programme: the paper supplies no named computational-complexity result to mirror, and the proposed anchors yield no nontrivial computational task.
