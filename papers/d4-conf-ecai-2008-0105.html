What the mirror covers

Propositions 1–8 establish semantic preservation conditions for individual preference change; none supplies a computational result for the proposed population tally.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive reading is a population of people in a large deliberative community who receive the same piece of information. A type would be a complete mental state in the paper’s sense: a normality ordering and a preference ordering over the possible worlds. Mass \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of the community with mental state \(t\). The common revision rule updates each type after learning \(\alpha\). One could then ask what fraction satisfies a chosen formula-level preference \(P(\beta)\) afterward.

That gives a concrete possible extension: given a finite support of such types with rational masses, \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), a revision function, and one of the paper’s preference semantics, compute
\[ \sum_{t:\,M_t\star\alpha\models P(\beta)}\mu_t. \]
The scenario is plausible when many people share a relatively small number of coarse mental-state types. The update is the action; the requested output is the resulting mass. But this is an aggregate evaluation problem I have added. The paper has no population, collective objective, or optimization over actions.

The anchor requirement is the decisive limit. The paper has no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its closest result is Proposition 1, proved in the paper: the preference-satisfaction postulate \((P1)\), “\(P\alpha\to[\star\alpha]P\alpha\),” holds if the revision function satisfies positive and negative uniformity, and it also holds for any lifting operator under the LTW semantics. That is a semantic preservation result for an individual model, not a result about the complexity of a computational problem. Propositions 2–8 have the same character. So there is no qualifying anchor, and no honest anchor-specific continuous problem or complexity prediction to hand to a prover.

The weakest point is also the central one: moving from an individual’s preference change to the distribution of preference changes in a population adds an aggregation question the authors do not study. The proposed extension may interest them, but I would not claim that they would recognize it as the continuous form of a problem established by this paper. The positive case is therefore an unanchored research direction, not a strong mirror case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 is its closest anchor, but it says when an individual preference-satisfaction postulate holds: if the revision function has the stated uniformity properties, or the LTW semantics is used, then \(P\alpha\to[\star\alpha]P\alpha\). Propositions 2–8 are similar semantic claims. None studies an algorithmic problem, its complexity, or an optimization task.

The best population version of Proposition 1 is straightforward. Treat each complete mental state \(t\) as a type, and let all types receive the same \(\alpha\) under a revision setup covered by the proposition. Then the types satisfying \(P\alpha\) before revision are a subset of those satisfying it afterward, so
\[ \sum_{t:M_t\models P\alpha}\mu_t \;\le\; \sum_{t:M_t\star\alpha\models P\alpha}\mu_t. \]
That is a valid mass-level statement. But it is just Proposition 1 summed over a distribution. Computing either sum for a finite support is a tally over types; the paper supplies no complexity question for which that tally would be a mirror.

One could make the proposal more ambitious: choose a fact or information campaign to maximize the share of a population that prefers some formula afterward. That is a plausible new project, but its campaign options, costs, and collective objective would have to be added. The paper studies how a given belief revision changes one agent’s formula-level preferences; it does not choose inputs to shift a population, define a group decision, or optimize across agents. Any hard computational problem would come from those added choices, not from Proposition 1.

The proposed cohort of people with shared mental-state types is coherent, and high multiplicity is not itself a problem here. The difficulty is the programme’s gate: a sensible population model does not supply a computational-social-choice problem established by this paper. A mass distribution over independent updates gives aggregate evaluation; adding a campaign or group-choice problem may be worthwhile, but it would be a new research direction rather than a continuous mirror of a named computational result.

So the negative case is strong against the offered anchor, but it is not airtight as a universal claim. The paper’s game-theoretic motivation leaves room for a population study its authors might recognize. What the text does not support is treating that possibility as an anchored computational mirror of this paper’s results.
