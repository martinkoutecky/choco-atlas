What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Theorem 2’s weighted FAST lower bound; it leaves Corollary 3 and Theorem 4 outside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Continuous Pairwise-Judgment Aggregation, anchored on Theorem 2. In a large panel comparing a fixed set of candidates or products, an agent’s type is their complete pattern of pairwise judgments. A type may be cyclic: someone can judge \(a\) above \(b\), \(b\) above \(c\), and \(c\) above \(a\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the panel with type \(t\). The support lists the \(\tau\) observed judgment patterns and their binary-rational masses.

Given this society, find a ranking \(\pi\) minimizing
\[ \mathrm{cost}_\mu(\pi)=\sum_t \mu_t\,U_t(\pi), \]
where \(U_t(\pi)\) is the number of pairwise judgments in type \(t\) that disagree with \(\pi\). A solution is an optimal ranking and its cost. The instance is the candidate set and the explicit weighted support; its natural parameters are \(m,\tau,L\), and \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\). This is exactly weighted FAST: set \(w_{uv}\) to the mass of types that orient the pair toward \(u\) over \(v\), and the objective becomes the weighted number of backward arcs.

Theorem 2, proved in this paper, states that unless ETH is false, weighted FAST has no parameterized algorithm with runtime \(O^*(2^{o(\sqrt{\mathrm{OPT}})})\). The paper’s reduction makes the population interpretation particularly direct. It maps Vertex Cover to weighted FAST with arc weights in \(\{0,\tfrac12,1\}\). Each such instance is represented by two complete tournament types, each of mass \(\tfrac12\): pairs of weight \(1\) or \(0\) have the same orientation in both types, while pairs of weight \(\tfrac12\) have opposite orientations. Thus the theorem’s hardness already appears with \(\tau=2\). Replicating each cohort gives arbitrarily many agents without changing the society or the objective.

I expect hardness transfers (Class B). The reduction’s combinatorics live in the number of candidates, not in the number of agents. The continuous model preserves the weighted FAST objective exactly, so it gives no reason to expect the hardness to dissolve. This mirror covers Theorem 2’s weighted FAST result; I would not stretch it to the Kemeny or BetweennessTour results.

The authors should recognize the problem: the paper motivates ranking through paired comparisons and studies precisely the weighted FAST objective. The scenario is a large panel repeatedly producing complete comparison patterns over a fixed agenda, with agents grouped when their full patterns match. It has \(N\gg\tau\), and the theorem’s reduction uses only two types.

My weakest point is that the type is a complete pairwise-judgment pattern, not necessarily a transitive preference ranking. If ChoCo requires every type to be a ranking, this anchor does not establish the same hardness for that narrower model. The paper’s tournament and paired-comparison setting makes the broader type defensible, but the restriction should be explicit. A second limitation is that weighted FAST already stores pairwise weights and is mathematically equivalent for this objective to a population mixture of tournament types. That makes the mirror unusually well grounded, while leaving open whether it counts as a distinct continuous-population formulation. Further questions include what happens when types must be transitive rankings, and which restrictions on pairwise judgments change the complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed Theorem 2 mirror runs into a collision with the paper itself. Weighted FAST is defined here as a convex combination of unweighted tournaments. Given a society of complete pairwise-judgment types, set \(w_{uv}=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[t\text{ prefers }u\text{ to }v]\). Then, for every ranking, its expected disagreement with the society is exactly the weighted FAST cost. The type list adds no information needed by the objective; the paper already studies the resulting weighted instance. More pointedly, its ETH reduction uses weights \(0,\tfrac12,1\), representable by just two tournament types of mass \(\tfrac12\). So the claimed hardness at \(\tau=2\) is already hardness for the paper’s weighted problem, not a new continuous-population result.

The strongest repair is to require each type to be a transitive ranking. That avoids cyclic judgment types, but the objective then becomes expected Kendall–Tau distance: precisely the Kemeny objective. Its pairwise margins again give a weighted FAST instance, and the paper’s weighted FAST algorithm already applies. A sharper lower bound for this transitive-only restriction might be worth asking, but it would be a new restricted-case question, not a surviving mirror of Theorem 2.

That leaves BetweennessTour as a possible alternative, but I cannot honestly rule it out universally. Its paper instance ranks named objects and gives one hard constraint per triple; a continuous panel of ranking types would instead induce fractional, population-weighted triple costs. That is a different model, and the paper does not establish the proposed population version. Still, a panel of experts whose judgments concern which item belongs in the middle of each triple is plausible, and the result is a coherent aggregation problem. Dismissing it would depend on arguing that the authors would not recognize that weighted ranking CSP as their problem—an uncertain claim.

So the negative case is strong against the proponent’s anchor and its transitive-ranking repair: both are already represented by the paper’s weighted FAST model. It is weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists, because the BetweennessTour panel variant remains plausible.
