The continuous mirror question

For each rational \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), determine the sharp supremum and infimum of \(C_\mu(a)/\min_{b\in F}C_\mu(b)\) over all finite-support line societies \(\mu\) with strategic mass \(\alpha\) and positive optimal cost, where \(a\) ranges over outcomes of strong equilibria under mass-weighted \(GM^*\). Decide whether the sharp upper envelope is \((1+\alpha)/(1-\alpha)\) for \(\alpha\le 1/2\) and \(3\) for \(\alpha>1/2\), and give matching witness societies or witness families.

The model it lives in

Use finite-support types \(t=(x,\sigma)\), with rational mass \(\mu_t\), location \(x\) on a line, and status \(\sigma\in\{T,L\}\); let \(F\) be a finite ordered facility set and \(\alpha=\sum_{\sigma=L}\mu_t\). Truthful mass reports \(x\), while each strategic type chooses a distribution \(q_t\) over reports. \(GM^*\) selects the facility minimizing reported mass-weighted distance, with the paper’s tie-breaking rule; true cost is \(C_\mu(a)=\sum_t\mu_t|x_t-a|\). A strong equilibrium is a report plan from which no positive-mass coalition of strategic residents can jointly deviate so that almost every coalition member strictly prefers the new outcome.

The objection that survived

The finite-agent proof does not establish stability against arbitrary divisible positive-mass coalitions, so the weighted equilibrium bounds and their tightness need a new proof.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns the line-metric strong-equilibrium welfare bounds of Theorem 4.7. It does not establish the weighted analogue of Theorem 4.2’s equilibrium construction or cover the paper’s general-metric Nash-equilibrium results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the nearest-facility report plan from Theorem 4.2 remain a strong equilibrium against every measurable positive-mass coalition in this weighted line model?
  • For fixed \(\alpha>1/2\), is the sharp upper envelope exactly \(3\), and what finite-support witness families attain or approach it?
  • Can strong equilibria and their best and worst social costs be computed in time polynomial in support size, facility count, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the paper’s line-metric strong-equilibrium results. A plausible regime is a large city choosing a hospital site from a finite list: many residents share the same recorded location and the same truthful or strategic status, so the number of residents is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of location-and-status types. That fits the paper’s model, which already allows multiple clients at one location.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper. It says that on a line, if every strategic client reports the facility location closest to their true location, the resulting profile is a strong Nash equilibrium for \(GM^*\). The paper gives this as an explicit construction, though it does not state a running-time classification.

A second, quality-of-equilibrium anchor is Theorem 4.7, also proved here. On a line, both the strong price of anarchy and the strong price of stability for \(GM^*\) are at most \((n+k)/(n-k)\) when \(k\le n/2\), and at most \(3\) when \(k>n/2\); these bounds are asymptotically tight. This is an equilibrium-quality guarantee, not a hardness result or a complexity classification.

For these results, define a continuous society by a finite support \(\mathcal T\subseteq\mathbb Q\times\{T,L\}\), where a type \(t=(x,\sigma)\) has true location \(x\), status \(\sigma\), and rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(F\subset\mathbb Q\) be the finite ordered facility set. Truthful mass reports its true location. A strategic type chooses a report distribution \(q_t\) over the line, so its mass is distributed across reports. The mechanism chooses the site minimizing total reported distance, with the paper’s tie-breaking rule; true social cost at site \(a\) is \(C_\mu(a)=\sum_t\mu_t|x_t-a|\). Let \(\alpha=\sum_{t:\sigma=L}\mu_t\).

Continuous Line-SNE Search is the mirror of Theorem 4.2: given \((\mathcal T,\mu,F)\), return a report plan and winning site that form a strong equilibrium. Here a coalition may consist of any measurable submass of strategic residents; it is profitable only if, after jointly changing reports, almost every member of that coalition strictly prefers the new winning site. The natural candidate is \(q_{(x,L)}=\delta_{f(x)}\), where \(f(x)\) is a closest facility to \(x\). It can be computed from the support and facility list. I expect the result to extend by weighted-median arguments, making this a tractable, narrow Class A question.

Continuous Line-SNE Price Envelope mirrors Theorem 4.7: for a given rational \(\alpha\), determine the sharp worst- and best-equilibrium social-cost ratios over all finite-support line societies with strategic mass \(\alpha\), and provide tight witness societies. The expected upper envelope is \((1+\alpha)/(1-\alpha)\) for \(\alpha\le 1/2\), and \(3\) for \(\alpha>1/2\), with the exact sharpness at a fixed \(\alpha>1/2\) requiring proof. I expect the weighted version to be tractable through the same line structure, but that transfer is a conjecture, not a result of this paper.

The weakest point is the change in deviation semantics. In an atomless society, one person has zero influence on the selected site, so ordinary Nash equilibrium would be vacuous. The strong-equilibrium formulation avoids that collapse by allowing positive-mass coalitions, but divisible coalitions can split a type’s mass, so the finite theorem does not transfer automatically. The paper’s line-specific SNE result makes this a plausible extension; the required weighted proof remains the central open obligation. The scope here is only the line-metric SNE results, not the paper’s general-metric NE bounds or a new complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about fit with ChoCo’s computational target. Theorem 4.2 is a constructive equilibrium-existence result, and Theorem 4.7 is a bound on equilibrium welfare. Neither states a complexity result for a computational problem. Theorem 4.2 even supplies the reporting rule, but gives no input encoding or running-time analysis. So the proposed Line-SNE Search and Price Envelope are new algorithmic or extremal questions built around the paper’s results, rather than continuous mirrors of computational results the paper established.

A stronger algorithmic variant would ask, for a rational finite-support line society, whether there is a strong equilibrium whose social cost is below a given threshold, or ask for the best and worst social cost over its strong equilibria. That is a coherent computational problem, but it adds an equilibrium-selection objective absent from Theorem 4.7. The paper offers no reason to treat that objective as its problem. This is a real scope objection, though it does not show the new question is uninteresting.

The more fundamental modelling objections do not hold up. Residents at repeated locations, grouped by truthful or strategic status, form a plausible high-multiplicity society; the status belongs in the type. The social cost is exactly population-level, \(\int d(x,a)\,d\mu(x)\). And the proponent’s positive-mass coalition definition avoids the vacuity of individual Nash deviations in an atomless population. A weighted version of Theorem 4.7, with strategic fraction \(\alpha\), is a natural question about that society, even if it is an analytical welfare question rather than a complexity classification.

So I can challenge whether these anchors belong in a computational-complexity programme. I cannot honestly show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The proposed line-metric strong-equilibrium setting remains well-posed and plausible; the negative case is weak precisely where the universal claim demands strength.
