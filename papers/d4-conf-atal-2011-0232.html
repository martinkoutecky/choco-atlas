The continuous mirror question

Given a finite horizon \(H\), a finite set of complete EV-owner types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supply \(s_t\), can one compute causal fluid versions of the paper’s greedy allocation, threshold payments, and immediate or on-departure burning rules in time polynomial in \(H\), the support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\), while proving that truthful reports of each tagged owner’s availability and non-increasing marginal values are weakly dominant under the paper’s limited-misreport rule?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic online charging market with finite support of complete owner types \((a,d,v)\), rational mass per type, per-capita supply \(s_t\), and integral units for each owner. The mechanism allocates type mass causally, splits it into cohorts receiving individual schedules, and charges a tagged zero-mass owner marginal clearing thresholds under the two burning rules.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s DSIC guarantee for immediate and on-departure burning. It leaves the simulation results on efficiency, utility, and burning rates without a continuous analogue.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can both fluid burning rules and their threshold payments be computed in \(\mathrm{poly}(H,\tau,L)\) time?
  • Does the tagged-owner DSIC guarantee hold for both rules under the same admissible availability and valuation misreports?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: the paper’s DSIC charging mechanism has a natural high-multiplicity version. It does not give a named worst-case complexity result, so this would be a mirror of its mechanism-design result, not a continuization of a proved \(P\) or NP-hardness classification.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that, when agents have non-increasing marginal valuations and can only misreport arrival later or departure earlier, Greedy allocation with either immediate or on-departure burning, paired with the payments in Equation 2, is DSIC. Each agent pays the sum of the relevant marginal clearing thresholds for the units it receives. The proof is here; it uses threshold properties, with weak monotonicity cited as background. The theorem’s guarantee is truthful reporting, not optimal allocative efficiency.

I would mirror it as High-Multiplicity Online EV Charging with Threshold Truthfulness. An instance has a finite horizon \(H\), a finite set of complete owner types \(\Theta\), rational type masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), and per-capita supply \(s_t\) at each time \(t\). A type \(\theta=(a,d,v)\) gives an arrival and departure slot and a non-increasing marginal-value vector \(v\). Reports obey the same limited-misreport rule as the paper. Owners may receive at most one unit in each available slot; total allocated mass at slot \(t\) cannot exceed \(s_t\). The operator sees arrivals as they occur, so its policy cannot use future reports. A continuum within each type lets the mechanism split ties by assigning different individuals in that type’s mass different slots.

The decision variable is the allocation of mass by type and time, with payments to individual owners given by marginal thresholds. The service objective is allocative efficiency, \(W=\int\sum_{k\le q_i}v_{i,k}\,di\), subject to online feasibility and truthful incentives. The precise task for a prover is: given \(\Theta\), the masses, supply sequence, and arriving reports, compute the causal mass allocation and threshold payments for the fluid versions of both burning rules; prove that truthful reporting is a dominant strategy for each individual; and give a running-time bound in \(H\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\). A solution is the allocation and payment algorithm plus the DSIC proof. It need not prove that the greedy policy maximizes total welfare over all online policies.

I expect this question to be tractable. In the fluid model, each slot’s greedy allocation and clearing threshold can be found by ordering the \(\tau\) active type-level marginal values and taking the supply mass up to the boundary. An individual has zero mass, so removing that owner to compute a threshold leaves the aggregate unchanged; the discrete mechanism’s recursive recomputation for immediate burning should therefore simplify. The threshold argument in Theorem 1 appears to carry over, with ties handled by splitting mass or randomizing among individuals. That is a plausible route to a polynomial algorithm and proof, not a result the paper already establishes.

A credible regime would be a large fleet or campus parking system with recurring commute patterns, standard charging contracts, and bid values in fixed monetary increments. The complete type includes the exact availability and valuation vector used by the mechanism; under those standardized schedules there could be thousands of owners but tens or hundreds of distinct types. The paper’s own use of real EV data supports the domain, though its simulated agents remain finite. Its sampled valuation distributions are not themselves a continuous population, and the fact that charging power is allocated in units does not make the population continuous.

My weakest point is the atomless incentive assumption. A single owner cannot move the market-clearing threshold in the limit, whereas an individual can matter in the finite mechanism. The mirror therefore preserves individual DSIC but does not address coordinated misreports by a positive mass of owners. Exact types may also proliferate if owners’ travel patterns and valuations are highly personalized. I would treat those as follow-up questions: how well does the fluid rule approximate the finite-\(N\) mechanism, what guarantees hold for efficiency and burning, and what changes when a non-negligible cohort can deviate together? The authors should recognize the core mirror—the same online charging problem, with the same private types, capacity constraint, and truthful-reporting goal—even if they would want those limits made explicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed anchor is not a complexity result. Theorem 1 proves that a specified charging mechanism is DSIC; the paper gives no worst-case complexity classification for a computational problem. Its scalability claims come from simulation. So a fluid allocation algorithm with a new DSIC proof would be an adjacent continuous-mechanism result, not a direct mirror of a complexity result in the paper.

There is also a real modeling change. In the finite mechanism, an owner’s threshold is computed by removing that owner and rerunning the market. In an atomless population, removing one owner leaves the aggregate unchanged. The proposed fluid mechanism therefore becomes an anonymous, price-taking threshold rule. A type-mass allocation alone also does not specify the outcome for a particular zero-mass owner; the model needs a pointwise allocation rule or a tagged infinitesimal owner to state individual DSIC. Those additions are manageable, but they make explicit that this is a new continuum mechanism model, not just the paper’s theorem with \(n\) replaced by infinity.

That case does not defeat the strongest mirror. A large fleet with recurring schedules, standardized vehicles, and a per-capita supply regime is plausible; the paper itself studies charging schedules and vehicle heterogeneity. A tagged owner can have an individual allocation while affecting aggregate capacity by zero mass, and mass can be split among owners who receive integral bundles. The authors could recognize this as their charging problem. I therefore cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The negative case is limited to saying that the proposed anchor is a weaker fit for ChoCo’s complexity focus than its proponent suggests.
