The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\) over \(\tau\) complete linear-utility and budget types \(t=(u_t,b_t)\), and rational per-capita supplies \(s_j>0\), compute equilibrium prices \(p\ge 0\) and per-person bundles \(x_t\ge 0\) such that each \(x_t\) maximizes \(u_t^\top x\) subject to \(p^\top x\le b_t\), while \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=s_j\) for every good; equivalently, compute a rational \(\epsilon\)-KKT point of the weighted Eisenberg–Gale program.

The model it lives in

A finite-support distribution over complete utility-budget types, with mass \(\mu_t\), per-person decision bundle \(x_t\), common price vector \(p\), and per-capita supply \(s\). The objective is \(\max_{x\ge0}\sum_t\mu_t b_t\log(u_t^\top x_t)\), subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=s_j\); rescaling gives the paper's Fisher program on \(\tau\) consumers.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Fisher equilibrium computation anchored by Theorem 1. It leaves the Arrow–Debreu extension and the paper's GPU experiments without separate continuous-population mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the quadratic-growth constant \(\alpha_\xi\) and restart length be bounded in terms of \(m\), \(\tau\), input bit length, and \(\epsilon^{-1}\), including dependence on the smallest positive mass?
  • Can PDHCG provide a certified \(\epsilon\)-KKT guarantee with controlled inexact inner solves, and how does the type-mass equilibrium relate to finite replications?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s linear Fisher equilibrium computation, in a market where many buyers share a small number of exact utility-and-budget templates. Think of a large procurement platform whose branches buy from the same catalogue under one of \(\tau\) standardized valuation schedules and credit limits. If the population has size \(N\), type \(t\) has mass fraction \(\mu_t\), and goods are supplied at \(s_j\) units per person, total supply is \(Ns_j\). Here \(N\gg\tau\); type \(t\) is the complete pair \((u_t,b_t)\), and its mass means that fraction of the population has exactly that linear utility vector and budget.

Call the problem Finite-Support Type-Mass Fisher Equilibrium. Given binary-rational \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), utilities \(u_{tj}\ge0\), budgets \(b_t>0\), and per-capita supplies \(s_j>0\), find a per-person bundle \(x_t\in\mathbb R_+^m\) for each type and common prices \(p\ge0\). An exact solution clears each market, \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=s_j\), and makes each type’s bundle utility-maximizing subject to its budget. Equivalently, it optimizes the weighted Eisenberg–Gale program
\[ \max_{x\ge0}\ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t b_t\log(u_t^\top x_t) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t x_{tj}=s_j\quad(j\in[m]). \]
For an approximation version, ask for a rational \(\epsilon\)-KKT point of this program, with market-clearing, dual-feasibility, and complementary-slackness residuals at most \(\epsilon\).

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For the Fisher minimax problem (9), it claims that with primal and dual step sizes \(\tau_{\rm alg}=\sigma_{\rm alg}=1/(2L)\) and inner-loop length \(K\ge\max\{4L/\xi,4L/\alpha_\xi\}\), each outer restart halves the distance to the optimal saddle-point set:
\[ \operatorname{dist}(z_{r+1,0},Z^\star) \le \tfrac12\operatorname{dist}(z_{r,0},Z^\star). \]
Here \(L=\sqrt n\) for the paper’s \(n\)-consumer instance, \(\xi>0\), and \(\alpha_\xi\) is the quadratic-growth constant established in Corollary 1. The analysis assumes the inner subproblem is solved exactly (Remark 2); the paper reports solving it numerically to high precision.

There is a direct reduction from the type-mass problem to the paper’s Fisher program, so the connection is more than an analogy. Set \(z_{tj}=\mu_t x_{tj}/s_j\). Then \(\sum_t z_{tj}=1\), and the objective differs by a constant from the paper’s Eisenberg–Gale objective with \(\tau\) consumers, budgets \(\mu_t b_t\), and utilities \(s_j u_{tj}\). Thus the theorem’s consumer dimension becomes the support size \(\tau\), not the actual population \(N\). Under the corresponding assumption that every type values some good and every good is valued by some type, PDHCG gives a concrete route to equilibrium computation for the mirror.

I expect Class A for approximate computation, with a necessary qualification: Theorem 1 gives instance-dependent linear convergence, not a uniform polynomial-time bit-complexity bound. In particular, its iteration threshold depends on \(\alpha_\xi\), and the paper does not bound that constant uniformly in \(m,\tau\), input bit length, or the smallest type mass. The main follow-up questions are whether that dependence admits useful bounds, whether the convergence guarantee survives controlled inexact inner solves, and how the type-mass solution relates to finite replications of the market. This case covers the Fisher-market result in Theorem 1; it does not claim to mirror the Arrow–Debreu extension or the paper’s GPU experiments.

The weakest point is the regime assumption. The paper’s synthetic and JD.com instances may have nearly unique buyer profiles, so they need not admit much type compression. The mirror is strongest for markets that really do have \(N\gg\tau\) repeated templates, and its supply scaling deliberately describes a market that grows with its population. For that regime, the paper’s equilibrium model and computational method remain intact, while the algorithm works on the number of types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the best anchor, but it gives a much thinner continuization result than its statement first suggests. It proves instance-dependent linear convergence for a finite Fisher market; it does not give a polynomial bound in the input bit length or the number of types. The restart length depends on the quadratic-growth constant \(\alpha_\xi\), which the paper does not bound in terms of those parameters, and the proof assumes exact inner solves. So it does not establish the kind of complexity result ChoCo would want for an \(\epsilon\)-KKT mirror.

The strongest plausible population scenario is many buyers sharing a small set of exact utility and budget templates. But that version reduces exactly to the paper’s existing Fisher program. For type mass \(\mu_t\), per-capita bundle \(x_t\), and per-capita supply \(s_j\), set \(z_{tj}=\mu_t x_{tj}/s_j\). Then \(\sum_t z_{tj}=1\), and
\[ \sum_t \mu_t b_t\log(u_t^\top x_t) = \sum_t \mu_t b_t\log\bigl((s\odot u_t)^\top z_t\bigr) -\sum_t\mu_t b_t\log\mu_t. \]
The last term is constant. This is the paper’s Eisenberg–Gale program with \(\tau\) consumers, budgets \(\mu_t b_t\), and utilities \(s_j u_{tj}\). Under the corresponding nondegeneracy assumptions, Theorem 1 applies directly with \(n=\tau\).

That identity handles the better mirror too: include every feature the market uses—preferences, budgets, prices, or constraints—in the type. If there are many repeated templates, the problem is an ordinary finite Fisher instance after aggregation; if nearly every buyer has a distinct complete type, then \(\tau\) is nearly the population size and the proposed support-size advantage disappears. The paper’s JD.com data, where utilities come from individual click histories and budgets are drawn independently, do not demonstrate the repeated-template regime. They do not rule it out, but the theorem supplies no new method for it: the gain is an input compression that the same Fisher formulation already permits.

A richer mirror could take a genuine continuous distribution over valuation vectors instead of finite support. That would escape the direct reduction, but the paper gives no representation or access model for such a distribution, and its explicit matrix updates do not apply as stated. Establishing an algorithm there would be a new problem, not a consequence of this result.

So the case against is that Theorem 1 offers no distinct complexity landscape for a continuous population: in the strongest finite-support regime, it is inherited by an exact reformulation, while the theorem itself gives no uniform bit-complexity guarantee. That is a credible argument that this is a low-yield mirror, but not an airtight case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The type-mass Fisher model is coherent, and proving useful support-size or bit-complexity bounds for it could still be valuable.
