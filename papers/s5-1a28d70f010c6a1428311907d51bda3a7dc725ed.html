The continuous mirror question

Given \(k\ge3\), rational ideal points \(p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\in\mathbb{Q}^k\), positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), and rational \(\epsilon>0\), define a hyperplane as median when each closed side has mass at least \(1/2\), and limiting when it contains positive mass. Let \(r_Y(\mu)\) be the minimum radius of a ball intersecting every median hyperplane, and \(r_{LP}(\mu)\) the minimum radius for every limiting median hyperplane. Assuming \(r_Y(\mu)>0\), decide whether \(r_{LP}(\mu)\le\epsilon r_Y(\mu)\).

The model it lives in

A society is a finite rational distribution over ideal-point types in \(\mathbb{Q}^k\). The decision variable is a policy-space ball with center \(c\) and radius \(r\); the two radii minimize \(r\) over the median-hyperplane constraints and, respectively, the positive-mass limiting constraints.

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible mirror is the paper’s question about whether the LP yolk is a good approximation to the yolk. That is an approximation question about a computationally useful surrogate, even though the paper does not give a named NP-hardness or runtime result.

Take a high-multiplicity electorate in a \(k\)-dimensional policy space. A type is an ideal point \(p_i\in\mathbb{Q}^k\), shared by all voters with the same preferences; its mass \(\mu_i\) is their fraction of the electorate. There are \(\tau\) supported locations and \(N\gg\tau\) voters. The decision object remains the paper’s: a policy-space ball, with center \(c\) and radius \(r\), that intersects median hyperplanes. A hyperplane is median when each of its closed sides contains mass at least \(1/2\). The yolk radius \(r_Y(\mu)\) is the smallest radius of a ball intersecting every median hyperplane.

For the continuous LP-yolk analogue, restrict those constraints to median hyperplanes \(H\) with \(\mu(H)>0\), and call the resulting minimum radius \(r_{LP}(\mu)\). This is a natural high-multiplicity reading of “limiting”: if a hyperplane contains a positive-mass type, then in a sufficiently large replication it contains at least \(k\) voters at that location. Rational masses also preserve the bridge to discrete electorates through replication.

The concrete mirror is the Weighted Yolk Gap problem. Its instance is \(k\ge3\), rational supported locations \(p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\), positive rational masses summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(\epsilon>0\); assume \(r_Y(\mu)>0\). The task is to decide whether
\[ r_{LP}(\mu)\le \epsilon\,r_Y(\mu). \]
The solution is a correct yes-or-no answer. Its uniform version asks whether, for fixed \(k\), some positive constant lower-bounds \(r_{LP}(\mu)/r_Y(\mu)\) for every society. That is the central approximation question this mirror puts to a prover.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that the LP-yolk radius can be arbitrarily small relative to the yolk radius when \(k\ge3\), or when \(k=2\) and the number of voters is even. I rely on the \(k\ge3\) clause: embed a lower-dimensional society with positive yolk radius in a hyperplane of \(\mathbb{R}^k\). Its lifted median hyperplanes retain the positive-radius lower bound for the yolk, while the hyperplane containing all supported types is itself median and has positive mass. It therefore forces the LP-yolk radius to zero in the mirror. So for \(k\ge3\), the uniform approximation guarantee fails: for every \(\epsilon>0\), there is a yes instance of the gap problem.

The scenario is plausible in policy spaces where many voters share a relatively small number of ideological locations, perhaps because their positions lie in a lower-dimensional policy subspace. That is a regime claim, not a claim about every spatial electorate. In ChoCo terms, the expected result is a negative approximation guarantee for the LP surrogate. Theorem 1 does not establish that evaluating the gap is tractable or hard; that complexity question would need its own analysis.

The mirror covers the paper’s comparison of the LP yolk and the exact yolk, specifically its higher-dimensional worst-case gap. It does not try to continuize the core, voting dynamics, or policy outcomes. The authors should recognize the question: their paper is already about when an easier linear-programming solution concept approximates a more demanding one, and the mirror asks how that guarantee behaves when voter locations are supplied as population masses.

The weakest point is the definition of “limiting” in the weighted model. Positive mass on a hyperplane is a natural replication-based analogue, but it is a co-modification of the paper’s finite-voter definition. The sharp zero-radius construction also uses support contained in a lower-dimensional hyperplane. That gives a clear mirror of the theorem, but a stronger case would establish how robust the gap remains for weighted societies with full-dimensional support.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a poor fit for ChoCo’s central computational agenda: it proves a geometric approximation bound, not the complexity of a computational problem or the performance of an algorithm. The paper’s own computational contribution is mainly backgrounded in its discussion of algorithms for computing the yolk; its new results compare two geometric objects. That leaves no named computational result here for a continuous mirror to carry forward.

The proponent’s Weighted Yolk Gap is the strongest repair, and it is well posed. But its \(k\ge3\) example is a finite geometric configuration with rational weights. Replicating each support point in the corresponding proportion gives the same weighted median geometry. Even the full-dimensional, small-perturbation version leaves the gap in a constant-size support. So this anchor does not expose a phenomenon caused by population multiplicity; it restates the paper’s geometric comparison in a weighted input format. That is a weak reason to prioritize it as a ChoCo complexity question, though it is not a reason to dismiss high-multiplicity models generally.

There is also a problem with carrying the LP-yolk distinction into a genuinely diffuse population. If “limiting” means that a median hyperplane contains at least \(k\) supported ideal points, then for a distribution with full-dimensional continuous support, median hyperplanes that cross the support contain infinitely many such points. The LP-yolk constraints then include all median hyperplanes, making the two yolks coincide. If instead “limiting” means that the hyperplane carries positive population mass, an atomless distribution gives no such constraints. An intermediate definition would need a new scale or threshold, and it is unclear that the authors would recognize that as the same approximation question.

That still does not defeat the atomic, high-multiplicity mirror. Rational masses on finitely many ideological locations are a sensible population model, and Theorem 1’s gap persists there. The honest negative case is therefore weak at its hardest point: the proposed mirror survives in an in-scope regime. The strongest objection is about its value for this programme—the result is geometric, and its weighted version adds little population-driven structure—not that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
