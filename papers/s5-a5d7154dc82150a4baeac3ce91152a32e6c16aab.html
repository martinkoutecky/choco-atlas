The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification theorem. Its strongest computational anchor is an approximation impossibility result.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.6, proved in this paper. It says that for constant-cost public excludable goods, no deterministic cost-sharing mechanism that is individually rational, anonymous, truthful, upper semicontinuous, and Sybil-proof can achieve an \(o(n)\) worst-case social-cost approximation. If the mechanism also has no deficit, its approximation factor is at least \((n+1)/2\). The result is about the efficiency guarantee achievable by an entire class of mechanisms, rather than the running time of a particular algorithm.

A direct mass version would ask for the Continuous Sybil-Proof Cost-Sharing Frontier. An instance has a population scale \(N\), a finite support \(V\subseteq\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) of valuations, and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(V\), with \(|V|=\tau\). There is one public good, whose cost is \(1\) if any positive mass receives it. The population measure is \(N\mu\): mass \(N\mu_v\) has valuation \(v\). A mechanism takes a finite-support measure of reported bids and returns which mass is served and each served identity’s payment. It must be deterministic, anonymous, individually rational, truthful, and upper semicontinuous. Sybil-proofness means that for any tagged agent with value \(v\), against any background of other reports, truthful participation gives at least as much utility as replacing that identity with any finite set of aliases; the agent values access if any alias is served and pays for all aliases.

The objective on a truthful population is

\[ \operatorname{SC}(N,\mu) = \mathbf 1[\text{positive mass is served}] + \sum_{v\in V}N\mu_v v\,(1-x(v)), \]

where \(x(v)\) is the served fraction of type \(v\). The optimal social cost is \(\min\{1,N\sum_v\mu_vv\}\). The problem is to find a mechanism rule with the best worst-case approximation factor as a function of \(N,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), or prove a lower bound ruling out a better factor. The no-deficit variant adds the requirement that total payments cover the cost whenever the good is provided.

I expect the \(\Omega(N)\) barrier to transfer when the support may grow with \(N\): the continuous problem retains the same unit-cost good and identity-splitting incentives, and integer-mass profiles are special cases of its rational measures. At integer \(N\), the no-deficit lower bound should likewise remain \((N+1)/2\) in an identity-aware formulation. The more pointed ChoCo question is whether the barrier survives when \(N\gg\tau\). The theorem does not establish that: it gives no bound parameterized by the number of distinct valuation types. Determining the frontier \(\alpha^*(N,\tau,L)\), especially for fixed or small \(\tau\), is a natural follow-up.

The regime is a large permissionless subscription service or community-funded platform with many users but a small menu of willingness-to-pay levels. A type is the complete relevant economic description here: the user’s valuation, under the paper’s shared cost function. Mass is the number of users in that valuation class. This is a sensible high-multiplicity setting for the paper’s own public-good model, and the mirror preserves its central question about anonymous funding under Sybil identities. It covers Theorem 3.6’s constant-cost impossibility, not the paper’s separate Shapley-value result for general symmetric submodular costs.

The weakest point is that Sybil-proofness concerns an individual’s deviation, while a purely atomless measure model makes that deviation massless and may make the constraint vacuous. The mirror therefore needs to retain a tagged identity—and its aliases—as finite atoms in the otherwise mass-based population. That is a deliberate modeling choice, and the authors may challenge it. But it preserves the paper’s actual strategic object while letting the rest of a large society be represented by a low-support distribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.6 is the strongest anchor, but it does not assert a computational result. It bounds the welfare approximation achievable by mechanisms satisfying incentive, anonymity, and continuity axioms. It gives no algorithm, running time, or input-to-output computational task. Asking for the best such guarantee as a function of \(N,\tau,L\) remains a mechanism-design frontier: the mechanism is quantified over profiles and deviations, rather than computed from an instance. Encoding that frontier as a new computational problem would require additional choices the paper does not make. The same issue applies to the Shapley result in Theorem 4.4, which concerns welfare under equilibrium.

The proposed mirror also has a granularity problem at its core. With an exact finite population, the mass of valuation \(v\) must be \(q_v\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}\), so \(\mu_v=q_v/N\). This is a high-multiplicity encoding of discrete counts, and it can be a sensible model of a large subscription service. But the theorem’s Sybil-proofness condition acts on one identity at a time. A continuum measure over types has no operation corresponding to removing one person from a type and replacing that person with several aliases.

Allow fractional type masses and partial service, and the mechanism no longer represents the paper’s deterministic anonymous game. At a truthful profile, anonymity forces agents with identical bids to receive the same allocation; a deterministic mechanism cannot serve a fractional subset of that class while preserving anonymity. Allowing such a split requires randomization, identity-based selection, or a different type space. Meanwhile, a finite set of aliases has zero mass in an atomless society, so its deviation is invisible to a mechanism that acts only on the measure. The public-good cost being triggered by any positive served mass does not restore the missing identity-level deviation.

A hybrid with a continuum background and a tagged agent can retain that deviation, but then the strategic content lives in the tagged finite identity layer. At finite \(N\), its mass is \(1/N\) and the model retains the relevant discrete granularity; in the limit, a finite number of aliases has vanishing mass. If alias identities must still affect allocation or payment, the mechanism must preserve information beyond the aggregate distribution. That can support a useful model, but the continuous population itself is no longer doing the work claimed for the mirror.

The fixed-small-\(\tau\) frontier is the most promising version of the proposal: large user cohorts with a small menu of valuations are plausible, and the paper’s lower bound does not settle that regime. It could be worthwhile mechanism-design research. I would still vote against it as a ChoCo mirror: the result asks how much efficiency a rule can guarantee under strategic constraints, not how hard it is to compute an outcome over a continuous society. That is where this negative case is weakest: a programme broad enough to include mechanism-design frontiers could reasonably take up the high-multiplicity reformulation.
