The continuous mirror question

Given a finite directed delegation template with rational-mass voter cohorts \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each with accuracy \(p_t\) and specified delegate options, plus finitely many individually stochastic delegate roles with zero initial mass, choose an acyclic permitted routing of the cohort mass and delegate roles to maximize the probability that the correct voting mass exceeds \(1/2\). What is the approximation complexity of this optimization problem, and does the set-cover inapproximability of Theorem 4.1 persist in this model?

The model it lives in

A hybrid liquid-democracy model with diffuse cohorts whose direct votes aggregate to deterministic expected mass \(p_t\mu_t\), and finitely many atomic delegates whose independent Bernoulli votes determine the outcome for all mass routed to them.

The objection that survived

The set-cover construction relies on a finite set of zero-initial-mass delegates whose votes remain stochastic; the proponent gives them a plausible institutional role but does not establish that every reader would count them as part of a continuous society.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1’s approximation hardness for \(\mathrm{ODP}_r\) and the strongly connected best-guru guarantee in Theorem 4.4; it leaves Proposition 1, the MILP, heuristics, and simulations outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • What approximation guarantees hold when the instance size is measured by the delegation template and the binary encoding length of the cohort masses?
  • In the strongly connected subcase, does the best-guru strategy retain the \(1/2\)-approximation guarantee when cohort votes aggregate continuously?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(r\in(0,0.5)\) and every constant \(C>0\), unless \(P=NP\), no polynomial-time algorithm approximates \(\mathrm{ODP}_r\) within a factor \(\alpha\ge(\ln n)^{-C}\), where \(\mathrm{ODP}_r\) restricts ODP to instances whose voters all have accuracy greater than \(r\). ODP asks for an acyclic delegation function maximizing the probability that weighted majority elects the ground truth. The paper proves this result by reducing minimum set cover to ODP instances with misinformation and poorly connected networks.

I would mirror it with Continuous ODP with Atomic Delegates. An instance has a finite directed network of delegate roles; positive-mass roles \(t\) have rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), accuracy \(p_t\), and specified delegate options. These roles are complete types: their members share the accuracy and network behavior relevant to delegation. The network can also contain finitely many distinguished individual delegates, with their own accuracies and options, whose population share tends to zero as the ordinary population grows. A feasible solution routes mass along allowed arcs, with each distinguished delegate choosing at most one outgoing arc or voting directly; the resulting delegation graph must be acyclic. A continuum mass that votes directly contributes its deterministic share \(p_t\mu_t\), while each voting delegate contributes its received mass times an independent Bernoulli vote of accuracy \(p_g\). The objective is to maximize the probability that the total correct voting mass exceeds \(1/2\). The solution is the routing plan and the delegates’ choices.

This mixed limit fits a concrete liquid-democracy regime: a large party, union, or public consultation with many members in a modest number of expertise and access cohorts, plus a finite set of recognized delegates. For a fixed network template, replicate the bulk cohorts \(q\) times while keeping the delegate roles fixed. Then \(n\) grows with \(q\), while the number of bulk types and distinguished delegates stays fixed. The many members share a type’s accuracy and delegate access; the delegates remain individual because their pooled voting weight is precisely what the paper’s model gives them. Low connectivity and misinformation, the features behind the hardness reduction, have direct interpretations in such a setting.

The reduction has a particularly natural limiting form. Keep the isolated background voters and the element-follower cohorts as continuous mass, and retain one atomic relay per element and one atomic delegate per set. Each element cohort can reach only its element relay; that relay chooses one set delegate containing the element. Thus the cohort cannot fractionally split among sets. In the replicated limit, independent direct votes from the isolated background concentrate at their expected mass. The paper’s threshold inequalities then make the election succeed exactly when every selected set delegate votes correctly, giving success probability \(2^{-|X|}\) for a selected cover \(X\). Minimizing the number of selected set delegates is therefore still minimum set cover. The reduction’s combinatorics live in the delegate-option network, so I expect hardness to transfer: this is a Class B mirror, not hardness that depends on having many distinct voters. With input size measured by the network and binary-encoded masses, the same reduction suggests a super-polylogarithmic approximation barrier in the continuous problem as well.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their ODP question: it keeps the same delegation choices, acyclicity, expert accuracies, random guru votes, and weighted-majority objective. The direct votes of a diffuse population become deterministic by aggregation, while voting power delegated to a named guru remains stochastic. The mirror covers the paper’s ODP hardness result; it does not claim to continuize the simulations or every theorem. It also prompts a useful follow-up: does the paper’s \(1/2\)-approximation result for strongly connected networks, Theorem 4.4, survive in the same limit?

The weak point is the finite set of zero-share delegates. They are natural for this paper’s model—many voters can delegate to a single guru—but a referee might require every voter type in the continuous society to have positive mass and reject this mixed limit as too permissive. I do not know that the set-cover reduction survives a strictly nonatomic model with no persistent individual delegates. The case is strongest if the programme accepts negligible-share delegate roles as part of the population’s network structure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.1 is the right anchor, and the case against it is weak. The strongest objection is that the continuum may remove only the duplicated voters, while leaving the hard part of ODP finite.

A type for this problem must include its accuracy and its position in the delegation network. In the set-cover reduction, the isolated voters and many element followers can be grouped into cohorts, but each element relay and set delegate has a distinct network role. Those roles carry the reduction’s choices. Collapsing the cohorts therefore gives a weighted finite network in which each element relay still chooses one set delegate and each selected delegate still makes a random vote. That is a real compression of the population, but it leaves the central combinatorics intact and offers no obvious continuous-optimization structure.

A fully diffuse version has a different problem: by concentration, the aggregate votes of every positive-mass cohort become deterministic. To preserve the paper’s stochastic objective and its reduction, the proposed mirror retains delegates with zero initial population mass but potentially large voting weight after receiving delegations. That is a plausible liquid-democracy model, especially for a large organization with a finite set of recognized delegates. But it is a hybrid: the electorate is diffuse while the outcome-relevant decision makers remain individual network nodes.

That boundary is the best negative case, not a decisive rebuttal. An author could reasonably recognize the hybrid model as ODP with a continuous electorate: mass cohorts delegate through the same network, and the delegates’ independent signals remain random. The set-cover construction may then carry over, with the background mass concentrating around its expected value. If so, this is a Class B result: continuity compresses repeated voters but does not erase the finite network’s hardness. The programme explicitly counts hardness transfer as useful, so the fact that the answer may stay hard cannot disqualify the mirror.

Theorem 4.4’s strongly connected case seems like a straightforward follow-up in that same hybrid model. It does not strengthen the objection to Theorem 4.1; nor does its likely simplicity make the question invalid.

I would argue against prioritizing the paper on the grounds that its most convincing continuous model preserves the hard core as a finite delegation network, while a fully diffuse model loses the paper’s central voting uncertainty. But I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror survives. The mixed model is plausible, and the programme’s own criteria leave room for it.
