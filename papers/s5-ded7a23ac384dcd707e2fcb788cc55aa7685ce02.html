The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many advertiser types \(T\), each encoding a class and a complete eligibility profile, with rational population masses \(\mu_t\), and an adversarial online stream of divisible supply cohorts \((b_r,A_r)\) revealing supply and compatible types on arrival, characterize the attainable CEF and USW competitive guarantees for causal allocation policies. In particular, does a continuous population analogue of EFTT attain \((1-e^{-\gamma})\)-CEF and \(\left(1-\frac{e^{\gamma-1}}{\gamma+1}\right)\)-USW, and does every randomized non-wasteful policy obey the Theorem 4.4 USW cap \(\frac{1+\alpha-e^{\alpha-1}}{1+\alpha}\)?

The model it lives in

Types \(t\in T\) carry mass \(\mu_t\), class labels, and compatibility with each arriving cohort. A causal allocation assigns cohort mass \(x_{t,r}\ge 0\), subject to type capacities \(\sum_r x_{t,r}\le\mu_t\) and cohort supplies \(\sum_t x_{t,r}\le b_r\). For class \(i\), welfare is \(V_i=\sum_{t\in T_i,r}x_{t,r}\); its optimistic envy value \(V_i^*(y_j)\) is the maximum rematching mass into types in \(T_i\), subject to their capacities \(\mu_t\), cohort caps \(y_{j,r}=\sum_{t\in T_j}x_{t,r}\), and the revealed compatibility constraints. The policy must preserve irrevocable online decisions and compare welfare with the offline optimum on the same stream.

The objection that survived

The finite theorem and clone construction may settle the continuous guarantees without creating a new algorithmic or runtime question; this bounds the mirror’s novelty, but the stated criteria accept competitive guarantees and known answers.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered NP-hardness or polynomial-time complexity result. Its strongest eligible anchors are named online competitive-ratio theorems, so this is an algorithmic case, not a claim that the paper supplies a complexity classification.

A plausible regime is a large ad marketplace: advertisers belong to fairness classes such as regions, and many advertisers share the same class and the same eligibility profile for incoming impression categories. A type \(t\) is the pair consisting of that class and profile. Let \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) be its share of the advertiser population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and let \(\tau\) be the number of positive-mass types. As the market grows, \(N\gg\tau\). Inventory arrives online in cohorts: cohort \(r\) has per-capita supply \(b_r\), and on arrival reveals which types can accept it. Both advertiser capacity and inventory scale with the population; a finite market with \(N\mu_t\) advertisers and \(Nb_r\) impressions gives the corresponding rational clone instance.

The continuous decision is a flow \(x_{t,r}\ge 0\) assigned irrevocably when cohort \(r\) arrives, with \(\sum_r x_{t,r}\le\mu_t\) for each type and \(\sum_t x_{t,r}\le b_r\) for each cohort. For class \(i\), its value is \(V_i=\sum_{t\in i,r}x_{t,r}\). If \(y_{j,r}\) is the mass of cohort \(r\) allocated to class \(j\), define \(V_i^*(y_j)\) by maximizing \(\sum_{t\in i,r}z_{t,r}\), subject to \(\sum_r z_{t,r}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t z_{t,r}\le y_{j,r}\), and \(z_{t,r}=0\) for incompatible pairs. This is the paper’s optimistic envy value written over type masses. The impression allocation remains divisible as in the paper’s divisible-matching variant; the continuized object is the advertiser population.

My lead is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(\gamma\in[0,1]\), EFTT is an online divisible-matching algorithm achieving \((1-e^{-\gamma})\)-CEF and \(\left(1-\frac{e^{\gamma-1}}{\gamma+1}\right)\)-USW simultaneously. The mirror question, Continuous EFTT for Online Class Matching, is: given rational type masses, class labels, \(\gamma\), and a stream of arriving supply cohorts whose compatible types are revealed on arrival, find a causal allocation policy satisfying both guarantees against every adversarial stream. A solution is the policy itself, with CEF measured using the type-mass rematching value above and USW compared with the offline optimum on the same stream. The expected direction is constructive: apply the threshold rule at type level, raising load in the least-loaded compatible type cohorts while equal-filling active classes up to \(\gamma\), then switch to water-filling. This is close to the authors’ actual problem because it preserves their classes, compatibility, online information, and fairness test.

A second, complementary anchor is Theorem 4.4, also proved here. It states that no randomized non-wasteful online algorithm with an \(\alpha\)-CEF guarantee for divisible matching can have USW competitive ratio greater than \(\frac{1+\alpha-e^{\alpha-1}}{1+\alpha}\). The mirror, Continuous Price-of-Fairness Cap, asks whether any randomized, non-wasteful policy on the type-mass model can guarantee \(\beta\)-USW and \(\alpha\)-CEF when \(\beta\) exceeds that bound. Its solution is an impossibility proof: a family of adversarial mass streams rules out such a policy. This is a competitive-ratio ceiling, not a P/NP hardness claim. The finite witnesses can be fixed for any desired approximation to the bound and then cloned by an arbitrary factor, so the population can grow while each witness has finite type support.

The scope is these two divisible-matching results. They give a constructive guarantee and a cap on the achievable fairness–welfare frontier; together they motivate the further question of whether that frontier is tight, and whether it improves when \(\tau\) is bounded.

The weakest point is that this mirror may be an exact high-multiplicity restatement more than a new complexity landscape. The source already handles finite agent graphs, and the fractional item allocation is already part of its divisible variant. The mirror does not show that population continuization removes hardness or creates a new optimization problem. Its credible claim is narrower: these particular online guarantees and limits remain meaningful for markets described by a small distribution of agent types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent chose the paper’s strongest anchors. Theorem 4.1 gives an online policy with simultaneous fairness and welfare guarantees; Theorem 4.4 limits the guarantees any non-wasteful policy can achieve. I would not object that fractional assignments already make the population continuous: the agents remain finite, and the proposed advertiser setting is a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

My case against is that these are competitive-ratio results, not the kind of complexity landscape ChoCo is set up to chart. Theorem 4.1 proves a policy guarantee; Theorem 4.4 proves a ceiling on policy guarantees. Neither studies runtime, an optimization oracle, or the complexity of deciding or computing an intervention. A continuous question that asks for the same guarantee over type masses changes the representation of the population, but does not supply that missing computational question.

For Continuous EFTT, even the stronger type-level formulation appears to be an exact rescaling of the paper’s divisible matching model. With rational type masses and cohort supplies, clear denominators and replace each type mass by identical agent copies and each cohort by identical arriving items. Aggregate fractional flows map to fractional matchings and back; class envy values and welfare ratios scale with them. Thus the proposed policy and guarantees carry over directly. This is a well-posed mirror, but its result is already determined by the finite theorem. That fact alone should not count against a mirror under the programme’s rules; the negative point is that here the mass formulation adds no distinct algorithmic object or complexity question.

Continuous Price-of-Fairness Cap faces the same issue. Its adversarial construction is built from finite class sizes and triangular compatibility patterns. Represent those roles as types, scale their masses and supplies, and the same fairness-versus-welfare mechanism remains. A more refined mass-specific bound might be mathematically worth proving, but the proposed question asks whether the existing cap holds in the type-mass model, where the finite construction already supplies the relevant obstruction.

That is the strongest case against, and it is limited. It makes the mirrors look redundant with the paper’s results, not ill-posed or nonsensical. Since the programme explicitly treats a sensible continuous question as worthwhile even when its answer is known, I cannot honestly claim these anchors are defeated on that ground. If ChoCo includes online competitive analysis and values a formal finite-to-mass dictionary, this paper has a credible mirror; the negative case depends on keeping the programme’s focus on complexity questions narrower than that.
