The continuous mirror question

Given two candidates \(a,b\), rational initial plurality scores, and a finite ordered list of rational-mass cohorts with per-capita weights and roles \(\mathrm{M}\) or \(\mathrm{N}\), where a manipulator cohort chooses \(x_{r,a},x_{r,b}\ge 0\) with \(x_{r,a}+x_{r,b}=\lambda_r\) after observing prior aggregate ballots and a nonmanipulator cohort chooses adversarially, does there exist a history-dependent coalition policy forcing \(S_a=S_b\) for every adversarial policy?

The model it lives in

Types are \(t=(r,\rho,\omega,\sigma)\) with mass \(\mu_t\); cohort variables allocate mass among integral ballots, coalition and adversarial policies act on revealed aggregate history, and the objective is robust feasibility of a nonunique final winner under \(S_c=s_c^0+\sum_r\omega_r x_{r,c}\).

The objection that survived

Batching replaces the paper's per-voter observation and quantifier order with aggregate cohort moves, and the proponent does not establish that this batching is forced by high multiplicity or accepted by the original authors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity, finite-round version of the paper’s weighted online manipulation problems. The continuum is over voters, not over candidates, time, or uncertainty: there are finitely many voting rounds, but each round contains a large mass of otherwise identical voters.

A dynamic voter type is therefore \(t=(r,\rho,\omega,\sigma)\), where \(r\) is the public round, \(\rho\in\{\mathrm{M},\mathrm{N}\}\) records coalition membership, \(\omega\) is per-capita voting weight, and \(\sigma\) is the coalition’s preference order. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the electorate in that role and round. Within a round, every individual still casts an integral ballot; a vector such as \(x_{r,c}=0.37\) means that \(37\%\) of that cohort votes for \(c\), not that an individual casts a fractional ballot. The aggregate report is revealed before the next round.

This is plausible for a large federation, platform, or organizational election in which standardized cohorts vote at scheduled checkpoints. Millions of members may share a preference template, coalition status, voting weight, and checkpoint, while the number of distinct types remains small relative to the population. Clearing denominators gives the discrete high-multiplicity expansion: \(N\mu_t\) identical voters of type \(t\). Future nonmanipulator actions remain adversarial, as in the paper; the continuum is not a probability model.

My lead anchor is Theorem 8(1), proved in this paper. It states that \(\mathrm{online\mbox{-}plurality\mbox{-}DWCM}_{UW}\) is NP-hard, even with only two candidates; the same holds with three, four, or any larger fixed number of candidates. The result is proved here by reducing from Partition. The construction gives the future manipulators weights proportional to the integers \(w_i\), and avoiding a unique winner requires splitting those weights exactly equally between two candidates.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\(\mathrm{Cont\mbox{-}online\mbox{-}Plurality\mbox{-}DUCM}^{UW}_\infty[2]\).

An instance consists of two candidates \(a,b\), coalition order \(a\succ_\sigma b\), distinguished candidate \(a\), an initial rational plurality-score vector \(s^0\), and a finite ordered list of future cohorts. Each cohort \(r\) has rational mass \(\lambda_r\), per-capita weight \(\omega_r\), and role \(\mathrm{M}\) or \(\mathrm{N}\). At a manipulator cohort, the coalition chooses \(x_r=(x_{r,a},x_{r,b})\) with \(x_{r,a}+x_{r,b}=\lambda_r\); at a nonmanipulator cohort, an adversary chooses such a vector. The coalition’s choice may depend on all previously revealed aggregate votes, while the adversary knows the same history.

The final scores are \(S_c=s^0_c+\sum_r\omega_r x_{r,c}\). The question is whether there exists a coalition policy such that, for every adversarial policy, no candidate is the unique plurality winner. With two candidates this means precisely \(S_a=S_b\). A solution is the coalition’s history-dependent policy.

This is recognizably the paper’s destructive unique-winner problem: the coalition must robustly prevent a unique winner under sequential, partially unknown voting. The only substantive change is that a high-multiplicity cohort may divide its mass among integral ballots. That is exactly the population continuization, not a change to fractional outcomes.

I expect this mirror to be Class A. In the paper’s reduction, the hard choice is whether each indivisible weight \(w_i\) goes wholly to one candidate or the other. In the continuous problem, a cohort can send any mass fraction to either candidate, so its contribution to the score difference ranges over an interval. Let \(\Delta=S_a-S_b\). A cohort of effective mass \(\omega_r\lambda_r\) can change \(\Delta\) by any value in \([-\omega_r\lambda_r,\omega_r\lambda_r]\). Backward induction over the finite rounds therefore maintains intervals of winning states: manipulator rounds use existential interval expansion, nonmanipulator rounds use universal interval erosion, and the terminal set is \(\{0\}\). This should give a polynomial-time rational interval algorithm.

The important point is not merely that the Partition reduction fails. The continuous question is still the same online robust manipulation question, with the same plurality rule, unique-winner condition, coalition, adversarial future votes, and sequential information. What disappears is only the indivisibility that encoded Partition. The result would be a clean example where discrete NP-hardness dissolves under population continuization.

My second anchor is Theorem 12, also proved in this paper, which states that \(\mathrm{online\mbox{-}veto\mbox{-}WCM}\) is \(\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{NP}}\)-complete, even when restricted to four candidates. This is the paper’s default nonunique-winner constructive problem: the coalition must ensure that the final winner set contains some candidate at least as preferred as the distinguished candidate. The upper bound is proved through two optimization problems that partition manipulator and nonmanipulator weights among candidates; the lower bound reduces from \(\mathrm{MAXSATASG}_{=}\), using Wagner-style subset-sum encodings.

The continuous mirror is:

\(\mathrm{Cont\mbox{-}online\mbox{-}Veto\mbox{-}WCM}_\infty[4]\).

An instance has four candidates \(C=\{a,b,c,d\}\), a coalition order \(\sigma\), distinguished candidate \(d^\star\), initial rational veto loads \(v^0_c\), and finitely many ordered manipulator and nonmanipulator cohorts with rational masses and per-capita weights. Each member casts one veto, so a manipulator cohort chooses \(y_r=(y_{r,a},y_{r,b},y_{r,c},y_{r,d})\) with \(\sum_c y_{r,c}=\lambda_r\); a nonmanipulator cohort is chosen adversarially. The final veto load is \(V_c=v^0_c+\sum_r\omega_r y_{r,c}\), and the winners are the candidates minimizing \(V_c\). The coalition asks whether it has a history-dependent policy guaranteeing \(W(V)\cap\{c:c\succeq_\sigma d^\star\}\neq\varnothing\) against every future nonmanipulator policy.

Here too I expect the continuous version to be tractable, probably in polynomial time for four candidates. The paper’s proof computes minimum thresholds by asking whether integer voter weights can be partitioned among veto targets. In the continuum, those partitions become real allocation variables. A typical subproblem becomes a linear program such as \(\min t\) subject to \(v^0_c-\sum_j z_{j,c}\le t\), \(\sum_c z_{j,c}=W_j\), and \(z_{j,c}\ge0\). The same applies to the adversarial cohorts. The open technical issue is to prove that the paper’s threshold characterization remains exact for the finite-round mass policies; the LP part itself is straightforward.

This second mirror is especially valuable because the discrete hardness persists with only four candidates, so it is not obviously caused by a large alternative set. The reduction’s combinatorics instead live in indivisible weighted population units and subset sums. That is precisely the kind of population-driven hardness that a continuous relaxation is supposed to test.

The scope is deliberately limited. These mirrors cover Theorem 8(1) and Theorem 12, not the paper’s artificial election systems from Theorems 3 and 4, whose hardness is encoded in candidate names and formulas, nor Theorem 13’s schedule-robust result, where the main difficulty is quantifier alternation rather than population multiplicity. Those would more plausibly be Class B problems whose hardness survives continuization.

The weakest point is the finite-round cohorting. The paper lets one named voter act at a time and observe every preceding individual vote. My mirror instead treats a large same-type block’s aggregate vote as the observable action. If that change is judged too substantial, the construction may be criticized as a continuous cohort-control problem rather than the paper’s exact online problem. I think it is defensible because high multiplicity naturally replaces named same-type actions by aggregate mass reports, while retaining finite sequential information and adversarial uncertainty. But it should be stated openly.

The resulting boundary questions are themselves useful: whether the two-candidate mirror admits the claimed interval algorithm; whether four-candidate veto collapses from \(\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{NP}}\) to LP; and whether retaining individual-by-individual observation would require a genuinely different, continuum-time model. Those questions preserve the paper’s computational core while testing exactly where its discrete hardness comes from.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both proposed mirrors replace the paper’s essential object—an individual, sequential, information-revealing move—with a divisible aggregate control. That is not forced by making the population continuous.

For Theorem 8(1), the paper proves hardness for \(\mathrm{online\mbox{-}plurality\mbox{-}DWCM}_{UW}\), not \(\mathrm{DUCM}_{UW}\): the voters are weighted, and the Partition reduction depends on each future manipulator making one indivisible choice. In the proposed mirror, a cohort may split its mass continuously between \(a\) and \(b\). That does eliminate the subset-sum obstruction, but only because the cohort has become a single batched control with action set \([-\omega_r\lambda_r,\omega_r\lambda_r]\). In the paper, the coalition can react after each individual future vote; members of a cohort do not act simultaneously or submit an aggregate report.

There are only two faithful ways to repair this. One can serialize \(N\) copies of each type and preserve individual observation. Then the current voter’s effect on normalized scores is \(O(1/N)\), and the nontrivial limit is a continuous-time sequential game—continuization of the time/information structure, not merely of the population. Alternatively, one can batch the copies and reveal their aggregate vote. That produces the proponent’s interval game, but batching changes the quantifier structure and policy space. It is a new robust-control problem, not the high-multiplicity version of the paper’s online problem. Requiring a cohort to vote unanimously preserves the original action structure, but then the continuous mass no longer supplies any relaxation.

Theorem 12 has the same defect in a less obvious form. Its \(\mathsf{P}^{\mathsf{NP}}\)-hardness comes from integer weights encoding subset sums and lexicographically maximal satisfying assignments. Those weights are numerical coding devices attached to individual moves, not population multiplicities. Splitting a weighted cohort continuously turns the reduction into a divisible allocation problem; retaining indivisible weighted moves leaves no genuine atomless population limit.

More fundamentally, the paper does not provide a distribution of future voter preferences. Future nonmanipulators are universally quantified over arbitrary votes. Consequently, the proposed \(\mu\) does no substantive work as a distribution over voter types: only the schedule, coalition role, and voting weight matter. The four-candidate veto mirror is therefore best understood as a game over divisible veto mass, or a flow of voting power, rather than as computation over a continuous society. Adding truthful preference types would make the population meaningful, but would change the paper’s adversarial online-manipulation problem into a different uncertainty or possible-winner model.

The claimed LP intuition is also insufficient. Even with four candidates, finite alternating cohorts create an existential/universal polyhedral reachability game. One-step allocations are linear, but repeated projection and intersection can create exponentially many facets; the threshold characterization from the discrete veto proof does not automatically survive. This is a technical obstacle rather than the central objection, but it reinforces that the proposed result is conjectural.

This negative case is not airtight. A large federation with finitely many voting checkpoints could reasonably define simultaneous cohort voting, and the resulting interval or polyhedral game could be a worthwhile new Class A problem. If the programme permits that batching as a legitimate co-modification, Theorem 8 supplies a credible continuous mirror, and Theorem 12 supplies another plausible one. The honest conclusion is therefore not that every continuous scenario fails, but that the proponent has not shown a faithful population continuization of the paper’s sequential model: its surviving mirrors rely on replacing sequential individual agency by batched divisible control.
