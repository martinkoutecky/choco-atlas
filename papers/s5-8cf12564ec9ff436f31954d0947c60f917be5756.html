The continuous mirror question

For each \(k\), draw \(k\) utility vectors \(v^1,\ldots,v^k\), with entries independently distributed as \(U(0,1)\). Give each type mass \(1/k\) and each of \(k\) item categories supply \(1/k\). Let \(O_k(v)\) be maximum welfare over feasible type-to-category mass flows, and let \(R_k(v)\) be the limiting expected welfare per person under finite Random Priority markets with \(q/k\) agents of each type and \(q/k\) copies of each category, as \(q\to\infty\). Establish whether \(\sup_k \mathbb{E}_v[O_k(v)/R_k(v)]\leq 1+e\), including existence of the limits, and characterize the sharp ratio if possible.

The model it lives in

A continuum assignment market with \(k\) types of mass \(1/k\), each carrying a utility vector over \(k\) item categories with supply \(1/k\). The optimum is a maximum-welfare mass flow; Random Priority welfare is defined by the large-market limit of the paper’s finite mechanism.

What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(k\) and utility vectors \(v\), does finite-market Random Priority welfare per person converge, and what is the limit?
  • Does the expected ratio satisfy the uniform bound \(1+e\), and what is its sharp or asymptotic value?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that in one-sided matching with \(n\) agents and \(n\) items, when the valuation entries are drawn independently from \(U(0,1)\), Random Priority’s average-case approximation ratio—the expected value of \(SW_{\mathrm{OPT}}(A)/SW_{\mathrm{RP}}(A)\)—is at most \(1+e\). This is a welfare guarantee for the mechanism, not a runtime or hardness result. I would anchor on it rather than Theorem 1: the latter is explicitly about perturbing inputs, while this mirror makes the population the continuous object.

Here is a population regime in which the mirror seems plausible. Consider a large assignment market with \(q\) people, such as students choosing among categories of school seats. People fall into \(k\) archetypes that share the same complete utility vector over the seat categories. Each category has \(q/k\) interchangeable seats. Take \(q\) much larger than \(k\): there are many people per type, while the number of types and categories is moderate. The paper’s random valuation law can serve as a stylized way to draw the archetypes’ utility vectors; it need not be a claim about how real preferences are distributed.

For each \(k\), draw \(k\) vectors \(v^1,\ldots,v^k\in[0,1]^k\), with all entries independent and uniform. Type \(a\) has mass \(1/k\), and values category \(j\) at \(v^a_j\). In the continuum market, each category has supply \(1/k\). An allocation is a mass flow \(x_{aj}\geq0\), where \(x_{aj}\) is the mass of type \(a\) assigned category \(j\), with
\[ \sum_j x_{aj}=\frac1k \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_a x_{aj}=\frac1k. \]
Its welfare per person is \(\sum_{a,j}v^a_jx_{aj}\); \(SW_{\mathrm{OPT},\infty}\) is the maximum of this quantity over feasible flows.

Define \(SW_{\mathrm{RP},\infty}\) as the limiting expected welfare per person under Random Priority: for each multiple \(q\) of \(k\), make \(q/k\) agents of each type and \(q/k\) seat copies of each category, run the paper’s random-priority mechanism, and take expected total welfare divided by \(q\) as \(q\to\infty\). Establishing that this limit exists is part of the question.

I would call the resulting problem Continuum Random-Priority Average Ratio. For each \(k\), its instance is the random type profile just defined. Its target is
\[ \bar r_k = \mathbb E_{v^1,\ldots,v^k} \left[ \frac{SW_{\mathrm{OPT},\infty}}{SW_{\mathrm{RP},\infty}} \right]. \]
The prover’s task is to establish or refute the uniform bound \(\sup_k\bar r_k\leq 1+e\), and, ideally, characterize the limiting or sharp ratio. A proof of the bound, or a sequence of counterexamples, is a clear solution. My expectation is Class A: a constant welfare guarantee should be approachable through the continuum allocation dynamics and the transportation structure of the optimum. Whether the paper’s particular \(1+e\) bound survives is open in this mirror; it does not follow just from Theorem 2.

This is recognizably the paper’s problem: the types still have utility vectors over items, each person receives one indivisible item copy, Random Priority is unchanged, and welfare is still compared with the optimal assignment. The continuous variable is the population distribution over types. The item supplies scale with the population, and the type-level flows can be fractional because they describe mass; those are the natural co-modifications for a large assignment market. This covers Theorem 2 only. It does not turn the paper’s smoothed analysis into a population-continuity result.

The weakest point is that the paper samples a new valuation vector for each agent, whereas the mirror samples \(k\) archetype vectors and gives each positive mass. Cloning the vectors changes the dependence structure and the competition for interchangeable seat categories. So Theorem 2 motivates this question but does not settle it. Also, the iid uniform archetypes are a useful stress test, not a realistic preference model by themselves. Further questions include whether the bound holds for nonuniform type masses or unequal category supplies, how the ratio depends on support size, and whether a distributional assumption is needed for any constant guarantee.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is that Theorem 2 is an efficiency analysis of an existing mechanism, not a computational result in ChoCo’s sense. It proves a bound on Random Priority’s expected welfare ratio under a specified distribution. It gives no complexity classification, algorithm for a hard problem, or computational bottleneck for continuization to remove. The optimum it compares against is a maximum weight matching, already polynomial-time computable.

The proposed \(\bar r_k\) is a coherent new welfare question, but asking whether \(\sup_k \bar r_k\leq 1+e\) remains a question about stochastic mechanism performance. A more computational version would take rational type masses, utilities, and item supplies as input and ask for the continuum Random Priority welfare and the optimum. That version appears tractable by direct means: in the fluid limit, types consume their most-preferred available item until a supply is exhausted; there are at most \(m\) such exhaustion stages. The optimum is a transportation LP. So this formulation does not expose the kind of complexity landscape or continuous-optimization obstacle that motivates ChoCo. Proving a sharp expected-ratio theorem might still be mathematically interesting, but it would not turn this paper into a computational mirror.

There is also a real change in the instance law. With independent continuous valuations, agents’ utility vectors are distinct with probability one, so the paper’s own distribution has no moderate-support high-multiplicity regime. The archetype construction repairs that by cloning \(k\) sampled vectors, but it changes the independence structure: agents in a cohort now share all their values. That does not make the scenario illegitimate; it means the proposed theorem concerns a new random market model, not a limit that follows naturally from Theorem 2. The paper’s smoothed result is not a second anchor: its perturbations randomize valuations, not the population.

This is a strong reason to reject the paper as a ChoCo computational case study. It is not an honest proof that no useful continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The cloned market passes the basic high-multiplicity plausibility test, and a continuous welfare analysis of random serial dictatorship could be worthwhile in mechanism-design theory. The negative case is strongest when “worthwhile” means advancing ChoCo’s computational programme; it is weak as a universal claim about the value of continuous matching models.
