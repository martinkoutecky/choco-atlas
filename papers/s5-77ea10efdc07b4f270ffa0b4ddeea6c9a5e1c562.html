What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers block-augmentation versions of Theorems 4.3, 4.4, and 4.5, while leaving the general scoring-rule bounds, other rule analyses, and group-manipulation appendix untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the block-augmentation threshold for Borda equal \(m-2\) throughout the rational high-multiplicity regime?
  • Can thresholds for general scoring and runoff rules be computed from succinct type distributions, or does searching over rankings create a genuine complexity problem?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible positive case, with one important qualification: this paper contains no named theorem asserting NP-hardness, membership in P, W[1]-hardness, or FPT. Its named results are exact quantitative and impossibility results about manipulation potential. So I would not claim a complexity-class anchor that the paper does not contain. Under the broader reading in which a named theorem computing the paper’s central measure is a valid anchor, the mirror is strong.

The natural mirror is a continuous, high-multiplicity electorate. Let \(T=\mathcal L(C)\) be the rankings of the \(m\) candidates and let \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\) be the population distribution. Choose a truthful ranking \(r\in T\) and designate a block of mass \(\rho>0\) holding that ranking, with \(\mu_r\ge \rho\). A strategic report \(r'\) changes that block’s mass from \(r\) to \(r'\), producing \(\mu-\rho e_r+\rho e_{r'}\). Truthful resource augmentation by \(\lambda\) block-copies produces \(\mu+\lambda\rho e_r\). Here \(\lambda\) is measured in units of the focal block, exactly as \(k\) counts copies of the focal voter in the paper.

For a rule \(F\), define the continuous block-augmentation threshold of an instance as the least \(\lambda\ge0\) such that, for every \(\lambda'\ge\lambda\), every alternative report \(r'\), and every tie-breaker, the winner under \(\mu+\lambda'\rho e_r\) is weakly preferred according to \(r\) to the winner under \(\mu-\rho e_r+\rho e_{r'}\). The rule-level continuous manipulation potential is the worst case of this threshold over \(\mu,r,\rho\). This is the same question as the paper’s: how much truthful weight reproduces the best strategic outcome? It is not continuity of the outcome space.

The regime is plausible. Think of a large electorate containing many communities with identical complete rankings, or a corporate/blockchain election in which a community can buy additional voting weight. The type is the complete ranking; mass is the fraction of society with that ranking; the action is either reporting a different ranking for the focal community or recruiting additional truthful members. This is explicitly one of the paper’s own interpretations of manipulation potential, not an unrelated coalition model.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper. It states that Borda Count has manipulation potential \(MP(BC)=m-2\), with the lower-bound construction assuming even \(n\), and that Borda is not \(k\)-augmentation strategyproof for any \(0\le k<m-2\). In full, the theorem says that \(m-2\) truthful copies always suffice to make truthful augmentation at least as good as any manipulation, while for every smaller \(k\) there is a profile, voter, manipulation, and tie-breaker for which the manipulation is strictly better.

The corresponding problem is:

“Given \(m\), a rational continuous society \(\mu\), a focal ranking \(r\) of mass \(\rho\), and a Borda rule, compute the least real \(\lambda\ge0\) such that every \(\lambda'\ge\lambda\) truthful block-augmentations weakly dominate every possible report of the focal block.”

The expected worst-case answer is \(m-2\) in the high-multiplicity regime. The proof scales directly: replacing one voter by mass \(\rho\), and \(k\) copies by mass \(k\rho\), multiplies all Borda-score differences by \(\rho\). The lower-bound profile also works with fractional masses; the paper’s parity requirement is an integrality artefact. Thus the continuous question preserves the paper’s central phenomenon: Borda’s augmentation threshold depends on the number of candidates but not on the size of the electorate.

This is plausibly Class A. Borda scores are linear in \(\mu\), and the winning constraints are linear inequalities. The continuous problem therefore has the exact structure the continuization programme is meant to expose: a mass-transfer problem whose decisive object is a finite-dimensional linear score system, rather than an enumeration of named voters.

A second strong anchor is Theorem 4.3, also proved here. It states that Plurality with Runoff and Instant Runoff both have manipulation potential \(n-1\), under the lower-bound assumptions \(m\ge4\), \(n\ge5\), and \(n-1\) divisible by \(4\). It further states that neither rule is \(k\)-augmentation strategyproof for any \(0\le k<n-1\).

The continuous problem is the same block-augmentation problem with \(F\) equal to PWR or IRV. In normalized mass, the discrete correspondence is \(\rho=1/n\), so \(n-1=(1-\rho)/\rho\). The natural continuous conjecture, and the direct fractional-mass extension of their proof, is that the worst-case threshold is \((1-\rho)/\rho\). The upper bound follows because adding mass \(1-\rho\) makes the focal ranking’s top candidate a strict majority; the lower-bound construction keeps the runoff outcome worse than the manipulation for every smaller augmentation. Unlike Borda, the threshold diverges as the focal community becomes negligible. That is an informative continuous result, not a defect: the mirror distinguishes rules whose manipulation potential is population-independent from rules whose vulnerability grows with population scale.

This anchor is also tractable in the quantitative sense for these rules: the worst-case answer is an explicit function of the focal mass. More generally, one could ask for exact or approximation algorithms for the augmentation threshold of other runoff rules, or for a succinctly represented continuous society where winner changes must be searched over many ranking types.

A useful boundary anchor is Theorem 4.5, proved here. It states that every positional scoring rule \(F^s\) with \(s_1=s_2\) has \(MP(F^s)=\infty\); equivalently, it is not \(k\)-augmentation strategyproof for any \(k\ge0\). Its continuous mirror asks whether any finite truthful mass can dominate all manipulations. The answer remains no: let essentially everyone rank \(c_1\) above \(c_2\), let the focal truthful ranking have \(c_1\) first and \(c_2\) second, and let the tie-breaker prefer \(c_2\). Since \(s_1=s_2\), adding arbitrarily much truthful mass never gives \(c_1\) an advantage over \(c_2\), while a report placing \(c_2\) last makes \(c_1\) win. The continuous model therefore preserves the paper’s structural impossibility boundary exactly.

The main weakness is unavoidable: a literal individual has zero mass in a continuum, so an individual manipulation disappears. My mirror therefore treats the strategic voter as a positive-mass community or weighted ballot. That is a genuine change of granularity, and the opposing analyst can reasonably call it a group model. I think it survives because the paper itself motivates manipulation potential through communities with identical preferences and increased voter weight, while the continuous programme explicitly permits type-level mass actions. Moreover, taking \(\rho=1/n\) recovers the paper’s discrete model exactly, and taking \(\rho\) as a community’s mass gives the high-multiplicity regime the paper’s interpretation already invites.

Thus I would accept this as a genuine continuous-population mirror of the paper’s central problem, led by Theorem 4.4. I would describe its expected landscape as Borda: tractable and population-independent; PWR/IRV: tractable with a threshold scaling as \(1/\rho\); plateau scoring rules: structurally unbounded. I would not claim that this paper itself supplies a Class B hardness result: it does not.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the programme’s gate: this paper has no named computational result. Theorems 4.3, 4.4, and 4.5 characterize a worst-case quantitative property of voting rules; they do not give a complexity classification, an algorithmic problem, or an optimization formulation over a society. The paper’s “computing” section proves bounds analytically. Thus the proposed task of computing a continuous augmentation threshold is a new problem wrapped around the paper’s measure, not a high-multiplicity version of a computational problem in the paper.

There is also a more fundamental modelling problem. A genuine individual manipulation has mass \(0\) in a continuum. If the focal voter changes from \(r\) to \(r'\), the society remains \(\mu\); for every non-tied profile, neither the manipulation nor any finite number of infinitesimal truthful copies changes the winner. To preserve an effect, the proponent assigns the focal voter a positive mass \(\rho\):

\[ \mu^{\mathrm{lie}}=\mu-\rho e_r+\rho e_{r'},\qquad \mu^{\mathrm{truth}}_\lambda=\mu+\lambda\rho e_r. \]

But this is no longer individual manipulation. It is manipulation by a bloc of mass \(\rho\). The paper itself distinguishes such questions in Definitions 6–7, where group manipulation receives a separate notion and can behave radically differently; Theorem B.1 gives an infinite group manipulation potential for Copeland. Calling the bloc a “community” makes the scenario plausible, but does not make it the same strategic problem. Calling it a weighted voter makes it a weighted finite-electorate model, not a continuum of agents.

Theorem 4.4 is therefore the proponent’s strongest anchor, but also exposes the limitation. For Borda, once a positive block is admitted, everything reduces to a one-dimensional perturbation of linear score inequalities. The claimed \(m-2\) threshold is simply the discrete proof with counts replaced by weights. That may be a sensible weighted-community question, but it creates no continuous-population computational structure: no mass-transfer optimization, no nontrivial type aggregation, and no separation problem. If one instead preserves an actual individual by taking \(\rho\to0\), the effect disappears. Retaining \(\rho\) as an additional “particle size” parameter merely reintroduces the finite-granularity parameter that continuization was supposed to remove.

Theorem 4.3 is weaker still. The proposed value \((1-\rho)/\rho\) is not the natural real-valued analogue of \(n-1\); it is a sufficient integer-style augmentation. With total initial mass \(1\), adding \(\lambda\rho\) mass makes the focal top choice a strict majority once

\[ \rho(1+\lambda)>\frac{1+\lambda\rho}{2}, \]

equivalently \(\lambda\rho>1-2\rho\). For \(\rho=1/n\), the discrete least integer is \(n-1\), while the continuous threshold is an unattained infimum \(n-2\). This is repairable by defining an infimum, so it is not by itself decisive, but it shows that the claimed mirror is chiefly a change from integer rounding to real weights. Moreover, if the continuous rule-level quantity ranges over arbitrarily small \(\rho\), the threshold diverges; fixing \(\rho\) leaves an exogenous particle-size parameter rather than an intrinsic property of \(\mu\).

Theorem 4.5 does not rescue the case. Its \(s_1=s_2\) argument survives for a positive bloc, but it is a structural tie-breaking impossibility, not a computational result. For a zero-mass individual it becomes vacuous; for a positive bloc it is again a group version. It identifies a boundary of the scoring rule, not a boundary of continuous-population computation.

One can construct a respectable weighted-community research question from this paper. The paper’s own discussion gives it some legitimacy, and Borda’s theorem would likely survive that reinterpretation. But that is precisely why the universal negative claim cannot honestly be defended. The best negative verdict is narrower: the proponent has not supplied a continuous computational mirror of this paper; they have supplied a plausible weighted-bloc reinterpretation of its quantitative measure. Under the programme’s broader willingness to treat that reinterpretation as legitimate, Theorem 4.4 remains a credible green anchor.
