The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Its closest hook is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: on the domain of Average Externalities, for populations of size \(n\ge3\), any social ordering satisfying Strong Pareto, Unchanged-Contour Independence, Separability, and Transfer to Disadvantaged must strictly rank \(x\) above \(y\) whenever \(\min_i u_i(x)>\min_i u_i(y)\). That is an axiomatic characterization, not a complexity result, so it cannot be an anchor under your rule.

The strongest positive case is therefore a natural computational follow-on to Theorem 1, rather than a mirror anchored by one of the paper’s computational results. A plausible regime is a large benefit or resource allocation among residents who fall into a modest number of preference types concerning their own consumption and average consumption. A type is a complete preference relation of the kind used in the paper; the society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of residents of type \(t\). Think millions of residents and tens or hundreds of types. The paper’s good is already divisible; the continuization here is of the population.

One precise follow-on is Average-Externality Maximin Allocation. Its input is a rational total resource \(B>0\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a rational threshold \(q\), and for each type an explicit rational piecewise-linear utility representation \(v_t(x,\bar x)\) satisfying the paper’s Average Externalities assumptions. The question is whether there are per-person allocations \(x_t\in[0,B]\) such that
\[ \sum_t \mu_t x_t=B \quad\text{and}\quad v_t(x_t,B)\ge v_t(q,q)\ \text{for every }t. \]
A solution is such a vector \((x_t)_t\); finding the largest feasible \(q\) gives the maximin egalitarian-equivalent allocation.

Allocating the same amount to every person of a type is without loss for this threshold problem. At the fixed average \(B\), each person of type \(t\) must receive at least the type’s minimum acceptable consumption for \(q\). If an allocation splits that type across consumption levels, replacing those levels by their mean preserves the resource total and still meets that minimum. Thus, at a fixed \(q\), feasibility reduces to checking whether the mass-weighted sum of the types’ minimum acceptable consumptions is at most \(B\). With the stated piecewise-linear input, I would expect this restricted problem to be Class A, solvable in time polynomial in the representation size. This is a proposed algorithmic result, not one established by the paper.

This is a plausible extension because Theorem 1 supplies the maximin egalitarian-equivalent comparison, and the paper explicitly motivates social rankings for settings where the feasible allocations are limited. It covers only the Average Externalities result’s welfare criterion. A positional-externality version would be a separate question: there, each person’s welfare depends on their rank in the allocation distribution, so the rank calculation becomes endogenous.

The weak point is decisive for the requested anchor-based case: the paper studies axioms and social rankings, not algorithms or computational difficulty. The proposed question adds a resource constraint, a maximin selection among the refinements the paper leaves open, and a finite encoding of ordinal preferences. Those are sensible choices for a follow-on study, but the paper neither makes them nor proves anything about their complexity. So there is a credible population-continuization research direction here, but no honest computational anchor from which to claim that this paper already supplies a positive mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize social orderings from axioms; Proposition 2 checks that a particular ordering satisfies those axioms. None states the complexity of a computational problem. The paper also leaves some comparisons unresolved: its maximin egalitarian-equivalent condition does not decide ties. So there is no named computational conclusion here whose continuous-population analogue could be classified.

The proposed Average-Externality Maximin Allocation problem is a plausible follow-on, but it adds the computational task. It introduces a fixed resource constraint, a particular maximin refinement, piecewise-linear utility encodings, and a threshold-feasibility question. Those choices make a precise problem, but they are not part of Theorem 1. A better-designed problem could compute or optimize the theorem’s welfare criterion over a feasible set; it would still need to choose that set, encode the ordinal preferences, and decide how to handle ties. That may be worthwhile research, but it is computational work built on the paper’s ethical criterion, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

I would not support stronger objections. Large populations sharing a modest number of preference types are a sensible regime for these models, and neither the divisible good nor the ordinal welfare comparison rules out a population continuum. The honest negative case is therefore strong against calling this paper a computational mirror, but weak against the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous-population research could grow from it.
