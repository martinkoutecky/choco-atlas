What the mirror covers

The proposed question echoes Theorem 3(i)'s welfare bound, but covers no computational result; the paper's other equilibrium and composition bounds are also left without computational mirrors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow. The paper has no named result about runtime, NP-hardness, or parameterized complexity. Its strongest usable anchor is a named welfare-efficiency theorem, so this fits ChoCo only if “and so on” includes performance guarantees.

The anchor is Theorem 3(i), proved in this paper: for submodular valuations, the Bayesian Price of Anarchy of the Discriminatory Auction is at most \(e/(e-1)\), under either the standard or uniform bidding interface. This is a worst-case bound on the ratio of expected optimal welfare to expected equilibrium welfare, over the paper’s Bayesian instances and equilibria.

A plausible population regime is a large Treasury bond auction in which many investors follow a small number \(\tau\) of investment mandates. A type is an investor’s complete valuation curve, demand limit, and any other parameter affecting their payoff; mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of investors of type \(t\). The number of investors can be large while \(\tau\) stays small. This is a natural extension of the paper’s own bond-auction setting, though the identical-type mandate assumption would need to be credible in a particular market.

I would call the mirror Nonatomic Discriminatory Auction PoA. An instance gives a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) summing to one, a demand limit \(d\), a per-capita supply \(\kappa\), and each type’s nondecreasing, submodular valuation curve \(v_t:\{0,\ldots,d\}\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\). Agents still receive integral bundles; averages across a type’s mass may be fractional. Each agent chooses a nonincreasing vector of \(d\) marginal bids. The auction allocates the highest \(\kappa\) mass of marginal bids, splitting cutoff ties proportionally, and charges each winner’s submitted bids. A type’s strategy is a distribution over bid vectors; equilibrium means no individual of that type can improve by deviating against the aggregate bids. The problem is to compute, or approximate to a specified additive error, the supremum over equilibria of \(W^*/W\), where \(W^*\) is optimal feasible welfare and \(W\) is equilibrium welfare. The result to test is whether this ratio is always at most \(e/(e-1)\).

I expect that bound to carry over: Theorem 8 gives the finite auction a smoothness inequality for submodular values, and its welfare and payment terms appear to extend by integration over the population. That would make the bound question tractable as a proof. It does not establish a polynomial-time algorithm for computing equilibria or the exact worst-case ratio, so I would not call it Class A in ChoCo’s algorithmic sense. Natural follow-ups are whether the bound is tight in the nonatomic model, whether equilibria exist under the chosen bid and tie rules, and how this limit relates to finite high-multiplicity auctions.

The soft spot is substantial: making each bidder infinitesimal removes their ability to affect the clearing outcome, which changes their incentives. Authors might recognize the model as a nonatomic limit of their auction, but the theorem they prove is an equilibrium welfare bound, not a computational-complexity result. So this is a credible population mirror of one result, and a weak fit for ChoCo’s central complexity programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3(i) is the only plausible anchor: it bounds the Bayesian Price of Anarchy for the Discriminatory Auction with submodular valuations by \(e/(e-1)\). But this is an analytic welfare guarantee, not a result about the complexity of a computational problem. The paper gives no algorithm, hardness result, or complexity classification to continuize. That is the central mismatch with ChoCo.

The strongest proposed mirror is still a sensible auction model: investor mandates can define complete types, rational masses can describe their proportions, and supply can scale per capita. But its nonatomic equilibrium makes an individual too small to affect the market outcome. Asking whether the PoA bound persists is a legitimate question about a different equilibrium model; it does not continue a computational result from this paper.

One could add a computational task—for example, given type masses and valuation curves, compute or approximate the worst welfare ratio over nonatomic equilibria. That would give ChoCo an input-output problem, but it changes what Theorem 3 asks. The paper’s bound is uniform over instances, priors, and equilibria; the per-instance optimization task is new. Conversely, keeping the bound as the target leaves a theorem to prove, with no stated algorithmic problem or complexity parameters. Extending the paper’s smoothness argument by integration would remain welfare analysis.

So the case against a *ChoCo computational mirror* is strong: even the best anchor is not computational. The case that no worthwhile continuous question exists in any scenario is weaker. The proponent has found a credible nonatomic-auction question that the authors might recognize as theirs; its weakness is that it belongs to continuous equilibrium analysis, not the complexity programme ChoCo set out to pursue.
