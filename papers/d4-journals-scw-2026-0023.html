What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror covers the witness existence theorem and its implications for the minimal extending set and tournament equilibrium set; majority realization supplies an outside-literature answer, and the paper gives no computational procedures for finding or changing witnesses.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a population version of the paper’s separation witness. Take a large electorate over 36 alternatives, with each type a complete ranking and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of society holding ranking \(t\). For each pair \(x,y\), define the majority margin
\[ \Delta_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t-\sum_{t:y\succ_t x}\mu_t. \]
Require every margin to be nonzero, and let \(T_\mu\) orient each pair toward its majority. A search problem would ask for a rational distribution \(\mu\) such that \(BA(T_\mu)\cap BP(T_\mu)=\varnothing\) and \(BA(T_\mu)\cup BP(T_\mu)=A\); a solution is the supported ranking types and their masses. A plausible regime is millions of voters sharing a relatively small set of ballot types, so the number of voters is much larger than \(\tau\).

But this is only a candidate extension, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of the paper. Its sole relevant result is the unnumbered Theorem, proved here: there exists a tournament \(T=(A,\succ)\) of order 36 for which the Banks and bipartisan sets are disjoint and together cover \(A\). The authors describe a manual construction and also report computer verification. The stated consequences for the minimal extending set and tournament equilibrium set follow from earlier inclusions. None of these results asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or any other complexity class.

The population formulation has some social-choice plausibility: the paper itself describes tournaments as pairwise comparisons commonly supplied by majority rule. Its weak point is that the paper’s object is already the tournament; it neither models a society of ranking types nor asks how to find or analyze a distribution. Asking whether a *given* \(\mu\) induces disjoint sets would be a new computational problem. Asking for some such \(\mu\) would make the theorem’s existence claim a population formulation, but would add no complexity result. I can’t honestly predict a Class A, B, or C outcome from this paper. So the positive case is a plausible population interpretation, but under the requested anchor standard, the paper contains no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest on the theorem in §3, which is the sole anchor. It proves a structural fact about one finite tournament: its Banks and bipartisan sets partition the alternatives. The paper takes the tournament as its input; it states no complexity result about constructing one, recognizing one from voter data, or changing one. The consequences for the minimal extending set and tournament equilibrium set inherit the same witness.

A society of ranking types is plausible here, since pairwise comparisons often come from majority rule. But the proposed search over distributions \(\mu\) adds no computational question to this theorem. Its margins,
\[ \Delta_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t-\sum_{t:y\succ_t x}\mu_t, \]
just determine the tournament’s edge directions. Asking for some \(\mu\) that induces the paper’s witness lifts that fixed witness into ballot space; every finite tournament has a finite majority realization. Asking whether a given \(\mu\) induces disjoint sets first computes those edge directions, then asks the original tournament question. Neither formulation mirrors a complexity result about population masses, support size, or high multiplicity.

The strongest improved version would ask for the minimum mass of voters whose rankings must change, at some specified cost, to make the induced tournament’s Banks and bipartisan sets intersect—or to create disjointness from a given society. That is a meaningful population-level robustness question. It also adds a baseline, costs, and an intervention objective absent from the paper. Its continuous geometry is the distance between majority-margin regions; the paper gives no result about that distance or its computation. It could be a worthwhile new project, but it cannot be presented as the computational content of this theorem.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed witness-search mirror: it is a population encoding of a known finite construction, with no computational result from the paper to carry over. It is weak against the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. A mass-based robustness problem could be worth studying, and the paper’s majority-voting setting makes the population regime credible. I can’t honestly rule that out; it would be a new computational question prompted by the paper, rather than a mirror of its theorem.
