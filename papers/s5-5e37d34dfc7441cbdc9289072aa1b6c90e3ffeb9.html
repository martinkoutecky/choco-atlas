The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 characterizes when a preference relation is compatible with dominance (AC1–AC3), and when it is strictly compatible (AC4). Theorems 2 and 3 establish numerical representation results. These are structural and representational theorems; none asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithm for a computational problem. The paper also does not compute with an encoding in a way that would make one of those theorems an algorithmic anchor.

A large panel of decision makers with shared preference-relation types could motivate a new continuous question—for example, whether their aggregate preference relation is compatible with dominance. But that shifts the paper’s object from a relation over multi-attribute alternatives to a population of people and a rule for aggregating their relations. I can’t present it as a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results. The strongest honest positive case is that its characterizations could inform a future computational project; this paper itself supplies no required anchor or per-anchor mirror problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational anchor here. Theorem 1 characterizes dominance compatibility through AC1–AC3 and AC4; Theorems 2 and 3 give numerical representations. These are structural results about a relation \(h\), not complexity results about an encoded computational problem. Their constructions do not specify an input model, an algorithmic task, or a complexity measure. So none supplies a problem whose population-continuous version could be a mirror.

The strongest repair of the panel idea would give each decision maker a complete preference type, put rational mass on those types, choose a voting rule such as majority, and ask whether the resulting relation satisfies AC1–AC3 or AC4. That is a coherent new computational aggregation problem. But the population, voting rule, and computational task all come from the extension; the paper takes \(h\) itself as its object and does not study how a population generates or modifies it. Adding mass-transfer costs would introduce still more of the problem from outside the paper. The characterization might inform such work, but that makes it a new project, not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

The negative case is strong on that distinction, though it cannot rule out a worthwhile project inspired by the paper. It rules out the claimed mirror: no population scenario can supply the missing computational result to mirror.
