What the mirror covers

The mirror covers type-compressed BPS, integral schedule bundles, per-capita capacities, and continuous profile metrics. It leaves the empirical comparisons, survey evidence, truthfulness claims, and externally cited mechanism guarantees without an in-paper computational anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does exact type-mass BPS remain polynomial when bundles are generated from compact timetable and preference parameters?
  • What is the precise equivalence between rational finite BPS instances and their normalized type-mass representations?
  • Can a finite lottery or \(\varepsilon\)-implementation be recovered while preserving meaningful capacity guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a qualified one: this paper has a very natural continuous-population problem, but it has no qualifying named computational anchor under the programme’s strict rule.

The only named in-paper result is “Lemma [AUPCR]” (printed without a number in the supplied text and proved in Appendix 6). It states that, for bundle rankings with \(R\) possible ranks, the normalized area under a matching’s profile curve is

\(\operatorname{AUPCR}(M)=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\frac{|\{(i,b)\in M:\operatorname{rank}_i(b)\le r\}|}{|S|}\).

This is a correct reformulation of the profile metric, but it is not a complexity or algorithmic result. The claims that BPS is polynomial-time, ordinally efficient, envy-free, and weakly strategy-proof are presented as results of Nguyen et al.; the PPAD-completeness of A-CEEI and the \(\#P\)-completeness of RSD probabilities are likewise cited from elsewhere. The paper’s own BPS pseudocode and empirical runtime tables are not named theorems or propositions.

Subject to that limitation, my lead mirror would be Continuous Type-Compressed BPS.

Consider a large university or education system with recurring cohorts of students. There are \(\ell\) course classes, a finite set \(G\) of tutor groups, and a feasible bundle set \(\mathcal B\), where a bundle contains at most one tutor group from each class. Each group \(g\) has a per-capita capacity \(q_g\). Thus a finite realization with \(N\) students has \(Nq_g\) seats in group \(g\).

A type \(t\) is a complete student description for this problem: course eligibility, feasible bundles, and the complete strict ranking \(\succ_t\) over \(\mathcal B\), including the tie-breaking used by the paper’s schedule-scoring procedure. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_t)_{t\in T}\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students of type \(t\), not a fractional course bundle assigned to an individual. Rational masses can be cleared to obtain \(N\) ordinary students with identical preferences in each type.

The decision variable is a type–bundle mass allocation \(x_{tb}\). It must satisfy

\(\sum_{b\in\mathcal B}x_{tb}\le\mu_t\)

and

\(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{b:g\in b}x_{tb}\le q_g\).

The continuous BPS rule runs exactly as in the paper, but with type mass in place of individual students: while \(s<1\), every type sends all currently unassigned mass at unit speed to its most-preferred currently available bundle. When a tutor group is exhausted, every bundle containing it is deleted. The output records the event times and the resulting \(x_{tb}\). At the continuum level, a type may split across bundles because different portions of its mass receive different integral schedules; no individual receives a fractional schedule.

The problem is:

Given \((G,\mathcal B,q,\mu,\{\succ_t\}_{t\in T})\), compute the exact BPS event sequence and the resulting allocation \(x\), and report its continuous profile metrics, including

\(\operatorname{AUPCR}_\infty(x)=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\operatorname{rank}_t(b)\le r}x_{tb}\).

An optional implementation version asks for a finite-support lottery over integral schedule assignments whose expected type–bundle masses equal \(x\), or approximate \(x\) within \(\varepsilon\), with the paper’s \(\ell-1\) capacity-violation guarantee.

This is a genuine population continuization. The continuous object is the society: students with identical complete preferences are represented by mass. Course schedules remain integral, and the per-capita capacity scaling is the natural co-modification required to prevent a growing population from exhausting a fixed finite timetable.

The regime is plausible in a recurring undergraduate programme, a multi-campus university, or a large online education provider. Students in the same programme and year may share course eligibility, timetable constraints, commute windows, and the same automated ranking parameters. In that regime \(N\) can be in the thousands while the number \(\tau\) of exact preference types is much smaller. The paper’s own \(1{,}400\)- to \(1{,}800\)-student applications are evidence that the population scale is realistic, although the exact-type assumption becomes weaker if students manually modify their rankings idiosyncratically.

With an explicit bundle list and explicit rankings, this problem is expected to be Class A: there are only finitely many capacity-exhaustion events, and the trajectory can be simulated in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|\mathcal B|\), \(\ell\), and the input bit length, independently of \(N\). The interesting version is the one matching the paper’s missing-bids motivation, where \(\mathcal B\) is implicit and preferences are generated by compact schedule parameters. Then each event requires finding the best remaining bundle for each type. For fixed \(\ell\) and structured daily scores this may admit dynamic programming or enumeration; with arbitrary timetable conflicts or complementarities, the pricing problem may become hard. That is precisely the kind of continuous computational question the programme is meant to expose.

The paper’s authors should recognise this as their problem: it preserves BPS, bundles, course capacities, ordinal schedule preferences, random assignment, and the practical reason they care about polynomial computation. It also separates the population compression from the paper’s other continuous-looking object. The paper’s \(x_{ib}\) variables are probabilities in a randomized assignment; they do not themselves make the population continuous.

The mirror does not cover the survey, the field comparison with FCFS, or the empirical claims about satisfaction and truthfulness. Those require a model of reporting behaviour and are not computational results in the paper. Nor would I claim that the cited BPS guarantees are anchors of this paper.

The weakest point is therefore decisive under the formal rules: there is no numbered NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or approximation result proved in the paper to anchor the mirror. If the anchor requirement is non-negotiable, this is not a compliant positive case. If the requirement is relaxed to include the paper’s unnumbered and cited claim that BPS is polynomial-time, Continuous Type-Compressed BPS is a credible Class A mirror, with compact-bundle pricing, finite-population implementation, and type-level strategyproofness as the natural follow-up questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest on the programme’s formal gate: this paper contains no qualifying named computational result of its own. Its only named result in the supplied text is “Lemma [AUPCR],” which merely rewrites a profile statistic:

\[ \operatorname{AUPCR}(M) = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^{R} \frac{\left|\{(i,b)\in M:\operatorname{rank}_i(b)\le r\}\right|}{|S|}. \]

That is an accounting identity, not a complexity, optimization, approximation, or algorithmic theorem. The polynomial-time claim for BPS is attributed to Nguyen et al.; the PPAD- and \(\#P\)-completeness claims are also imported from other papers. The pseudocode and runtime tables are implementation material, not named computational results. Thus, under the stated anchor rule, there is nothing in this paper for a continuous mirror to mirror.

The proposed AUPCR mirror does not repair this. Replacing counts by type masses gives

\[ \operatorname{AUPCR}_\infty(x) = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{r=1}^{R} \sum_{t} \sum_{\operatorname{rank}_t(b)\le r}x_{tb}, \]

but computing this is just summation after an allocation has been supplied. Optimizing it over allocations would be a new assignment problem, not a continuous version of the paper’s Lemma [AUPCR].

The proposed continuous BPS model is mathematically coherent, and this is where the negative case is genuinely weak. Recurring cohorts with repeated eligibility, timetable constraints, and ranking parameters are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. I cannot honestly claim that no sensible type space exists.

The stronger objection is that BPS already eliminates the individual identity relevant to its computation. If \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and capacities are scaled per capita, the type-mass process is exactly the normalized process obtained by replacing each type \(t\) by \(n_t\) identical students. Every event is determined by aggregate demand and residual capacities. This is not objectionable merely because the answers coincide; rather, the paper’s own computational mechanism has no identity-sensitive object left for continuization to expose. The proposed mirror is a type-compressed implementation of the existing anonymous fractional mechanism, not a new computational question supported by a result in this paper.

The continuum also removes the paper’s most distinctive implementation difficulty. In the nonatomic model, \(x_{tb}\) can be implemented directly by assigning submasses of type \(t\) to integral bundles. There is no need to decompose \(x\) into a finite lottery, and the \(\ell-1\) capacity-violation issue disappears. If one insists on a lottery over finite integral assignments, one has returned to the finite problem. If one accepts direct mass allocation, the paper’s lottery algorithm is no longer the object being studied.

The implicit-bundle variant is more promising, but it is no longer a mirror of a named result either. Asking for the best currently available bundle under compact schedule parameters could indeed produce a pricing or separation problem. Yet that question comes from the paper’s preference-elicitation and bundle-generation problem, not from population continuization. It would be a worthwhile new problem under a broader programme, but not evidence that this paper supplies a qualifying anchor.

Truthfulness fares worse in the continuum. A unilateral report by one agent has measure zero and cannot affect aggregate BPS demand, so individual strategy-proofness becomes a price-taking or vacuous notion. A positive-mass misreport is a coalition deviation, not the paper’s individual incentive question. Preserving nontrivial individual manipulation requires atoms and therefore gives up the nonatomic limit.

So the universal claim is not mathematically airtight: the recurring-cohort type-compressed model is legitimate and could be useful if the programme admits cited results and newly generated computational questions. But under the stated rules, the proponent’s own concession is decisive. AUPCR is not a computational anchor, and BPS’s computational guarantees are not results of this paper. No qualifying anchor survives.
