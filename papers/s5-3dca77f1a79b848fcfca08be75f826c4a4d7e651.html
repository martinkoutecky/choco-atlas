The continuous mirror question

Given items \(M\), a finite set \(T\) of rational monotone valuation types \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), consider a measurable population in which each person receives an integral bundle. A feasible aggregate allocation has masses \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:j\in S}x_{t,S}\le q_j\); let \(W^*=\max\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\). For a monotone, loser-independent \(c\)-approximate allocator acting pointwise on reports, define an individual’s critical price by varying a tagged person’s report while holding the rest of the population fixed. If each round applies SIMPLIFY and charges these critical prices, does the average welfare reach at least \(W^*/(c+1)-o(1)\) when almost every individual has vanishing external regret, and what runtime bounds follow from mass-allocation and tagged-price oracles?

The model it lives in

A repeated, atomless combinatorial-auction market with \(\tau\) valuation types of masses \(\mu_t\) and per-capita item supplies \(q_j\). Each individual receives a whole bundle; aggregate assignments are described by \(x_{t,S}\), while the allocator and critical-price rule must also specify pointwise outcomes for tagged individuals and their deviations.

The objection that survived

Although the proponent flags threshold and tie-rationing details, the proposed aggregate variables \(x_{t,S}\) do not specify a single agent’s counterfactual: changing that person’s report has zero effect on the type distribution. The mirror needs a pointwise tagged-agent allocation rule and threshold oracle, which Theorem 3.4 does not supply.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.4’s general no-regret implementation guarantee for monotone, loser-independent approximate allocators. It leaves Proposition 3.5’s Byzantine-agent extension, the best-response guarantees in Theorems 4.7 and 4.8, and Conjecture 4.1 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a finite-agent monotone, loser-independent allocator be lifted to a measurable pointwise rule with well-defined tagged-agent thresholds and feasible per-capita supplies?
  • For specified valuation representations, does integrating almost-everywhere individual regret yield the \(W^*/(c+1)-o(1)\) guarantee, and what are the runtime bounds for its mass-allocation and tagged-price oracles?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is Theorem 3.4. The paper contains no numbered NP-hardness result to anchor on; the hardness cited in §1.3 is background, not a theorem proved here. Theorem 3.4 is a proved approximation guarantee for a computational mechanism-design problem.

A plausible regime is a large digital advertising market: many advertisers compete repeatedly for bundles of ad slots, but most share one of a relatively small number of valuation templates. A type is a complete valuation function, including any parameters that affect the advertiser’s incentives. With \(n\) advertisers and \(\tau\) distinct types, the intended regime has \(n\gg\tau\). To keep competition meaningful as the population grows, let the supply of each slot grow with the population too. This is a replicated combinatorial auction: in the limit, the population has mass \(1\), and slot \(j\) has per-capita supply \(q_j\). That scaling is a co-modification forced by the population limit.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: No-Regret Implementation for Replicated Combinatorial Auctions. An instance has a set \(M\) of \(m\) items, a finite set \(T\) of monotone normalized valuation functions \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\). An allocation assigns each person an integral bundle. In aggregate, it is described by masses \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), with
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T,\;S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le q_j\quad(j\in M). \]
Its per-capita welfare is \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\); call the maximum \(W^*\). The variables \(x_{t,S}\) are fractional masses, not fractional bundles: each person still receives a whole bundle.

Each round, individuals make declarations as in the paper. A monotone, loser-independent allocation rule \(\mathcal A_\infty\) acts on the population of declarations, returns integral bundles to individuals, and is a \(c\)-approximation to \(W^*\). The mechanism simplifies each person’s declaration as in the paper, runs \(\mathcal A_\infty\), and charges each winner the threshold price for their bundle. The question is whether this mechanism guarantees average welfare at least \(W^*/(c+1)-o(1)\) over repeated rounds whenever almost every individual’s average external regret vanishes. A solution is the mechanism and a proof of that guarantee; for a computational instantiation, it should also give the running time in \(m,\tau,L\) and the cost of the allocation and threshold-price oracles.

The anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper: *any monotone loser-independent \(c\)-approximation algorithm can be implemented as a mechanism with price of total anarchy \(c+1\).* In the paper’s finite-agent setting, this means that under vanishing external regret, the mechanism’s average welfare approaches at least a \(1/(c+1)\) fraction of optimal welfare. The proposed mirror keeps the same allocation problem, approximation objective, regret benchmark, and critical-price mechanism; it replaces a finite list of agents with a measure over types and allocations. The theorem is not a result about an LP encoding: the mirror asks whether the underlying allocation-and-implementation problem retains the same guarantee.

I expect this mirror to be Class A when the mass-allocation and threshold-price oracles are efficiently computable. The proof strategy has a natural continuous form: apply the regret and loser-independence inequalities to individuals, then integrate over the population. For an actual combinatorial-auction instantiation, however, the exponential bundle space leaves a real pricing question. The theorem alone does not establish a polynomial-time oracle for arbitrary valuation functions. Further questions include which valuation classes admit efficient mass-allocation and pricing oracles, what runtime dependence on \(\tau\) is achievable, and whether aggregate type-level regret is enough or the guarantee needs almost-everywhere individual no-regret.

My weakest point is the market scaling and the threshold interpretation. The source has finitely many agents and a fixed inventory; this mirror scales inventory with population and must define threshold prices and tie rationing for infinitesimal agents. Those choices need to be made carefully, and authors might regard them as a replicated-market variant rather than their exact original auction. I still think the question is theirs: it is the same repeated combinatorial-auction implementation problem under high multiplicity, with discrete bundles and the same welfare objective. The population, not the outcome space, is what has been continuized.

I would not use Theorem 4.8 as a second anchor. It is a proved \(O(\sqrt m)\) welfare guarantee for the paper’s general-CA mechanism under random single-agent best-response updates, but one such update has zero mass in a continuum. A faithful mirror would need a population-flow update model, making that anchor a weaker fit than the regret result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.4 is the strongest anchor, and I would not dismiss it because its guarantee might survive unchanged. The problem is that the theorem’s proof is built around a named bidder’s unilateral deviation: fix that bidder’s optimal bundle, compare it with the critical price at which the bidder would win, then sum those individual inequalities. The proposed mass-allocation variables \(x_{t,S}\) describe aggregate welfare, but not that comparison. In an atomless population, one person’s deviation has zero mass and leaves the aggregate report distribution unchanged. Cohort deviations would matter, but they change the strategic model: a whole type mass acts together, rather than each bidder minimizing regret against their own fixed declaration.

There is a plausible repair. Give a tagged infinitesimal bidder a pointwise allocation and threshold price against the background distribution, and scale inventory with population so per-capita supply stays positive. That could make a replicated advertising market coherent. But the mass-allocation LP alone cannot define those tagged outcomes or prices. They need a rule for assigning integral bundles to individuals, resolving rationing at the cutoff, and evaluating each bidder’s counterfactual insertion. The paper’s computational result is then conditional on those allocation and threshold oracles. Its runtime depends on their representation and cost; the aggregate welfare program does not provide them. The mirror would require a new nonatomic auction model and a new implementation proof, rather than a direct continuous version of the stated mass-allocation problem.

That leaves a real case against this being a strong ChoCo target: Theorem 3.4 is a named implementation guarantee, but it is not itself a complexity classification for a concrete allocation problem. To make it computational, one must choose a specific allocator, valuation representation, and tagged-agent pricing model. The paper’s theorem supplies no particular pricing problem for continuization to expose.

This is not a decisive objection. Standardized bidder cohorts and a well-defined tagged-agent rule could make the mirror worthwhile, and the authors might recognize replicated combinatorial auctions as their problem. The negative case is therefore weak at its boundary: it shows that the proponent’s aggregate formulation does not yet preserve the theorem’s strategic object, but it cannot rule out a careful tagged-agent formulation.
