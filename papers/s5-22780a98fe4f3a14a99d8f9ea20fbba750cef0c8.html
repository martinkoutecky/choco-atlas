The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no qualifying computational anchor. It contains numbered hypotheses and simulation findings, but no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a complexity or algorithmic result. For example, Section 4.7 supports Hypothesis 1: in the simulated settings, the LMSR generally produces more trades than the CDA. That is an empirical result of the agent-based model, not a result about an underlying problem being in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. I can’t honestly turn it into an anchor by relabeling it.

The strongest positive lead is an unanchored extension of that comparison. In a *Continuum Prediction-Market Volume Comparison* problem, an instance would specify outcomes, a true outcome, a finite set of trader types, rational masses \(\mu_t\), a finite-state trading policy and per-capita endowments for each type, a horizon, market parameters, and explicit mass-clearing rules for the CDA and LMSR. A type’s mass is its share of the participating population. Each update aggregates the orders generated by each type; the solution computes the two markets’ trajectories and compares their per-capita executed contract mass. This replaces transaction count, which does not carry over directly when individual transactions have vanishing mass.

A plausible regime is a large organization or platform where many participants share the same information category, training, policy, and endowment: \(N\) could be in the thousands while the number of distinct types \(\tau\) is much smaller. The authors might recognize this as a population-limit study of their mechanism comparison: their paper already varies strategies, information distributions, and trader counts, and discusses markets drawing on large, dispersed groups. Under fixed finite-state policies and explicit clearing rules, I would tentatively expect the bounded-horizon version to be tractable through aggregate flow updates. That is a conjecture about a new problem, not a result of this paper.

The case is weak at its central point: the paper’s finite, randomized interaction process is part of what it models and validates. A continuous CDA needs a specified mass-matching rule, and replacing individual dynamics with aggregate flows could change the behavior the authors studied. The paper’s own examples also include prediction markets with only 12 or 20–30 participants. So this may be a sensible follow-up for a suitable large-population setting, but it is not a mirror anchored in a named computational result from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its numbered hypotheses and findings concern simulated outcomes: trade counts, prediction accuracy, and price variation under a particular agent model. It gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or computational problem. That is a decisive gap for an anchored mirror; it does not mean the paper’s findings would survive continuization or that a dull answer would be uninteresting.

The proponent’s large-organization scenario is plausible. A market could have many participants grouped by shared information, strategy, and endowment, with relatively small support \(\tau\). So neither the paper’s 12-agent default nor the fact that types group similar participants rules out a continuous model. The difficulty is that the proposed mirror changes what is being measured and how the market works. Per-capita contract volume is not transaction count. In a CDA, trades arise from matching individual orders, and order priority and sequence affect prices; a continuum of mass needs new matching and rationing rules. Under the LMSR, aggregate orders can be defined more directly, but replacing individual responses with type-level flows changes how actions affect price. Those choices may yield a sensible new model, but they are not a consequence of taking the paper’s population to a continuum.

There is a similar problem with accuracy and price variation. They are outcomes of stochastic, path-dependent interactions in the paper. A deterministic aggregate-flow model may erase the fluctuations that the paper’s standard-deviation result studies. Retaining those fluctuations requires a stochastic continuous-market model and a specified distribution over orders and interactions. Either route could support useful research, but neither supplies the missing computational result. With fixed policies and a bounded horizon, calculating aggregate trajectories may simply be simulation; adding an optimization problem could create a richer computational question, but it would be a new research question rather than a mirror of a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s paper-level criterion: none of the paper’s results is a qualifying computational anchor, and the proposed extension does not supply one. The broader universal claim is weaker: a continuous prediction-market model in a high-multiplicity organization could be worthwhile in its own right. I would not claim that no plausible scenario exists; I would conclude that this paper does not establish a worthwhile continuous computational mirror without adding a new problem and new market semantics.
