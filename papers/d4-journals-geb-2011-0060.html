The case FOR (proponent)

The most plausible setting is industry wide wage bargaining, with many employers and workers grouped into a modest number of complete types. A type would include occupation, productivity, recognition probability, discount factor, and every other attribute used by the bargaining model; its mass would be its fraction of the bargaining population. At a remaining-mass state \(r\), a proposer would be drawn according to type mass and recognition weight, then choose a coalition mass \(x \le r\) and transfers by type. The proposer’s objective is to maximize their expected payoff given the other types’ acceptance decisions and continuation values; accepted mass leaves bargaining. A candidate problem is to decide whether such an encoded game has a type-symmetric stationary equilibrium that forms the grand coalition immediately, and return its type-wise transfers and payoffs if it does.

That is a plausible extension of the paper’s bargaining question, but it needs new modeling choices: an atomless proposer has zero mass, and unanimity among a continuum of responders needs a precise interpretation. More decisively, I find no qualifying computational anchor in the paper. Its named results characterize equilibrium existence, efficiency, or payoffs; none gives a complexity classification or a computational formulation of the kind the anchor rule requires. The published abstract likewise describes an equilibrium characterization and a wage-bargaining application, not a computational result ([Okada’s published article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geb.2011.01.008)).

So the labor-market mirror is a research direction, not a result-backed case for this paper. I can’t honestly attach it to a numbered computational anchor or predict whether its continuous version is tractable, inherits discrete hardness, or is continuum-specifically hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its results characterize equilibrium existence, efficiency, and payoffs in a finite-player random-proposer bargaining game. They do not define a decision or optimization problem with an algorithm, a complexity classification, or an approximation guarantee. The proposed question—whether an encoded type-symmetric game has a stationary equilibrium forming the grand coalition immediately—is therefore a new computational problem, not a continuous version of a named computational result in the paper.

The best alternative would stay close to the paper’s own model: take one employer and a large workforce with finitely many complete worker types, then ask how to compute the equilibrium transfers and payoffs. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting; I would not object that workers cannot share types. But it still requires choosing a new computational input model for the production function, outside options, recognition probabilities, and discounting. The paper’s equilibrium characterizations do not select a unique such problem. The resulting project could be worthwhile bargaining research, but it is not a mirror anchored in this paper’s computational contribution.

There is also a deeper obstacle to treating the paper’s bargaining game as a society described by type masses. Each round selects one active player as proposer, and coalition agreement depends on individual responses. In a continuum, each person has zero mass. Giving a type’s mass the proposer probability makes the proposer a zero-mass representative whose role and payoff are not captured by the aggregate state. Keeping a particular proposer as an atom preserves that role but adds identity-level state. Likewise, acceptance by almost every member makes an individual veto invisible, while literal unanimity lets a zero-mass person block agreement. A mass-threshold acceptance rule resolves that tension by changing the coalition-formation rule.

Those are not objections that the continuous answer might be easy, or that the finite answer might carry over. They concern whether the paper’s central mechanism—one individually recognized proposer bargaining with a coalition whose members must accept—survives in a type-mass model. The proposed type-symmetric formulation assumes away the issue; a formulation that retains individual recognition and vetoes needs information beyond the population distribution.

This negative case is strongest against a *mirror of this paper*. It is not airtight against every adjacent research direction: a carefully specified hybrid model with an employer atom and a continuum of workers might be interesting. But establishing that model would be a new bargaining-game project, not a continuous computational result supplied by this paper.
