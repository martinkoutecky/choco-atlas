The continuous mirror question

Given finite complete agent types \(T\) with masses \(\mu_t\), entitlement intensities \(\alpha_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t\alpha_t=1\), additive per-unit values \(v_t(e)\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\rho_e\) of item classes \(E\), choose a mass \(y_{t,b}\) of type-\(t\) agents receiving each integral bundle \(b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{E}\), subject to \(\sum_b y_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}y_{t,b}b_e=\rho_e\). With \(P_t=\alpha_t\sum_e\rho_ev_t(e)\), minimize \(\sum_{t,b}y_{t,b}(P_t-v_t(b))^+\), and determine exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithms and whether the optimum is always at most \(\frac14\max_{t,e}|v_t(e)|\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity indivisible-bundle allocation model: agent types carry mass, item classes have per-capita supply, each individual receives an integral bundle, and the decision variables \(y_{t,b}\) distribute bundle types across the population while minimizing average subsidy.

The objection that survived

The polynomial guarantee in Result 2 relies on enumerating \((m+1)^{\binom{n}{2}}\) fPO allocations for fixed \(n\), so it does not directly establish an algorithm for the variable-\(n\), variable-catalogue mass problem; that problem requires new configuration-LP or pricing analysis.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the WPROP allocation-with-subsidy problem and the limiting \(\tau(n)/n\to 1/4\) threshold. It leaves the KKM proof, fPO and PO guarantees, fixed-\(n\) enumeration, and the purely existential content of Theorem 4.19 untransferred.

Open questions for a prover

  • For variable \(|E|\), can the configuration LP and its pricing problem be solved exactly, approximately, or parameterized by \(|T|\) or \(|E|\)?
  • Under denominator-clearing finite approximations, does the optimum converge to the mass optimum while preserving the \(\frac14\max_{t,e}|v_t(e)|\) threshold?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead anchor is Result 2 in Section 1.1, proved in this paper. It states that, for mixed manna with additive valuations and general weights, when the number of agents \(n\) is fixed, one can compute in polynomial time a WPROP allocation whose total subsidy is at most \(\tau(n)\). This is an algorithmic result about the underlying allocation problem, not merely about the LP encoding.

The strongest continuous mirror is a high-multiplicity version of precisely that problem.

Take a finite set of complete agent types \(T\). Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), additive valuation \(v_t\), and entitlement intensity \(\alpha_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t\alpha_t=1\). There is also a finite catalogue \(E\) of item classes. Class \(e\) has per-capita supply \(\rho_e\), and each individual receives an integral bundle \(B\subseteq E\); items are never divided between individuals. The type-level allocation is a distribution \(y_{t,B}\), where \(y_{t,B}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving bundle \(B\). Thus

\(\sum_B y_{t,B}=\mu_t\)

and

\(\sum_{t,B:e\in B}y_{t,B}=\rho_e\).

Let \(V_t=\sum_e\rho_ev_t(e)\) be type \(t\)'s per-capita value of the whole manna, and let \(P_t=\alpha_tV_t\) be its proportional benchmark. The total subsidy is

\(\sum_{t,B}y_{t,B}(P_t-v_t(B))^+\).

The continuous problem is:

HM-WPROP\(_\infty\). Given \((\mu,\alpha,v,\rho)\), compute an integral-bundle mass allocation \(y\) and subsidies making every agent WPROP, minimizing the displayed average subsidy; in particular, determine whether there is a solution of cost at most \(\frac14\operatorname{range}(v)\).

This is genuinely a population continuization. The \(y_{t,B}\) are fractional only because they describe masses of agents; every individual still receives an indivisible bundle. The many agents are recurring cohorts—say households with finitely many needs profiles, or workers sharing the same valuations and entitlements—while the manna is replicated at a fixed per-capita rate.

The scaling is exactly the paper’s high-multiplicity scaling. For a finite approximation with \(N\) agents, take \(N\mu_t\) copies of type \(t\), give each such agent weight \(\alpha_t/N\), and create \(N\rho_e\) copies of each item class. Then every type-\(t\) agent has benchmark \(\alpha_tV_t\), and the paper’s total subsidy divided by \(N\) converges to the continuous objective. Since \(\tau(N)/N\to\frac14\), Result 2 points to the sharp continuum threshold \(\frac14\operatorname{range}(v)\). The paper’s cited lower-bound family with identical valuations and equal weights also survives this replication, so this constant is not arbitrary.

For fixed numbers of types and item classes, this is plainly Class A. Enumerate the integral bundles and solve

\(\min\sum_{t,B}y_{t,B}(P_t-v_t(B))^+\)

subject to the mass and supply equations above. The resulting LP has \(O(|T|2^{|E|})\) variables and is polynomial for fixed \(|T|\) and \(|E|\). This is not outcome-space continuization: integrality is retained at the individual level, and fractionality appears only as an aggregate population distribution.

The second anchor is Theorem 4.19, proved here. It states in full that every mixed-manna instance with \(n\) agents, additive valuations, and arbitrary positive weights admits a WPROP1 allocation that can be made WPROP with total subsidy at most \(\tau(n)\operatorname{range}(\mathcal I)\), where \(\tau(n)=n/4\) for even \(n\) and \(\tau(n)=(n^2-1)/(4n)\) for odd \(n\).

Its continuous counterpart is:

HM-WPROP1\(_\infty\). Under the same type-and-supply model, find a mass allocation \(y\) such that every bundle in the support of \(y\) is WPROP1 for its type, meaning that for every used bundle \(B\), either \(v_t(B)\ge P_t\), or adding one item, or removing one item, reaches \(P_t\). Minimize the integrated subsidy, and ask whether the bound \(\frac14\operatorname{range}(v)\) is always attainable.

This is again a meaningful Class A problem for a fixed item catalogue: simply delete from the LP every bundle that is not WPROP1. For variable \(|E|\), the natural configuration LP has exponentially many bundle variables. Its pricing problem involves choosing a bundle under a trade-off between item prices and \((P_t-v_t(B))^+\), which can encode knapsack-like structure. I would therefore expect the fixed-catalogue version to be tractable, while the unrestricted-item version is a serious candidate for continuum-specific hardness rather than transferred discrete hardness.

The authors should recognise these mirrors as their problem. Their Section 5 already studies agents divided into a constant number of identical-valuation groups and obtains a subsidy bound of \(\tau(n)+\tau(k)+\varepsilon\). Passing to a continuum of agents with fixed type masses is the natural high-multiplicity limit of that regime. The \(k\)-dependent term becomes negligible after normalizing total subsidy per capita, while the \(\tau(n)\) term converges to \(1/4\).

The mirror deliberately does not cover the paper’s Pareto-optimality claims, the KKM proof, or the fPO-enumeration machinery. Those are methods and side guarantees; the covered object is the weighted proportional allocation-with-subsidy problem itself.

My weakest point is that the paper’s actual polynomial-time theorem fixes the number of agents, whereas the mirror fixes the number of types and, in the cleanest algorithmic version, the item catalogue. The passage from the finite fPO algorithm to the continuous configuration LP is therefore a new result, not something already proved in the paper. If the referee insists that the number of item classes must remain unrestricted, the pricing problem may be hard. But that weakness affects the expected complexity of the mirror, not whether the high-multiplicity problem is a coherent analogue of the paper’s allocation problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proponent has not actually continuized the computational content of Result 2. Its polynomial-time guarantee is polynomial only because \(n\) is fixed: the algorithm enumerates at most \((m+1)^{\binom{n}{2}}\) fPO allocations. In the proposed high-multiplicity regime, \(n\) tends to infinity, so that argument disappears entirely.

The proponent’s fixed-\(|T|\), fixed-\(|E|\) formulation does not repair this. With finitely many item classes, there are only constantly many bundles \(B\), and the displayed mass-allocation problem is an ordinary finite transportation LP. Its tractability follows without the paper’s KKM argument, fPO structure, or fixed-agent enumeration. If \(|E|\) is unrestricted, the configuration LP has exponentially many bundles and its pricing problem can encode knapsack-like choices. The proposed mirror is then a new item-packing problem, not a continuous version of the paper’s algorithmic theorem. Thus the proponent has shown either a trivial LP or an unproved new problem, not a meaningful limit of Result 2.

The same objection applies even more strongly to Theorem 4.19. That theorem is existential rather than computational, so it does not itself provide a complexity result to continuize. The proponent’s HM-WPROP1 problem is nevertheless a coherent optimization problem, but it is essentially the same new configuration problem as the first anchor, rather than an independent computational consequence of the theorem.

However, this negative case does not meet the requested universal burden. The stronger version of the mirror survives the objections. Let \(|E|\) vary, retain integral bundles for each individual, and study exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithms for the resulting configuration LP. This is genuinely a continuous population model: only masses of repeated agent types are fractional, while item assignments remain indivisible. Agent identity is irrelevant because valuation and entitlement are part of the type, and the finite problem embeds by clearing denominators.

Nor does the continuum degenerate. With one identical agent type, unit-valued goods, equal weights, and per-capita supply \(\rho=1/2\), half the population receives one item and half receives none. Each agent’s proportional benchmark is \(1/2\), so the average subsidy is exactly \(1/4\). This is the limit of the paper’s \(N/4\) lower-bound family, since \(\tau(N)/N\to 1/4\). The population limit therefore preserves a nontrivial sharp phenomenon rather than washing it away.

So the honest negative verdict is limited: the proponent overstates how directly Result 2 transfers, and its fixed-catalogue LP is too routine to showcase the paper’s machinery. But a stronger, variable-catalogue high-multiplicity mirror remains well-posed, natural, and potentially suitable for configuration-LP and pricing analysis. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
