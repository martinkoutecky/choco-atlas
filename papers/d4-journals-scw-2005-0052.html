The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is at the level of the population model. The paper explicitly studies societies of infinitely many agents, including a continuum \(N=[0,1]\) with Lebesgue measure. A type can be a complete preference relation over the alternatives; mass is the measure of agents with that preference. A plausible computational regime would have a very large electorate but relatively few distinct rankings, represented by a finite-support distribution with rational masses. That is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, and the authors would recognize it as part of their subject.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor the requested mirror. Proposition 1 is an existence result for aggregation rules, relying on Armstrong’s work and Zorn’s lemma. Theorem 1 gives an axiomatic impossibility condition; Theorems 2 and 3 characterize consensual and measuring rules. None asserts that a computational problem is in \(P\), hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise classifies its complexity. So I cannot state a qualifying continuous problem per anchor without inventing one that the paper does not provide.

For ChoCo, this paper is strong evidence that a continuous-population regime is natural in social choice. It could motivate new computational questions about finite-support preference distributions. But its own results are axiomatic, and its society is already continuous. Turning its theorems into algorithmic questions would be a new computational project, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the boundary between a mirror and a new project. The proponent identifies no named computational result to mirror: Proposition 1 is an existence result, and Theorems 1–3 give axiomatic impossibility and characterization results. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or supplies an algorithmic task with an input and output. The proponent’s point that the paper makes population mass a natural object is sound, but it establishes a plausible regime, not a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s results.

The best candidate extension would restrict to finitely many alternatives and a finite-support distribution of preference types with rational masses, then ask for the cost of changing the social outcome by transferring mass between types. That would fit the programme’s interests. But this paper supplies neither a cost or intervention model nor a computationally represented rule to optimize against. Its rules are maps over measurable profiles on broadly specified coalition measure spaces; the characterization theorems allow collections of coalitions, and Proposition 1 invokes ultra-filters. There is no canonical finite encoding for these objects. If one adds a succinct, computable representation of a measuring rule and a cost model, those choices supply the computational problem’s essential structure. Its complexity would be a result about the added model, not a mirror of a complexity result in this paper.

The paper’s existing continuous population model is not itself a collision: its results are axiomatic, not computational. It does, however, make the distinction sharp. A computational study based on this model could be worthwhile, but it would extend the paper into a new line of work rather than continuize one of its computational results. The negative case is strong for the requested notion of a mirror; it cannot establish that no valuable computational project could grow from the paper.
