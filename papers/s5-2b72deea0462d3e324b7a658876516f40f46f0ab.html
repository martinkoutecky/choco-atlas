The continuous mirror question

Given finite projects \(C\), per-capita costs \(q(c)\), budget \(B\), and rational utility types \(t\in T\) with masses \(\mu_t\), compute an integral \(W\subseteq C\) with \(\sum_{c\in W}q(c)\leq B\) using mass-weighted PropRank, and verify that every submass \(\sigma\leq\mu\) of mass \(\eta>0\) satisfying \(\sum_{c\in X}q(c)\leq B\eta\) and \(\sum_{c\in X}\min_{t:\sigma_t>0}u_t(c)\geq\alpha\) obeys \(\eta^{-1}\sum_t\sigma_tu_t(W)\geq(\alpha-u_{\max})/2\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest conclusion is mixed: this paper has a very plausible continuous-population mirror, but it has no qualifying computational anchor in the strict sense of the programme. Its numbered results prove proportionality guarantees, not membership in \(P\), hardness, FPT algorithms, or approximation complexity. The claims about known algorithms and hardness in the introduction are literature summaries. Thus, formally, there is no eligible anchor. Still, the substantive mirror is strong.

My lead would be a continuous version of Theorem 4, proved in this paper. The theorem says that PropRank, under participatory-budgeting constraints, returns an outcome with proportionality degree \(d(\alpha)=(\alpha-u_{\max})/2\): for every \(\alpha\)-cohesive voter group \(S\), the average utility of \(S\) in the selected outcome is at least \((\alpha-u_{\max})/2\), where \(u_{\max}\) is the largest utility assigned to any candidate. This is a theorem about the paper’s own algorithm and is proved there, not cited.

The natural mirror is Continuous PropRank for High-Multiplicity Participatory Budgeting. An instance consists of a finite candidate set \(C\), per-capita project costs \(q(c)\), a per-capita budget \(B\), and finitely many utility types \(T\). Type \(t\) has utility vector \(u_t:C\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\), and \(\mu_t\) is its population mass, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A feasible outcome is an integral set \(W\subseteq C\) with \(\sum_{c\in W}q(c)\leq B\); projects remain indivisible.

A coalition is now a submass vector \(\sigma\) satisfying \(0\leq\sigma_t\leq\mu_t\), with mass \(\eta=\sum_t\sigma_t>0\). Put \(\underline u_\sigma(c)=\min_{t:\sigma_t>0}u_t(c)\). The coalition is \(\alpha\)-cohesive if some \(X\subseteq C\) satisfies \(\sum_{c\in X}q(c)\leq B\eta\) and \(\sum_{c\in X}\underline u_\sigma(c)\geq\alpha\). Its average satisfaction from \(W\) is \(\bar u_\sigma(W)=\eta^{-1}\sum_t\sigma_t\sum_{c\in W}u_t(c)\).

The problem is: compute a feasible \(W\) such that, for every \(\alpha\)-cohesive submass \(\sigma\), \(\bar u_\sigma(W)\geq(\alpha-u_{\max})/2\). The canonical solution is the type-level PropRank process: each unit of population earns virtual money at rate one, type \(t\) has aggregate earning rate \(\mu_t\), and all voter sums in the paper’s affordability and payment equations become mass-weighted sums. A candidate is bought when \(\sum_t\mu_t\min(x_t(c),u_t(c)\rho)=q(c)\), with the same scaling and tie-breaking rules as PropRank. The output is \(W\), optionally together with this payment trajectory.

This is a genuine population continuization. A large city or university could have millions of residents but only a few dozen recurring utility profiles, determined by stable demographic or service-need classes. The costs and budget are naturally per-capita: an instance with masses \(\mu_t\), costs \(q(c)\), and budget \(B\) corresponds to \(n\) voters with \(n\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\), project costs \(nq(c)\), and budget \(nB\). Thus the finite-to-continuous dictionary is exact for rational masses, while the algorithm works with \(\tau\) types rather than \(n\) named voters.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A. The type-level PropRank dynamics involve finitely many candidates and types, and each affordability calculation is a weighted, piecewise-linear computation. The likely research question is whether the exact event sequence and the proportionality certificate can be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length, together with a separation algorithm for violated cohesive submasses. That complexity result is not in the paper; it would be new work.

A second, worthwhile mirror uses Theorem 6, also proved in the paper. It states that under matroid constraints, PropRank has weak proportionality degree \(d(\alpha)=(\alpha-u_{\max})/2\): every strongly \(\alpha\)-cohesive group receives average utility at least that quantity. The continuous problem replaces the electorate by \(\mu\) as above, gives \(C\) a matroid \(\mathcal M\), and keeps unit candidate costs. A submass \(\sigma\) is strongly \(\alpha\)-cohesive if, for some integer \(\beta\), every \(T\in\mathcal M\) with \(|T|<\beta(1-\eta)/\eta\) can be extended by a set \(X\) of \(\beta\) candidates such that \(X\cup T\in\mathcal M\) and \(\beta\min_{t:\sigma_t>0,\ c\in X}u_t(c)\geq\alpha\). The task is to output \(W\in\mathcal M\) with \(\bar u_\sigma(W)\geq(\alpha-u_{\max})/2\) for every such \(\sigma\). Again, the natural expectation is a type-level Class A algorithm given a matroid independence oracle.

The weakest point is decisive: these are substantively good continuous analogues of the paper’s algorithms and guarantees, but they are not mirrors of named complexity results. Moreover, rational mass distributions can encode ordinary finite elections exactly, so one cannot claim that discrete hardness dissolves merely from this paper. The positive case is therefore: the paper supplies an unusually natural high-multiplicity model and algorithmic guarantees that should transfer to mass dynamics; the formal case under ChoCo’s anchor rule is nevertheless incomplete unless a later paper proves an actual complexity theorem for one of these continuous problems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case has a decisive formal core: neither Theorem 4 nor Theorem 6 is a computational result in ChoCo’s sense. The paper proves proportionality guarantees for proposed rules. It does not prove membership in \(P\), hardness, approximation, parameterized tractability, or any comparable complexity statement. The introduction’s algorithmic claims are literature summaries, not results established here. Thus the proposed continuous tasks are new follow-up problems, not continuous mirrors of named computational results. The proponent effectively concedes this.

Theorem 4 is nevertheless the strongest substantive rescue, so consider its best formulation. Let voters be utility types with masses \(\mu_t\), scale project costs and the budget per capita, retain integral projects, and run PropRank with mass-weighted balances and payments. This is a sensible high-multiplicity model. But it does not create a new computational object. PropRank’s finite execution already depends on voters only through utility vectors, balances, and sums such as
\[ \sum_i x_i(c). \]
Voters with identical utility vectors are interchangeable. Replacing these sums by
\[ \sum_t \mu_t x_t(c) \]
is an aggregation of repeated rows, not a new continuous optimization problem. The outcome remains an integral subset of a finite candidate set, and the process still consists of finitely many candidate purchases and piecewise-linear arithmetic.

The proposed requirement
\[ \bar u_\sigma(W)\geq \frac{\alpha-u_{\max}}{2} \]
for every cohesive submass \(\sigma\) does not repair this. If \(W\) is produced by PropRank, the requirement is simply the theorem’s universal quantification rewritten over masses; for rational masses it follows by cloning types and applying the finite argument. If instead one asks to recognize violations or search over cohesive submasses, one has introduced a new separation problem involving the candidate set and feasibility constraints. That may be worth studying, but it is not a computational question posed or solved by Theorem 4, and its difficulty would come from the discrete project-selection structure rather than from continuizing the population.

The only ways to make population continuity do more work are to fractionalize projects or permit fractional purchases. The former continuizes the outcome space, which is outside the programme’s scope; the latter changes the rule and its proportionality statement. Conversely, adding individual-specific prices, budgets, or utility parameters merely enlarges the type description. If many agents share them, the same aggregation applies; if almost none do, the high-multiplicity regime disappears.

Theorem 6 has the same problem, even more plainly. With a matroid \(\mathcal M\), the best mirror retains an integral independent set \(W\) and replaces voter counts by masses. The integer \(\beta\), the candidate-level minimum utilities, and the matroid exchange argument remain unchanged. For rational masses, the entire statement can again be recovered by replicating each type sufficiently many times. The mass affects only the entitlement threshold
\[ \beta\frac{1-\eta}{\eta}, \]
not the combinatorial object being selected. A type-level implementation is therefore a compressed implementation of the finite rule, not a new continuous-computational regime.

The proponent’s stronger claim—that one could study certificates or separation over all strongly cohesive submasses—does not help. That would be a worthwhile new problem only after introducing an additional computational contribution absent from this paper. It cannot serve as a mirror of Theorem 6 merely because the theorem’s quantifiers have been rewritten using measures.

This is not an objection that the continuous answer would be identical, nor that hardness might survive. Identical answers and transferred hardness are both legitimate outcomes under ChoCo. The objection is that the paper supplies no qualifying computational result and its two most promising continuizations reduce to mechanically aggregating an already anonymous finite algorithm. The continuous population is mathematically sensible, but it is not where this paper’s contribution lives.

The honest weakness of the negative case is that, if ChoCo relaxes its anchor rule to include algorithmic proportionality theorems, then the proponent has identified plausible high-multiplicity follow-up work. Under the programme as stated, however, these are sensible new projects rather than worthwhile continuous mirrors of this paper.
