Why no mirror

The proposed mass-based decision problem is well-defined, yet it optimizes over proposals rather than mirroring a computational result proved in this paper.

What the mirror covers

Theorem 6 gives a universal fairness ceiling; Theorems 1–5 bound proportionality guarantees for MES, Phragmén, and EJR rules. The experiments compare rule outcomes but state no computational guarantee.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the rule-independent satisfaction ceiling in Theorem 6 persist for arbitrary rational population masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow. This paper has no numbered computational-complexity result that qualifies as an anchor under your criterion. Its numbered results prove proportionality guarantees and bounds; they do not classify a problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. The statement that MES is polynomial-time computable appears in related work, not as a numbered result proved or cited here. So I can’t honestly present this as a direct complexity result for ChoCo.

The closest substantive foothold is Theorem 6, proved in Appendix A. It says that there is a participatory-budgeting instance, a project set \(T\), and a \(T\)-cohesive group \(V\) such that, under the outcome of any PB rule, \(V\)’s average satisfaction is at most
\[ \frac{\operatorname{cost}(T)}{\max_{t\in T}\operatorname{cost}(t)}-1. \]
That is a rule-independent obstruction: even choosing a different rule cannot beat the bound on the constructed instance. It is a useful anchor for a continuous analogue of the paper’s proportionality question, though it is not a qualifying complexity anchor.

A concrete mirror would be Continuous \(T\)-Cohesive Satisfaction. An instance has a finite project set \(P\), a per-capita budget \(b>0\), positive rational per-capita costs \(q_p\), a target set \(T\subseteq P\), and a rational-mass distribution \(\mu\) over approval types \(A\subseteq P\), with support size \(\tau\). A cohesive submass is a vector \(\nu\) with \(0\leq\nu_A\leq\mu_A\), supported only on types approving every project in \(T\), and with total mass \(\alpha=\sum_A\nu_A\geq q(T)/b\). The question, given a threshold \(\lambda\leq |T|\), is whether there is a feasible proposal \(W\subseteq P\), with \(q(W)\leq b\), such that
\[ \frac{\sum_A \nu_A\,|A\cap W|}{\sum_A\nu_A}\geq\lambda \qquad\text{for every T-cohesive submass \nu.} \]
A solution is such a proposal \(W\), or a “no” answer. The target is the paper’s underlying object—cohesive groups’ average satisfaction—not a continuous outcome. Here each type gets the same integral project bundle; only its population mass and group averages are fractional.

This is a plausible large-municipality regime: many residents, a finite proposal slate, and far fewer distinct approval ballots than voters. The type is a complete approval set, and equal per-capita budgets match the paper’s model. Costs and budget are normalized per resident, so \(q(T)/b\) plays the role of \(\operatorname{cost}(T)/B\). That scaling is a co-modification needed for a population limit. It does not make projects divisible. Theorem 6 then motivates asking whether there are continuous instances where no proposal clears a specified satisfaction threshold; its bound gives a candidate universal cap.

I would expect this decision problem to be hardness-transfer territory when \(m=|P|\) varies: the proposal still consists of binary project choices, so the continuum does not remove that combinatorial core. For a fixed proposal \(W\) and fixed \(T\), finding a least-satisfied cohesive submass is a finite optimization over the \(\tau\) types, so the population continuum itself looks tractable. That forecast is a research direction, not a claim established by the paper. Natural follow-ups are whether Theorem 6’s cap persists exactly for arbitrary rational masses, whether it is tight in the continuous model, and how the decision problem behaves when parameterized by \(m\) or \(\tau\).

The weakness is substantial: the proposed decision problem optimizes over proposals, while the paper analyzes the guarantees of named rules. Theorem 6’s “any rule” bound gives a bridge, but the paper supplies no complexity result about this optimization problem. Thus the model is a credible continuous-population extension of its proportionality analysis, but the positive case for a ChoCo complexity mirror is suggestive rather than anchored by a qualifying result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper does not give a named computational result to continuize. Its theorems bound the proportionality guarantees of MES, Phragmén, EJR rules, and arbitrary PB rules. MES’s polynomial running time is mentioned as prior work; the paper does not analyze the complexity of computing either rule or of any other problem.

Theorem 6 is the best anchor, and its population model is sound. In a large municipality, approval sets are natural types; per-capita costs and budget give a coherent mass version, while each type still receives an integral bundle. The theorem’s universal bound can be expressed as a cap on
\[ \max_{W:\,q(W)\le b}\ \min_{\nu\ \text{T-cohesive}} \frac{\sum_A \nu_A |A\cap W|}{\sum_A \nu_A}. \]
That is a legitimate continuous fairness question. I cannot dismiss it on grounds of multiplicity, degeneration, or an existing continuous treatment.

But this formulation changes the object of study. The paper bounds the outcomes of named rules and proves a worst-case ceiling for any rule; it does not study the computational problem of finding the proposal that maximizes worst-case cohesive satisfaction. The proposed threshold problem is a plausible new optimization problem motivated by Theorem 6, rather than a continuous version of a computational result the paper establishes. Direct continuous versions of its other results would likewise be statements about fairness guarantees, not complexity.

So the negative case is limited but clear: the paper may motivate a continuous-population study of proportionality, but it offers no computational-complexity anchor for a ChoCo mirror. The proponent’s model is credible; its link to the paper’s results is about the fairness question, not the computational question.
