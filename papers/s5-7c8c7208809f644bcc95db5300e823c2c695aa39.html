The continuous mirror question

Given \(K\ge2\) outcomes, \(\tau\) trader types with rational masses \(\nu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational belief parameters \(\theta_t\in\mathbb{Q}^K\), positive rational risk aversions \(a_t\), positive rational per-capita liquidity \(\beta\), a cost family \(C\) chosen from LMSR or IND, and rational accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\), compute a rational price vector \(\hat p\) within Euclidean distance \(\varepsilon\) of the unique minimizer \(p^\star\) of \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\frac{\nu_t}{a_t}\bigl(T^*(p)-\theta_t^\top p\bigr)+\beta C^*(p)\) over \(p\in\Delta_K\), where \(T(\theta)=\log\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\theta_k}\).

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete trader types \((\theta_t,a_t)\), with mass \(\nu_t\) per type, finite outcomes and securities, and per-capita market-maker liquidity \(\beta\). The decision variable is the equilibrium price vector \(p\in\Delta_K\); the objective is \(\sum_t\frac{\nu_t}{a_t}(T^*(p)-\theta_t^\top p)+\beta C^*(p)\), with \(C\) equal to LMSR or IND.

The case FOR (proponent)

On the strict complexity reading, this paper has no named result saying an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity. Under your allowance for an optimization formulation that the paper computes with, there is one defensible anchor: Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper (Appendix B.2), using techniques from prior work. It says that under the exponential trader model, a market-maker equilibrium always exists and its prices are unique; the equilibrium allocations minimize \(F(r)=\sum_i F_i(-r_i)+C_b(\sum_i r_i)\), where \(F_i(s)=T(\tilde\theta_i+a_i s)/a_i\); and the equilibrium prices are exactly the minimizer of \(\sum_i F_i^*(p)+bC^*(p)\). The paper uses this optimization characterization in its analysis and numerical experiments. It gives a credible, if narrow, computational mirror.

My lead problem would be Typed Continuum Market-Maker Equilibrium. Keep the paper’s finite set of outcomes and securities; make only the population continuous. An instance has \(K\ge2\) mutually exclusive outcomes, a finite list of types \(t=1,\ldots,\tau\), and rational masses \(\nu_t>0\) summing to one. Each type is specified by its exponential-family belief parameter \(\theta_t\in\mathbb Q^K\) and risk aversion \(a_t>0\). All types trade through the same cost function, chosen here from the paper’s LMSR and IND families. The market has per-capita liquidity \(\beta>0\): in a finite population of \(n\) agents, use total liquidity \(b=n\beta\). This scaling makes the total population and the market-maker’s liquidity grow together.

For \(T(\theta)=\log\sum_{k=1}^K e^{\theta_k}\), define the market-maker equilibrium price as the unique minimizer
\[ p^\star=\arg\min_{p\in\Delta_K} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\frac{\nu_t}{a_t}\bigl(T^*(p)-\theta_t^\top p\bigr) +\beta C^*(p) \right]. \]
The problem is: given the listed types, masses, liquidity, cost family, and rational accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\), return a rational price vector \(\hat p\) with \(\|\hat p-p^\star\|_2\le\varepsilon\). The target is the equilibrium price itself, not its encoding. For this complete market, \(T^*(p)=\sum_k p_k\log p_k\) on \(\Delta_K\); \(C^*\) is this entropy for LMSR and \(\sum_k[p_k\log p_k+(1-p_k)\log(1-p_k)]\) for IND. The formula is Theorem 4.2 with the finite sum over agents replaced by mass-weighted contributions from types.

I expect this restricted problem to be tractable (Class A). The support list has size \(\tau\), and the optimization is over \(K\) prices; with these explicit cost functions it is a finite-dimensional convex optimization problem. For LMSR the solution even has the form \(p_k^\star\propto\exp(\eta_k/(W+\beta))\), where \(W=\sum_t\nu_t/a_t\) and \(\eta=\sum_t\nu_t\theta_t/a_t\). Establishing a bit-complexity bound for IND, and for poorly conditioned inputs generally, would be part of the new work; Theorem 4.2 itself proves no runtime bound. Further questions include the dependence on \(K,\tau\), input bit length, and \(\varepsilon\), and how far the result extends to other explicitly represented convex cost functions.

A plausible regime is a large organizational forecasting market with a bounded menu of standardized information and risk profiles: many participants share the same modeled belief and risk aversion, while the number of such profiles stays small relative to the population. A type is complete for this model because it includes both parameters the paper uses to distinguish traders. This is plausible for a deliberately standardized market; it is a weaker story for an open market where individual beliefs vary freely. The authors could recognize the mirror: it computes the equilibrium prices for their trader model when the population is represented by repeated types. It does not change the outcome space into a continuum.

The mirror covers the equilibrium-price computation in Theorem 4.2, not the paper’s sampling-error bounds or its analysis of discrete trade rounds. Those address forecast accuracy and trading dynamics, rather than this static population-computation question. The weakest point is also the central limitation: the paper offers no complexity result, and its experiments use \(N=10\) traders rather than a demonstrated large population with small support. So this is a credible high-multiplicity extension of one of the paper’s core mathematical objects, but the positive case depends on accepting an optimization characterization as the computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only plausible anchor is Theorem 4.2. It characterizes the equilibrium through convex optimization, but it gives no algorithm or complexity bound. The proposed approximate-price task is a legitimate problem one could define from that characterization. The strongest case against it is that the population contributes no computational structure for that task.

Write \(w_i=1/a_i\), \(W=\sum_i w_i\), and \(\eta=\sum_i w_i\tilde\theta_i\). The theorem’s price objective is
\[ \sum_i F_i^*(p)+bC^*(p) = W T^*(p)-\eta^\top p+bC^*(p). \]
For the proposed type distribution, the corresponding inputs are just \(W_\mu=\sum_t\nu_t/a_t\) and \(\eta_\mu=\sum_t\nu_t\theta_t/a_t\). Thus the price depends on the society only through these weighted moments, not on its support size or arrangement into types. This is true in the paper’s finite model as well: the population has already been compressed before the optimization begins. A type list does not create a new pricing problem over a large or structured society.

The finite-profile scenario is plausible, and I would not object that high multiplicity is inappropriate or already studied. But broadening it to an arbitrary population law over beliefs does not restore a population-computation question. The equilibrium still depends only on the corresponding weighted moments. If those moments are provided, the population is absent from the computational input; if a density or oracle is provided instead, the added difficulty is in representing and integrating that law, not in computing this market’s equilibrium. The paper’s noisy-belief model makes the population law natural, but its equilibrium characterization still reduces the relevant information to those aggregates.

The other results do not supply a stronger computational anchor. The sampling analysis is a concentration argument as the number of traders grows. The convergence analysis studies randomized trader updates and local rates; a population limit that turns those updates into type-level flows would require a new dynamics model, with analytic questions of its own. Neither result asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational task.

So I would not call Typed Continuum Market-Maker Equilibrium a worthwhile ChoCo mirror: it is a sound, newly posed numerical optimization problem, but the continuum adds no algorithmic question about the society. The negative case is weakest at precisely that point. If the programme counts any approximate computation attached to a theorem’s optimization characterization as a sufficient anchor, the proponent has found one; the paper itself does not establish that this price-computation question is a substantive complexity problem.
