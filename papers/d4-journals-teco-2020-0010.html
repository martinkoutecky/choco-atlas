The continuous mirror question

Given finite supports of worker types \((F_i,C_i,e_i^{\max})\) and task types with qualities \(g_j\), rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to one on each side, and a fixed payment parameter \(\alpha\), under the paper's task-homogeneity and high/low quality assumptions, find a long-term mechanism that maximizes total long-run revenue among admissible mechanisms while satisfying worker incentive compatibility and individual rationality. If mass-level stability is required, also impose the paper's productivity-cost ordering assumption; determine whether a mass version of FILI attains the optimum.

The model it lives in

A large long-term labor market represented by distributions over complete worker and task types. The mechanism assesses individuals, elicits preferences, and produces a measure-preserving matching of worker mass to task mass; each individual still receives one whole task. The objective is total long-run revenue, with the paper's incentive and participation conditions and, when Assumption 1 is imposed, mass-level stability.

The objection that survived

The paper assumes the planner does not know the distribution of worker characteristics, and nonatomic masses may alter individual incentive constraints; the proponent specifies known aggregate masses and flags proof obligations but does not establish that these changes preserve the source game's incentives.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result classifying a problem as \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its strongest computationally relevant anchor is a constructive optimality result, so I would use it as an algorithmic anchor while being clear that it is not a complexity-class theorem.

Lead mirror: High-Multiplicity FILI Revenue Maximization. The anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: *if Assumptions 2 and 3 hold, the FILI matching mechanism \(m^F\) achieves optimal total long-run revenue among all mechanisms in \(\mathcal M\).* Here the payment rule is fixed, as in the main model; Assumption 2 makes each worker’s productivity, effort cost, and maximum effort independent of task, and Assumption 3 separates task qualities into a high range where every worker exerts effort and a low range where none does.

The mirror’s regime is a large, standardized labor market: for example, a platform clearing a very large cohort of long-term contracts, with many workers sharing the same productivity, cost, and effort profile, and many tasks sharing a small number of quality classes. There might be hundreds of thousands of agents but only a few dozen distinct profiles. Worker types contain the complete worker-side parameters \((F_i,C_i,e_i^{\max})\); task types contain their quality \(g_j\). The aggregate masses are known for the computation, while individual identities and types remain hidden during operation and are learned through the mechanism’s assessments. Each worker still receives one whole long-term task. A type’s mass can split across destinations because it aggregates many interchangeable individuals.

Precisely, give the prover worker masses \(\mu_i\), task masses \(\nu_j\), a fixed payment parameter \(\alpha\), and rational type parameters satisfying the paper’s assumptions. Both sides have total mass one. The question is: find a long-term matching mechanism that maximizes total long-run revenue, while workers’ effort and reports are incentive compatible and the resulting equilibrium is individually rational and coalitionally stable at the mass level. A solution consists of the assessment and reporting rules, a final mass-preserving matching \(x_{ij}\), and a proof that the mechanism attains the maximum over all admissible mechanisms.

The expected tractable route is a finite optimization over the type masses. Under the stated assumptions, the equilibrium revenue from pairing worker type \(i\) with task type \(j\) is
\[ r_{ij}=F_i e_i^{\max}g_j\, \mathbf{1}\{\alpha F_i^2g_j-C_i\geq 0\}. \]
The final assignment has row sums \(\mu_i\), column sums \(\nu_j\), and \(x_{ij}\geq 0\); its aggregate revenue is \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}r_{ij}\). A key part of the research question is to prove that a mass version of FILI, including assessment, truthful preference reporting, and worker-proposing deferred acceptance, implements an optimal assignment among all allowed mechanisms. With finite support, the resulting assignment problem has \(\tau_W\tau_S\) variables and is a natural candidate for a polynomial-time exact algorithm in the support sizes and input bit length. I expect Class A in this restricted regime. The paper’s sorting argument points toward a monotone mass assignment; the continuous implementation and stability proof still need to be supplied.

This is a population continuization: the inputs are distributions over complete worker and task types, and the decision variable allocates mass between those types. The workers and tasks keep their original long-term roles, and individuals still receive integral matches. For discrete cohorts with rational type proportions, scaling the masses by a common denominator gives the corresponding typed finite population; transportation integrality also makes an integral aggregate assignment possible when the scaled margins are integer.

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s optimal long-run revenue result. It does not claim continuum versions of every equilibrium result in the paper. The mass-level stability condition should prohibit a positive-mass coalition of workers and tasks from rematching so that every participant strictly gains; for the paper’s separate stability guarantee, the mirror would also need Assumption 1.

The weakest point is that this is an unusually favorable regime: task homogeneity and the high/low quality gap drive the structural simplification, and Theorem 4 itself provides no running-time bound. Also, the paper assumes distinct worker productivities, whereas high multiplicity creates identical worker types and ties. Extending the assessment, deferred-acceptance, and stability arguments to those ties is a real proof obligation. The next questions are whether the optimization stays tractable with several quality bands or task-dependent worker skills, and how small type masses affect the assessment horizon.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only plausible anchor is Theorem 4. Theorems 1–3 concern dominance, stability, and equilibrium payoffs; none asserts a complexity result. Theorem 4 proves that FILI attains optimal long-run revenue under Assumptions 2 and 3. It gives no running-time bound or complexity classification in \(N\), support size, or input bit length. The paper’s general remark that the planner’s problem is “computationally intractable” is not a formal result about a specified computational problem. On ChoCo’s stated criterion, there is no computational result here to continuize.

The proposed mass-transport LP does not close that gap. It takes worker and task type parameters as known input and optimizes a static assignment with revenues \(r_{ij}\). Theorem 4’s benchmark is broader: mechanisms operate with hidden characteristics, strategic effort and reports, learning, and incentive and stability requirements. A coupling \(x_{ij}\) does not say how the mechanism learns the parameters or implements that assignment while preserving those properties. Proving that a mass version of FILI does so would be substantive work; the LP alone does not establish it.

One could pose a more faithful question by specifying finite rational priors over hidden types, a finite representation of policies, and mass-level incentive and blocking constraints. But the paper supplies none of that: it assumes no known distribution of worker characteristics and allows matching rules and strategies to depend on arbitrary histories. Adding the missing prior and policy representation would define a new computational mechanism-design model, rather than mirror a complexity result in this paper.

There is a further, weaker mismatch: the paper assumes distinct worker productivities and strictly ordered task qualities, so exact repeated types—the simplest fixed-support high-multiplicity regime—are excluded. Ties could likely be handled by a recognized extension, so I would not rely on this as decisive.

That is the strongest negative case: Theorem 4 is a constructive optimality result, not a computational-complexity result, and the suggested LP captures only a known-parameter terminal allocation. But I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. If ChoCo treats constructive optimization theorems as anchors, a finite-type, hidden-information version of Theorem 4 is a plausible mirror; my objection is that the proponent’s LP has not yet shown it is that mirror.
