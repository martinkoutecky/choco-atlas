The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a continuous-population mirror of the paper’s multi-valued RBTS mechanism. There is, however, an important limitation: the paper contains no named theorem about NP-hardness, polynomial time, W[1]-hardness, FPT, or any comparable complexity class. Thus this cannot support a ChoCo Class A/B/C claim in the strict sense. The case below treats its named incentive-compatibility theorems as the relevant computational results.

The most plausible regime is a crowdsensing or polling population with very many agents but finitely many informational types. Let \(S=\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\) be the signal alphabet. A unit-mass continuum of agents is partitioned into sets \(I_s\) of mass \(\mu_s\), where every agent in \(I_s\) has observed signal \(s\), has the same posterior vector \(p_s\) about a peer’s signal, and has the same reporting preferences and costs. The number of agents is effectively infinite, while the number of types is \(m\), or a small finite multiple of \(m\). A plausible example is millions of sensors using the same calibration and firmware, observing the same categorical environmental variable. The mass \(\mu_s\) is the fraction of sensors of signal type \(s\), and payments are evaluated per unit mass.

The lead problem is Continuous Multi-valued RBTS. An instance consists of \(m\), the type masses \(\mu\), a fully mixed posterior kernel \(P=(p_s(r))\), a peer relation whose conditional peer distribution for type \(s\) is \(p_s\), and a strictly proper scoring rule \(R\). The mechanism does not know the common prior or \(P\). Each agent chooses an information report \(x\in S\) and a prediction report \(y\in\Delta_m^\circ\). If \(j\) is the agent’s peer, the mechanism pays

\[ \tau(x_i,y_i,x_j,y_j) = \frac{\mathbf{1}\{x_j=x_i\}}{y_j(x_i)} + R(y_i,x_j). \]

The decision variable is the report pair used by each type, together with the payment rule. The objective is to make truthful reporting a Bayes–Nash equilibrium: type \(s\) should choose \(x=s\) and \(y=p_s\). A solution is therefore a report/payment mechanism together with a proof that the truthful type-level strategy is an equilibrium for the continuum population.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states: “If the self-predicting assumption is satisfied, the multi-valued RBTS is Bayes-Nash incentive-compatible.” The self-predicting condition is \(p_{\tilde{s}}(s)<p_s(s)\) for every \(s\neq\tilde{s}\). The theorem concerns the two-report mechanism above, with cyclic peer assignment and a strictly proper prediction score.

The theorem transfers cleanly to the continuum. If a truthful peer has type \(r\), then an agent of true type \(s\) who reports \(x\) receives expected information score

\[ \sum_r p_s(r)\frac{\mathbf{1}\{r=x\}}{p_r(x)} = \frac{p_s(x)}{p_x(x)}. \]

Truthful reporting \(x=s\) gives \(1\), while self-prediction implies \(p_s(x)<p_x(x)\) for \(x\neq s\), so every false information report gives less than \(1\). Strict propriety makes \(y=p_s\) optimal for the prediction report. Since every type has positive mass but each individual has measure zero, the type-by-type incentive argument is preserved, and the mechanism’s aggregate score is simply the integral of these per-agent payments.

The expected direction is therefore an explicit, tractable construction: the mechanism can be written down and checked using the finite matrix \(P\), with no dependence on the number of agents. This is A-like in spirit, but it is not a formal ChoCo Class A result because the paper gives no complexity analysis and no optimization algorithm.

The second useful anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. It states: “If no restrictions are placed on the updating process, there exists no decomposable payment scheme that is Bayes-Nash incentive-compatible when observations \(S_i\) take more than two values, \(m\ge 3\).” A decomposable scheme is one whose total score is an information score independent of the agent’s prediction report plus a prediction score independent of the agent’s information report.

Its continuous mirror, Continuous Decomposable-Elicitation Feasibility, asks whether there is a single decomposable payment rule, not knowing the common prior or updating kernel, that truthfully elicits both reports from every continuum population satisfying the paper’s basic full-mixing and stochastic-relevance assumptions. The answer is expected to be infeasibility, not transferred NP-hardness and not continuum-specific hardness. The proof’s incentive inequalities are pointwise in the agent’s signal type; positive masses \(\mu_s\) merely replicate those constraints over a continuum. Passing from finitely many agents to masses does not remove the contradiction.

These two problems preserve the paper’s substantive content. They retain non-binary signals, unknown common priors, posterior beliefs, peer comparison, decomposable scoring, and the self-predicting restriction. The continuous version is not obtained by making outcomes fractional or by adding noise; the society itself is the continuous object. The finite-to-continuous dictionary is also natural: a finite population with \(n_s\) agents of type \(s\) maps to \(\mu_s=n_s/n\), while total payment becomes \(n\) times the per-capita continuum payment.

The weakest point is serious: RBTS is motivated partly by its ability to work for small populations, whereas a continuum has infinitely many agents. The mirror therefore does not preserve the paper’s finite-population motivation literally; it tests whether the same incentive mechanism remains meaningful in a high-multiplicity sensor or polling regime. Peer matching is also an additional modeling choice in the continuum. Still, the authors explicitly motivate their setting through crowdsourcing and sensor communities, and Theorem 3’s proof does not depend on smallness. I would therefore defend this as a credible continuous analogue of the paper’s truth-elicitation problem, but not as evidence of a new computational-complexity phenomenon.

The natural follow-up questions are whether one can minimize per-capita payment among continuous decomposable mechanisms, whether approximate self-prediction gives quantitative robustness, how large a finite population is needed to approximate the continuum incentives, and whether the self-predicting condition is necessary. Those questions would turn this paper’s mechanism result into a genuine continuization programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case begins before high multiplicity: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 are named results, but neither asserts the complexity of an input-output problem, nor gives an exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithm. Theorem 3 verifies that a fixed payment formula is a Bayes–Nash equilibrium under a stated belief condition; Theorem 2 proves that a broad class of formulas cannot work. Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 have the same character. Under ChoCo’s computational gate, that is decisive. A continuous reformulation may be mathematically legitimate, but it is not a continuization of a computational result from this paper.

The sensor example is nevertheless a fair high-multiplicity story. Millions of sensors can share one of finitely many signal, calibration, and posterior types. So the objection is not that mass is meaningless or that agents must remain individually named. The problem is that the population mass does no work in the paper’s result.

For Theorem 3, the strongest version would use masses \(\mu_s\), a peer coupling \(P=(p_s(r))\), and a per-capita version of the multi-valued RBTS payment. For a type \(s\) reporting \(x\), the information-score calculation remains

\[ \sum_r p_s(r)\frac{\mathbf{1}\{r=x\}}{p_r(x)} = \frac{p_s(x)}{p_x(x)}. \]

The self-predicting condition then proves truthfulness type by type. The mass vector appears only afterward, when averaging payments as \(\sum_s \mu_s \cdot \text{payment}_s\). It is absent from the incentive constraints, the proof, and the mechanism’s construction. The continuous society is therefore merely a weighting device for a finite signal-type calculation.

One can repair the peer issue properly, by replacing the paper’s cyclic assignment with a measure-preserving matching or coupling. That makes the model cleaner, but it does not create a computational problem. If \(P\) is unknown, the mechanism is being required to work universally over an ambiguity class; that is a quantified existence statement, exactly what the theorem already proves. If \(P\) is supplied as input, checking the self-predicting inequalities is a finite calculation such as \(O(m^2)\), not a new population-level optimization problem.

A better proposal would minimize expected payment, impose a budget, or seek approximate incentive compatibility. Those could be worthwhile mechanism-design problems, but they are new problems. With finitely many types, rational \(\mu_s\) simply corresponds to \(n_s\) repeated agents, and the same weighted finite mechanism-design formulation exists before taking any continuum limit. If instead one makes the belief vectors themselves continuously varying, the continuous object is the belief/type space, not the population; that leaves the programme’s central continuization.

Theorem 2 is an even weaker anchor. Its contradiction is generated by finitely many posterior distributions and pointwise incentive inequalities. Giving each corresponding type positive mass preserves the contradiction exactly. Removing a type by assigning it zero mass merely weakens the domain and changes the theorem’s quantifier. Thus the proposed continuous infeasibility question is not a computational feasibility problem over a continuous society; it is the same universal impossibility theorem with population weights attached.

The obvious stronger rescue would ask for the minimum payment, the largest support on which a decomposable scheme exists, or robustness against a specified family of posterior kernels. Again, these may be sensible new projects. But their difficulty would come from the chosen mechanism class, belief ambiguity, payment constraints, or peer-matching structure—not from replacing named agents by population mass. Adding spatial peer networks, heterogeneous risk preferences, or continuously varying beliefs could produce genuine computation, but it would no longer be a mirror of the results proved here.

The proponent’s caveat is therefore central rather than minor: this paper offers an admissible high-multiplicity story but no computational result to continuize. I cannot logically rule out inventing a valuable new mechanism-design problem inspired by RBTS. I can say that every such rescue either reduces to a finite type-weighted formulation in which the continuum is inessential, or adds the computational substance that the paper itself does not contain. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, that warrants a red verdict.
