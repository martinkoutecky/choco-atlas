The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow, and the paper has no named result establishing a computational complexity class. Its closest anchor is Proposition 1, proved in the paper’s Appendix 7.1: for every finite claims problem \((c,E)\in B\), the limit allocation exists for each of procedures \(P1\), \(P2\), and \(P3\). The proof handles the procedures separately: claims decrease to a limit under \(P1\), the concessions telescope under \(P2\), and the residual estate tends to zero under \(P3\). That is a genuine result about the procedures’ outputs, but it does not say they are in P, or give any complexity bound. So it is the nearest defensible anchor, not one that meets your stated complexity-anchor test.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large pool of creditors with standardized claim amounts: for example, depositors or customers grouped by contract or loss band. Let a type be a claim amount \(q_t\), with mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and the number of creditors is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct claim types. Let \(E\) be the estate per capita, with \(0<E<\sum_t\mu_tq_t\). Each creditor of type \(t\) claims \(q_t\); the reported rule is an action, not part of the type. The rules extend naturally to this society: CEA awards \(\min(q_t,\lambda)\), proportional awards \(Eq_t/\sum_t\mu_tq_t\), and CEL awards \(\max(0,q_t-\lambda)\), with each threshold chosen to make total awards equal \(E\).

The corresponding question, Continuous Claims Procedure Evaluation, would be: given rational finite-support masses \(\mu_t\), claims \(q_t\), an estate \(E\), a choice of \(P1\), \(P2\), or \(P3\), and a rational mass \(\nu_{t,r}\) of each claim type reporting each of the three rules, compute the procedure’s limiting per-capita award \(x_t\) for every type. A valid approximate answer would return \(\hat x_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t|\hat x_t-x_t|\le\varepsilon\). The recursive steps use the same rule allocations as the paper, evaluated over the distribution: \(P1\) updates claims by the pointwise maximum of the reported rules’ awards; \(P2\) subtracts proportional concessions from half the remaining estate at each step; \(P3\) subtracts the pointwise minimum reported award and the corresponding aggregate concessions. Stop when the reported rules agree on the residual problem, or return the limit. The inputs are measured by \(\tau\), rational bit length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\).

I would provisionally expect the approximation problem for these three fixed rules to be tractable: the support is finite and each step uses explicit, piecewise-linear allocation formulas. Proposition 1 supplies convergence, though, not a rate or an algorithm, so a polynomial-time claim would need proof. Natural follow-up questions are whether the exact limit is always rational, what convergence rate the input bit lengths permit, and whether the result extends beyond finite support.

This mirror covers the well-definedness and evaluation of \(P1\)–\(P3\), not the paper’s findings about human choices, framing, questionnaires, or the majority procedure \(P0\). The main reason to keep it modest is that Proposition 1 establishes convergence rather than complexity. The other tempting anchors are less suitable: Lemma 1 is cited from earlier work and relies on a particular individual being pivotal, while Lemma 2 characterizes strict equilibria in \(P0\). In a nonatomic population, one person’s deviation has zero mass, so those strategic properties do not carry over directly. The procedural-evaluation question is plausible as a high-multiplicity computational extension, but it does not explain the paper’s experimental results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named result about computational complexity. Its main findings are experimental: \(P_1\)–\(P_3\) produce behavior consistent with their equilibrium incentives, while proportional-rule coordination is common in \(P_0\) and in the survey. Proposition 1—the proponent’s strongest anchor—proves that three procedures have well-defined limits. That is an analytic existence result, not a complexity result or an algorithm for choosing an intervention.

A large pool of creditors grouped by claim amount is a sensible high-multiplicity setting. The obstacle is how the procedures respond to reports. By Lemma 1, a *single* agent choosing the designated rule can determine the outcome of \(P_1\), \(P_2\), or \(P_3\), regardless of how many other agents there are. For example, in \(P_2\), one proportional-rule report among \(n-1\) CEA reports yields the proportional allocation; unanimous CEA reports yield the CEA allocation. As \(n\) grows, the first profile’s reported-rule mass converges to the second’s, while the discrete outcomes remain different. An atomless mass model that records only positive-mass reports cannot preserve that singleton trigger as its mass tends to zero.

The proponent’s evaluation problem remains definable if the input records the mass of each claim-and-report combination. But for these procedures, a report’s mass matters to the trigger only through whether it is positive at all. That makes the mirror sensitive to the support boundary and leaves the pivotal individual in the original result without a continuous counterpart. A model of positive-mass blocs coordinating on reports could avoid this issue; it would study bloc control over the allocation, a new question rather than a high-multiplicity evaluation of Proposition 1.

Lemma 1 and Lemma 2 do not supply a stronger computational target. Lemma 1 reports strategic properties established in earlier work, with the same singleton dependence. Lemma 2’s \(P_0\) result also has a natural population version: the unanimous profiles remain strict equilibria, since an individual who switches away from the winning rule receives the penalty. But there are only three rules, and characterizing those unanimous equilibria is immediate. The experiment’s result that subjects coordinate especially often on proportional allocation concerns framing and learning; an equilibrium computation does not answer it. Modeling those effects would require specifying population dynamics and learning behavior that the paper does not provide.

A researcher could still choose to study mass-coalition control or a learning model for large creditor populations. Those may be worthwhile questions on their own. The negative case is strongest when limited to this paper: its mathematical results offer no compelling computational problem for ChoCo, and the most plausible extension changes the strategic unit or adds a new objective. The claim that no possible related scenario could ever be worthwhile would go beyond what the paper can establish.
