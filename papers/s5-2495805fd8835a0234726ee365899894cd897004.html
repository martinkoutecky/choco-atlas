The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is only a plausible setting, not an anchor-backed mirror. A large roommate market could have many people in recurring cohorts, with each cohort sharing a complete ranking of roommate types; the cohort’s mass would be its share of the population. But replacing named people and permutation cycles with type masses would create a new fractional stable-partition model. The paper neither defines nor analyzes that model.

I find no qualifying computational anchor. The closest named result is Lemma 2.1, proved here: it gives an integral formula for the probability that a *fixed, finite, labelled* cyclic partition is stable under uniformly random preferences, and the paper uses the formula in its asymptotic analysis. It is a probabilistic identity, not a computational formulation or complexity result about an underlying problem that can be carried over to a mass distribution.

Likewise, Theorem 4.12, proved here, estimates the expected number of odd stable partitions as \((1+O(n^{-1/4}))\frac{\Gamma(1/4)}{\sqrt{\pi e}\,2^{1/4}}n^{1/4}\), and upper-bounds the expected number of odd cycles by \(\frac{\Gamma(1/4)}{4\sqrt{\pi e}\,2^{1/4}}n^{1/4}\log n\). Those are asymptotic probability results, not algorithmic or complexity classifications. The paper’s mention of NP-completeness for exchange-stable matching is unnumbered prose citing Cechlárová and Manlove, so it does not supply a numbered anchor from this paper.

Thus there is no lead anchor, no anchor-specific continuous problem to hand to a prover, and no honest Class A/B/C prediction. The cohort scenario may motivate future work, but presenting it as a continuous mirror of a named result here would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the paper’s results: none is a computational result. Its numbered theorems and lemmas give probabilities, asymptotic estimates, and structural facts about random instances. The NP-completeness statement concerns exchange-stable matching and cites earlier work; it is not a result of this paper. Under ChoCo’s anchor standard, that leaves no result here whose complexity landscape a continuous mirror could chart.

Lemma 2.1 is the closest candidate, but it gives the probability that one fixed, labelled permutation is stable under independently randomized preference lists. Its integral uses continuous random variables; the society is still a finite set of named people. Replacing those people with masses of types would change the stability event, which depends on each person’s predecessor and successor. A type-level stability model might be worth defining, but the lemma does not pose or analyze its computational question.

Theorem 4.12 estimates expected counts of stable partitions and odd cycles. Those counts range over labelled permutations, which a type-level mass assignment would collapse together. Normalizing the expected number of partitions by population size gives a quantity tending to zero; retaining the raw count has no direct mass-level counterpart. One could invent a rescaled statistic or a measure over fractional solutions, but that would be a new observable, not a continuous version of the theorem’s computational result.

The strongest alternative is to start from Corollary 4.13 and ask for the maximum matched mass in a market with repeated cohorts. That is a plausible high-multiplicity roommate model. But it requires specifying how agents rank members of the same type, how within-type matches work, and what counts as a blocking pair when assignments are fractional. Even if those choices produce a sensible problem, the paper’s corollary is a probabilistic bound for random rankings over named individuals, not an algorithmic result about that model. It could motivate a separate study; it cannot supply the missing computational anchor. The honest limitation of this negative case is that it rules out a paper-anchored mirror, not every potentially worthwhile continuous roommate problem.
