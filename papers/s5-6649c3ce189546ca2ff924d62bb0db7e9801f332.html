The case FOR (proponent)

The formal anchor requirement blocks a fully compliant case: this paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. Section 2 gives the \(\mathrm{CA}(\boldsymbol p)\) formulation and its integrality argument; Section 3 describes a minimum-cost-flow formulation and the authors’ computations. Those are real computational contributions, but they are not numbered results of the kind you require. The cited “Theorem 4.5” belongs to Conforti et al., not this paper. There is no hardness result to anchor on either.

The strongest candidate mirror is Maximum-Utility Class Assignment\(_\infty\). Consider a large university allocating a required course across a fixed catalog of classes. A type is a complete preference list together with any utility or eligibility information the assignment model uses. Students of the same type are interchangeable; \(\mu_t\) is their fraction of the cohort. This is plausible when a large cohort has many repeated preference lists, so the number of students greatly exceeds the number \(\tau\) of types. Let each class have lower and upper capacity shares \(\ell_c,u_c\), scaled with cohort size, as the programme permits.

Given classes \(C\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational capacity shares, and the paper’s rank utilities \(p_{tc}\), choose assignment masses \(x_{tc}\geq 0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{c\in C}x_{tc}=\mu_t \quad\text{for every }t,\qquad \ell_c\leq\sum_t x_{tc}\leq u_c \quad\text{for every }c, \]
to maximize
\[ \sum_{t,c}p_{tc}x_{tc}. \]
The solution is the mass of each type assigned to each class. This is the population-level form of the paper’s maximum-utility assignment, including its restricted-rank variants when assignments outside the permitted ranks are disallowed. The authors would likely recognize it: it preserves their preferences, capacities, assignment decision, and welfare objective. The continuous version is a transportation LP with \(O(\tau |C|)\) variables, hence polynomial in the support size, number of classes, and input bit length. For compatible finite replications, transportation integrality recovers an integral assignment to students.

This is a credible Class A mirror, but a modest one. The paper already formulates and solves the finite problem as a transportation LP, so the continuous version compresses repeated students rather than dissolving known hardness. It covers the maximum-utility assignment, not the paper’s comparisons with DA or Boston mechanisms, which concern stability and incentives.

The soft spot is the regime: the case study has about 1,100 students and 50 classes, but reports no preference-type support size. Its actual instance might have nearly as many distinct types as students. The large-cohort scenario is plausible, but the paper does not establish that its data occupy that regime. Thus this is the best substantive positive mirror I see, but it cannot satisfy the required named-anchor standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The formal objection is stronger than the proponent allows: this paper states no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. It formulates \(\mathrm{CA}(\boldsymbol p)\), invokes total unimodularity, gives a minimum-cost-flow formulation, and reports assignments on two cohorts. The quoted “Theorem 4.5” is in a cited book. Those are computational contributions, but they are not a named complexity result of this paper to anchor a mirror on.

The proponent’s maximum-utility mirror is nevertheless the strongest possible candidate. A large university could assign a cohort by preference type, with class capacities scaled to the cohort; the mass-assignment LP preserves the paper’s decision and objective. That is a sensible high-multiplicity model. But it is a proposed new problem, not a continuous counterpart to a named computational result in the paper. The paper already treats assignment as transportation and solves it by min-cost flow, so the mirror offers no bridge from one of its stated complexity findings to a continuous complexity question. The paper’s DA comparisons and fairness experiments do not supply that missing anchor either.

That case against is narrow. It defeats the proposed anchor under the required standard; it does not show that the mass-assignment problem is ill-posed or that the authors would reject it. A large cohort with repeated preference lists and proportionally scaled class capacities is plausible. So the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is not established here: the negative case rests on the absence of a qualifying result, while the best candidate remains a coherent mirror of the paper’s central optimization model.
