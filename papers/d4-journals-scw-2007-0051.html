The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational complexity result. Equations (1) and (2) define its equal-representation criterion; they are not complexity results. So it has no qualifying anchor under your rule, and I can’t honestly provide a compliant problem “per anchor.”

The strongest substantive finding to build on is the simulation evidence in §3, especially Tables 1–3, computed by the authors here rather than proved as a theorem or cited as one. They report that weights proportional to the square root of constituency population give close to equal representation across many tested configurations. The fit is less clear when constituency sizes are tightly clustered or highly skewed and the number of constituencies is small; it improves as that number grows. The EU and US simulations also favor the square-root rule among the power laws tested. That is a useful result to mirror, but it is not a named complexity anchor.

A plausible lead question, if a mirror based on this empirical result is admissible, is Continuous Equal-Representation Weight Design. Take \(m\) constituencies with rational population masses \(p_j>0\), where \(\sum_j p_j=1\). A voter’s type is \((j,x)\): constituency membership \(j\) and ideal point \(x\in[0,1]\). In the high-multiplicity regime, each constituency contains \(Np_j\) voters for large \(N\), whose ideal points are independent draws from a shared smooth distribution \(F\). The median’s centered fluctuation, after scaling by the common factor that does not affect rank order, converges to \(Y_j\sim\mathcal N(0,1/p_j)\), independently across constituencies.

The decision variable is a nonnegative weight vector \(w\) with \(\sum_j w_j=1\). Sort constituencies by their realized \(Y_j\); let \(P_w\) be the representative whose cumulative weight first exceeds \(1/2\). Set \(\pi_j(w)=\Pr(P_w=j)\). Since the paper’s equal-representation target is that a constituency’s chance of being pivotal match its share of voters, a precise task is: given \(p_1,\ldots,p_m\) and rational \(\varepsilon>0\), return rational weights \(w\) whose value is within \(\varepsilon\) of the minimum of
\[ \sum_{j=1}^m \frac{(\pi_j(w)-p_j)^2}{p_j}. \]
This objective is the paper’s per-voter quadratic deviation criterion, up to a common scaling factor. It mirrors the problem of choosing voting weights to equalize indirect influence, rather than the encoding used to compute that choice.

The regime is plausible for a federal or supranational assembly: millions of citizens are partitioned into perhaps tens of constituencies, and the number of constituencies is small relative to the voter population. The finite constituencies can contain high-multiplicity populations, with a finite-bin approximation to ideal points giving many voters of each type. The authors should recognize the weight-design question: it preserves their median-representative model, majority quota, and equal-influence objective. It covers their weight-allocation question, not their broader claims about the square-root rule’s empirical robustness.

My tentative expectation is tractability for fixed \(m\), with variable-\(m\) hardness likely coming from the weighted-coalition structure and therefore more like hardness transfer than a continuum-specific barrier. That is a conjecture about this proposed problem, not a conclusion of this paper. Further questions include how hard it is to compute \(\pi_j(w)\) exactly, whether the square-root rule is optimal beyond the tested power-law family, and how the optimum changes with the quota or the distribution \(F\).

The soft spot is substantial: in an exact nonatomic society with a fixed ideal-point distribution, every constituency’s median is simply \(F^{-1}(1/2)\). The random ordering that makes representatives pivotal disappears. The Gaussian fluctuation model preserves the high-multiplicity limit at the scale relevant to ranking medians, but an author or referee could reasonably say it retains finite-population sampling noise rather than giving the continuous society itself the decisive role. Combined with the absence of any qualifying named anchor, this leaves a plausible candidate mirror, but no anchor-backed positive case under the stated rubric.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in the paper: it gives no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary about the complexity of a computational problem. Equations (1) and (2) state an egalitarian criterion; the central findings are Monte Carlo evidence about weight rules. That evidence can motivate a new algorithmic question, but it does not give us a named result to mirror.

The strongest candidate is the proposed optimization of weights to match constituency pivotality probabilities to population shares. Its difficulty is that those probabilities rely on finite-sample variation. With a fixed continuous population distribution \(F\) in every constituency, each constituency’s median is exactly \(F^{-1}(1/2)\). The ordering that makes the pivotal representative random disappears. The Gaussian variables in the proposal are instead limits of the *scaled sampling errors* of finite electorates: the medians converge to the common median, while their rescaled deviations retain a random ordering. That is a coherent asymptotic experiment, but its decisive randomness comes from finite sampling, not from the continuous society itself.

A more faithful continuous model could allow different fixed distributions of ideal points across constituencies. Their medians would then differ, but for a given society the pivotal representative would be determined, not random. To recover probabilities, one could put a prior over issue-specific distributions or random societies. That may be a sensible constitutional-design question, but it changes the source of uncertainty from individual voters’ sampled ideal points to a distribution over societies. It is adjacent to the paper’s model, not a continuous-population version of its equal-representation calculation.

So I would reject the proposed mirror as evidence that this paper fills the programme’s computational gap: its weight optimization is worth considering only if the authors accept a fluctuation limit whose relevant variation vanishes in the exact population model. The negative case is weakest at the universal claim. A well-motivated prior over issue-specific population distributions could yield a worthwhile continuous computational problem, and I cannot rule that out. But that would be a new modelling choice, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper.
