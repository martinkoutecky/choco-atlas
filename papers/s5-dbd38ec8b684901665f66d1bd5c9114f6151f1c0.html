Why no mirror

Theorem 1 is a structural axiomatization, not a result about computing an object or the hardness of computing it. The proposed distribution-over-types act-selection problem is statable and potentially interesting, but it adds policy optimization, type-level weights, and altered uncertainty machinery. Thus the paper fails the computational anchor requirement, despite suggesting a possible future ChoCo problem.

What the mirror covers

The proposed extension covers the relative-fair functional form and its utilitarian and maximin special cases, but no computational result of the paper is mirrored; the axiomatic characterizations remain unanchored.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case for a continuous mirror is a plausible one, but it fails the programme’s anchor gate: this paper contains no named computational result. Theorems 1–5, Lemmas 1–4, and Proposition 1 are axiomatic, representation, or existence results; none asserts membership in \(P\), hardness, approximation, parameterized tractability, or an algorithm. The paper’s computational-sounding claims are literature discussion, not results proved here.

The closest substantive anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that an aggregation rule satisfies weak Pareto, event continuity, belief irrelevance, independence of redundant outcomes, weak preference for mixing, and restricted certainty independence if and only if it is a relative fair aggregation rule: there is a nonempty closed convex \(\mathcal M\subseteq\Delta_N\) such that social evaluation is the minimum, over \(\mu\in\mathcal M\), of the weighted sum of individuals’ \(0\)-\(1\)-normalized expected utilities. This is a strong structural result, but it is not a computational theorem.

If the anchor requirement were relaxed, I would propose the following recognizable high-multiplicity problem. A type \(t\) consists of a normalized utility function \(u_t:X\to[0,1]\) over finite outcomes and a belief \(p_t\) over a finite state partition \(S\). A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta_T\) over finitely many such types, representing many citizens with \(N\gg |T|\). Given a deterministic contingent policy \(f:S\to X\), define its type-\(t\) evaluation by \(U_t(f)=\sum_{s\in S}p_t(s)u_t(f(s))\). For a specified closed convex set \(\Lambda(\mu)\subseteq\Delta_T\) of population-level ethical weights, define \(V_\mu(f)=\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda(\mu)}\sum_{t\in T}\lambda_tU_t(f)\).

The continuous problem would be:

\(\textsc{Continuous-Relative-Fair-Act-Selection}\): given \(X,S,T,\mu,(u_t,p_t)_{t\in T}\), a rational description of \(\Lambda(\mu)\), and a threshold \(q\), decide whether there exists a deterministic act \(f:S\to X\) with \(V_\mu(f)\ge q\), and output such an act when one exists.

This is a faithful population interpretation of the paper’s object: mass represents the fraction of citizens of each preference-and-belief type; the action is a common state-contingent social decision; and the objective is exactly the paper’s normalized, inequality-aware aggregation. The relative-utilitarian special case uses \(\Lambda(\mu)=\{\mu\}\); the relative-maximin case uses \(\Lambda(\mu)=\Delta_{\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\).

The high-multiplicity bridge is clean for these extremes. If \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), replace type \(t\) by \(n_t\) identical citizens. Relative-utilitarian values become normalized finite sums, while relative-maximin values become the minimum over the cloned individuals. Thus the model is socially plausible for large policy constituencies with repeated survey or demographic types, and it preserves the paper’s \(0\)-\(1\) normalization rather than fractionalizing outcomes.

The expected classification is mixed. Fixed-act comparison is an LP over \(\Lambda(\mu)\), hence tractable when \(\Lambda(\mu)\) is explicitly represented. Relative-utilitarian act selection is also polynomial for unrestricted statewise acts because each state can be optimized independently. Maximin or general robust act selection may be hard, but that hardness would come from deterministic policy choice and state/outcome structure, not from population multiplicity; it would therefore be a Class B-style extension, not a result showing that this paper’s discrete difficulty dissolves in the continuum.

The weakest point is decisive: the proposed problem is a new computational layer placed on top of the paper’s axiomatic framework. The paper never asks for an optimal act, gives an input encoding for \(\mathcal M\), or proves any complexity result. Moreover, for arbitrary \(\Lambda(\mu)\), the exact lift from finite individual weight sets to type-level weights still needs to be specified. So this is a credible future ChoCo problem that the authors would likely recognize, but not a compliant continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 cannot pass the programme’s anchor gate. It is a characterization of aggregation rules, not a computational result: it proves neither an algorithm, a complexity bound, an approximation guarantee, nor hardness. The same is true of Theorems 2–5, Lemmas 1–4, and Proposition 1. Thus there is no named computational problem in this paper whose continuous-population counterpart could be a compliant mirror. This is decisive under the programme’s rules, independently of whether an interesting computational problem could be invented from the paper’s framework.

The proposed \(\textsc{Continuous-Relative-Fair-Act-Selection}\) is precisely such an invention. The paper does not ask a planner to choose an optimal act. It takes a feasible outcome set and a profile of individual preferences as given, and defines a social preference over all acts. Adding the question “does there exist an act with value at least \(q\)?” changes aggregation into policy optimization. That may be a worthwhile ChoCo problem inspired by the paper, but it is not a computational mirror of Theorem 1.

Nor is the proposed population lift canonical. The theorem’s weight set is \(\mathcal M\subseteq\Delta_N\), whose coordinates belong to named individuals. The theorem does not impose anonymity; anonymity appears only later, as an additional hypothesis in Corollary 1. Consequently, two agents with identical utilities and beliefs need not be interchangeable under the paper’s rule: their positions in \(\mathcal M\) may differ. The proponent’s type \(t=(u_t,p_t)\) omits exactly this socially relevant information. To obtain \(\Lambda(\mu)\), one must add anonymity or exchangeability and choose a new rule for aggregating individual-level weights into type-level weights. Different choices—mass-proportional weights, arbitrary type weights, or a worst-case set over types—produce different models. The resulting \(\Lambda(\mu)\) is not supplied by Theorem 1.

The finite-state reformulation also discards a central part of the paper. Event continuity, coin-toss events, and pseudo-mixtures rely on a nonatomic state space and Lyapunov’s convexity theorem. With a finite \(S\), the acts \(f:S\to X\) generally do not admit the paper’s required \(1/2\)-probability events or arbitrary pseudo-mixtures. Replacing them by lotteries or a common randomization device could make a sensible model, but that is another modification of the decision theory, not merely continuization of the population.

A better version could certainly be constructed: impose anonymity, make \((u,p)\) the complete type, retain a nonatomic state model, and define a carefully justified type-level weight correspondence. But that would be a new computational theory of anonymous robust social choice, with a newly introduced act-selection problem and newly encoded uncertainty. It would not be anchored in a computational theorem of this paper. The negative case is therefore strong on the question actually posed: this paper supplies no worthwhile *continuous mirror* for ChoCo’s computational programme, even though it may inspire a worthwhile future problem if the anchor requirement is abandoned.
