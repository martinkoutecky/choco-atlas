The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a named computational result in this paper to use as an anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says that under mutual independence, individual sovereignty holds exactly when social utility is a positive linear combination of individuals’ self-regarding utilities. Theorem 2, also proved here, characterizes geometric self-regarding utilitarianism: when \(|J|\geq 3\), representative consistency, homogeneity, monotonicity, faithfulness, anonymity, and continuity hold exactly for that aggregation rule. Theorem 3 gives the sovereignty characterization for finite constrained outcome sets; Theorem 4 gives the subjective-uncertainty analogue, including an affine combination of individual beliefs. These are welfare and preference characterizations, not results about computational complexity, algorithms, or reductions.

The closest positive lead is a continuous-population extension of Theorem 2. Imagine many voters choosing outcomes for a fixed set of subjects \(J\), with voters grouped by complete preference type: a vector \(\alpha_{\theta,j}>0\) describing how type \(\theta\) weights each subject’s common self-regarding utility \(v_j\). If type \(\theta\) has population mass \(\nu_\theta\), the natural geometric aggregate has weights
\[ w_j=\exp\!\left(\sum_\theta \nu_\theta\log\alpha_{\theta,j}\right), \]
and social utility \(u_\nu(x)=\sum_{j\in J}w_jv_j(x_j)\). A plausible regime is a large constituency with many voters but relatively few recurring patterns of concern for a fixed set of affected groups.

That gives a concrete population-level extension, but turning it into a computational problem—such as finding the feasible outcome maximizing \(u_\nu\)—adds an optimization question the paper does not study. Its complexity would depend on the separately specified feasible-outcome problem. So this is at most a promising application of the paper’s welfare rule, not a continuous mirror of a named result in it. Under the stated anchor requirement, there is no eligible anchor and thus no defensible complexity verdict to give.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s main concession is the strongest point against a mirror: this paper states no computational problem whose complexity, algorithm, or reduction could be carried over. Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 characterize when individual sovereignty yields self-regarding utilitarianism; Theorem 2 characterizes a welfare function from axioms; Theorem 4 gives a related characterization under subjective uncertainty. None asks how to compute an outcome or classify the complexity of doing so.

Theorem 2 is the best candidate for a population-level extension. With recurring voter types of masses \(\nu_\theta\), its geometric aggregation rule naturally suggests weights \(w_j=\exp(\sum_\theta \nu_\theta\log\alpha_{\theta,j})\). A large constituency with a small support of concern patterns is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. But this extends a normative aggregation rule; it does not supply a computational task. The theorem produces a social preference over outcomes, not a chosen outcome. To ask for an outcome maximizing that preference, one must add a feasible set and its representation. If that set is explicit, the task may be straightforward; if it is succinctly encoded as a matching, allocation, or policy-design problem, its computational content comes from that added problem. The population continuum does not select or define it.

The same gap applies to the other results. Theorem 3’s finite constrained outcome set does not turn its characterization into an algorithmic result. Theorem 4’s nonatomic beliefs concern uncertainty over states, not a continuous population. And the “continuity” in Theorem 2 is continuity of the welfare function under changes in preferences; it is not a continuum-agent model.

So the strongest defensible case is that there is no continuous *computational mirror of a result in this paper*: the paper provides no computational result to mirror. A separate computational study could combine its aggregation rule with a concrete high-multiplicity allocation or policy problem, but that would add the central computational object rather than continue one already present here. The universal claim is less secure: the proposed continuum aggregation is coherent and could motivate worthwhile work under a broader scope. Its weakness as a case for ChoCo is that the paper itself supplies no computational question for that work to answer.
