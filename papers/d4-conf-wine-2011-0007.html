What the mirror covers

A continuous population could mirror the convergence and learning-performance claims in Theorems 8, 11, and 17, but none supplies a computational result for ChoCo to continuize.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case under the anchor rule: the paper has no numbered result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity. Its remarks that fully rational calculations may be intractable motivate bounded rationality, but the paper does not analyze that intractability.

The closest result is Theorem 17, proved here: on the complete graph, penultimate prediction achieves perfect learning—each agent’s estimator has the minimum variance attainable using the initial estimates and all measurements up to that time. That is a learning-performance result, not a complexity result. Theorem 8’s fast convergence and Theorem 11’s fixed-response convergence are likewise statements about dynamics, not polynomial-time algorithms for a computational social choice problem. So there is no eligible anchor, and no continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover.

There is a plausible but out-of-scope analytic mirror: take a large complete-network population with finitely many measurement-precision types, assign each type its population mass, and study the limiting estimator dynamics and mean squared error. Theorem 17 is the natural motivation for asking whether perfect learning persists in that limit. But that asks a mean-field learning question; it does not turn the paper’s results into computational-complexity questions.

The weakest point is decisive here: the finite-agent dynamics in the paper could inspire a continuous-population model, but the paper’s named results provide no computational anchor for a ChoCo mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper has no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. Theorems 8 and 11 establish convergence of covariance dynamics; Theorem 13 compares steady-state performance; Theorems 16 and 17 concern asymptotic and perfect learning. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The introduction’s claim that fully rational calculations can become intractable is motivation for bounded rationality, not a formal complexity result.

The best population mirror is still easy to imagine: take a complete network with finitely many measurement-precision classes, give each class a rational mass, and study its limiting covariance dynamics and per-agent estimation error. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; identity is not a convincing objection, and Theorem 17 gives it a natural motivation. But the resulting question is about mean-field learning dynamics. To make it computational, one would have to add a task—such as choosing response weights to meet an error target—and specify its inputs and outputs. The paper does not pose or analyze such a task. That extension may be worthwhile research, but it is not a continuous mirror of one of these results.

So the negative case is strong as a scope judgment: there is no eligible computational anchor. The universal claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile work is weaker. A continuous-population learning model could be valuable on its own; the case against is that it would not fill ChoCo’s stated computational gap.
