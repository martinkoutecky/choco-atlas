The continuous mirror question

For a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over bank-location types \((r,z)\in\{1,2\}\times\mathbb{R}\), design a randomized bridge rule that is GSP against deviations by positive-mass coalitions and has worst-case expected social-cost ratio at most \(2\). Is \(2\) the best possible ratio under this incentive requirement?

The model it lives in

Let \(a=\mu(\{1\}\times(-\infty,1/2))\) and \(b=\mu(\{2\}\times(1/2,\infty))\). For facilities at \(1\) and \(0\), define \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(s)=\int c_r(z,s)\,d\mu(r,z)\). The direct analogue of Mechanism 5 chooses bridge \(0\) with probability \(a^2/(a^2+b^2)\) and bridge \(1\) otherwise; when \(a=b=0\), it may choose either endpoint. A coalition is a nonzero submeasure \(\nu\leq\mu\) whose members jointly report locations on their respective banks; GSP requires that no such deviation makes almost all coalition members strictly better off.

What the mirror covers

Covers the social-cost GSP approximation results in Theorems 5 and 7. It leaves the maximum-cost results, the SGSP results, and the paper’s lower bounds for individual-SP mechanisms outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the best worst-case approximation ratio among randomized positive-mass-coalition-GSP bridge rules equal to \(2\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is the paper’s social-cost bridge problem. Its population can naturally be large and repetitive: residents on the two banks of a river, grouped by building or block location. A city may have millions of residents but far fewer distinct location types. The type is a bank and a location \(t=(r,z)\); its mass is that fraction of the whole population. This keeps the paper’s private information, bridge decision, service costs, and aggregate objective intact. The bridge is already real-valued in the paper, so the continuization here is specifically of the population.

For precision, take an instance to be a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over \(\{1,2\}\times\mathbb{R}\), with rational masses summing to \(1\). Let \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\) be its mass on the two banks, and let \(\tau\) be its number of supported bank-location types. With facilities at \(1\) on bank 1 and \(0\) on bank 2, a bridge at \(s\) gives type \((1,x)\) cost \(c_1(x,s)=\min\{|x-1|,|x-s|+|s|\}\), and type \((2,y)\) cost \(c_2(y,s)=\min\{|y|,|y-s|+|s-1|\}\). Social cost is the population average, \(\mathrm{SC}(\mu,s)=\int c_r(z,s)\,d\mu(r,z)\). Dividing the original total cost by population size preserves approximation ratios.

The incentive notion needs one deliberate adaptation. A single resident has zero mass in a continuum and cannot change an aggregate-report mechanism’s input, so individual strategyproofness would become vacuous. Instead, define group-strategyproofness against positive-mass coalitions: a coalition is a nonzero submeasure \(\nu\leq\mu\); its members may jointly report new locations on their own bank, changing the distribution to \(\mu'=\mu-\nu+\rho_\#\nu\), where \(\rho\) is their report map. A mechanism is GSP if no such coalition can make almost all its members strictly better off. This is a genuine co-modification of the incentive model, but it preserves the paper’s concern with coalitional manipulation and gives that concern meaning in a mass population.

My lead anchor is Theorem 7, proved in this paper in Section 4.1. It proves that Mechanism 5 is a randomized GSP mechanism with a social-cost approximation ratio of \(2\): it chooses bridge \(0\) with probability \(n_1^2/(n_1^2+m_2^2)\) and bridge \(1\) with probability \(m_2^2/(n_1^2+m_2^2)\), where \(n_1\) counts bank-1 agents left of \(1/2\) and \(m_2\) counts bank-2 agents right of \(1/2\). When both counts are zero, either endpoint can be selected. The theorem is proved here, not cited.

The corresponding continuous problem is Mass-GSP Randomized Bridge Design: over all such distributions \(\mu\), find a randomized map \(F\) from \(\mu\) to a distribution over bridge locations that is GSP against positive-mass coalitions and minimizes its worst-case ratio
\[ \sup_{\mu}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{s\sim F(\mu)}[\mathrm{SC}(\mu,s)]}{\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{SC}(\mu,s)}. \]
The solution is the mechanism map, with a proof of its incentive guarantee and ratio. The direct mirror of Mechanism 5 uses \(a=\mu_1((-\infty,1/2))\) and \(b=\mu_2((1/2,\infty))\), choosing \(0\) with probability \(a^2/(a^2+b^2)\) and \(1\) otherwise; if \(a=b=0\), choose either endpoint. Counts divided by the common population size give exactly these masses. The theorem’s inequalities also have a natural mass version: sums become integrals, and the key count inequality becomes \(2ab\leq a^2+b^2\). Thus a 2-approximation is a credible expected result for this mirror. The open follow-up is whether \(2\) is the best possible ratio under this mass-coalitional incentive definition.

A second anchor is Theorem 5, also proved here in Section 4.1. It proves that Mechanism 4 is deterministic GSP and gives a \(3\)-approximation for social cost: it returns bridge \(0\) when \(n_1\geq m_2\), and bridge \(1\) otherwise. Its mirror is Mass-GSP Deterministic Bridge Design: find a deterministic GSP map \(F(\mu)\in\mathbb{R}\) minimizing the same worst-case social-cost ratio. The direct candidate returns \(0\) when \(a\geq b\), and \(1\) otherwise. The finite rule and its proof use the relative sizes of the two affected populations; replacing those counts and costs by masses and integrals gives a plausible \(3\)-approximation. This is a Class A style algorithmic mirror: the rule needs only two threshold masses, computable in \(O(\tau)\) time, and no exponentially large type space.

These anchors concern the same problem as the authors’ paper rather than a softened substitute: agents still have private locations, the mechanism still chooses a bridge, facilities and costs are unchanged, and the target is still low social cost subject to GSP. The mass statistics are also not an objection to the mirror; a building cohort’s common location is precisely its complete type for this model. Existing results on continuum or high-multiplicity populations would support the regime’s plausibility rather than collide with it.

The scope is deliberately limited to the paper’s social-cost GSP approximation results, Theorems 5 and 7. The paper presents no NP-hardness, \(P\), W[1]-hardness, or FPT theorem, so this case does not claim that population continuization dissolves a known complexity barrier. Nor do the paper’s individual-SP lower bounds automatically transfer: in a non-atomic model, unilateral deviations have zero mass. My expected classification is tractable for the stated \(3\)- and \(2\)-approximation tasks; the exact approximation frontier remains a further question. The paper’s Proposition 2, proved here, supports the formulation by showing that an optimal social-cost bridge is at an endpoint, a fact that also follows by integrating its pointwise argument.

The weakest point is the incentive co-modification. Positive-mass coalitional GSP is a natural limit of group manipulation when a coalition represents a nonvanishing fraction of the population, but it is not literally the paper’s finite-agent definition, which includes even a single agent. A referee could reasonably ask whether the authors want this continuum incentive notion. The case survives because the proposed questions retain their mechanism-design problem in full and keep GSP nontrivial; but the mass-coalition convention must be stated openly, and the integral extensions of Theorems 5 and 7 would need proofs in the continuous model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither anchor states a computational problem of the kind ChoCo is meant to chart. Theorems 5 and 7 establish incentive and approximation guarantees for mechanisms; they do not give a complexity result or analyze a difficult computation. Their mechanisms reduce to counting agents on either side of \(1/2\), then choosing an endpoint. In the best repeated-resident scenario—many residents at a finite set of building locations—the corresponding mass statistics can be computed by scanning the \(\tau\) supported types. That makes a coherent high-multiplicity model, but it does not give the programme a computational landscape to investigate.

For Theorem 7, the direct mass version is especially easy to state: let \(a\) and \(b\) be the masses of bank-1 residents left of \(1/2\) and bank-2 residents right of \(1/2\), then choose endpoint \(0\) with probability \(a^2/(a^2+b^2)\). The key count inequality extends to masses as \(2ab\leq a^2+b^2\). This is a reasonable weighted restatement of the theorem’s guarantee. The stronger question—finding the best ratio among all mechanisms under positive-mass-coalition GSP—would be a game-theoretic characterization problem under a newly chosen incentive notion. The paper’s lower bounds for individual SP do not carry over: in the continuum, a unilateral report has zero mass. So the theorem supplies an upper-bound construction, but not a computational problem whose complexity or algorithmic frontier ChoCo could chart.

Theorem 5 has the same limitation. Its candidate mass rule returns \(0\) when \(a\geq b\) and \(1\) otherwise. The finite proof’s population counts become masses, and the rule again takes \(O(\tau)\) time. One could ask for the optimal approximation ratio over all mass-GSP rules, but that shifts the research question from computing an outcome to characterizing mechanisms under the proposed continuum incentive constraint. The paper provides no computational barrier or optimization task for that question.

I would not reject these mirrors on the grounds that locations are private, agents have identities, or a continuous answer might match the finite one. Repeated residents at a finite set of locations are plausible types, and the social-cost objective has a natural mass version. The negative case is narrower: both anchors lead to short weighted versions of existing mechanism proofs, while a more ambitious mirror requires a new incentive model and asks a different kind of question from the programme’s complexity focus.

That case is not airtight. If ChoCo includes approximation-mechanism design as a computational question in its own right, then the mass-GSP frontier is well-posed and could be worthwhile; I cannot rule out that broader reading. My case against rests on the programme’s stated computational remit, not on a fundamental failure of the continuous population model.
