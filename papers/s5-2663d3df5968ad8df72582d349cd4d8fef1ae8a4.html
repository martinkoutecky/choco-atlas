The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified one. The paper has no named computational-complexity result: no numbered theorem proves NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), W[1]-hardness, FPT, or an algorithmic formulation with a stated complexity bound. Its results are axiomatic, probabilistic, or dynamical. Thus a strictly compliant positive case cannot be made. The best available mirror is nevertheless a credible high-multiplicity computational problem based on Theorems 3 and 4.

Take a large liquid-democracy organisation with many members but finitely many recurring roles. A type \(t\) contains a member’s complete initial binary opinion \(o_t:P\to\{0,1\}\), their issue-by-issue delegation behaviour, and any information relevant to rationality checks. For every issue \(p\), let \(d_p(t)\) be the type of the trustee to whom type \(t\) delegates. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}^{T}\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Thus \(10^6\) members may instantiate only \(10^2\) or \(10^3\) types: members of one type share the same ballot, delegation pattern, and role-level trustee.

The delegation process remains binary and individual-like; only the population is continuous. If \(o_t^r\) is the opinion of type \(t\) after round \(r\), define the proposed opinion

\[ \widehat{o}_t^r(q)=o_{d_q(t)}^r(q). \]

The type updates exactly as in the paper’s Boolean DeGroot process:

\[ o_t^{r+1}(p)= \begin{cases} \widehat{o}_t^r(p),& \{\gamma\}\cup\{q:\widehat{o}_t^r(q)=1\} \cup\{\neg q:\widehat{o}_t^r(q)=0\} \text{ is satisfiable},\\ o_t^r(p),&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

When the process stabilizes at \(\bar o\), the collective opinion is computed from mass:

\[ M_\mu(p)= \sum_{t:\bar o_t(p)=1}\mu_t - \sum_{t:\bar o_t(p)=0}\mu_t. \]

The issue is accepted when \(M_\mu(p)>0\), rejected when \(M_\mu(p)<0\), and undecided when \(M_\mu(p)=0\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that when \(\gamma=\top\), the BDP stabilizes if and only if, for every issue \(p\), there is no cycle \(S\) in the delegation graph \(G_p\) containing two agents \(i,j\in S\) with different initial opinions \(O_i(p)\neq O_j(p)\).

This yields the following precise mirror:

Continuous Homogeneous-Cycle Liquid-Democracy Stabilization. Given \(P\), a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu\), initial binary opinions \(o_t\), issue-specific delegation maps \(d_p\), and a target issue \(p^\star\), decide whether the type-level BDP stabilizes and, if it does, output its limiting type profile and the mass-weighted collective opinion on \(p^\star\).

A negative solution is a type-level issue \(p\) and a directed cycle containing two types with different initial \(p\)-opinions. A positive solution is the stabilized profile together with the exact mass comparison determining the collective opinion. Theorem 4 transfers directly: heterogeneous cycles generate nonconvergence, while homogeneous cycles eventually transmit a common opinion through the trees feeding them.

This mirror should be tractable, hence Class A. Cycle detection is polynomial in \(|P|\) and \(|T|\), and once stabilization is known, the limiting profile can be computed by propagation through the finite type graphs. Rational masses only require exact rational comparisons. The population mass is not merely decorative: two societies with the same delegation structure and opinions but different \(\mu\) can produce different collective outcomes. The continuous object is the society, while individual opinions remain integral.

A second, weaker anchor is Theorem 3, also proved here. It states that for an arbitrary integrity constraint \(\gamma\), if every cycle \(C\) in every issue graph \(G_p\) is initially homogeneous—\(O_i(p)=O_j(p)\) for all \(i,j\in C\)—then the individually rational BDP stabilizes in at most

\[ k=\max\{\operatorname{diam}(G_p)\mid p\in P\} \]

steps.

Its continuous counterpart is:

Continuous Rational Proxy-Diffusion Limit. Given \(P,\gamma,T,\mu,o_t,d_p\), under the promise that every delegation cycle is homogeneous on every issue, output the stabilized type profile, a bound \(r\le k\) witnessing stabilization, and the resulting mass-weighted collective opinion.

This is again Class A provided consistency with \(\gamma\) can be checked efficiently, for example when \(\gamma\) belongs to a tractable propositional fragment. With unrestricted propositional \(\gamma\), hardness may instead come from satisfiability checks in the agenda; that would be agenda-driven hardness, not continuum-specific hardness.

The regime is plausible, though narrower than ordinary peer-to-peer liquid democracy. It describes large organisations in which members delegate through recurring proxy offices, party roles, workplace constituencies, or issue-specific representatives. If every person delegates to an individually named and idiosyncratic trustee, then the number of types is effectively the number of people and the high-multiplicity interpretation loses force. The positive case therefore depends on role-based delegation being a genuine institutional feature, not merely a mathematical convenience.

I would not use Proposition 2 or Proposition 3 as computational anchors. They establish asymptotic probabilities of collective abstention under impartial culture, but neither formulates a complexity problem. Likewise, Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 are axiomatic characterizations, and Theorem 2 is an impossibility result cited from elsewhere.

The weakest point is decisive: even the lead mirror does not show a new complexity phenomenon caused by continuization. Stabilization is governed by the delegation graphs; \(\mu\) affects the final aggregation but not whether the process stabilizes. Moreover, Theorem 4 itself is not stated as a polynomial-time theorem in the paper. So the honest conclusion is: there is a recognizable and mathematically sensible continuous-population mirror, probably tractable, covering the paper’s stabilization results; but under the programme’s strict named-computational-anchor rule, this paper does not supply a fully qualifying positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 are structural statements about a finite dynamical system; they assert when iteration stabilizes, but give no complexity classification, optimization problem, approximation problem, or algorithmic theorem. Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 are axiomatic, Proposition 2 and Proposition 3 are probabilistic, and Theorem 2 is an impossibility result. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, that is already fatal: the proposed computational problem is invented after the fact rather than extracted from the paper.

The best possible defence is the proponent’s block-homogeneous model: each type has one initial opinion and delegates, for every issue, to a fixed target type. Granting that restriction, the construction is mathematically coherent. But it reduces Theorem 4 to the same finite functional-graph theorem on \(T\) instead of \(N\). Stabilization depends only on the type graph and type opinions; \(\mu\) does not enter the dynamics at all. The quantity

\[ \sum_{t:\bar o_t(p)=1}\mu_t- \sum_{t:\bar o_t(p)=0}\mu_t \]

is merely a final weighted majority calculation appended after stabilization. Thus the proposed “continuous” problem is a weighted finite-graph evaluation problem, not a computational question created by continuizing the population. It contains no mass transfer, robustness, campaign action, or other problem whose high-multiplicity form could expose a new algorithmic structure.

There is also a fidelity problem outside that narrow block model. In the paper, a delegation is to a named individual: \(r_p:N\to N\). Saying that a voter delegates to “a type-\(B\) trustee” does not specify the individual graph. Different couplings among voters and trustees can have the same type masses and the same marginal type-level delegation data while producing different cycles, basins, and proxy weights. Preserving those identities leaves a discrete trustee skeleton, or requires an atomless identity space in which agents are again individually indexed. Averaging them into a delegation kernel produces a mean-field model, but not the paper’s cycle theorem. The role-based construction avoids this only by imposing a strong new institutional model in which all members of a role are behaviorally interchangeable; then the result concerns a finite role automaton rather than liquid democracy’s individual delegation graph.

Theorem 3 fares no better. Its stabilization bound is the diameter of the delegation graph, and its updates are determined by \(\gamma\), opinions, and delegation edges—not by population mass. With unrestricted \(\gamma\), the computational difficulty is satisfiability of the shared agenda constraint, hence agenda-driven rather than multiplicity-driven. Restricting \(\gamma\) to a tractable fragment makes the invented problem easier, but does not create a continuous computational result.

The proposed mirror also omits the paper’s central non-stabilizing case. When a cycle contains conflicting opinions, the paper discusses the resulting loss of representation and collective abstention. The proponent’s problem simply has no output in that case. A better version could report the mass lying in basins of mixed cycles, but computing that still amounts to weighted reachability in the finite graph. If one instead studies the probability of such cycles, one is modelling the paper’s impartial-culture random experiment, not a fixed distribution over finitely many voter types; the number of possible named trustees grows with \(n\).

A role-based liquid-democracy model might be an interesting new project. I cannot honestly prove that no such model could ever be worthwhile. But it would be a new weighted role-network or delegation-design problem, not a continuous computational mirror of this paper. The paper’s own results offer no computational anchor, and the two proposed anchors become either a finite graph theorem with an irrelevant mass label or a changed model that no longer preserves the individual delegation structure.
