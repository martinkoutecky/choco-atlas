The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results concern bargaining prices, arbitrage, pairwise stability, and welfare. None asserts a complexity classification or an algorithm for an underlying computational problem.

The closest high-multiplicity foothold is Proposition 8, proved here: when the buyer and seller population sizes are relatively prime and sufficiently close, a sufficiently large market has a pairwise-stable network that is inefficient; if the size difference is less than one third of the smaller population, then for every \(\zeta>0\), sufficiently large markets admit such a network whose matching process produces a welfare loss of at least \(\zeta\) with positive probability in each period. This is a structural existence result, not a computational result, so it cannot serve as an anchor under the requested rule.

The paper does sketch very large markets with continuous cost and valuation distributions in Section 4, but uses them to analyze prices. A continuous computational problem would have to be posed anew. Proposition 8’s construction also depends on individual network positions and maximal matchings, making a low-type-count population mirror a substantive modeling step.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a worthwhile mirror is strong at the programme’s first gate: this paper states no computational problem and proves no complexity or algorithmic result. Propositions 1–9 characterize bargaining outcomes, arbitrage, network stability, and welfare. The closest candidate, Proposition 8, proves that certain finite markets admit pairwise-stable networks with large welfare losses; it does not ask how to compute such a network. Turning its existence claim into a search problem would add a new computational objective, rather than mirror a computational result in the paper.

The stronger versions of that candidate do not fix the gap. One could ask whether a continuum market admits inefficient pairwise-stable networks, or ask for an algorithm that finds a stable network with a large welfare loss. The first is a continuum equilibrium question, not a complexity question. The second needs a new input-output task and a representation of the endogenous trading network. In the paper, individual links, maximal matchings, and bilateral deviations drive the results. A type space that preserves those features may need to encode network position, leaving no evident small-support high-multiplicity regime. A graphon or other continuum network model might be possible, but it would be a substantial new model whose computational question the paper does not supply.

Section 4’s continuous cost and valuation distributions are not a collision or a computational anchor: they are used to compare bargaining and competitive prices analytically. They show that continuous populations can be meaningful in this economic setting, but do not bridge the paper to computational social choice. So the negative case rests on a clear mismatch of research questions, not on the claim that a continuous version would be uninteresting or have the same answer.
