Why no mirror

Theorem 1 constructively characterizes the finite-player Bayesian equilibrium, so it has computational content. But the proposed mirror removes the finite-sample uncertainty that drives that equilibrium and asks about a different nonatomic contest; the proponent acknowledges this change without showing that the theorem’s question survives it.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the decentralized college-admissions game in Theorem 1, §2.4. The theorem is proved in the dissertation. It is an exact equilibrium characterization, so it can anchor an equilibrium-computation question. It is not, however, a formal complexity result: the paper gives no running-time bound or classification such as \(P\) or NP-hardness.

Theorem 1’s finite-player result is this. There are \(n\) students, two colleges with capacities \(q_1,q_2\), values \(0<v_1<v_2\), and privately known abilities drawn independently from a distribution \(F\) with positive continuous density \(f\). A student of ability \(a\) who exerts effort \(e\) pays \(e/a\). In the decentralized mechanism, each student chooses one college and an effort; each college admits its \(q_j\) highest-effort applicants.

The theorem says there is a unique symmetric, monotone equilibrium \((\gamma,\beta_D;c)\). Below a cutoff \(c\), a student of ability \(a\) applies to college 1 with probability \(\gamma(a)\) and otherwise applies to college 2; above \(c\), she applies to college 2 for sure. Students use the same increasing effort function \(\beta_D(a)\) whichever college they choose. To state the characterization fully, define

\(p_{j,k}(x)=\binom{j+k}{j}x^j(1-x)^k\),

\(\pi(a)=\int_0^a\gamma(x)f(x)\,dx\),

\(G(a)=\pi(a)/\pi(c)\), and

\(H(a)=(F(a)-\pi(a))/(1-\pi(c))\) for \(a\le c\), while \(H(a)=(F(a)-\pi(c))/(1-\pi(c))\) for \(a\ge c\).

Let \(h_{r,m}\) be the density of the \(r\)-th order statistic among \(m\) draws from \(H\). The equilibrium effort is

\(\beta_D(a)=v_2\int_0^a x\sum_{m=q_2}^{n-1}p_{n-m-1,m}(\pi(c))h_{m-q_2+1,m}(x)\,dx.\)

The cutoff and mixing strategy are fixed by these conditions. First, \(\pi(c)\) is the unique solution \(x\) to

\(v_1\sum_{m=0}^{q_1-1}p_{m,n-m-1}(x)=v_2\sum_{m=0}^{q_2-1}p_{n-m-1,m}(x).\)

Given \(\pi(c)\), \(c\) is the unique solution to

\(v_1=v_2\sum_{m=0}^{q_2-1}p_{n-m-1,m}(\pi(c))+v_2\sum_{m=q_2}^{n-1}p_{n-m-1,m}(\pi(c))\sum_{j=m-q_2+1}^{m}p_{j,m-j}\!\left(\frac{F(c)-\pi(c)}{1-\pi(c)}\right).\)

For each \(a<c\), \(\pi(a)\) is the unique solution \(x\) to

\(v_2\sum_{m=q_2}^{n-1}p_{n-m-1,m}(\pi(c))\sum_{j=m-q_2+1}^{m}p_{j,m-j}\!\left(\frac{F(a)-x}{1-\pi(c)}\right) =v_1\sum_{m=q_1}^{n-1}p_{m,n-m-1}(\pi(c))\sum_{j=m-q_1+1}^{m}p_{j,m-j}\!\left(\frac{x}{\pi(c)}\right).\)

Finally, with

\(A(a)=v_1\sum_{m=q_1}^{n-1}p_{m,n-m-1}(\pi(c))\,m\,p_{m-q_1,q_1-1}\!\left(\frac{\pi(a)}{\pi(c)}\right)\)

and

\(B(a)=v_2\sum_{m=q_2}^{n-1}p_{n-m-1,m}(\pi(c))\,m\,p_{m-q_2,q_2-1}\!\left(\frac{F(a)-\pi(a)}{1-\pi(c)}\right),\)

the mixing probability is

\(\gamma(a)=\frac{\pi(c)B(a)}{(1-\pi(c))A(a)+\pi(c)B(a)}.\)

A plausible regime is a national admissions cohort: millions of applicants compete for seats at two tiers of universities, and many applicants share the same relevant preparation and effort-cost category. For example, types could be discrete entrance-score or preparation-cost levels; each type includes every trait the model uses, including ability and effort cost. Then \(n\) can be vastly larger than the number \(\tau\) of types. The continuous quantity is each type’s fraction of the cohort. College capacities must scale with the cohort, so use per-capita seat supplies; this is a necessary co-modification of the large-market model. The dissertation itself motivates college admissions with millions of applicants, and its discussion cites large-contest and approximate-equilibrium work. Those are evidence that the regime is sensible, not a collision: this dissertation’s students are still a finite population.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover:

DCA\(_\infty\) approximate-equilibrium computation. An instance consists of a finite set of ability-cost types \(a_i\in(0,1]\), rational masses \(\mu_i\ge0\) summing to one, college values \(0<v_1<v_2\), rational per-capita capacities \(\rho_1,\rho_2>0\) with \(\rho_1+\rho_2\le1\), and rational \(\varepsilon>0\). Each infinitesimal member of type \(i\) chooses one college \(j\in\{1,2\}\) and effort \(e\in[0,a_i v_2]\), paying \(e/a_i\). At college \(j\), the highest-effort applicant mass up to \(\rho_j\) is admitted; if a positive mass ties at the cutoff, it is admitted pro rata. A strategy profile gives each type a distribution \(\sigma_i\) over college-effort choices, inducing the aggregate applicant and effort masses.

The task is to return finite-support rational mixed strategies \((\sigma_i)\) that form an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium: for every type \(i\), its expected utility under \(\sigma_i\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of the utility from its best unilateral choice of college and effort against the induced aggregate masses. A solution also reports the induced admission and effort masses by type. Utility from choosing college \(j\) with effort \(e\) is \(v_j\) times that action’s admission probability, minus \(e/a_i\); an unassigned agent receives zero admission value. This keeps the problem about the same college choice, effort, and admission competition as Theorem 1. It is a search problem, not a welfare-maximization problem.

I would initially expect this restricted, two-college version to be Class A, especially with fixed \(\tau\): the theorem’s cutoff and monotonicity structure suggest that an algorithm might reduce equilibrium computation to a finite system of type-level indifference and admission conditions, then approximate it with error guarantees. A useful target would be running time polynomial in \(\tau\), input bit length, and \(1/\varepsilon\). That is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 1. The theorem’s order-statistic formula is evidence for structure, not an algorithm for the continuum game.

The mirror covers only the decentralized equilibrium result in Chapter 2. It does not claim to continuize the TTC reporting experiment or the workplace contest experiment, and it does not cover the paper’s centralized-versus-decentralized welfare comparisons.

The main further questions are whether DCA\(_\infty\) has an equilibrium for every such instance; whether its equilibrium is unique or retains a cutoff-and-mixing structure; whether finite-\(n\) equilibrium outcomes converge to it as the type counts grow; and whether the low-ability/high-ability welfare comparison in Propositions 2 and 3 survives when seat supplies are population fractions.

The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 1 is about a finite number of players and relies on binomial order-statistic probabilities. In the continuum, individual uncertainty and the finite-\(n\) admission probabilities disappear; the equilibrium can change, and the theorem’s uniqueness proof does not transfer. The dissertation also has no complexity classification to carry over. Still, the proposed problem preserves the paper’s actual strategic choices and admissions rule in a plausible mass-cohort regime. The discrete-to-continuous equilibrium relationship—and the complexity of computing that limit—is precisely what a mirror study would need to establish.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the best anchor in this thesis, and the national admissions cohort is a convincing high-multiplicity scenario: applicants can share ability and effort-cost types, while capacities scale with the cohort. That makes the population mirror sensible. It does not, by itself, make the proposed task a computational mirror of the theorem. Theorem 1 characterizes the existence and form of a unique symmetric, monotone Bayesian equilibrium. It gives no algorithmic problem or complexity result. Treating it as an equilibrium-computation task is a reasonable new question, but it adds the computational objective that the paper did not study.

There is also a deeper gap. The theorem’s equilibrium depends on finite-population uncertainty: order-statistic probabilities determine each student’s chance of beating the other applicants. As the population grows and capacities scale with it, those random shares concentrate. Against a fixed aggregate, an infinitesimal applicant’s admission becomes essentially a threshold in effort. That changes the incentives underlying the theorem. If a positive mass ties at the threshold, an applicant with positive surplus can secure admission by bidding just above it for an arbitrarily small extra cost. If there is no mass at the threshold, the best response can instead be to bid just above it, with no maximizing effort. Either way, the theorem’s exact equilibrium and uniqueness claim do not pass directly to the population limit.

The proponent’s \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium formulation is the strongest repair. It makes a limit contest studyable, but it changes the target: it asks for approximate equilibria of a deterministic nonatomic contest, with a chosen tie rule and strategy encoding, rather than computation of the paper’s exact Bayesian equilibrium. A better mirror could instead ask whether finite-\(n\) equilibria converge to a population limit. That is a legitimate research question, but its central issue is convergence between different game forms; it is not a complexity result from this thesis. Keeping enough finite sampling uncertainty to preserve the theorem’s order-statistic incentives, in turn, retains a finite contest rather than a continuous population.

The contest equilibria in Chapter 3 face the same tension: their finite groups and random ability draws generate rank uncertainty that the continuum removes. The honest weakness in this negative case is that proving the limiting behavior—or showing that a useful approximate-equilibrium formulation survives—could itself be worthwhile. But the thesis offers no named computational result to carry over, and its strongest theorem relies on a strategic uncertainty that vanishes in the proposed mirror. That makes this a weak fit for ChoCo’s computational programme, even though the underlying population regime is plausible.
