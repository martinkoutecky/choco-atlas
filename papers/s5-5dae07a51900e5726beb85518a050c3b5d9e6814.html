The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of schools \(C\), types \(T\) with strict school rankings and a strict priority order over types at each school, rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), a rational downward-closed feasible load polytope \(\mathcal F\subseteq[0,1]^C\), and \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), compute an assignment \(x_{t,d}\geq 0\) for \(d\in C\cup\{\emptyset\}\), with \(\sum_d x_{t,d}=\mu_t\) and school loads in \(\mathcal F\), satisfying both conditions below. For every type-school pair with \(x_{t,c}>0\), the mass \(E_x(t,c)=\sum_{u\succ_c t,\ d:\ c\succ_u d}x_{u,d}\) that envies its recipients is at most \(\kappa\). For every type-\(t\) mass at \(d\) that prefers \(c\) to \(d\) and can move an arbitrarily small positive amount to \(c\) while preserving feasibility, the total mass of objectors is greater than \(\kappa\); an objector is higher priority than \(t\) at \(c\), prefers \(c\) to its current assignment, and cannot move an arbitrarily small positive amount to \(c\) while preserving feasibility. When masses are \(1/n\) per applicant, \(\kappa=k/n\) is the population-scaled threshold corresponding to the paper’s integer parameter.

The model it lives in

A type-level assignment over rational population masses, with each type specifying a school ranking and priority class; the decision variables are \(x_{t,d}\), and the output must satisfy mass-bounded envy and objection conditions under a hereditary feasible-load polytope.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3 assumes strict priorities over named students, while grouping students into priority classes changes who can envy whom; its polynomial running time also gives no bound in the number of types or the bit length of their masses.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the search task in Theorem 3: finding a feasible matching satisfying the paired fairness and non-wastefulness relaxations. It leaves aside the paper’s strict individual-priority guarantee, its cutoff and deferred-acceptance implementations as discrete algorithms, its other structural results, and its experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can this assignment be computed in time polynomial in the number of schools, the support size \(|T|\), the input bit length, and the representation size of \(\mathcal F\)?
  • Can a computed mass assignment be rounded to a finite matching while controlling violations of the two guarantees?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is the paper’s computation of an envy and non-wastefulness relaxed matching in a large, type-compressed admissions market. The regime is a statewide school or university placement system with many applicants but a moderate number of recurring profiles. A type records an applicant’s complete ranking of schools and their priority category at each school. For example, many applicants may share the same ranking and the same residence or eligibility categories that determine priority. The relevant regime has \(n\) applicants and \(\tau\) such profiles, with \(\tau \ll n\). This is a property of those admissions systems, not of matching markets in general.

The type-level priority categories need to be explicit: applicants of one type are tied with one another, while colleges order the types. That is a natural high-multiplicity version of priority rules based on categories, though it abstracts away any individual tie-break lottery. Each unit of mass still represents an applicant who gets one school; mass can be split because a large cohort can be divided among schools.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that under hereditary constraints, the \(k\)-admissible cutoff algorithm always computes a feasible matching satisfying both ER-\(k\) and NW-\(k\), and runs in polynomial time. Here ER-\(k\) requires that no student be envied by more than \(k\) other students; NW-\(k\) requires that every feasible claim by a student to a preferred empty seat face more than \(k\) objections. The result is proved here, not merely cited. The paper gives no comparable named computational hardness result, so this is the appropriate algorithmic anchor.

I would ask a prover to study the following problem, Continuous ER–NW Matching. An instance consists of a finite set of schools \(C\), a finite set of types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), each type’s strict ranking of \(C\cup\{\emptyset\}\), and each school’s priority order over types. It also has a rational, downward-closed feasible load region \(\mathcal F\subseteq[0,1]^C\), such as one given by local and regional quota inequalities, and a rational tolerance \(\kappa\in[0,1]\).

A solution is an assignment \(x_{t,d}\geq0\), for \(t\in T\) and \(d\in C\cup\{\emptyset\}\), with \(\sum_d x_{t,d}=\mu_t\) and load vector \(\nu_c=\sum_t x_{t,c}\in\mathcal F\). The task is to output any such assignment satisfying the following population versions of the paper’s two properties; there is no welfare objective in the paper’s anchor, so this is a feasibility and search problem.

For a type-\(t\) applicant assigned to \(c\), the mass envying them is
\[ E_x(t,c)=\sum_{\substack{u\in T,\ d\in C\cup\{\emptyset\}:\\ u\succ_c t,\ c\succ_u d}}x_{u,d}. \]
Require \(E_x(t,c)\leq\kappa\) whenever \(x_{t,c}>0\). This is ER-\(\kappa\): no recipient is envied by more than a \(\kappa\) fraction of the population.

For NW-\(\kappa\), consider any type-\(t\) mass currently at \(d\) that prefers \(c\) to \(d\). It has a claim to \(c\) if moving an arbitrarily small positive amount of that mass from \(d\) to \(c\) preserves feasibility. A type-\(u\) mass at \(e\) objects if it has higher priority than \(t\) at \(c\), prefers \(c\) to \(e\), and moving an arbitrarily small positive amount of it from \(e\) to \(c\) is infeasible at the current load vector. Require every claim to have more than \(\kappa\) mass of objectors. The parameter is population-scaled: on a discrete profile of \(n\) equally weighted applicants, the corresponding threshold is \(\kappa=k/n\).

I expect this problem to be tractable when \(\mathcal F\) has a polynomial-time feasibility or separation representation. The paper’s algorithm builds a matching through preference-improving moves while preserving an envy bound, then stops when no admissible empty-seat claim remains. In the type-compressed setting, the combinatorial information lives in the finite set of preference and priority profiles, so a mass-level version of that process seems plausible. This would be a Class A result: the central object remains a matching under hereditary constraints, with the two same tensions measured across cohorts.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors Theorem 3’s task of computing a jointly relaxed matching. It does not claim that the paper’s experiments, all hereditary constraint representations, or every finite-market result have a continuous counterpart.

The main proof obligation is substantial: Theorem 3’s polynomial running time is for an explicitly listed population, and its steps may depend on the number of individual students. It does not establish a running time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the binary encoding length of the masses. A prover would need a finite, efficient way to move cohort mass and show that the process terminates without expanding the population. The infinitesimal definition of a claim also needs care near quota boundaries; it is the natural population-scale analogue, but does not automatically preserve every one-person feasibility test in a finite market. These are real limitations, but they leave a recognizable question about the paper’s underlying problem: computing envy-bounded, non-wasteful matchings for a population described by its distribution over applicant types.

Further questions follow directly: whether such an algorithm runs in \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\); how to round its output to a discrete matching while controlling violations; and how the answer changes when the tolerated mass tends to zero, corresponding to fixed \(k\) as \(n\) grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only computational anchor is Theorem 3: a polynomial-time algorithm for finding an ER-\(k\), NW-\(k\) matching. The strongest objection is that its population is not compressible under the paper’s exact priority model. Colleges have strict orders over individual students, so even students with identical school preferences occupy different priority positions. If those positions are part of a student’s complete type, then \(\tau\) grows with \(n\); describing the market by type masses gives no high-multiplicity compression.

The proposed priority categories avoid that problem by changing the model. That change affects the property the theorem guarantees: students in the same category no longer have justified envy toward one another, whereas a strict tie-break can make them envy one another. Restoring that tie-break restores the individual distinctions and the large type count. Likewise, replacing the integer threshold \(k\) with a mass threshold \(\kappa\) changes the guarantee: fixed \(k\) tends to zero as the population grows, while fixed positive \(\kappa\) permits violations by a linear number of students. The proposed infinitesimal NW test also needs a real-valued feasibility region; the paper’s feasibility sets are integer-valued. With quota inequalities this extension is plausible, but it is an additional modeling choice, not a limit specified by Theorem 3.

That makes the proposed problem a worthwhile candidate for a new model of admissions with priority classes, but a weaker mirror of this paper’s result. Its algorithmic question is whether the result survives after replacing strict individual priorities, discrete envy counts, and integer feasibility with their population-scale counterparts. The paper’s polynomial-time theorem does not answer that question.

This is the strongest negative case, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Priority classes and regional quotas describe plausible large admissions markets, and the paper’s load-based constraints make a mass assignment natural. I cannot honestly rule out that stronger version; the case against the sole anchor remains incomplete.
