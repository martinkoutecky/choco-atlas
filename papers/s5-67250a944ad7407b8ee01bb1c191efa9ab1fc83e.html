The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(S\), a finite support \(T\) of strict student rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita school capacities \(\kappa_s\) with \(\sum_s \kappa_s \ge 1\), and a common strictly increasing nonnegative rational cost \(f\) on ranks, compute a rational assignment \(x_{ts}\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in T,s\in S} x_{ts} f(\operatorname{rank}_t(s))\), subject to \(x_{ts}\ge 0\), \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\le\kappa_s\). Establish whether every minimum-cost assignment is Pareto efficient when atomless type mass can be partitioned among schools and each individual receives one school.

The model it lives in

A transportation min-cost-flow model: types are complete rankings carrying masses \(\mu_t\); variables \(x_{ts}\) allocate each type’s mass to schools; school capacities scale per capita as \(\kappa_s\); the objective is the paper’s common rank cost \(f\). Atomless mass can be partitioned among schools while every student receives an integral school assignment.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the common-cardinalization rank-cost assignment objective and the Pareto-efficiency guarantee of Theorem 3.2. It leaves aside Theorem 3.3, the strategic-action analysis including Theorem 3.6, school priorities and stability, and tie-breaking choices.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every minimum-cost mass allocation satisfy an appropriate Pareto-efficiency notion for the atomless population?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper’s best anchor is Theorem 3.2, proved in the paper: if the Utility-Based Hungarian Mechanism outputs a matching \(M\) for a strictly increasing cardinalization \(f\), then \(M\) is Pareto efficient. There is no numbered result classifying a problem as in P or proving hardness. The paper does say that the Hungarian algorithm is polynomial-time; that claim appears in its discussion of the algorithm, rather than as a numbered complexity theorem. So Theorem 3.2 is an algorithmic guarantee, not a hardness result dressed up as one.

A direct mirror is Continuous Rank-Cost School Assignment. Consider a large recurring cohort in a district with \(m\) schools. A type \(t\) is a complete strict ranking of those schools, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students with that ranking; the input lists only the \(\tau\) rankings with positive mass. For each school \(s\), let \(\kappa_s\) be its seats per student, with total capacity at least one. Fix a common, strictly increasing, nonnegative rational cost \(f(j)\) for assigning a student their \(j\)-th choice.

The question is to find assignment masses \(x_{ts}\) minimizing \(\sum_{t,s}x_{ts}f(\operatorname{rank}_t(s))\), subject to \(x_{ts}\ge 0\), \(\sum_s x_{ts}=\mu_t\) for every type, and \(\sum_t x_{ts}\le\kappa_s\) for every school. A solution is an exact rational matrix \(x\) satisfying those constraints and attaining minimum cost. This is the paper’s rank-cost assignment objective stated over a distribution of students. Although \(x\) is fractional, it need not mean that any student receives a fractional school assignment: each type’s atomless mass can be partitioned among schools so each individual receives exactly one.

The regime is a large school-entry cohort with many students but a fixed, relatively small menu of schools and comparatively few observed rankings: \(N\gg\tau\). For example, with six schools there are at most \(720\) strict rankings, even if the cohort contains many thousands of students. Students who share a ranking are interchangeable for this mechanism’s objective. The capacities must scale with the cohort, so \(\kappa_s\) represents seats per student. This is a plausible regime for a large district or recurring intake, not a claim that every school-choice setting is high-multiplicity.

This mirror preserves the paper’s underlying question: allocate students among capacity-limited schools to minimize the common rank-based cost. It does not make school outcomes fractional. Its expected direction is tractable: the instance is a transportation min-cost-flow problem with \(O(\tau m)\) assignment variables, solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the binary input length. There is also a useful discrete bridge. When the masses and capacities are scaled to integer student and seat counts, the flow polytope is integral, so an optimal mass flow corresponds to an optimal finite assignment. This gives an exact high-multiplicity formulation and compression by preference type, though it does not reveal a hardness barrier dissolving in the continuum.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Theorem 3.2 and the associated common-cardinalization, capacity-constrained assignment mechanism. It does not cover the paper’s strategic-action result, stability or school priorities, or the design choice of which \(f\) to use. In particular, the single-student manipulation setting of Theorem 3.6 does not carry over directly: an individual has zero mass in the continuum, so a meaningful strategic mirror would need to model a positive-mass coalition.

The weakest point is that this is a modest computational case. The paper’s discrete assignment is already polynomial-time, and its numbered anchor guarantees Pareto efficiency rather than proving a complexity classification. The continuous question is credible because it makes the population distribution itself the input and preserves the authors’ exact optimization objective, but its main gain is a support-based formulation of an already tractable problem—not a new tractability result from relaxing a hard one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against this mirror begins with the anchor: Theorem 3.2 proves Pareto efficiency, not a complexity result. The paper’s polynomial-time claim is about the Hungarian algorithm in the finite assignment model; the theorem does not classify a computational problem. So the proponent has not identified a named computational result whose complexity landscape the continuous version would chart.

That leaves a plausible but modest mirror: minimize rank cost over assignment masses \(x_{ts}\), subject to type-mass and school-capacity constraints. It is a sensible high-multiplicity model, and a continuous Pareto-efficiency statement can be formulated for it. But its connection to Theorem 3.2 is indirect: the theorem concerns the output of the paper’s finite mechanism, while the proposed task is to solve a new mass-allocation formulation. The formulation is an ordinary transportation problem with \(O(\tau m)\) variables; the paper’s central algorithm already supplies the relevant assignment machinery. This makes the mirror a weak fit for a programme aimed at charting computational landscapes, though its tractability is not itself an objection.

I cannot make the universal negative case stick. The cohort of students with repeated rankings is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and capacity scaling is a natural part of that model. The proposed continuous question survives as a well-posed mirror; the strongest honest case against is that the paper offers no named computational result for it to anchor, and the resulting contribution may be limited.
