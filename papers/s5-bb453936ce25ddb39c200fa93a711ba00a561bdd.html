The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest anchored case for a continuous computational mirror here: the paper contains no named result asserting a computational complexity classification or giving an algorithm for an underlying problem.

The closest result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that there is a unique risk-allocation solution satisfying Obligation Lower Bound, Lower Composition, and Null Consistency while also being \(K\)-fair; that solution is a baseline allocation at the best outcome plus constrained-egalitarian rationing of the incremental risk. This is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result. Theorem 3 is likewise a characterization by Local Symmetry and \(K\)-fairness. Neither supplies a qualifying anchor.

There is a plausible setting for a *new extension*: many insurance divisions with repeated actuarial profiles. A type could specify a division’s risk endowment, liquidity limit, preferences, and capital-allocation parameters; mass would be the fraction of divisions of that type; the decision would be a risk-allocation schedule. But the paper fixes a finite set of agents and asks which allocation satisfies its axioms. Turning that into a continuum-type computation would require new modeling choices, and a computational task such as computing the characterized allocation would be newly posed, not mirrored from a named computational result in this paper. So the strongest honest verdict is that there are zero qualifying anchors and zero continuous problems to state per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case raises no positive mirror anchor; it argues the same direction. Its repeated insurance divisions are the strongest candidate it mentions, but they do not supply the missing computational result.

The paper’s Theorems 2 and 3 characterize a risk allocation by axioms, and Theorems 4 and 5 give another characterization. Theorem 1 establishes existence using a fixed-point argument. None states an algorithmic problem’s complexity or provides an algorithm whose complexity could be mirrored. The paper’s uses of “continuous” concern risk realizations and rationing rules; they do not make the population continuous.

A stronger population model is plausible: let each type specify a division’s full risk profile, liquidity limit, and capital target, and let rational masses describe how common those types are. A type-level allocation schedule could then be required to satisfy mass-weighted feasibility and fairness conditions. This avoids objections based on identity or lack of multiplicity. But studying how to compute that schedule would add a computational question to the paper; it would not continue a computational question the paper asked. Making it finite-state and finitely encoded would also be a substantive modeling choice, since the paper permits bounded risks on a general probability space.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s stated criterion: there is no named computational result here to mirror, and the best proposed scenario cannot supply one retroactively. The broader claim that *no* worthwhile continuous computational study could grow from this paper is less secure. A replicated-division model could motivate one, but it would be a new extension rather than a continuous mirror of the paper’s results.
