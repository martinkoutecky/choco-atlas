The continuous mirror question

Given rational group masses \(\lambda_j>0\) summing to \(1\), a finite rational cost domain \(K\), and truthful cost masses \(\mu_{j,c}\) summing to \(\lambda_j\), determine the tight worst-case approximation ratio for a computationally efficient truthful, individually rational, budget-feasible mechanism. The mechanism receives reported masses \(\nu_{j,b}\), assigns selection rates \(q_j(b;\nu)\) and expected per-person transfers \(p_j(b;\nu)\), and must satisfy \(\sum_{j,b}\nu_{j,b}p_j(b;\nu)\le B\) for every report measure, interpreted as exact aggregate expenditure over selected mass. For each infinitesimal agent of true cost \(c\), require \(p_j(c;\nu)-c q_j(c;\nu)\ge p_j(b;\nu)-c q_j(b;\nu)\) for every report \(b\), holding the other agents' mass fixed. Compare its truthful representation \(\min_j \sum_c\mu_{j,c}q_j(c;\mu)/\lambda_j\) with \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu)=\max_{0\le y_{j,c}\le\mu_{j,c}}\min_j\sum_c y_{j,c}/\lambda_j\), subject to \(\sum_{j,c}c y_{j,c}\le B\). In particular, determine whether every such mechanism has a worst-case approximation lower bound of order \(\Omega(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min})\), or whether fractional selection beats it, with running time measured in \(m,\tau,L\).

The model it lives in

A population of mass \(1\) with complete types \((j,c)\), where group \(j\) has mass \(\lambda_j\) and cost class \(c\) has mass \(\mu_{j,c}\). The mechanism maps reported type masses to selection rates and expected transfers, with exact aggregate expenditure at most \(B\); its objective is the minimum selected fraction across groups, compared with the cost-aware fractional benchmark.

The objection that survived

The case leaves implicit whether budget feasibility binds for expected payments or every realized lottery; specify exact aggregate mass allocation and expenditure.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the single-group approximation question behind Theorems 4 and 5, including whether the group-imbalance lower bound persists. It leaves the multiple-group mechanisms and their guarantees in Theorems 8 and 10, and the discrete zero-selection branch, outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the \(\Omega(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min})\) lower bound persist when cost cohorts can be selected fractionally?
  • What is the tight truthful, budget-feasible approximation ratio as a function of group masses and the finite cost domain, and can it be attained in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s single-group setting, where each person belongs to one of \(m\) disjoint groups. A convincing regime is a large survey or recruitment market: millions of potential participants fall into a few demographic strata, and people in the same stratum and cost class are interchangeable for selection. Their private cost might be the compensation they require for their time or privacy. Let the total population have mass \(1\), with group \(j\) having mass \(\lambda_j>0\). A complete type is \((j,c)\): group and true cost. The number \(N\) of people grows while the number \(\tau\) of positive-mass group-cost types stays moderate.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Single-Group Budget-Feasible Proportional Procurement. An instance specifies rational group masses \((\lambda_j)\), a finite rational cost domain \(K\), and budget \(B\). At execution, the mechanism receives a measure of cost reports in each group; it does not know the true cost distribution. It chooses, for each reported cost \(b\), a selection rate \(q_j(b)\) and an expected per-person transfer \(p_j(b)\). The selected mass at that report is the reported mass times \(q_j(b)\), and total transfers must be at most \(B\) for every report measure.

Require dominant-strategy truthfulness for an infinitesimal individual, individual rationality, and budget feasibility. In particular, holding the other agents’ mass fixed, a person of cost \(c\) must prefer reporting \(c\): \(p_j(c)-c q_j(c)\ge p_j(b)-c q_j(b)\) for every report \(b\). At truthful costs \(\mu_{j,c}\), the achieved representation is
\[ \mathrm{ALG}(\mu)=\min_j \frac{\sum_c \mu_{j,c}q_j(c)}{\lambda_j}. \]
The natural benchmark is the maximum representation obtainable with costs known, allowing selection of fractional mass:
\[ \mathrm{OPT}(\mu)= \max_{0\le y_{j,c}\le \mu_{j,c}} \min_j \frac{\sum_c y_{j,c}}{\lambda_j} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{j,c} c\,y_{j,c}\le B. \]
A \(\rho\)-approximation has \(\mathrm{ALG}(\mu)\ge \mathrm{OPT}(\mu)/\rho\) for every truthful cost distribution. A solution to the mechanism-design problem is an explicit, computationally efficient rule taking reported mass measures to selection rates and transfers, with proofs of its incentive, budget, and approximation guarantees. Its running time should be measured in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the binary input length.

Theorem 4, proved in this paper, is my lead anchor. It says that BPSG achieves a \((3+\alpha)\)-approximation when it selects at least one agent from every group, where \(\alpha=n_{\max}/n_{\min}\) and the achieved minimum selection ratio is at least \(1/n_{\max}\). It also says that if BPSG cannot select even one agent from each group, so its achieved ratio is zero, no budget-feasible proportion-representative mechanism can achieve any positive ratio on that instance. Its continuous question is whether a mass-based, threshold-payment analogue achieves a factor bounded by \(3+\alpha\), with \(\alpha=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\), and what factor is optimal. I expect this to be Class A: with finitely many cost classes, candidate thresholds are finite, and the required mass for each group and its cost can be computed from sorted cost classes and piecewise-linear optimization. The paper’s common selection-ratio objective and private-cost procurement remain intact.

The zero-result branch of Theorem 4 needs a careful continuous translation. If costs are bounded and \(B>0\), the planner can generally buy a sufficiently small positive mass from every group. Thus the “cannot select one agent” obstruction is an indivisibility effect; its counterpart is a degenerate case such as \(B=0\), not a substantive positive-budget lower bound. The paper’s positive-ratio approximation guarantee is the stronger anchor for this mirror.

Theorem 5, also proved in the paper, supplies a useful second anchor: no budget-feasible proportion-representative mechanism achieves an approximation better than \(\Omega(\alpha)\), with \(\alpha=n_{\max}/n_{\min}\); the lower bound also applies to the multiple-group setting. Its continuous question is whether every mechanism in the defined model has worst-case approximation factor \(\Omega(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min})\) as group masses become imbalanced, even with unlimited computation. A solution is either a lower-bound proof for mass distributions or a mechanism that beats this dependence. I expect a Class B-style transfer of the approximation obstruction: \(\alpha\) measures relative group masses, not population size, and budget-feasible truthful procurement is still the operative problem. This is an approximation lower bound, not an NP-hardness claim.

The weakest point is whether the exact \(\Omega(\alpha)\) lower bound survives fractional selection. The discrete proof might depend on one-person granularity, and partial selection of a cost cohort could remove that construction. The same issue may improve Theorem 4’s factor: ceilings and the requirement to buy a whole person disappear. I would ask the prover to determine those effects directly rather than promise that either numerical bound carries over unchanged.

This remains the authors’ problem: the planner buys participation from private-cost agents under a hard budget while seeking proportional representation and truthful behavior. The continuum changes the population unit to mass; it does not replace their objective with an outcome-space or axiomatic question. The main scope claim is deliberately narrow: a strong single-group mirror for Theorems 4 and 5, with multiple-group models left as follow-on work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper’s guarantees are built around buying indivisible people. In a continuous population, the natural repair is to buy fractions of cost groups. That makes a new procurement problem, but it removes much of what Theorem 4 actually proves: its candidate ratios come from integer counts, and its zero branch comes from being unable to buy one person from a group. With positive budget and bounded costs, arbitrarily small positive mass can usually be bought from every group, so that branch has no substantive counterpart.

The better version of this mirror would ask for the best approximation achievable by a truthful, budget-feasible mechanism that selects fractional mass, with \(\alpha=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\). That is coherent, but the paper’s \((3+\alpha)\) analysis does not carry over: ceilings and next-agent thresholds disappear, and the proponent has not specified whether budget feasibility is required for realized selections or only in expectation. Still, those are specification and proof obligations, not reasons the repaired question is unworthy. The authors could recognize it as their problem.

Theorem 5 is harder to dismiss. Its \(\Omega(\alpha)\) bound concerns group imbalance, which remains meaningful for masses; it need not depend on one-person granularity. Fractional selection might defeat the construction, but finding that out is precisely a legitimate continuous question. The nearby large-market and divisible-item mechanism literature makes this less isolated, but does not, on the information here, already answer the group-proportional procurement problem.

There is a narrower concern that cost types may be numerous in real markets, leaving little computational benefit when \(\tau\) is large. But discrete cost classes and demographic groups give a plausible moderate-\(\tau\) regime, and the programme explicitly treats that as an instance question, not a universal objection. I therefore cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case: the positive-mass version of Theorem 5, and the repaired mechanism-design question behind Theorem 4, survive my objections. The case against is weak; at most, the proposed \((3+\alpha)\) mirror should not be accepted without a precise implementation model and a new analysis.
