What the mirror covers

The proposed question covers existence of state-revealing competitive equilibria over trader types. It leaves the paper’s experimental findings about repeated-trading dynamics, bidding behavior, market institutions, and observed efficiency unexplained.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a narrow one: a large market with many traders but a small catalogue of standardized information, payoff, and endowment profiles. Let a trader’s type include all the features the market model uses, including their private clue. Then \(\mu_t\) is the share of traders of type \(t\), and supplies are measured per capita. A plausible regime would have thousands of traders but only a few dozen such types. That extends the paper’s induced type structure; its actual experiments had only 8 or 12 traders, so they are not themselves a high-multiplicity regime.

One candidate problem is Fully Revealing Continuum Security Equilibrium. Fix the paper’s three states \(\Omega=\{x,y,z\}\). An instance gives a finite set of securities \(A\), rational conditional type masses \(\mu_t^\omega\) for each state \(\omega\), and each type’s dividend \(d_{ta}(\omega)\), endowment, information, and trading constraints. Given the per-capita supply of each security, ask whether there are state-contingent prices \(p^\omega\) and final holdings such that markets clear, holdings maximize aggregate dividends in each state, the prices support those allocations under the paper’s risk-neutral competitive benchmark, and \(p^\omega\) differs across states. A solution is those prices and type-level holdings, or a proof none exist. Price separation makes the information-aggregation condition explicit; the allocation objective captures the paper’s efficiency criterion.

For the fixed three-state version, I would expect this feasibility question to be tractable: the efficient allocation and supporting prices can be expressed using a linear program for each state, with only a polynomial number of price-separation cases to check. That is a candidate Class A question, not a result of this paper. It leads to natural extensions: what changes with many states, when do complete contingent claims make a revealing equilibrium possible where a single security does not, and do actual market dynamics reach such an equilibrium?

The closest numbered finding is Conclusion 7, reported from the paper’s experiments: information aggregation occurred in all markets where the authors could measure it except one, and aggregation improved with repeated periods. Their reported index was positive in all but four of the 52 measurable periods; 20 of 32 repeated-state changes moved toward full aggregation. This gives the candidate problem empirical motivation, but it is not a computational result.

In fact, the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Its numbered conclusions report experimental findings, not that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. So there is no valid anchor under your rules, and the equilibrium question above is an unanchored mirror of the paper’s rational-expectations model, not a compliant per-anchor deliverable.

That is also the mirror’s weakest point: the proposed question computes a static revealing equilibrium, while the paper’s central contribution is experimental evidence about whether different market institutions actually aggregate information over time. A static feasibility problem leaves that dynamic question out.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is the anchor audit: the paper states no computational result. Its numbered conclusions report experimental comparisons and observations. Conclusion 7 says that aggregation appeared in nearly all measurable markets and tended to improve with repetition; it gives no algorithmic problem, complexity claim, or method for computing an outcome. Replacing the finite traders with type masses and recomputing an aggregation index would be a population-scale version of the measurement, not a computational result to mirror. The proponent acknowledges this, so that anchor does not survive.

The proposed equilibrium problem is the best attempt to rescue a mirror. A large market with repeated payoff and information profiles is a plausible high-multiplicity regime; the paper’s eight- and twelve-person experiments do not rule that out. But the proposed test assigns state-indexed prices and efficient holdings, then asks whether those prices support those allocations. That captures a static benchmark. The paper’s central finding concerns how information travels through bids and offers over repeated trading, and why different market institutions produce different outcomes. A state-by-state allocation calculation does not model that process or establish that prices reveal the state.

A closer mirror would ask whether a continuum of traders in a double auction converges to a fully revealing equilibrium, and how the answer changes with the securities and trader types. That is a coherent question. Its central difficulty, though, is the market’s dynamics and behavior. Studying those would require a dynamic economic model; turning them into a computational-complexity question would require supplying a new computational task that the paper itself does not pose. A static allocation or equilibrium-support calculation, meanwhile, has no obvious social-choice manipulation or control problem for the programme’s algorithmic toolbox to address.

So I would reject this as an anchored ChoCo mirror: the one result offered is empirical, and the proposed computational problem does not capture the paper’s distinctive contribution. The negative case is weaker as a universal claim. A continuous model of a large, standardized market could be sensible, and a complexity theory of revealing equilibria might be worthwhile as a separate project. The paper alone, however, gives little reason to treat that project as a computational mirror of its results.
