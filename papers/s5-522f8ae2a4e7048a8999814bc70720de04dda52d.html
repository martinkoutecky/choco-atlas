The continuous mirror question

Given goods \(\Omega\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over explicitly encoded multi-peak submodular valuation types \(v_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and fixed rational prices \(p_j\), decide whether there are assignments \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_{S\subseteq\Omega}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le q_j\) for every good \(j\), and \(x_{t,S}>0\) only when \(S\in\arg\max_{R\subseteq\Omega}(v_t(R)-p(R))\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete multi-peak valuation types. Each type's mass can be assigned across integral bundles demanded at the fixed price vector \(p\); aggregate use of each good is bounded by its per-capita supply \(q_j\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers fixed-price envy-free feasibility for the multi-peak valuation family through aggregate mass assignment; it leaves ascending procedures, minimal-price discovery, and other submodular valuation classes open.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can exact tie-breaking among demanded bundles be implemented with polynomial bit complexity, yielding a polynomial-time algorithm for the configuration LP?
  • Can the mass assignment be rounded for finite markets, or extended to an ascending procedure that finds a coordinatewise-minimal envy-free price?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a continuous type-mass version of envy-free price certification.

A natural regime is bulk procurement by a large network of operating sites. Sites choose bundles of standardized inputs, and millions may share one of a modest number of complete valuation profiles. A type is the site’s entire valuation function, including any budget or other parameter the model uses; \(\mu_t\) is its share of the network. As the network grows, inventories scale with it, so \(q_j\) is the supply of input \(j\) per site. Each site still receives an integral bundle. The continuum lets the aggregate mass of a type split among different bundles when those bundles are equally desirable.

My single anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that, assuming \(\mathrm{NPC}\cap\mathrm{coNP}=\varnothing\), there is no polynomial-size characterization certifying that a given price vector is not envy-free when all valuations are submodular. The paper derives this using earlier hardness results: allocation is NP-hard for budget-additive valuations, and the multi-peak submodular construction also has an NP-hard allocation problem. The paper then gives Corollary 1: under the same condition, no ascending auction can guarantee even a minimal envy-free price for all submodular valuations. Theorem 1 is a conditional witness-size impossibility result; it is not itself a standard claim that computing minimal prices is NP-hard.

The lead mirror is Continuous Mass Envy-Free Price Feasibility. Its input is a finite set of goods \(\Omega\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), a rational price vector \(p\), and explicitly encoded multi-peak submodular valuations \(v_t\). Let
\[ D_t(p)=\arg\max_{S\subseteq\Omega}\left(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\right). \]
The question is whether there is a mass assignment \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq\Omega}x_{t,S}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le q_j,\qquad x_{t,S}>0\Longrightarrow S\in D_t(p). \]
A yes-solution is a sparse rational list of type, bundle, and assigned mass; a no-solution can be a separating certificate for the configuration LP. For example, such a certificate can give \(\alpha_t\) and \(\beta_j\ge0\) with
\[ \alpha_t\le\sum_{j\in S}\beta_j\quad(S\in D_t(p)),\qquad \sum_t\mu_t\alpha_t>\sum_j q_j\beta_j. \]
The decision variable is the aggregate assignment; the price is fixed in the instance.

I expect this restricted mirror to be Class A. The paper’s Theorem 2 supplies an important algorithmic handle: for its explicitly represented multi-peak submodular valuations, a demanded bundle can be computed in polynomial time. The mass-feasibility problem is a configuration LP with exponentially many bundle variables. Its dual constraints can be separated by finding, among a type’s demanded bundles, one minimizing a given linear weight; a sufficiently small rational price perturbation makes the demand routine perform that tie-break. This gives a route to polynomial-time feasibility and compact certificates in \(m,\tau\), the number of peaks, and the input bit length. That algorithmic consequence is for the stated multi-peak encoding; I would not extend it to every succinct submodular valuation class.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s price-certification problem for a large cohort market, including the multi-peak valuation family used in the paper’s hardness discussion. It does not establish an ascending procedure that discovers a coordinatewise-minimal price. That is a natural next question, as are rounding guarantees for finite markets and whether other valuation classes have tractable separation.

The weak point is also the source of the expected gain: the mirror allows a type’s mass to split among its demanded integral bundles. That can remove the indivisibility obstruction behind the paper’s result. If the authors or a referee require every member of a type to receive the same bundle, the configuration LP no longer describes their problem and the hardness may return. I think the split is credible for a large cohort: identical sites can make different choices among bundles they value equally, and their aggregate allocation is exactly what the type mass records.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the proposed LP does not preserve the paper’s result. Theorem 1 rules out polynomial-size certificates for recognizing a non-envy-free price in the paper’s bidder-level model; Corollary 1 uses that obstruction to rule out ascending auctions. The paper explicitly frames the issue around what an auctioneer can learn and certify from current demands. The proposed problem instead gives an offline solver explicit type valuations, adds per-capita supplies, and allows a type’s mass to split across equally demanded bundles. Its Farkas certificate answers whether that aggregate allocation LP is feasible. The demand-oracle tie-break may make this a sound and tractable continuous problem, but it does not capture the paper’s impossibility about ascending-auction certificates.

A closer mirror would ask whether an ascending-price process can find a minimal envy-free price for a large population of types. That raises a supply-scaling problem. If the paper’s fixed stock of \(m\) goods is held constant as the population grows to \(N\), per-capita supply becomes \(1/N\) per good. In the limit, goods disappear from the population-level allocation, and the price question shifts toward making the empty bundle demanded. Keeping scarcity meaningful requires scaling the inventory with the population. That is a plausible market model, but it changes the finite-goods auction whose witness-size obstruction Theorem 1 establishes.

This objection does not dispose of the better version. A market with replicated goods and large cohorts of identical buyers is plausible, and the paper’s demand algorithm gives a credible route to solving its fixed-price feasibility problem for multi-peak valuations. The authors might recognize the resulting continuous market as their problem. The strongest negative claim I can support is therefore that the proponent has not shown their LP to be a mirror of Theorem 1 or Corollary 1. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario: the cohort market is sensible, and its tractability could itself be a useful result.
