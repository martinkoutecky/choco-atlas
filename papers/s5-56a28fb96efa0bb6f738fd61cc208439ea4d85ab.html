The continuous mirror question

Given \(C\), \(k\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval types \(A\subseteq C\) satisfying \(|C\setminus A|\ge k\), compute a mass-report plan \(\rho_{A,B}\) and quota-removal schedule such that every sufficiently large denominator-clearing lift with \(k\mid n\) is a costly-voting equilibrium of GreedyMonroe and every supported committee satisfies mass-PJR.

The model it lives in

Types are approval sets \(A\subseteq C\), with mass \(\mu_A\); \(\rho_{A,B}\) is the mass of type \(A\) reporting \(B\), including abstention \(B=\varnothing\). GreedyMonroe removes quota mass \(1/k\) for each first-stage winner. The computational objective is to output a feasible witness whose finite lifts satisfy costly equilibrium and whose committees satisfy mass-PJR: every coalition vector \(x\) of mass at least \(\ell/k\) with at least \(\ell\) common approved candidates receives at least \(\ell\) approved committee members.

The objection that survived

The finite-lift notion is not literal nonatomic individual Nash equilibrium: a zero-mass individual cannot cross a quota, so a strict atomless interpretation would eliminate the pivotality used in the proof.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the constructive GreedyMonroe costly-equilibrium/PJR result; it leaves the strong-equilibrium implication, approval-voting failure, non-strategyproofness, tightness example, and full-implementation impossibility without computational mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite-lift witness be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length?
  • Can a literal nonatomic equilibrium with positive-measure coalition deviations preserve the PJR guarantee?
  • What is the complexity of verifying strong-costly equilibrium for a mass-report plan?

The case FOR (proponent)

On the strict ChoCo gate, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor: none of its named results proves NP-hardness, polynomial-time solvability, FPT, approximation, or a computational formulation. Its references to computational work are literature summaries. So the strongest honest positive case is substantive rather than formally compliant.

The best mirror is the GreedyMonroe equilibrium result. The voter type is a complete approval set \(A\subseteq C\), which determines utility \(u_A(W)=|W\cap A|\), the upward-lexicographic lottery preference, and the abstention behaviour. A society is a rational mass vector \(\mu=(\mu_A)\) over finitely many approval types, with \(\tau\ll n\) in the intended regime. A natural scenario is a large party-list electorate: many voters share the same approved parties, each party has at least \(k\) candidates, and individual names are irrelevant.

The committee remains integral: \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|=k\). Only the population is continuous. A report plan is \(\rho_{A,B}\), the mass of true type \(A\) submitting approval ballot \(B\), including \(B=\emptyset\) for abstention. GreedyMonroe uses quota \(1/k\): a candidate with reported support at least \(1/k\) is selected, exactly \(1/k\) supporter mass is removed, and the process continues until either \(k\) candidates are selected or the remaining seats are filled randomly.

The continuous PJR condition is the direct mass analogue of the paper’s definition: every \(W\) in the support of the outcome lottery must satisfy, for every \(\ell\le k\) and every \(x\) with \(0\le x_A\le\mu_A\), \(\sum_Ax_A\ge\ell/k\), and \(\left|\bigcap_{A:x_A>0}A\right|\ge\ell\), that \(\left|W\cap\bigcup_{A:x_A>0}A\right|\ge\ell\).

My lead anchor would be the unnumbered theorem in §4, internally labelled “theorem: costly voting PJR under greedyMonroe,” proved in this paper:

Let the voting rule be GreedyMonroe, and assume \(k\) divides \(n\). If every voter disapproves of at least \(k\) candidates, then there exists a costly voting equilibrium supporting a PJR outcome. Furthermore, this costly voting equilibrium coincides with a sincere voting outcome under GreedyMonroe.

The supplied manuscript prints the inclusion \(A_i\subseteq\hat A_i\), but the construction and proof use the reverse inclusion \(\hat A_i\subseteq A_i\). That apparent typo must be repaired before relying on the statement.

The corresponding problem is:

High-Multiplicity GreedyMonroe-PJR Equilibrium. Given \(C\), \(k\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval types satisfying \(|C\setminus A|\ge k\) for every positive-mass type, output a mass-report plan \(\rho\), a quota-removal plan, and the induced lottery \(L\), such that every denominator-clearing finite realization of \(\rho\) is a costly voting equilibrium and every committee in the support of \(L\) satisfies \(\mathrm{PJR}_\mu\).

This is a genuine high-multiplicity version of the paper’s question. The paper’s construction scales: run the sincere GreedyMonroe process, assign exactly \(1/k\) mass supporting each first-stage winner to that winner’s singleton report, and let the remaining mass abstain. The resulting mass plan can be lifted to arbitrarily large finite electorates after clearing denominators. I would expect this version to be Class A: the quota process and mass allocation are computable from the explicit type list, probably in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length. That is an extrapolation, not a complexity theorem proved in the paper.

The paper’s cited Proposition [SFF+17a] is supporting background rather than an anchor: if \(k\) divides \(n\), sincere GreedyMonroe satisfies PJR. It is cited, not proved here.

A useful second anchor is the unnumbered theorem immediately after the definition of strong costly voting equilibrium, also proved here:

Let the voting rule be GreedyMonroe, and assume \(k\) divides \(n\). Every strong costly voting equilibrium satisfies PJR.

Its continuous counterpart is:

Continuous Strong-Costly-PJR. Given \((C,k,\mu)\) and a mass-report plan \(\rho\), determine whether \(\rho\) is a strong costly equilibrium, where a deviation may move any positive-measure coalition of types to new reports, and, if so, output the induced PJR lottery.

The theorem’s proof transfers naturally. If a committee violates \(\mathrm{PJR}_\mu\), there is a coalition of mass at least \(\ell/k\) whose types share at least \(\ell\) approved candidates but receive fewer than \(\ell\) approved representatives. The coalition can jointly report its common intersection. GreedyMonroe must then elect at least \(\ell\) candidates from that intersection, improving every member’s worst possible outcome. Thus any valid continuous strong-costly equilibrium automatically satisfies PJR.

I would expect the search problem here to be harder or even existence-sensitive for continuum-specific reasons: coalition deviations are measure-valued, equilibrium need not exist, and checking all profitable mass deviations may require a nontrivial separation argument. Further questions include existence, best-response dynamics, and whether the implication survives randomized mechanisms.

The central weakness is the equilibrium limit. In a genuinely atomless population, an individual voter has zero mass and cannot change the aggregate report, so ordinary Nash incentives disappear. The first problem therefore uses a high-multiplicity finite-lift interpretation; the second explicitly uses positive-mass coalitions. A referee could reasonably say that this is a co-modification of the equilibrium concept rather than a literal atomless continuation.

So the honest verdict is: the paper supports a plausible, concrete continuous-population research programme around GreedyMonroe and PJR, especially in large party-list electorates. But because it contains no named computational result, it does not pass ChoCo’s strict computational-anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the level of the programme’s gate: this paper contains no named computational result. Its theorems concern existence of equilibria, strategic robustness, PJR, and implementation impossibility. None asserts NP-hardness, polynomial-time solvability, approximation, parameterized complexity, or even a computational input-output problem. The computational literature appears only in the related-work discussion. Thus there is no result here whose continuous-population complexity can be charted.

The proposed GreedyMonroe mirror is substantively plausible as a high-multiplicity model. A large party-list electorate gives a perfectly sensible type space, and the typo in the theorem statement can be repaired. But it does not preserve the central object of the theorem. The costly-equilibrium proof relies on individual voters being pivotal: one voter’s report can move a candidate across the finite quota \(n/k\). In an atomless population, one individual has mass zero and cannot change the aggregate report plan or induced lottery. Since voting carries a positive cost, abstention weakly dominates every non-pivotal report, so the constructed quota-filling report plan is not a costly Nash equilibrium.

There are only three ways to repair this, and each changes the object materially. One can make each type an atomic bloc, but then the game has \(\tau\) strategic players rather than a continuous population. One can retain atomless individuals, in which case the strategic result collapses to abstention. Or one can allow positive-measure coalitions to coordinate, which is precisely a different equilibrium concept—the second proposed anchor, not a continuation of the first theorem. Clearing denominators does not resolve this: it merely produces a family of finite instances to which the original proof already applies. It does not create a new continuous computational problem.

The best version of the first mirror would therefore ask for a mass-report plan whose finite lifts are costly equilibria. But the paper already supplies the plan constructively: run the sincere GreedyMonroe process, assign mass \(1/k\) to singleton reports supporting each first-stage winner, and let the remainder abstain. This is a normalized witness to a finite existence proof, not an optimization or complexity result. Asking for its computation would be a newly designed problem, rather than a computational mirror of a named result in the paper.

The strong-costly-equilibrium anchor does not escape this difficulty. Its proposed continuous version replaces coalitions \(S\subseteq N\) by measurable positive-mass coalitions, and therefore requires a new game: whether a coalition may split a type, how participation costs scale with mass, and whether strict improvement is required almost everywhere or only in aggregate. Those choices are not technical translations; they determine the equilibrium notion.

Even after choosing the natural measure-theoretic version, the paper’s proof transfers almost verbatim. If a mass group of at least \(\ell/k\) shares \(\ell\) approved candidates but receives fewer than \(\ell\) representatives, it can report their common intersection and force at least \(\ell\) of them into the committee. Hence every valid strong equilibrium satisfies the mass version of PJR. That is a qualitative implication, not a computational result. “Verify whether a proposed mass plan is strong” would be a new equilibrium-verification problem with a newly specified encoding and deviation model; it is not what the theorem proves.

The remaining results are no better anchors: sincere GreedyMonroe’s PJR property is axiomatic, non-strategyproofness is a finite counterexample, the approval-voting failure is an equilibrium nonexistence example, and the full-implementation theorem is an impossibility result about deterministic mechanisms. They may motivate a new continuous game-theoretic paper, but they do not supply the computational question ChoCo is meant to continuize.

So the honest negative verdict is strong under ChoCo’s stated standard. A continuous-population treatment of this paper is imaginable, especially for party-list electorates, but only by either trivial normalization or by changing the strategic model from individual Nash equilibrium to bloc or coalition equilibrium. The resulting work could be worthwhile game theory; it is not a continuous computational mirror of this paper, and the paper itself offers no qualifying computational anchor to mirror.
