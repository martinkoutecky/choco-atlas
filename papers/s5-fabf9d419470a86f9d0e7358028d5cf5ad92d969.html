What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns only the impossibility theorem; the paper’s discussion of fairness, efficiency, and strategyproofness also contains no computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is only a possible high-multiplicity *setting*: a large population whose members share a small number of complete weak preference orders over the alternatives. A type is one such order, and its mass is the fraction of the population holding it. That regime is plausible for large consultations or repeated preference surveys.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Its sole main result is the unnumbered Theorem in Section 4, proved in the paper: when \(m,n\ge4\), no anonymous, neutral, SD-efficient, SD-strategyproof social decision scheme extends random dictatorship to the full domain of weak preferences. This is an axiomatic impossibility theorem, not a result about computational complexity or algorithms.

The nearest continuous analogue would ask whether a rule from population distributions over weak-order types to lotteries can satisfy corresponding fairness, efficiency, and incentive conditions while extending random dictatorship on strict preferences. That is a precise direction for axiomatic work, but it is outside ChoCo’s stated computational scope. And in an atomless population, an individual’s report does not change the distribution, so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous; making a positive mass of agents report together instead changes the incentive notion. I therefore would not present this as a credible continuous computational mirror of the theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only result to anchor is the paper’s impossibility theorem: no anonymous, neutral, SD-efficient, SD-strategyproof extension of random dictatorship exists on the full weak-preference domain for \(m,n\ge4\). It is an axiomatic existence claim. The paper gives no input problem, algorithm, or complexity bound for a continuous version to mirror.

The strongest population model would take complete weak preference orders as types and define a rule \(F(\mu)\) from their mass distribution to a lottery. That makes the society a sensible high-multiplicity object. But an individual’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged in an atomless model, so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous. Requiring a positive mass of agents to misreport instead asks for a coalitional incentive axiom. Keeping finite \(N\) and deviations of mass \(1/N\) preserves individual incentives, but leaves an axiomatic mechanism-design question; it does not supply a computational problem from the paper.

One could add a computational task—such as finding a violation for a given encoded rule, or synthesizing a rule from a restricted representation. Each needs choices about the rule’s encoding and the allowed mechanism class that the paper never makes. The theorem itself is not a named computational result whose continuous counterpart could be classified by the programme. So the strongest case against is that even the best high-multiplicity scenario leads to a different, axiomatic research project. That does not rule out worthwhile continuous mechanism design in general; it rules this paper out as a natural ChoCo computational mirror.
