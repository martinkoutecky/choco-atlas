The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive reading is that this paper could motivate a follow-on study, but it supplies no qualifying computational anchor for a continuous mirror. The closest is Theorem 2.4, proved here: under the Bayes-law and finite-information assumptions, on a connected finite graph, agents’ sampling-model posteriors converge to a common limit, and that limit equals the Bayes posterior given all agents’ private signals, almost surely. This is a learning theorem; it gives no complexity classification or algorithmic running-time result.

The other named main results have the same character. Theorem 2.1 proves convergence and agreement of posteriors on a connected finite graph. Theorem 2.9 proves that, in the Bernoulli sampling model, posterior means converge and agree at the optimal Bayes posterior mean. Proposition 2.10 proves that in the specified two-agent misspecified model, posteriors converge to a common random point mass that differs almost surely from the optimal posterior mean. These are substantial analytic results, but none says a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

There is a plausible population setting nearby: many copies of an LLM deployment, grouped by signal law, updating rule, and network role, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction in each type. A continuum version would also need to specify how types interact—perhaps with a type-interaction kernel—and could ask for the limiting posterior by type. But the paper fixes a finite graph and sends time to infinity; a population mirror sends the number of agents to a limit. Turning the former into a computational question about the latter would require new modeling choices and new results. The paper does not provide an anchor for that problem, and its endpoint is an asymptotic Bayes posterior, not an optimization objective.

So my positive case is thematic: many agents, probabilistic communication, agreement, and learning make the paper relevant to a broader population-level research programme. I would not present that as an anchored ChoCo mirror. The weakest point is decisive for this task: the paper contains no named computational result to mirror, so there is no defensible lead problem per the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is the missing computational result. Theorem 2.4, the closest candidate, proves that agents’ posteriors converge to the Bayes posterior given all private signals. Theorem 2.1 proves agreement; Theorem 2.9 gives the corresponding result for posterior means; Proposition 2.10 proves convergence to a misspecified point mass. These are substantive learning results, but none classifies a computational problem or gives an algorithm with a running-time guarantee. That leaves no result of the kind ChoCo is set up to continuize.

The best population version of Theorem 2.4 would put many similar agents on a connected network and ask whether a continuum of them learns the aggregate Bayes posterior. But the theorem’s inputs include each agent’s realized private signal and position in the network. With continuous signals, those realized signals will usually all be distinct; treating signal laws as types instead loses information the posterior calculation uses. Tracking network roles and the message histories that differentiate otherwise similar agents requires additional structure, such as an interaction kernel and a distribution over evolving beliefs. That could define an interesting mean-field learning model, but it changes the object into an analytic population-limit question. It does not produce a computational mirror of Theorem 2.4.

Theorem 2.1 and Theorem 2.9 have the same problem in sharper form: their conclusions depend on the connected graph and on individual agents repeatedly observing particular neighbors. A distribution over types alone does not encode those paths. Adding a graph limit might preserve some interaction structure, but then the research question becomes whether agreement holds in that new continuum dynamics. The paper’s Bernoulli variant remains a convergence result, not a computational one.

Proposition 2.10 is a weaker anchor still. It studies exactly two agents in a specified misspecified process. Replicating that pair across a population gives independent copies of the same urn process; connecting the copies changes the process and no longer mirrors the proposition. Either way, there is no computational classification to carry over.

One could propose new algorithmic questions about computing finite-time posteriors or convergence rates in a graphon model. The paper itself identifies convergence rates as open. Those questions may be worthwhile in a broader programme on computation in social learning, but they require a new model and a new computational objective. The paper has no collective-choice outcome, manipulation problem, or named complexity result to anchor them. I cannot rule out every future model someone might invent; the honest negative case is narrower: none of the proposed results supports a worthwhile, anchored ChoCo mirror.
