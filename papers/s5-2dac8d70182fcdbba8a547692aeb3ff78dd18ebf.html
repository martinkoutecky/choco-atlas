The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper. Its numbered statements are Definitions 3.1–5.4; none asserts a complexity or algorithmic classification. Section 4 formulates the sequential problem as an MDP and describes an A2C method, while Section 5 reports empirical comparisons. Those are not the numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition anchors requested. I would not promote a definition or an experimental result into one.

The strongest positive case I can make is therefore an unanchored candidate mirror. In a large crowdsourcing platform, let a type describe workers who share a full response profile: effort costs, task skills, and how they respond to payments, observations, and collusion. Let \(\mu_\theta\) be each type’s population share. A continuous version of sequential EIWV would take as input the type masses, task mix, finite worker-state and response models, payment choices, inference rule, oracle signal and surcharge, and horizon. Each round, a policy chooses payments by type and whether, or what fraction of task mass, to send to the costly oracle. It observes aggregate reports and oracle signals, then seeks to maximize expected cumulative platform utility—estimated accuracy minus weighted worker and oracle spending. A solution is such a policy; a decision version asks whether one can achieve utility at least a given threshold.

The regime is plausible for a platform handling many repeated tasks with workers drawn from a modest number of stable cohorts. The paper itself points toward this setting: it studies heterogeneous workers, private effort costs, repeated interaction, and collusion, and evaluates crowds of up to 284 workers. That makes a type-mass model recognizable as a population version of the authors’ incentive-design problem, though their experiments do not establish that such a regime has few types.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s policy pays workers individually, and arbitrary worker histories or collusion patterns may require many distinct types. Aggregating workers could then remove behavior the method is designed to handle. So this is a credible direction to investigate, but the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror, and I cannot honestly assign this candidate a tractability or hardness-transfer verdict on the basis of its results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Its numbered statements are definitions; the A2C section proposes a learning method, and the experiments report its performance. There is no complexity theorem, algorithmic guarantee, or formal policy-optimization result. The proponent is right to leave the anchor blank. The utility-threshold decision problem they add would be a new question, not a restatement of a result in this paper.

The proposed type-mass model also needs strong assumptions to preserve the paper’s problem. The platform tracks estimated accuracy and chooses payments per worker. Workers may differ in costs, skills, task histories, and responses to earlier payments; inference uses which workers answered which tasks. Aggregating workers by a coarse label such as skill or cost can lose information relevant to both inference and future rewards. An exact type would need to capture those details, along with history-dependent behavior and potentially collusion relationships. The resulting support could grow with the roster and horizon, leaving little high-multiplicity simplification.

A stronger repair is possible: posit exchangeable cohorts with a fixed task mix, shared costs and skills within each cohort, and a finite response model. Then a distribution over cohort states and a policy optimizing expected utility could be a coherent continuous-population EIWV problem. But the paper does not specify that transition model or analyze policy computation. Supplying a known finite model changes the target to policy optimization in a stochastic game; leaving the dynamics unknown requires a separately defined learning objective, such as a sample or regret guarantee. Either could be worthwhile research, but neither mirrors a computational claim this paper makes.

So the negative case is strong for this paper’s results: there is no qualifying anchor, and the proposed mirror needs substantial new modeling. The universal claim is weaker. A cohort-based continuous EIWV problem is plausible and might interest the authors; the paper alone gives little reason to say no scenario could be worthwhile.
