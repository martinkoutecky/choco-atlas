The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many goods, a finite support of complete positive-bid SSPMA types \(t\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and a feasible rational per-capita target \(q\), find an equilibrium price vector \(p\) and distributions \(\lambda_{t,x}\) over each type's integral bundles \(x\). Require \(\sum_x\lambda_{t,x}=1\), \(\lambda_{t,x}>0\) only when \(x\) is demanded by type \(t\) at \(p\), and \(\sum_t\mu_t\sum_x\lambda_{t,x}x=q\).

The model it lives in

Each type retains its complete positive-bid preference description and integral bundle choices; its mass weights the distribution over demanded bundles. Equivalently, scale that type's bid capacities by \(\mu_t\), impose the per-capita supply \(q\), and use the dual prices of the resulting LP.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers positive-bid pricing from Proposition 4 and has mixed-bid counterparts for Theorem 3 and Proposition 5. It leaves the paper's geometric characterization, expressiveness and compactness claims, and experimental comparisons aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For valid mixed-sign SSPMA types with \(n\) goods and \(r\) negative-bid records, is continuous equilibrium pricing fixed-parameter tractable in \(r\), or does it have a matching hardness barrier?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a defensible mirror in equilibrium-price computation. The natural regime is a large liquidity or collateral market with many small participating institutions and a comparatively small set of recurring treasury types. Goods are unitized amounts of funding distinguished by collateral class or maturity. A type is the institution’s complete SSPMA preference description, including its bids and capacities; two institutions share a type only when those descriptions match. For example, a market might have thousands of participants but dozens or hundreds of types. Each participant still receives an integral bundle, while a continuum of participants lets a type’s mass split across demanded bundles, making its average allocation fractional.

Typed continuous SSPMA pricing with positive bids is the cleanest anchor. Its instance is a finite set of goods, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a positive-bid SSPMA bid list \(B_t\) for each type, and a feasible rational per-capita target supply \(q\). It asks for an equilibrium price vector \(p\) and a market-clearing allocation. More precisely, give each type a distribution \(\lambda_{t,x}\) over its integral bundles, with \(\sum_x\lambda_{t,x}=1\), positive weight only on bundles demanded at \(p\), and
\[ \sum_t\mu_t\sum_x\lambda_{t,x}x=q. \]
The paper’s supply convention can include the auctioneer’s bids in the market description.

The anchor is Proposition 4, proved in the paper by complementary slackness. It says that, in an SSPMA with only positive bids, equilibrium prices for target supply \(t\) are the optimal dual variables \(p=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\) of the displayed network linear program, which is solvable in polynomial time in the number of goods and bids:
\[ \max \sum_{b\in B}\sum_i b_i x_{bi} \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_i x_{bi}\le w(b),\qquad \sum_b x_{bi}=t_i,\qquad x_{bi}\ge0. \]
In the continuous mirror, each type bid’s capacity becomes \(\mu_t w(b)\), and the target becomes per-capita \(q\). The same LP and price-dual argument apply; fractional flows can be decomposed into the demanded bundles of a nonatomic population. I expect this question to be Class A, polynomial-time solvable in goods, supported types, bids, and input bit length. This is my strongest anchor for a definite tractability claim.

The closer mirror of the paper’s headline contribution allows positive and negative bids. Call it Typed Continuous SSPMA Pricing. Its instance is the same kind of rational type distribution and target, but each type has a valid mixed-sign SSPMA bid list. Its solution is a price \(p\) and typewise bundle distributions that clear the target with every type’s mass assigned only to bundles demanded at \(p\). The equivalent positive/negative-auction certificate is a supplementary per-capita bundle \(s\) minimizing
\[ V_+(q+s)-V_-(s), \]
where \(V_+\) and \(V_-\) are the mass-weighted values of the positive-bid auction and the auction formed from the absolute weights of negative bids. The price must support \(q+s\) in the positive auction and \(s\) in the negative auction.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in the paper: for a valid set \(B\) of SSPMA bids, Algorithm 2 terminates in a Walrasian equilibrium price and a supplementary bundle. That is a correctness and termination result; by itself it does not establish polynomial time. The continuous input reduces exactly to the paper’s integer-weight setting: clear the rational denominators in the type masses and target, replicate each type accordingly, and combine its repeated bids by scaling their weights. The price is unchanged. This is a plausible mirror of the authors’ problem because the decision remains equilibrium pricing for their full bid language; mass-weighted types are precisely repeated agents with identical preferences.

A useful complexity refinement is the bounded-feature Typed Continuous SSPMA Pricing question: how many iterations suffice when the support has \(n\) goods and \(r\) negative-bid records? Its anchor is Proposition 5, also proved in the paper. It states that Algorithm 2 requires at most \(O\!\left(\binom{n+|B^-|}{n}\right)\) iterations, where \(|B^-|\) is the number of negative bids. After identical bids across types are combined, scaling masses changes their weights but does not increase the number of negative-bid records, so the iteration bound does not grow with the number of named participants or the denominator used to encode masses. This supports tractability when the number of goods or negative-bid records is fixed, but it is not a general polynomial-time bound. My expectation is tractability in those restricted regimes; the unrestricted problem’s class remains unresolved by these results.

The scope is equilibrium pricing and its exact algorithmic complexity in a continuous population. The goods remain finite and the allocation of each unit of mass remains integral; fractional type averages arise because mass can be divided among agents. The mirror does not continuize outcomes in place of population, and it does not cover the paper’s geometric or axiomatic claims about strong substitutes. Further questions include whether the mixed-bid mirror is fixed-parameter tractable in \(r\), whether unrestricted hardness exists, and how the complexity changes if type preferences are represented more succinctly than explicit SSPMA bids.

The weakest point is the regime assumption. The paper’s motivating bank auctions may have too few bidders to make a continuum convincing. The stronger scenario requires a genuinely large market of small participants whose complete bid schedules fall into relatively few exact types. Small differences in preferences create new types, so a realistic market may have large \(\tau\). That is a real limitation, but it is about where the high-multiplicity regime applies; it does not make equilibrium pricing for a distribution over complete SSPMA types a different underlying problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper’s computational input is already an aggregate bid collection. Its equilibrium-price algorithms do not track which named bidder supplied each bid. If several bidders have identical complete bid descriptions, their bids can be coalesced by adding their weights. Thus a typed society seems to add a population interpretation without adding a new computational structure.

That objection reaches Proposition 4 most directly. For rational type masses \(\mu_t\), choose a common denominator \(D\), replace each type’s mass by \(D\mu_t\) copies, and scale the target \(q\) to \(Dq\). The resulting price problem is the paper’s network LP; scaling the whole market leaves its equilibrium prices unchanged. The same LP can use rational capacities directly, so expanding to \(D\) copies is not necessary computationally. A large standardized market might therefore give a convincing story, but the proposed continuous question appears to be the paper’s existing price calculation with normalized quantities.

For Theorem 3, the same reduction works with valid mixed-sign bid collections. Replicating every type’s complete bid list preserves validity, and its equilibrium prices are those of the scaled finite instance. Proposition 5 also carries over: scaling weights does not increase the number of distinct supplementary bundles in its bound. The difficulty is that this bound is combinatorial in the number of goods and negative-bid records, so it does not establish polynomial time when both grow. A continuous version could ask for a sharper bound, but that would be a question about the same algorithm and parameters, rather than a new complexity phenomenon caused by population mass.

The objection has a real limit under ChoCo’s standard. A direct reduction or an unchanged answer does not make a well-posed mirror unworthy; the programme explicitly wants the discrete-to-continuous dictionary, and identifying a tractable continuous price problem could itself matter. Nor can I claim that no suitable population regime exists. Many small institutions using a finite menu of standardized collateral preferences could form a plausible high-multiplicity market, and the paper itself motivates its pricing problem with bidders who are small relative to the market. Allocations can remain integral for each institution while the type’s mass is split among demanded bundles.

So the best negative case is about low incremental yield: all three anchors appear to inherit their mathematics directly from the paper, and the natural market story requires repeated *complete* bid schedules. That makes the mirror look more like a normalization and complexity-parameter restatement than a new optimization problem. But this does not defeat the strongest versions of the anchors. The paper has named computational results, identity is not essential to its price objective, the continuous market need not degenerate, and the supplied material contains no continuous-population treatment that already answers the question. I cannot make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists; the case against is weak, especially against Proposition 4.
