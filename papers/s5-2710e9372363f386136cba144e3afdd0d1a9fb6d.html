The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of complete agent types, a symmetric valuation table \(v:T\times T\to\{0,\ldots,6\}\), and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), decide whether there are nonnegative masses \(z_M\) for multisets \(M\) of three types such that \(\sum_M a_t(M)z_M=\mu_t\) for every \(t\in T\), and no justified envy occurs between any two occupied team compositions. Specifically, for every \(M,R\) with \(z_M,z_R>0\), every slot type \(t\in M\), and every slot type \(u\in R\), it must not be the case that \(\sum_{s\in R\setminus\{u\}}v_t(s)>\sum_{s\in M\setminus\{t\}}v_t(s)\) and \(v_s(t)>v_s(u)\) for both slots \(s\in R\setminus\{u\}\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population with rational masses of complete valuation types, partitioned into whole teams of three. The decision variables \(z_M\) give the mass assigned to each team composition; feasibility requires type-mass balance and no justified envy between occupied compositions.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the existence question in Theorem 4.16 for justified envy-free triple partitions with symmetric valuations in \(\{0,\ldots,6\}\). It leaves the paper’s results for envy-freeness, weakly justified envy-freeness, and other preference restrictions unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of this existence problem as a function of the number of supported types \(\tau\) and the input bit length?
  • Do useful restrictions on the type-interaction graph or valuation table yield polynomial-time or parameterized algorithms?

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead mirror is Continuous Justified-Envy-Free Triple Partition. Its instance is a finite set of complete agent types \(T\), a positive rational mass \(\mu_t\) for each type with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and symmetric valuations \(v_t(u)\in\{0,\ldots,6\}\). A type captures everything the problem uses: an agent’s valuation of every possible type of teammate. This fits, for example, a large organization assigning staff to teams of exactly three, where many employees share one of a relatively small number of role and compatibility profiles.

For each multiset \(M\) of three types, let \(z_M\ge 0\) be the mass of teams with composition \(M\), and let \(a_t(M)\) count how many type-\(t\) slots it contains. The mass balance is \(\sum_M a_t(M)z_M=\mu_t\) for every \(t\); thus the total mass of teams is \(1/3\). Each team still has exactly three members. The continuous decision is how much population has each team composition.

Call the problem C-JEF-3: does there exist such a mass allocation with no justified envy? Precisely, for any two occupied team compositions \(M,R\), any type \(t\) in \(M\), and any type \(u\) in \(R\), a type-\(t\) member of the first team must not both prefer the other team’s two members to their own current teammates and be strictly preferred to \(u\) by *each* of those two members. Utility is the sum of valuations of the other two slots. A solution is the full vector \(z\). This is a population mirror of their underlying team-partition question: it continuizes who gets assigned to each whole team, not the size or divisibility of a team.

The anchor is Theorem 4.16, proved in this paper. It states that deciding whether a given ASHG has a justified-envy-free partition into triples is NP-complete even when preferences are symmetric and every valuation lies between \(0\) and \(6\), inclusive. The paper proves this by reduction from Partition Into Triangles. C-JEF-3 mirrors that same existence question and retains the theorem’s preference restriction; only the population description and allocation variable change.

I think the authors could recognize this as their problem in a high-multiplicity regime. Their motivation already includes assigning people to fixed-size project teams, and their model makes preferences additive. If employees with the same full valuation profile are interchangeable, describing them by cohort masses is a natural way to state the problem for a large workforce. The paper’s discussion of type-based fixed-size coalition models is also a useful neighboring precedent, though it does not establish that this particular JEF question has already been studied in continuous form.

My working forecast for the full C-JEF-3 problem is continuum-specific hardness (Class C): after choosing team compositions that can coexist without justified envy, one still has to find masses satisfying the type balances. The support choice may retain combinatorial difficulty. That is a research bet, not a hardness claim. In particular, Theorem 4.16’s reduction does not automatically transfer: it uses an integral triangle-partition obstruction, while continuous masses can split a type across several team compositions. A proof of hardness for C-JEF-3 would need to address that change directly. Further questions include whether the problem is polynomial for fixed \(\tau=|T|\), whether useful restrictions on the type-interaction graph yield algorithms, and how the continuous and finite high-multiplicity versions correspond as population sizes grow.

The weakest point is the gap between a plausible high-multiplicity *regime* and the paper’s hardness instances. In the reduction, agents encode graph vertices, so the number of distinct profiles may grow with the instance; the theorem itself does not show hardness when many agents share each type. That gap leaves the continuous complexity open, but it does not make the question a different social-choice problem: the objective remains to form triples with the paper’s justified-envy criterion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 4.16 does not establish a high-multiplicity case. Its reduction encodes a graph in agents’ pairwise valuations, so the types may be as numerous as the graph’s vertices. The theorem says nothing about instances with many interchangeable agents of each type. That weakens the proponent’s link to the paper’s hardness result, though it does not make the continuous question unworthy: the type-based team-assignment setting is still plausible.

The proposed mirror survives that objection. In a large organization, employees could share a small number of role and compatibility profiles, with symmetric valuations determined by profile pairs. A distribution over those types and a mass allocation over triples preserve the paper’s objective and justified-envy test. This is a population mirror, not merely fractional outcomes.

The main reason to give this mirror lower priority is that its structure may offer little purchase for the continuous-optimization toolbox. With \(\tau\) types, there are only \(O(\tau^3)\) triple compositions. One can mark which pairs of compositions are incompatible under justified envy, then ask whether a mutually compatible set of compositions can balance the type masses. For a fixed set, the balance question is linear; the selection of that set retains the combinatorial difficulty. That makes this a less direct fit for the programme’s column-generation examples, but it does not undercut it as a computational question.

I can’t honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The paper has a clear computational anchor, and a credible high-multiplicity scenario for it. The negative case is weak: it can question how much the paper’s particular hardness result supports the mirror, and how much continuous-optimization machinery the mirror is likely to invite, but neither point defeats the better version of the proposal.
