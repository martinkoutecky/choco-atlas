What the mirror covers

The proposal sketches a population version of Theorem 1’s chain allocation and mentions Theorems 2 and 3, but supplies no separate prover-ready mirrors for them; the remaining characterization and representation results are left alone.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest population regime is a large municipal housing waitlist. An applicant’s type is her complete priority-relevant category profile, together with the evidence menu that profile permits. Applicants with the same profile and menu are interchangeable. A city may have hundreds of thousands of applicants but only a modest number \(\tau\) of distinct types. Let \(\mu_\theta\) be type \(\theta\)’s share of the waitlist, and let \(q\in(0,1)\) be the share of applicants who can receive housing. The action is to submit a feasible certificate; the planner reads it and allocates the per-capita supply. A type’s allocation rate can be fractional even though each individual receives either a unit or none.

A precise candidate is Continuum Safe Chain Implementation. Its input is a finite type set \(\T\), a finite content set \(\M\), the evidence technology \(M(\theta)\), a target ranking \(R=(R_1,\ldots,R_L)\), a rational population distribution \(\mu\) over \(\T\), and capacity share \(q\). For each type, the full-information allocation serves every type above the cutoff class, none below it, and a common fraction of the cutoff class, chosen to use exactly \(q\) of the population. A solution is a safe reading \(\kappa:\M\to\{1,\ldots,L\}\) and feasible submissions \(s(\theta)\in M(\theta)\) that attain every type’s target rank and thereby reproduce that allocation. Safety means that a content is never read as a better priority than any type capable of submitting it. The question is whether such a witness exists, not the optimization of a separate objective.

The best named bridge is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that a chain is safely implementable by a reading if and only if every proper upper contour \(U_\ell^R\) is content-certifiable; equivalently, each type \(\theta\) has a feasible content \(m\) whose certifier set lies inside \(U_{r_R(\theta)}^R\). The theorem also shows this is equivalent to dominant-strategy implementation of the full-information chain allocation. The continuum formulation preserves the paper’s central claim: the relevant evidence need not reveal an exact type; it must establish the type’s side of each priority cutoff. Replacing applicant counts and integer capacity with population shares and per-capita capacity is a natural mirror of the housing application. Theorem 2 supplies a parallel reserve-system extension: each label’s eligibility class must be content-certifiable. Theorem 3’s conservative repairs suggest a further continuous question about repairing unsupported priorities or labels.

There is a crucial qualification: none of these is a qualifying computational anchor under your instructions. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize implementability; Theorem 3 characterizes repairs. They are proved here, but none asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class, and the paper gives no complexity analysis. So, strictly, there are no per-anchor continuous questions to submit. The model above is a promising structural mirror of Theorem 1, not a computational result this paper establishes.

For the proposed problem, I would expect the same upper-contour characterization to hold in the continuum. With explicit finite type and evidence tables, checking that condition looks directly finite; that is an expectation about this proposed encoding, not a result claimed by the paper. Succinctly represented category spaces or evidence technologies could create a substantive complexity question, but that would need a new formulation and analysis. The paper’s finite typewise conditions do not by themselves show that discrete hardness dissolves with population multiplicity.

The regime is credible, and the authors would likely recognize the mirror: their paper already studies large-scale housing allocation by categorical priority and scarce capacity. The weakest point is that the continuum changes the units of population and supply while apparently leaving the certification logic untouched. In the paper’s actual model, \(x_i\in[0,1]\) is already an allocation probability for each of finitely many applicants; that is outcome randomization, not a continuous society. The proposed mirror makes the population continuous, but the paper supplies no named computational result whose complexity can then be tested.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize when rules are implementable; Theorem 3 characterizes repairs. None studies an algorithm’s complexity. That is decisive under the programme’s stated gate: these results do not place a problem in any complexity class or ask for an exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithm.

The housing waitlist is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and a continuous version of Theorem 1 is easy to state. Given type masses \(\mu\) and capacity share \(q\), allocate all mass above the cutoff class and the required fraction of the cutoff class. But the theorem’s certification test is still typewise: for each type \(\theta\), check whether some feasible content \(m\) has \(M^{-1}(m)\subseteq U_{r_R(\theta)}^R\). With explicit type and evidence tables, one can compute \(\max_{\tau\in M^{-1}(m)} r_R(\tau)\) for each content and scan each type’s menu. The population masses affect the cutoff arithmetic, not the certification test. So this is a coherent structural mirror, but it does not turn the paper’s result into a computational question.

Theorem 2 has the same problem. For each reserve label \(r\), test whether every eligible type has a feasible content whose certifier set lies within \(D_r\). That is another direct scan of the finite evidence table, independent of \(\mu\). The theorem deliberately allows any feasible, label-monotone downstream rule; it does not provide a particular rule whose continuous allocation computation could be analyzed. Adding an aggregate rationing procedure might create a computational problem, but the paper leaves that procedure open.

Theorem 3 offers little more computationally. Its repairs are given by one-step formulas over the finite evidence menus, so an explicit-table version is directly computable. A natural population-weighted penalty for demotions or deleted reserve labels does not by itself help: the canonical repair is pointwise minimal for chains and pointwise maximal for reserves, so it already optimizes any penalty that worsens monotonically with each such change. A nonseparable allocation or welfare objective could make the choice harder, but the paper specifies neither applicant welfare rankings nor a particular downstream rule. Supplying them would define a new design problem, not continue the theorem’s repair result.

The best attempt to make these questions computationally substantive would represent the category space, evidence technology, or candidate rules succinctly, or ask the planner to choose an evidence technology or target rule. That could produce worthwhile complexity questions. But the difficulty would come from the compressed representation or the enlarged design problem, not from replacing applicant counts by masses. It would be a new computational agenda prompted by the paper, rather than a computational mirror of its named results.

So the negative case is strong against this paper as a ChoCo complexity anchor, though not airtight as the universal claim that no continuous model related to it could ever be worthwhile. A continuous housing population is sensible; what is missing is a computational result in the paper for that population model to carry into ChoCo’s complexity landscape.
