The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a plausible setting, not an anchor-backed mirror: large school or course-allocation cohorts in which many students share the same complete ranking. A type would be a ranking, and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of students with that ranking; capacities would scale with the cohort. That gives a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper itself discusses large-market assignment work. But scaling capacities also moves away from the paper’s central one-copy setting.

I can’t supply the required continuous computational problem per anchor. The named results here do not assert complexity classifications or algorithmic tractability. For example, Theorem 1, proved in the paper, says that RSD is ld-envyfree for any \(n\) and \(m\). Proposition 4, also established here via Example 5, says that when \(n<m\), PS is neither ld-strategyproof nor sd-strategyproof. These are results about mechanism properties, not about whether an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The paper’s empirical comparisons and conjectures do not provide such an anchor either.

So I would not name a lead mirror problem or predict its complexity class from this paper. The limiting issue is that its contribution concerns fairness, incentives, and comparisons between mechanisms; the continuous computational questions required by the programme are absent.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to carry across. Theorem 1 establishes an envy property of RSD, and Proposition 4 establishes a strategyproofness failure of PS. Neither classifies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. The experiments and conjectures describe how often outcomes have certain properties; they do not ask for an algorithm or a complexity bound. By ChoCo’s stated scope, that is a substantial gap.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: large school or course-allocation cohorts with repeated ranking types and capacities that scale with the cohort. I would not argue that mass is meaningless here; the paper’s large-market discussion supports that regime. But a sensible population model does not by itself supply the missing computational question. The large-market results cited in the paper are analytic results about mechanism properties, not computational classifications.

For Theorem 1, the stronger mirror would define a continuous RSD outcome over ranking masses and ask whether a representative of each type envies another type’s allocation under lexicographic preferences. That is a coherent continuous fairness question. It is still an axiomatic question, which the programme excludes. Asking an algorithm to check the property once the type-level assignments are given would be a new verification task; any harder part would be computing the continuous RSD outcome, which is not what Theorem 1 establishes.

For Proposition 4, the obstacle is more fundamental to its subject. In an atomless population, a single agent has zero mass, so a unilateral misreport cannot change the aggregate outcome. To retain a nontrivial manipulation question, one could ask for the smallest mass of a coordinated bloc whose misreport benefits its members. That is a plausible new problem, but it replaces individual strategyproofness with coalition manipulation. The paper gives no result about that question, and treating each ranking type as a coalition would need its own justification.

So I see no compelling ChoCo mirror of this paper’s stated contributions. The negative case is strong at that boundary, but the universal claim that no useful continuous computational project could arise in any related scenario goes further than the evidence supports: continuous RSD evaluation or coalition manipulation could motivate new work. Those would be adjacent projects with new computational targets, not mirrors of the named results.
