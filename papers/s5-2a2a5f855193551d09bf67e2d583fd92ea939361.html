The continuous mirror question

Given ordered men’s sites \(M_1,\dots,M_p\) and women’s sites \(W_1,\dots,W_q\), each with strict rankings of acceptable opposite-bank sites and rational supplies \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) summing to \(1/2\) on each bank, find a rational assignment \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with row and column sums bounded by supplies and no positive \(x_{ij},x_{i'j'}\) when \(i<i'\) and \(j>j'\). For each mutually acceptable pair \(i,j\) whose site edge crosses no edge in the support of \(x\), define \(A_{ij}(x)=\mu_i-\sum_k x_{ik}+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\) and \(B_{ij}(x)=\nu_j-\sum_h x_{hj}+\sum_{h:\,i\succ_j h}x_{hj}\), the masses unmatched or assigned to a less-preferred site; require \(A_{ij}(x)=0\) or \(B_{ij}(x)=0\). Does every such instance admit a construction algorithm running in time polynomial in support size \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A finite set of ordered macro-sites on each bank, each carrying rational population mass and a shared ranking over acceptable sites on the other bank. The variables \(x_{ij}\) specify matched mass, subject to site capacities, noncrossing support, and the mass analogue of weak stability; the objective is to construct any feasible stable assignment.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for an instance with \(n\) men and \(n\) women, distinct ordered points on two parallel lines, and strict preference lists, an \(O(n^2)\)-time algorithm finds a weakly stable noncrossing matching. Here “weakly stable” means the matching is noncrossing and has no blocking pair whose edge crosses none of the matching’s edges. The theorem’s algorithmic result also gives existence. The paper contains no named hardness result, so this mirror asks whether the algorithmic guarantee survives when the population is represented by masses.

Call the mirror Mass-WSNM Construction. An instance has ordered terminals on each bank, with one type at each terminal. A type records its bank, terminal position, and strict ranking of acceptable types on the other bank. Give each type a nonnegative rational mass, with total mass \(1/2\) on each bank; let there be \(p\) men’s types and \(q\) women’s types, so the support size is \(\tau=p+q\). The action variable is \(x_{ij}\geq 0\), the mass of type \(i\) matched to type \(j\), with row and column sums bounded by their respective supplies. Require that \(x_{ij}\) and \(x_{i'j'}\) cannot both be positive when \(i<i'\) and \(j>j'\), since those links cross.

A solution is any such assignment with no noncrossing blocking mass. More precisely, for an acceptable pair \((i,j)\), the mass of type \(i\) willing to switch to \(j\) is \(A_{ij}(x)=\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}+\mu_i-\sum_k x_{ik}\); define \(B_{ij}(x)\) symmetrically for type \(j\). The assignment is stable if, for every mutually acceptable \((i,j)\) whose edge crosses no edge in the support of \(x\), at least one of \(A_{ij}(x)\) and \(B_{ij}(x)\) is zero. Thus no positive amount of willing mass on both sides can form a blocking pair. As in the paper, there is no welfare objective: the task is to construct any weakly stable noncrossing matching.

A plausible regime is bulk, recurring assignment across a fixed set of ordered service terminals: many interchangeable requests and providers at each terminal, with shared rankings and parameters within each cohort, and straight connections between matched units that may not cross. The number of agents can be very large while the number of terminal-ranking types stays modest. Mass is the honest unit for a cohort-level assignment; targeting one named person at a time would not describe this regime. The continuous object is the population. The assignment masses are the necessary way to describe how its units are matched.

I would expect this problem to be tractable, though Theorem 2 does not prove that. Its ordered scan suggests an event-driven version that moves rational mass between types, batching transfers until a cohort’s available or willing mass is exhausted. The open algorithmic issue is whether such a process has only polynomially many events in \(\tau\), with controlled bit growth. There is already a slower baseline: scale rational supplies by a common denominator \(D\), apply Theorem 2 to the resulting discrete instance, and divide the matching by \(D\). That takes \(O(D^2)\) time, which can be exponential in the input bit length \(L\). So the sharp question for a prover is whether every Mass-WSNM instance has a solution computable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(L\). A natural further question is whether one can find a stable assignment maximizing total matched mass, corresponding to a future problem the paper itself raises.

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s construction of a WSNM, and its existence consequence. It does not claim a mirror for strongly stable noncrossing matchings, maximum-size discrete WSNMs, or the roommates variants discussed as future work.

My weakest point is the geometric co-modification: the paper gives each person a distinct point, whereas this mirror treats a terminal cohort as sharing a macro-location and treats its links as mass on one terminal-to-terminal edge. That is a real change in the model, and authors who require distinct point positions may reject it. I think the mirror remains recognizable because ordering and crossing remain central, while the cohort interpretation fits the paper’s immovable-terminal motivation and its tie-breaking and unequal-side extensions. But that recognition is the gate on which this positive case depends.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that position is part of a person’s type in this paper. Whether a proposed pair crosses a matched edge depends on the exact order of its two endpoints. With distinct positions, that can make every person a different type, so the type support grows with \(n\) and there is no obvious high-multiplicity regime.

The proponent’s terminal cohorts avoid that problem by putting many agents at the same macro-location. But then the proposed blocking test can lose geometric information. Its \(A_{ij}\) and \(B_{ij}\) measure how much mass at each terminal wants to switch; its crossing test checks only whether the terminal-to-terminal edge crosses an edge in the aggregate support. In a realization with distinct individual points inside each terminal cohort, a willing pair’s segment may cross a matched edge even when the aggregate test says it does not. To preserve the paper’s individual-level definition, the model must track those within-cohort positions and matching arrangements. That risks making position part of the type again, or requiring a richer spatial population model than the paper’s finite-support, rational-mass setting.

A stronger mirror could embrace macro-sites explicitly: treat each site as a capacity-bearing cohort, define noncrossing by the order of sites, and seek any stable fractional assignment—or one maximizing matched mass. That is coherent, and the maximum-mass objective connects to a future problem the paper itself raises. But it changes the geometric object and the blocking relation: it is a capacitated stable matching problem on ordered sites, rather than a continuum version of matching individually positioned points. The authors might accept that as their problem; the paper does not establish that they would.

There is no decisive objection from the other suggested grounds. Theorem 2 is a named computational result, and rational masses can be scaled to a discrete instance, though the resulting \(O(n^2)\) run may be exponential in the masses’ bit length. That leaves a real algorithmic question in the finite-type model. Nor does the fact that the discrete theorem already guarantees existence settle the bit-complexity question.

So the negative case is a model-identity objection: the literal geometry resists aggregation, while the clean cohort model changes what counts as crossing and blocking. Its weakness is substantial. A macro-site version is plausible and computationally meaningful, and the programme explicitly allows a necessary co-modification when authors recognize it as their problem. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile mirror survives; the best case against identifies a serious recognition gate, but does not defeat the strongest cohort-based mirror.
