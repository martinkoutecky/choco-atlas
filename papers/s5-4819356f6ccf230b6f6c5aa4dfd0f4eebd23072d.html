The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, panel size \(k\), and integer quotas \(\ell_{f,v},u_{f,v}\), produce an exact sampler for \(k\) independent draws from \(\mu\), conditioned on the resulting panel satisfying every quota, or report that no feasible panel exists.

The model it lives in

A citizens’ assembly draws a finite panel from an atomless volunteer population whose type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\). The panel members’ types are independent draws from \(\mu\); the selection rule conditions on the quota constraints, equivalently choosing the law closest in relative entropy to the population’s iid baseline among quota-feasible laws.

The objection that survived

The continuous sampler remains a finite-support counting and sampling problem, close to the profile-based dynamic programming already developed in the paper, so the computational novelty may be limited.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.3 through its large-pool limit and offers a type-level version of Theorem 4.2; it leaves the manipulation, transparency, empirical diversity, and deployment results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What are the exact-sampling and feasibility complexities in support size \(\tau\), quota dimension \(d\), panel size \(k\), and input bit length?
  • Can Theorem 4.2’s convergence guarantee be recovered in type-count coordinates for target expected counts, including targets on the boundary of the feasible polytope?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a limit of the paper’s central sortition problem: take a large pool in which many volunteers share each relevant profile, and describe the pool by the fraction of volunteers of each profile. The selected panel remains finite and quota-compliant. What becomes continuous is the society supplying its members.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.3, proved in this paper: given a sortition instance, MaxEntropy samples a panel uniformly from the set of all panels satisfying its quotas. The paper’s separate claim that feasibility is NP-hard, even with three features, appears in §3 as background attributed to Flanigan et al. [22]; it is not a numbered result here, so I do not present it as an anchor.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous MaxEntropy Sortition (CMES). An instance has a finite set \(T\) of complete feature profiles, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, a panel size \(k\), and integer lower and upper quotas \(\ell_{f,v},u_{f,v}\). Each type \(t\) specifies a value \(f(t)\) for every feature \(f\). The population consists of atomless mass \(\mu_t\) of type \(t\). Draw \(k\) distinct people independently from this continuum population, and condition on the drawn panel meeting every quota. The task is to report if no feasible panel exists; otherwise provide an exact sampler for the resulting law.

Equivalently, for a sequence \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_k)\in T^k\), let \(x_t(y)\) count its positions of type \(t\). It is feasible when \(\ell_{f,v}\leq\sum_{t:f(t)=v}x_t(y)\leq u_{f,v}\) for every feature-value pair. The target law assigns each feasible sequence probability proportional to \(\prod_{j=1}^k\mu_{y_j}\). Thus repeated types are allowed, while repeated *people* occur with probability zero. This is the conditional iid law, or equivalently the distribution closest in KL divergence to the iid population baseline among laws supported on quota-feasible panels.

This is a genuine population limit of the paper’s model. If a finite pool has \(n_t\) members of type \(t\), with \(n_t/n\to\mu_t\), a uniformly chosen \(k\)-person subset has type-count probability proportional to \(\prod_t {n_t\choose x_t}\). For fixed \(k\), this converges to the multinomial weights \(k!\prod_t\mu_t^{x_t}/x_t!\), exactly the type-count law induced by the conditional iid sampler. The continuous input is therefore the society’s type-mass distribution; the quota-respecting panel law remains the problem being computed. This is not a claim that the type-count vectors themselves should be sampled uniformly.

The regime I find convincing is a large volunteer pool for a recurring citywide or national citizens’ assembly: perhaps thousands or tens of thousands of opt-in volunteers, with many people in each of a comparatively small set of demographic profiles, and panels of tens or a few hundred. Type records the complete quota-relevant profile—age band, region, education, and so on. If per-person target selection probabilities matter, they too belong in the type description. In a small pilot pool, or where nearly every volunteer has a unique relevant profile, the limit is much less persuasive. But the authors’ question—how to randomize over quota-valid panels—is directly preserved in the large-pool regime.

For unrestricted types and quotas, I expect Class B: hardness transfers. There is a direct feasibility reduction: make the types the triples of a three-dimensional matching instance, take the three coordinates as features, and impose quota exactly one for every value. A feasible panel of size \(k\) is then a perfect three-dimensional matching. Give every type positive rational mass; the continuum supplies ample people of each type, while the quotas themselves rule out repeated triples. Thus infeasibility remains NP-hard in the continuous population. The combinatorics live in the feature values and support, not in the number of named people. There is also a plausible Class A regime: with \(d=|FV|\) fixed, a profile-state dynamic program has at most roughly \((k+1)^d\) states per type layer, so exact counting and sampling should be tractable with running time polynomial in \(\tau\) and \(k\) for fixed \(d\), with bit complexity depending on the rational masses. The natural complexity question is how far that tractability extends as \(d\) grows.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Theorem 4.2, also proved here. It says that for a sortition instance with target selection probabilities \(\pi\) in the relative interior of the feasible marginal polytope \(M\), after \(T\) iterations FairMaxEntropy returns a distribution \(\lambda\) that maximizes entropy among distributions with its own marginals \(\pi_\lambda\), and satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[\|\pi_\lambda-\pi\|_2^2]\leq O(1/\sqrt{T})\). The theorem uses stochastic gradient descent with the dynamic program as an oracle; the paper also discusses approaching arbitrary targets, but gives no comparable rate for boundary targets.

Its continuous mirror is Continuous FairMaxEntropy (CFMES). Add target expected type counts \(a_t\), with \(\sum_t a_t=k\), to the CMES input, and require \(a\) to lie in the relative interior of the feasible expected-count polytope. Find a quota-feasible panel law closest in KL divergence to the iid baseline \(\mu^{\otimes k}\), subject to expected type counts \(a\). The desired algorithmic result is a sequence of laws whose type-count marginals converge to \(a\), with a finite-iteration error guarantee, while each law is the KL-optimal one for its own marginals. This is the population-limit analogue of prescribing equal selection probabilities within a type: in a finite pool, a target expected count \(a_t\) corresponds to per-person selection probability about \(a_t/n_t\) for members of type \(t\). The relative-entropy baseline is the continuous limit of uniformity over finite named-person panels, rather than uniformity over type-count vectors.

I expect CF​MES to be Class A in fixed quota dimension when the required counting and sampling oracles are supplied by the dynamic program. For the unrestricted family, I expect the same support and feasibility hardness to matter, but Theorem 4.2 by itself does not establish a complexity classification for the type-aggregated version. In particular, its stated convergence rate should not be claimed for the continuous formulation without a separate analysis.

The paper’s scope mirrored here is its core quota-respecting maximum-entropy sampling objective and, secondarily, its prescribed-marginal variant. This does not mirror every empirical diversity finding, deployment detail, or named-person transparency guarantee. Further questions include the complexity of approximate sampling as \(d\) varies, how to handle boundary or infeasible target marginals, and whether the paper’s manipulation-resistance guarantees have a useful continuous coalition-mass formulation.

The weakest point is that the paper’s exact uniformity is over panels of named volunteers, while CMES uses the population distribution as its reference law. That changes the limit law’s weights across type profiles, so it would be wrong to call it uniform over feasible type-count vectors. I think the mirror survives because the conditional iid law is precisely what uniform finite-pool selection converges to when the pool is large and types are replicated. It is a limit of the authors’ problem, not a replacement of it with a different outcome-space question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is about the incremental research payoff. Theorem 3.3 is a real computational result: MaxEntropy samples uniformly from feasible panels. Theorem 4.2 gives an algorithm and a convergence guarantee for FairMaxEntropy. Neither can be dismissed as axiomatic work. Nor is a large volunteer pool an implausible high-multiplicity regime for sortition; I would not use the existence of high-multiplicity work as an objection.

For Theorem 3.3, the best mirror is the proposed one: a society with type masses \(\mu_t\), a fixed panel size \(k\), and integer quotas, with panels drawn from the population conditional on meeting those quotas. Its type sequences have probability proportional to \(\prod_{j=1}^k \mu_{y_j}\). That is a proper population limit: uniform sampling from a finite pool with \(n_t/n\to\mu_t\) induces the same limiting type-count law. The skeptical point is that the resulting computation remains a finite-support counting and sampling problem. For fixed \(d=|FV|\), a profile dynamic program has roughly \(\tau(k+1)^d\) states; with variable \(d\), quota feasibility retains its combinatorial hardness. The paper already aggregates identical feature profiles, and Appendix A supplies weighted counting machinery close to what the limit needs. So the continuous formulation may add a clean dictionary and a useful input representation without opening a new optimization problem. That is a case for lower priority, though the formal limit and its parameterized complexity could still be worthwhile.

Theorem 4.2 presents a stronger modeling wrinkle. Its targets are inclusion probabilities for named people. In an atomless population, each particular person’s inclusion probability is zero; moreover, the theorem’s \(n\)-coordinate squared error can hide a fixed type-count error as \(n\) grows. That error is \(O(1/n)\) when spread evenly across a type. The literal guarantee therefore does not survive the limit in a useful form. But the stronger mirror repairs this by targeting expected counts \(a_t\) for types. A maximum-entropy law under those constraints has an exponential tilt of the population baseline, with type weights \(\mu_t e^{\theta_t}\). Its gradients are expected type counts minus \(a_t\), and the paper’s weighted dynamic program can serve as the sampling oracle. One would need a new error bound in type-count coordinates; the paper’s stated rate does not supply it. Still, the altered target is a recognizable and sensible population version, as the programme’s scope permits.

This leaves a real negative case: both mirrors may largely repackage finite type-profile computation, and neither appears to require the continuous-optimization tools that motivate the programme. But that is an argument about research priority, not a fundamental objection to the models. The population limit is mathematically well-defined, the first anchor survives in a natural regime, and the second has a plausible type-level reformulation. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no scenario offers a worthwhile mirror.
