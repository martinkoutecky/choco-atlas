What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror targets equilibrium computation and induced cascades, but does not transfer the finite-player FPE characterization or the fixed-discount cascade theorem.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a continuous-population version of the paper’s structured equilibrium computation. It has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a very large product-adoption cohort in which users share the same discounting, contact process, payoff, and signal accuracy. The two types are the private signals \(x\in\{-1,+1\}\); type masses are fractions of the cohort. Conditional on the hidden product value \(V\), their masses are \(\mu_v(x)=Q(x\mid v)\). Millions of users and two types is a sensible regime for this question.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that if \(U^*\) solves the authors’ quadratic-dimension fixed-point equation FPE 2, then the strategy mapping in (25) and value functions in (26), constructed from \(U^*\), satisfy the original FPE 1. FPE 2 describes the policy and values using the acting agent’s revealed-status bit \(r\) and the aggregate variables \(y\) and \(w\), rather than the full vector of \(N\) agents’ states. Its state domain grows quadratically with \(N\). The paper then computes equilibria using this formulation and verifies numerical solutions in Section VII. This qualifies as a computational formulation the paper actually computes with. It is not a theorem that equilibrium finding is in P: the paper establishes neither a polynomial-time bound for solving the fixed point nor a complexity classification.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Atomless Non-myopic Adoption Equilibrium. The instance specifies a prior on \(V\in\{-1,+1\}\), a rational signal accuracy \(p\in(0,1/2)\), and a discount rate \(\rho>0\). There is a unit mass of otherwise identical users, with conditional signal masses \(\mu_v(x)=Q(x\mid v)\). Each user receives independent opportunities to act at rate one. On an opportunity, an unbought user can buy and leave, or wait for a later opportunity. Buying at time \(t\) yields \(e^{-\rho t}V\); waiting yields no immediate reward. Users observe the public aggregate flows of opportunities and actions, as well as their own signal.

The question is to find a stationary symmetric PBE: a measurable policy assigning an action to each signal and public state, continuation values, and public beliefs that are Bayes-consistent with the aggregate flows and sequentially rational for each user. A solution is those policies, values, and beliefs together with the induced aggregate flow. A natural state to investigate is the continuum counterpart of the paper’s \((r,y,w)\): own revealed status, signed mass of revealed signals \(y\in[-1,1]\), and mass already revealed or bought \(w\in[0,1]\), with the public posterior included if it is not determined by those statistics.

The time scaling is part of the mirror. In the finite model, \(N\) turns correspond to order-one activation time for each user, so retaining nontrivial patience calls for \(\delta_N=e^{-\rho/N}\). This is a co-modification to preserve the paper’s waiting incentives as the population grows. The mirror covers the structured-PBE computation of Theorem 2 and the downstream question of what cascades those equilibria induce; it does not claim that the paper’s fixed-\(\delta\) asymptotic cascade theorem is itself a continuous limit.

For this mirror, I would tentatively expect Class A for approximation: there are only two types and a low-dimensional aggregate state, which makes numerical Bellman or control methods plausible. Whether they yield a convergent \(\varepsilon\)-approximation algorithm with a useful complexity bound is the research question, not a result one can infer from Theorem 2. The further questions are whether such equilibria exist and have a finite-dimensional sufficient state, whether finite equilibria converge under the stated scaling, and whether the limiting equilibrium has cascades.

The weakest point is substantive: a single user has zero mass in the mirror and so cannot change the public belief by deviating. In the paper, one user’s action can reveal a signal and affect future beliefs. Aggregate flows can still carry information in the continuum model, but their learning incentives—and potentially their cascades—need not match the finite game. This is why the mirror is a credible new equilibrium-computation question, but not a ready-made limiting theorem. The paper has no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time result to anchor a stronger complexity claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is against the proposed atomless version of Theorem 2. The paper genuinely gives a computational characterization: Theorem 2 shows that a solution to the reduced FPE yields a solution to the original one, with a state domain quadratic in \(N\). But it gives no running-time bound for finding that solution. The numerical computation in Section VII is evidence that the formulation can be used; it does not establish an efficient equilibrium algorithm. A continuous mirror would therefore lack a tractability result from the paper to carry forward.

The proposed population regime itself is sensible: a large cohort of users with the same payoffs and signal accuracy is a plausible high-multiplicity society. The trouble is what happens to the paper’s information and incentives in the limit. In the finite game, public odds are proportional to \(q^{\sum_n \tilde x_n}\), where \(q=(1-p)/p\). With the proposed time scaling, a positive mass of users can act and reveal during an order-one interval. For fixed \(p\), any nonzero limiting signed mass of revealed signals makes those odds grow or shrink exponentially in \(N\), driving the public belief to certainty. If only \(o(N)\) users reveal, their contribution disappears from the aggregate mass state. The finite model’s gradual accumulation of information therefore has a singular limit.

The same limit removes a strategic channel used by the FPE. A single user has zero mass, so deviating cannot change aggregate flows or the public belief they induce. Waiting to reveal can no longer influence other users’ future behavior in the way it can when one person’s signal changes the finite public posterior. A mean-field equilibrium may still exist, but its incentives and equilibrium conditions would need a new derivation. And because the continuum has flows rather than isolated turns, the paper’s cascade definition as a sequence of turns with no revealing action also needs a new interpretation.

The strongest repair is to weaken signals with population size, for example by taking \(p_N\) toward \(1/2\) at order \(N^{-1/2}\). That can preserve a nondegenerate public belief limit. It also changes the paper’s fixed-accuracy signal model and produces a diffusion-style social-learning game whose representative agent has vanishing influence on the aggregate. This could be an interesting project, but its computational question and its relation to Theorem 2 would need to be established from scratch. Keeping finite agents influential instead would retain the original finite-population problem and its FPE.

So the proposed fixed-\(p\) atomless mirror is not yet a convincing continuation of Theorem 2, and the cascade extension inherits the same problem. The broader claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario is much harder to defend: a carefully chosen weak-signal mean-field game could be valuable. The honest negative case is therefore substantial against this mirror, but weak as a universal verdict.
