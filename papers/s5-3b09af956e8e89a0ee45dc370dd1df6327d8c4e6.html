The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting here: many industrial facilities with the same preferences over energy and money and the same baseline claims, grouped into a small number of types. Type mass would be each group’s fraction of the facilities, with resource supply scaled per capita. That is a natural population mirror of the paper’s cap-and-trade application.

But I find no named computational result in the paper to anchor that mirror to. Theorem 1 characterizes when an individually rational exchange rule can yield no-envy on \(E^0\): under resource monotonicity and full-award-out-consistency, the rights-assignment rule must be constrained equal awards. Theorem 2 gives the corresponding constrained equal losses characterization for sacrifice-no-envy, under resource monotonicity and zero-award-out-consistency. Theorem 3 characterizes the proportional rule for relative-no-envy. These are axiomatic characterizations, not results about algorithms or complexity. The propositions likewise concern existence, fairness, and compatibility. The paper gives no computational problem, input encoding, running-time bound, or complexity classification.

But that would add a computational problem to the paper; it would not mirror a named computational result of the paper. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–3 are the closest candidates: they characterize constrained equal awards, constrained equal losses, and proportional rights assignment through fairness axioms. Their subject is which rules have those properties, not the complexity of computing an outcome. Propositions 1–6 likewise establish characterizations, compatibility or existence, and a correspondence with cap-and-trade equilibria. None states an algorithmic problem or a complexity result.

The industrial-facility scenario is plausible: facilities with identical preferences and claims can form types, with type masses representing population shares. That makes a high-multiplicity version sensible. But translating Theorems 1–3 to that setting would give continuous versions of axiomatic fairness questions, which are outside ChoCo’s computational scope. The stronger computational proposal—ask how to calculate a fair, efficient market outcome from rational type masses—adds a problem the paper does not formulate. It would require choices about how preferences are represented and what counts as computing an equilibrium; it would be an extension, not a mirror of the paper’s results.

So the candidate anchors all fail for the same reason: Theorems 1, 2, and 3 do not classify computational problems. The paper may support a new computational project built around its economic model, but it does not provide a computational contribution for a continuous mirror to carry over. This is a strong negative about mirroring this paper, not proof that no worthwhile computational problem could ever be posed in its setting.
