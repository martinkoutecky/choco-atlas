The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and strict rankings \(\succ_t\) over partner types and a singleton option \(\bot\), does there exist a mass matching \(p_{tu}=p_{ut}\geq 0\), \(s_t\geq 0\), with \(s_t+\sum_{u\in T}p_{tu}=\mu_t\), that has no positive-mass weakly blocking coalition? For a nonempty support \(S\subseteq T\), let \(w_t(S)\) be the least-preferred type in \(S\) under \(\succ_t\), and let \(q_{t,u}=p_{tu}\), \(q_{t,\bot}=s_t\). A support \(S\) blocks when every \(t\in S\) has positive mass \(A_{t,S}=\sum_{v:\,w_t(S)\succeq_t v}q_{t,v}\) of weakly improving participants and some \(t\in S\) has positive mass \(B_{t,S}=\sum_{v:\,w_t(S)\succ_t v}q_{t,v}\) of strict improvers; the question is whether a feasible matching avoiding every such support exists.

The model it lives in

A continuum roommate market with finitely many complete preference types, each carrying rational mass. The decision variables are cross-type and within-type pair masses plus singleton masses; coalition payoffs use the least-preferred type present, and stability excludes any support with positive mass of weakly willing participants of every type and a positive mass of strict improvers.

The objection that survived

The paper’s pair-only guarantee and Phase II criterion are proved for strict rankings over named agents; with indistinguishable agents tied within types, the proposed pair-and-singleton outcomes may omit stable larger coalitions.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers ψ-strict-core existence in type-based roommate populations. It leaves the Τ-preference results, strategy-proofness, and the paper’s results concerning indifferences untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can mass-ψ-strict-core existence be decided in time polynomial in the number of types and the input bit length of the masses?
  • With type-level ties among indistinguishable agents, can stable outcomes always be represented by pairs and singletons, or must the model allow larger coalition configurations?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with the paper’s pessimistic \(\psi\)-preference model and a large roommate or co-housing platform. A plausible regime is thousands or millions of applicants but only tens or hundreds of recurring profiles: for example, people sharing a budget, schedule, household constraints, and the same strict ranking of other profiles. A type is that complete preference description; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of applicants of type \(t\). This makes the population continuous while keeping the roommate decision itself recognizable.

Call the mirror Mass-\(\psi\)-Strict-Core Existence. The input is a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and, for each type \(t\), a strict ranking \(\succ_t\) of the possible partner types and a singleton option \(\bot\). A candidate outcome is a mass matching: \(p_{tu}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents paired with type \(u\), and \(s_t\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents left single. Require \(p_{tu}=p_{ut}\ge 0\) and \(s_t+\sum_{u\in T}p_{tu}=\mu_t\). The diagonal \(p_{tt}\) represents type-\(t\) agents paired within their own type. The objective is feasibility: find such an assignment with no positive-mass weakly blocking coalition, or report that none exists.

Here is a precise block test. For an assignment, let \(q_{t,u}=p_{tu}\) and \(q_{t,\bot}=s_t\). For any nonempty set \(S\subseteq T\), let \(w_t(S)\) be the least-preferred type in \(S\) according to \(\succ_t\). This is the worst member type a type-\(t\) participant would face in a coalition whose type support is \(S\), as in the paper’s \(\psi\)-extension. Define
\[ A_{t,S}=\sum_{v:\,w_t(S)\succeq_t v}q_{t,v}, \qquad B_{t,S}=\sum_{v:\,w_t(S)\succ_t v}q_{t,v}, \]
where the sum ranges over \(v\in T\cup\{\bot\}\). A coalition with support \(S\) can block if every \(A_{t,S}>0\) for \(t\in S\), and at least one \(B_{t,S}>0\): there is positive mass of willing participants of every type, with positive mass of strict improvers. Thus the solution condition is that for every nonempty \(S\), either some \(A_{t,S}=0\), or all \(B_{t,S}=0\). With finitely many types, this gives a well-defined finite disjunctive feasibility question over \(O(\tau^2)\) mass variables. The mass in the model describes the population; it is not a fractional preference or a lottery over winners.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper. It states that, for a preference profile without indifferences, the \(\psi\)-strict core is empty if and only if some player’s preference list becomes empty during Phase II of the Stable Roommates algorithm. The theorem is an algorithmic characterization, though it does not itself state a bit-complexity bound. The paper’s surrounding results make the roommate mirror especially plausible: it proves that stable-roommates solutions are \(\psi\)-strict-core partitions, that the non-singleton part of any \(\psi\)-strict-core partition is a stable-roommates solution, and that such a partition cannot contain a coalition larger than two. Those are structural reasons to use pair flows and singletons as the outcome variables, rather than an unrelated optimization encoding.

I would initially expect the mass problem to be Class A, with low confidence. The finite theorem suggests that preference-list reductions may have a weighted version whose running time depends on \(\tau\) and the binary mass length \(L\), rather than on the represented population. But the block conditions above are disjunctive, so a polynomial-size flow description alone does not establish tractability. The concrete research questions are whether Phase II can be generalized to type masses, whether the mass problem has rational witnesses of polynomial bit length, and whether a discrete-to-mass dictionary gives controlled approximation when rational masses are rounded to a finite electorate. If the disjunctions encode choices that resist weighted reductions, this could instead be continuum-specifically hard.

I use only Theorem 6 as an anchor. The paper’s conclusion reports an NP-completeness result from cited reference [6] for the optimistic \(\mathcal B\)-core problems with indifferences; it is not a numbered theorem here and concerns the other preference extension. I would leave that extension outside this mirror: replacing its coalition-cardinality tie-break with coalition mass risks making ever-smaller coalitions improvements.

The weakest point is also the most consequential: Theorem 6 assumes strict preferences over named individuals, whereas agents of the same continuous type are indistinguishable and therefore tied at the individual level. The mirror preserves strict rankings *between types* and makes the resulting mass-level weak-blocking rule explicit, but the paper’s algorithm does not automatically carry over. That gap is part of the question for the authors: whether the high-multiplicity roommates problem is still tractable after the unavoidable aggregation by type.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 6’s computational content depends on preferences over named individuals. Its test runs the Stable Roommates algorithm on those individual preference lists. Compressing people into types with rankings over partner types changes the preference model: members of a type become indifferent among its individuals, while the paper’s theorem assumes strict preferences. The paper’s pair-only structure for \(\psi\)-strict-core partitions also relies on strictness. So the proposed mass matching does not follow from Theorem 6 simply by replacing player counts with masses.

That gap runs deeper than the proposed block test. The paper evaluates a coalition by its least-preferred member, with the evaluated player excluded from that set. A type-support description loses the coalition’s member counts, which can affect that least-preferred member; the proposed pair-flow model also assumes pairs and singletons remain sufficient after type aggregation. A more faithful version would need to represent distributions over coalition configurations and specify how ties within types and deviations by arbitrarily small masses work. That is a substantial new stability model, rather than an evident high-multiplicity encoding of the paper’s algorithm.

This is still not a decisive case against the mirror. Ranking partner types is a plausible roommate model, and a mass-level stability question could be valuable even if strict preferences no longer apply. Continuum roommate markets already have analytic formulations, but those results do not settle the computational question; under the programme’s rules, their existence is not a novelty objection. [Carmona’s continuum roommate-market model](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/TE5915) and [Greinecker and Kah’s large-market stability model](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/ECTA16228) support that distinction.

So the negative case exposes a real modeling gap in the proposed mirror, but it does not defeat the stronger type-based version. Theorem 6 is algorithmic even though it states no running-time bound, and the paper’s own strict-roommate result supplies a natural starting point. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; the case against is weak precisely where it must rule out a recognized type-based roommate model.
