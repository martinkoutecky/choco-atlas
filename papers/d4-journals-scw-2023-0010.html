What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow, algorithmic follow-on for choosing the leveling tax in a high-multiplicity population. A natural regime is a large jurisdiction with many residents but relatively few repeated income and preference types. Let a complete type be \((y,u)\), where \(y\) is talent-determined income and \(u\) is the agent’s utility function; mass is the fraction of residents of that type. The veil assumptions correspond to a product distribution \(\nu\times\beta\): income levels follow \(\nu\), utility types follow \(\beta\), independently. The tax decision is a per-capita payment \(\tau_j\) for each income level \(y_j\), with \(0\leq\tau_j\leq y_j\) and \(\sum_j\nu_j\tau_j=\rho\), where \(\rho\) is revenue per capita. A schedule by income class is plausible in this regime: it preserves the paper’s identity-blind taxation question while replacing headcounts with population shares.

The closest result to anchor that mirror is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: under the paper’s assumptions, including independence and equi-probability over incomes, the leveling tax is the only rational tax scheme for the impartial observer. In the proposed population version, the computational question would be: given a rational finite-support income distribution \(\nu=\{(y_j,\nu_j)\}_{j=1}^k\) and revenue \(\rho\), compute the rational tax schedule \(\tau\) selected by the leveling-tax result. A solution is a vector satisfying the revenue and bounds constraints, with a common post-tax income among taxpayers who pay positive tax; equivalently, \(\tau_j=\max\{y_j-\ell,0\}\) for a threshold \(\ell\) chosen to meet the revenue constraint. The expected direction is tractability: sorting the \(k\) income levels and solving for \(\ell\) gives an efficient exact algorithm for rational inputs.

There is a decisive qualification: Theorem 3 is not a named computational result. It characterizes the rational tax scheme; it does not establish that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classify its complexity. The other named results—Propositions 1–3 and Theorems 1–2—are likewise characterizations about anonymity and rationality, not complexity results. So under the requested anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. The leveling-tax computation is a well-specified continuous-population follow-on to Theorem 3, but it cannot honestly be presented as a mirror of a computational result the authors proved.

The softest point in the positive case is also its main modeling constraint: the mirror inherits the paper’s strong independence assumption between income and utility type. The large-population regime is sensible, but whether that independence is credible in a particular tax setting is a separate question. The paper’s result supports the mathematical formulation; it supplies neither a complexity contribution nor evidence that the assumption holds empirically.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the strongest anchor, and its population mirror is easy to formulate. A jurisdiction with many residents and a small number of income and utility types is a sensible high-multiplicity setting. Scaling revenue to a per-capita amount \(\rho\), one can represent the income distribution by finite support \(\{(y_j,\nu_j)\}\) and compute the leveling schedule \(\tau_j=\max\{y_j-\ell,0\}\), where \(\ell\) satisfies \(\sum_j\nu_j\tau_j=\rho\).

The difficulty for ChoCo is that Theorem 3 characterizes which tax scheme the impartial observer selects. It does not state the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1, Theorem 2, and Propositions 1–3 likewise establish anonymity and rationality properties; none classifies an algorithmic task. Under the programme’s stated standard, there is therefore no computational result in this paper to mirror.

The proposed computation of the leveling schedule is faithful to Theorem 3, but it is a computational task formulated after the fact. The paper already defines the leveling tax by a threshold, and the finite-support population version takes income masses and per-capita revenue as input. A richer task—such as computing schemes under income–utility correlation or incentive constraints—would add assumptions and questions the paper leaves aside. A continuum of arbitrary distributions would also need a finite input representation before its complexity could be assessed.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a computational anchor for ChoCo. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: the population model is sensible, and a reader who counts theorem-driven computation as a mirror could regard the threshold problem as a modest follow-on. That would broaden the programme’s notion of a mirror beyond the computational results the paper actually proves.
