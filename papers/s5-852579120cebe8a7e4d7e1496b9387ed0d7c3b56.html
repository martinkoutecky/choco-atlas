The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous population object here. Consider a large health system comparing policies for millions of patients who fall into a modest number of standardized health-state and lifetime categories. Let a type be a health duplet \((a,t)\), and let \(\mu_{(a,t)}\) be the fraction of patients with that outcome. Then \(\mu\) is a distribution over health types, with support size \(\tau\) potentially much smaller than the cohort size \(n\). For example, Theorem 4’s concave HYE evaluation has the natural per-person distribution form
\[ \int g(f(a,t))\,d\mu(a,t). \]
That makes a continuous-population extension of the paper’s population-health evaluation object intelligible. The authors could plausibly recognize the connection: their subject is comparing health distributions for a cohort, and this replaces individual counts with proportions.

But that is not an anchor-based case under your criteria. The paper’s numbered Theorems 1–5 characterize social preferences from axioms; none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. For instance, Theorem 4 characterizes concave HYE utilitarianism from BASIC plus worst-off priority at perfect health. It does not compute a policy, compare the complexity of computing that evaluation, or study an algorithmic optimization problem. The continuous lifespan variable \(t\) is also an outcome dimension: the paper keeps the cohort finite.

So I cannot honestly give a prover-ready continuous computational problem *per anchor* or predict whether any such problem is tractable, inherits hardness, or is continuum-specifically hard. Adding feasible policies, resource constraints, and an optimization objective could create one, but those would be new ingredients rather than a computational problem established by a named result in this paper. The positive case is therefore a credible population-level extension of its evaluative model, not a qualifying mirror of a computational result. Its weakest point is decisive for ChoCo’s stated scope: the paper studies axiomatic evaluation, not computational complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorems 1–5 characterize social preferences from axioms; none defines an algorithmic problem or studies its complexity. The other analyst concedes this, and Theorem 4—their example—is no exception: it characterizes concave HYE utilitarianism, but does not ask how to choose a policy or compute an allocation. So there is no qualifying anchor here.

The population-continuous version of the paper’s evaluation model is still coherent. For a health system with many patients in a modest number of standardized categories, one could replace the finite sum by
\[ \int g(f(a,t))\,d\mu(a,t). \]
That is a genuine population mirror, not merely continuity in the lifespan outcome. But with finite support and given \(f\) and \(g\), evaluating this expression is just a weighted sum. If \(f\) and \(g\) are unspecified functions, the paper gives no finite input representation from which to pose a complexity question.

The best computational extension would add treatment options, clinical effects, costs, and capacity constraints, then ask which treatment assignment maximizes that integral. Patient mass by category could make this a sensible high-multiplicity model. But the new problem’s algorithms and hardness would depend on those added choices and constraints. The paper supplies none of them; its axioms only say how to rank the health distributions those policies produce. That could be a worthwhile adjacent project, but it would borrow the paper’s objective rather than mirror one of its results.

So I would argue against presenting this paper as a ChoCo mirror. I would not claim to have proved the universal statement that no worthwhile continuous computational project can arise in any scenario: the large-cohort health-system setting is plausible, and policy optimization there may be worth studying. The honest negative case is narrower and stronger: this paper establishes a continuous-population evaluative object, but no computational question for the programme to classify.
