What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible high multiplicity reading of this paper, but it has no anchor that qualifies under your rule. No numbered result classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. Theorem 1 is a large deviations result; Propositions 1 and 4 concern equilibrium and election outcomes; Propositions 5 and 6 characterize optimal compromise locations. They are substantive results, but presenting them as complexity results would overstate the paper. So there are no anchor-based continuous problems to hand to a prover.

The strongest positive case is the setting of a large legislature with two party blocs and binary private signals. A type could be party affiliation together with signal, giving four types, \(\{L,R\}\times\{-1,+1\}\). As the number of legislators grows, the number of types stays fixed, and their masses describe the proportions in each party-signal group. Type-level voting at each stage, with the vote margin disclosed between stages, is recognizably a population version of the paper’s central model.

The nearest results to anchor on are Proposition 1 and Proposition 4, both proved in the paper, but neither meets the required computational-result criterion. Proposition 1 says that under successive voting with agenda \([L,\{C,R\}]\), if \(\gamma_{-1}\ge(1-x_C)/2\), the specified vote-shifting strategy is an equilibrium that always selects the complete-information Condorcet winner; if also \(\gamma_1\le(1+x_C)/2\), shifting can occur. If \(\gamma_{-1}<(1-x_C)/2\), no equilibrium always selects that winner. Proposition 4 says that under amendment voting, strategy \(\Pi\) is an equilibrium and always elects the complete-information Condorcet winner. These make a continuous-population equilibrium question plausible, but they establish equilibrium properties, not computational complexity.

The main obstacle is that an atomless continuum can erase the mechanism those propositions study. With fixed type masses, an individual voter is non-pivotal; vote margins no longer convey information through strategically pivotal choices in the same way. One could retain finite voter mass and study a high-multiplicity family parameterized by rational type proportions. That would preserve the paper’s game and give a concrete question—compute a type-symmetric equilibrium and determine whether it elects the Condorcet winner—but it is closer to a compressed finite game than to a genuinely atomless voting model.

That is the soft spot in the positive case: the regime is believable, and the population proportions are a natural description, but the paper’s named results do not provide computational anchors, while a literal continuum threatens to change its strategic question. I would call this a promising extension of the paper’s model, not a strong anchor-based continuization case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here for ChoCo to mirror. Propositions 1 and 4 characterize equilibria and their election outcomes; Propositions 5 and 6 characterize compromise locations. Theorem 1 supplies a large deviations tool. None asks a computational question or classifies its complexity. Computing an equilibrium could be a new problem, but the paper states no such problem, so these results cannot serve as computational anchors.

The strongest population story is credible: in a large legislature, party affiliation and private signal give four types, \((j,s)\in\{L,R\}\times\{-1,+1\}\). That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and I would not reject it on grounds of individual identity or lack of repeated types. It still does not supply the missing computational target.

For Proposition 1, one might ask whether a continuous version of successive voting has an equilibrium that elects the Condorcet winner. Proposition 4 suggests the analogous question for amendment voting. But the paper’s strategic mechanism depends on finite voters’ chance of being pivotal and on vote margins revealing information about a random signal count. With fixed type masses, that count becomes deterministic as the population grows, so the margin loses the uncertainty it was meant to reveal. If aggregate uncertainty is retained, an atomless voter has zero influence on the outcome: individual ballots cannot select or discipline the informative strategies that the finite game’s pivotality conditions support. This is a degeneration of the strategic object, not an objection that a continuous theorem might give a different answer.

Keeping the finite electorate and encoding its type counts compactly could support an algorithmic study of large Bayesian voting games. But it retains finite pivotality; it does not establish that an atomless population captures the propositions’ equilibrium question. Making positive-mass blocs strategic players or adding common shocks could restore some structure, but those are new primitives and a new game.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s stated criterion: there is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, but no named computational result to anchor a mirror, and the closest equilibrium results lose their strategic selection mechanism in the atomless limit. The universal claim is weaker if “any scenario” permits inventing a different computational problem around equilibrium computation. That could be worthwhile research, but it would be a new project rather than a continuous mirror of this paper.
