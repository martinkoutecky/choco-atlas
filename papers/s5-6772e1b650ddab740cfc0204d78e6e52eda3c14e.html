The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(T_U,T_W\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), allowed type pairs, strict rankings over opposite-side types, and a fixed link template \(H\), what is the maximum total matching mass among feasible type-level matchings for which there is no positive-measure set of mutually improving agent pairs at distance at most two in the graph of links and matching edges?

The model it lives in

Use a mass coupling \(x_{tu}\ge 0\) for each allowed pair, with row and column sums bounded by the corresponding type masses. Each link in \(H\) connects the associated type classes, and every individual still has at most one partner. Evaluate local stability in the induced population graph: rule out positive-measure sets of accessible agents who both prefer one another to their current partners, treating being unmatched as worst.

The objection that survived

The positive-mass blocking convention is not derived as a limit of the paper’s rule forbidding every local blocking pair, so its relationship to finite clone markets needs formalization.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers the maximum-cardinality locally stable matching objective in Corollary 4 and its independent-set reduction in Theorem 10; leaves reachability, memory dynamics, and roommates existence results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the positive-measure blocking rule agree with an appropriate limit of finite clone markets, and is type-level mass coupling sufficient to characterize it?
  • Does the independent-set construction or its \(1.5-\epsilon\) inapproximability gap survive when type mass can split among partners?
  • What is the complexity as a function of the number of types \(\tau\), especially for fixed \(\tau\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Corollary 4, proved in this paper using an independent-set hardness result cited from elsewhere. It says that, assuming the Unique Games Conjecture, maximum locally stable matching cannot be approximated within \(1.5-\epsilon\), with \(\epsilon\) tending to \(0\) as the instance size grows. The paper’s Theorem 10, also proved here, supplies the reduction: for every graph \(G\), it constructs a network whose maximum locally stable matching has size \(|V(G)|+k\) exactly when \(G\)’s maximum independent set has size \(k\).

A credible high-multiplicity setting is a large hiring market with many applicants and vacancies, where applicants and vacancies fall into recurring classes: the same qualifications or job category, the same ranking of partner classes, and the same role in the referral network through which agents discover one another. There may be many people per class, while the number \(\tau\) of distinct classes is much smaller than the population. A type must include its side of the market, its preference order, and its information-network role. Two agents with different referral roles are different types even if their preferences match. This is a regime claim about repeated market roles, not a claim that every matching market is high-multiplicity.

The mirror I would put to a prover is Continuous Maximum Locally Stable Matching. An instance has finite type sets \(T_U,T_W\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a set of allowed type pairs, and strict rankings of opposite-side types for each type. Individuals of one type are interchangeable, so their rankings are strict between types and they are indifferent among members of the same type. The instance also has a finite link template \(H\): a link between two types means every individual in one class has the corresponding fixed link to every individual in the other class. This gives agents of a type the same network role.

A feasible matching pairs individuals across the two sides, uses only allowed pairs, and gives each individual at most one partner. The type-level variable \(x_{tu}\) is the mass paired between types \(t\) and \(u\); its row and column sums cannot exceed the corresponding type masses. These variables describe aggregate mass, while each individual still receives an integral partner. Require local stability in the paper’s sense: in the graph formed by the fixed links and the matching edges, there must be no positive-measure set of allowed pairs at distance at most two such that both agents strictly prefer each other to their current partners, treating being unmatched as worse than any acceptable partner. The question is to find a locally stable matching maximizing \(\sum_{t,u}x_{tu}\), the mass of matched pairs. Equivalently, given rational \(K\), ask whether there is a locally stable matching with at least \(K\) mass of pairs.

This keeps the paper’s underlying problem in view: same matching decisions, fixed information links, two-hop accessibility, and mutual improvement. The mass table is the average over many agents, not a fractional bundle handed to an individual. The preference ties among indistinguishable members of a type are a consequence of grouping agents by the complete behavior the model uses; strict improvement still defines blocking.

My first prediction is Class B, hardness transferring from the discrete problem. Theorem 10’s independent-set combinatorics live in the network of agent roles, not in the number of copies of each role, so the graph structure could persist when each role has large mass. A prover would need to establish that the Unique Games approximation gap survives the type-level mass formulation. Useful follow-up questions are whether fixed \(\tau\) admits an efficient optimization method, whether the continuous optimum can be rounded to a large finite market with a controlled loss, and whether fractional mass assignments improve the optimum over every finite clone market.

The weakest point is that this transfer is not established by Corollary 4. Its reduction uses graph-specific network roles, so a direct encoding may require \(\tau\) to grow with the graph. Replicating each role gives many agents per type, but the continuous matching can split each type’s mass among partners and may erase the independent-set gap. That is the central question for the mirror, not evidence that the question is out of scope. My case covers the paper’s maximum-cardinality locally stable matching result only; it does not try to continuize the reachability or memory results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Corollary 4 as an anchor is that its independent-set reduction is built from agents with graph-specific identities. In Theorem 10, each vertex of \(G\) gets its own matching roles, and the links between roles encode that vertex’s particular neighbors. Those links are part of the definition of local stability, not incidental data.

That makes exact high multiplicity difficult. If we clone each role but retain strict rankings over individual partners and the original link semantics, clones are distinguishable by the identities they rank and the particular agents they link to. Their complete types then split with the population. If we merge them into role types, preferences become indifferent among same-type individuals and links between adjacent role types become all-to-all. Local accessibility and blocking now describe a dense, type-based market, rather than the paper’s identity-level network. In sparse networks, the problem is sharper: individual links have vanishing mass in a nonatomic limit, so a mass-based blocking condition can lose the locality the paper studies.

The proposed hiring-market mirror has not resolved that gap. It needs to show that its aggregate stability notion corresponds to individual local stability, and that the independent-set construction still works when type mass can split. The paper’s reduction alone establishes neither. A repeated-module construction also needs care: preserving each module’s private links while allowing matches across modules introduces correlations that a simple mass table by role may not capture.

But these objections do not defeat the best version of the mirror. One could use a dense network of recurring role classes, where each class has positive mass and fixed links connect whole classes. That is a coherent population model, and the independent-set constraints may survive through the support of the matched mass. The gap and the continuous optimum would need to be proved, but uncertainty about the answer is a reason to ask the question, not a reason to reject it. So the honest negative case is weak: the strict, sparse-network formulation may resist continuization, but I cannot rule out a worthwhile typed-network mirror of this anchor.
