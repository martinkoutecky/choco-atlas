The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a rule compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its findings are empirical: committees diverged from the core over time in Treatment 1, and an empty core showed no detectable increase in instability in Treatment 2. The McKelvey and Schofield results are cited background, not computational results established by this paper.

So there are no qualifying anchors, and no per anchor continuous problem or complexity class to give without inventing a result. A large committee with a finite set of ideal point types could be a plausible continuous population model, but asking about its majority stability or reachability would be a new computational formalization—not a mirror of a named result here. The paper studies five person committees experimentally, not a high multiplicity computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no anchors to defeat: the proponent identifies none, correctly. This paper proves no computational result. Its contributions are empirical findings from twelve five-person committees per treatment: outcomes moved away from the core in one treatment, while the other showed no detectable increase in instability. Those observations do not state the complexity of any problem.

The strongest candidate for a mirror would take the reported dynamics as its target: represent a larger committee by masses of voter types and ask whether its outcomes converge toward the core or cycle when the core is empty. But that would require a new behavioral model. The paper’s trajectories depend on who becomes agenda setter, what that person proposes, how the other four vote, and how the same participants act over twenty periods. Ideal-point masses alone do not specify those choices. A richer type space could encode behavioral dispositions and history, but then the resulting transition or equilibrium problem would be newly formulated; it would not continuize a computational result in this paper.

One could also ask for an algorithm to find an agenda setter’s best proposal against a continuous electorate. That may be a worthwhile problem in its own right, but the paper neither poses nor analyzes it. The nearest plausible mirrors therefore lead either to empirical or dynamic-behavior questions, or to new optimization problems with objectives and assumptions supplied from outside the paper. That is the strongest case against spending this programme’s effort on a continuous mirror of this article.
