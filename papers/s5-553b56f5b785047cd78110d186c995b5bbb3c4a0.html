The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(E\), an explicit partition matroid \(M\) of rank \(k\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over at most two complete rankings of \(E\), and a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether there is a ranking \(\sigma\) with \(\Top_k(\sigma)\in\mathcal B(M)\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t d_{\mathrm{KT}}(\sigma,t)\le q\).

The model it lives in

The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over at most two complete rankings of \(E\). The decision variable is one consensus ranking \(\sigma\); its objective is average Kendall distance to the population, subject to its top-\(k\) set being a base of the explicit partition matroid \(M\).

What the mirror covers

Covers the two- and three-voter threshold hardness results and their stated extension to every fixed \(m\ge2\); it leaves the closest-ranking algorithm and rank-aggregation approximation guarantees unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which restrictions on the partition matroid or candidate set make continuous Matroid-FRA exactly tractable?
  • What approximation guarantees hold for arbitrary rational mass vectors as functions of support size and mass bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Matroid-FRA problem, with its electorate represented by a distribution over complete rankings. A type is a strict ranking \(t\) of the same candidate set \(E\); voters with that ranking are interchangeable because the objective uses only their preferences. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types. For a rank-\(k\) matroid \(M=(E,\mathcal B)\), the output is still one ranking \(\sigma\), and its cost is the population-average Kendall distance, \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t d_{\mathrm{KT}}(\sigma,t)\). The task is to minimize that cost subject to \(\Top_k(\sigma)\in\mathcal B\). Equivalently, given a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether such a feasible \(\sigma\) exists with cost at most \(q\). The matroid can be given explicitly as a partition matroid, as in the hardness results.

Call this Two-Type Continuous Matroid-FRA when \(\tau\le 2\), and Three-Type Continuous Matroid-FRA when \(\tau\le 3\). The types are complete ballots, mass is the fraction of the electorate holding each ballot, the decision variable is the consensus ranking, and the objective is expected disagreement with a randomly selected voter. A plausible regime is a citywide consultation or large platform ranking a fixed slate of candidates or projects: millions of participants share a small number of ballot types, while the top-\(k\) recommendations must satisfy group quotas or reserved-slot feasibility. The paper’s partition matroids already model such prefix constraints. Its weighted-tournament formulation also points directly toward this mirror: replace the fraction of input rankings preferring \(e\) to \(f\) by \(\sum_{t:e\prec_t f}\mu_t\).

My lead anchor is Theorem 24, proved in this paper by a reduction from Perfect 3-Dimensional Matching. It states that it is NP-complete, given a Matroid-FRA instance with \(m=2\) voters and a threshold \(\theta\), to decide whether there is a feasible ranking whose sum of Kendall distances to the two input rankings is at most \(\theta\). The reduction uses a partition matroid. Set \(\mu\) to mass \(1/2\) on each input ranking. Then the continuous objective is exactly half the paper’s objective, so the threshold becomes \(q=\theta/2\), and the feasible rankings are unchanged. Thus the equal-mass subfamily of Two-Type Continuous Matroid-FRA is NP-hard; with an explicit partition matroid, its threshold version is in NP as well.

A second, independent anchor is Theorem 26, also proved here, by reduction from Exact Cover by 3-Sets. It states that the corresponding threshold decision problem for Matroid-FRA with \(m=3\) voters is NP-complete. Its reduction again uses a partition matroid. Assigning mass \(1/3\) to each input ranking scales the objective by \(1/3\), so the threshold becomes \(q=\theta/3\). This gives NP-hardness for Three-Type Continuous Matroid-FRA. Theorem 24 is my stronger anchor because it gives the cleaner two-type embedding; Theorem 26 shows the same phenomenon for three types through a separate construction.

Both mirrors should be recognizable to the authors as continuous versions of their own aggregation problem: the only change is to specify ballot frequencies as rational masses and use average rather than total disagreement. A population can then be arbitrarily large while retaining two or three types—for example, by replicating each ballot in its equal-mass proportion. I expect hardness to transfer in both cases. These reductions put the combinatorics in the alternatives and the matroid’s partition classes, not in a large number of voters; the continuum leaves those structures intact. This covers the two- and three-voter hardness results, not the paper’s closest-ranking algorithms or approximation guarantees.

The weakest point is also the boundary these anchors establish: their source instances have only two or three voters, so they do not demonstrate a complexity gain from high multiplicity. Replication makes the population regime sensible, and the continuous problem remains well posed, but the result is a hard continuous mirror rather than evidence that continuization makes aggregation easier. It also implies that parameterizing only by support size \(\tau\) cannot yield an FPT exact algorithm unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\), since hardness already holds at \(\tau=2\). Natural follow-up questions are whether additional restrictions on the matroid or agenda restore exact tractability, and how weighted continuous instances behave under approximation guarantees.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t honestly defeat either anchor under the programme’s rules. For Theorem 24, put mass \(1/2\) on each input ranking. The continuous objective is exactly half the paper’s objective, so the threshold scales to \(\theta/2\), with the same feasible rankings and partition matroid. A large consultation split between two ballot blocs is a plausible population regime: voters with the same ranking are interchangeable for every part of this problem. Theorem 26 transfers just as directly with mass \(1/3\) on each ranking and threshold \(\theta/3\). Replicating either bloc any number of times preserves the support size and the problem.

The stronger mirror—arbitrary rational masses on a support of complete rankings, with the same matroid constraint and average Kendall cost—also survives. It is a well-posed computational question whose input size can be studied in \(\tau\), the mass bit lengths, the candidate set, and the matroid representation. The paper’s weighted-tournament formulation is useful background, but it does not classify the exact complexity of this mass-specified problem. Nor can I object that the reductions use few types or that hardness transfers: those are expressly not grounds for rejecting a mirror.

The honest limitation is that these anchors show no computational gain from continuization; they do not establish one. That weakens the evidence for the programme’s tractability motivation, but it does not defeat the mirrors. Both anchors survive, so the negative case is weak.
