The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), delegate count \(D\), a finite support \(T\) of plurality ballot types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a viable set \(\mathcal V\), and a claimed integer allocation \(a\) summing to \(D\), let \(Q=\sum_{t:\,\mathrm{first}(t)\in\mathcal V}\mu_t>0\) and \(p_c=\sum_{t:\,\mathrm{first}(t)=c}\mu_t/Q\). With a fixed tie rule, decide whether Hamilton largest-remainder allocation from \((p_c)_{c\in\mathcal V}\) and \(D\) yields \(a\).

The model it lives in

A society is a finite distribution \(\mu\) over first-choice ballot types; \(\mathcal V\) is given and delegates remain integral. There are no optimization variables: decide whether the claimed allocation \(a\) equals the Hamilton allocation computed from the qualified masses.

What the mirror covers

Covers allocation correctness conditional on a supplied plurality-viable set \(\mathcal V\); leaves viability verification, finite-paper sampling, risk limits, and audit-workload selection outside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow: Theorem 5.1, proved in this paper, gives a continuous-mass certificate for the delegate-allocation stage. It says that if the reported integer delegate vector \(a\), with \(\sum_{c\in\mathcal V}a_c=D\), is wrong for the true qualified-vote shares \(p_c\), then some ordered pair \(n\ne m\) of viable candidates violates
\[ p_m > p_n+\frac{a_m-a_n-1}{D}. \]
Equivalently, if all these pairwise difference assertions hold, the reported allocation is correct. This is a correctness theorem used to construct the paper’s audits, not a theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. The paper proves no named complexity classification; its experiments and branch-and-bound procedure do not supply one. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.08510)

A precise mirror of that anchor is Continuous Hamilton Allocation Certification. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a plurality viability threshold \(\theta\), a delegate count \(D\), a finite support \(T\) of ballot types, rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), an already-established viable set \(\mathcal V\), and a claimed allocation \(a\). A type records the ballot’s first choice, including any rule-relevant invalid or unmarked category. Let \(Q\) be the mass of ballots for viable candidates and \(p_c\) candidate \(c\)’s mass divided by \(Q\). The task is to decide whether \(a\) is the Hamilton allocation. A YES certificate lists the pairwise assertions; a NO certificate gives a violated pair. Use a fixed tie rule, or promise that no relevant quota-remainder ties occur, to match the theorem’s strict inequalities. This is a decision problem: its objective is exact certification, not an optimization objective.

The regime is credible for plurality-based primaries: the paper reports 5,784,364 ballots and 21 candidates for California. In a first-choice model, those millions of voters fall into only \(O(m)\) rule-relevant types. The type is the ballot choice; mass is its share of the electorate. Delegates remain integral, as in the paper. The authors should recognize the pairwise inequalities as their allocation-correctness question: their theorem and audit already work with vote proportions.

I expect this restricted mirror to be tractable: tally the rational masses, compute \(p_c\), and check \(O(|\mathcal V|^2)\) inequalities, with polynomial dependence on \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. Its scope is only the delegate-allocation stage conditional on the viable set. A further, more ambitious question would choose assertions to minimize expected audit samples under a specified sampling and discrepancy model.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s main problem is a risk-limiting audit of finite paper ballots, with sampling and a full-count fallback. This mirror checks an allocation from an exact distribution; it does not preserve that statistical task. Also, at this stage the paper already expresses the allocation entirely through proportions, so the continuous formulation is natural but risks restating a part of the original model. The theorem supports a modest positive case for the allocation-certificate question, not a claim that continuization unlocks a new complexity landscape for the paper’s full audit problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed anchor is not a computational-complexity result. The paper’s sole theorem proves a correctness certificate: if the reported delegate vector is wrong, some pairwise difference assertion fails. Given exact masses and an already-known viable set, the proposed mirror checks those assertions in \(O(|\mathcal V|^2)\) rational comparisons. The proof and the computation carry over directly from ballot proportions to real masses. That makes the certificate a very thin mirror of the paper’s theorem, rather than a new computational question the paper leaves open.

It also leaves out the paper’s central task. Continuous Hamilton Allocation Certification assumes exact vote shares and a viable set; the paper builds evidence that those shares and that set are correct by sampling a finite paper trail, with a risk limit and a full-count fallback. A stronger population model could encode ballot types together with their reported records and discrepancies. If it supplies the true masses, however, the audit’s uncertainty has already been resolved. If it supplies only reported records, it cannot establish the true outcome. Modeling sampling from a distribution could yield a useful statistical-design problem, but it changes the guarantee from one about a finite election’s records to one about a sampling model. That problem would need its own formulation and results; Theorem 5.1 does not anchor it.

I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible here. Millions of first-choice ballots can fall into only a small number of rule-relevant types, and the paper’s use of proportions makes a mass representation natural. The negative case is narrower: the proponent’s exact-certification mirror is essentially the paper’s own allocation certificate with fractional masses substituted for ballot counts. A richer continuous audit-planning question may be worthwhile, but it is a new research direction rather than a computational result of this paper waiting to be continuized.

So the case against this anchor is credible, while the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror is weak. The paper’s scale and ballot-type structure make that claim hard to defend honestly.
