The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), distinct positive rational weights \(w_t\), a rational quota share \(q\) with \(0<q<\sum_t\mu_t w_t\), and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), consider every positive integer \(K\) for which all \(K\mu_t\) are integral; form the weighted game with \(K\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\) and quota \(Kq\). Let \(B_t(K)\) and \(G_t(K)\) be that type's raw Banzhaf and Public Good scores per voter, and set \(p_t(K)=\lambda B_t(K)+(1-\lambda)G_t(K)\). Decide whether some \(K_0\) exists such that for every admissible \(K\ge K_0\) and every pair \(t,s\) with \(w_t>w_s\), \(p_t(K)\ge p_s(K)\).

The model it lives in

A weighted voting society is given by rational masses \(\mu_t\) on types with weights \(w_t\). For each admissible replication size \(K\), compute the finite game's raw Banzhaf and Public Good score per member of each type, combine them with \(\lambda\), and decide whether the resulting type-level scores eventually respect weight order for all sufficiently large \(K\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror adapts Lemma 7's local-monotonicity verification task to fixed-support replications using the raw Banzhaf and Public Good indices; it leaves the paper's finite-\(n\) cost values, game-class characterizations, other index pairs, and full convex-region computations aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the eventual predicate be decided from binary-encoded \(\mu_t,w_t,q,\lambda\) without enumerating replication sizes \(K\)?
  • Can the threshold for \(\lambda\), or a finite certificate of eventual monotonicity, be characterized in terms of support size \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is narrow: a high multiplicity version of local monotonicity verification for weighted voting power indices. The paper gives a usable algorithmic anchor, but no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification result. I would not claim that the paper already establishes the complexity of the continuous problem.

Take a large cooperative federation whose members vote yes or no on proposals. Many members share one of a small number of constitutionally assigned voting weights. A type is a weight class, and \(\mu_t\) is that class’s fraction of the membership. A coalition is described in the continuum by its type-mass vector \(x\), with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\); it wins when \(\sum_t w_t x_t\ge q\). Thus the population becomes continuous while the binary decision and weighted voting rule stay intact.

Here is the precise question I would hand to a prover: Typed Replication Local-Monotonicity Verification. An instance consists of rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, distinct rational weights \(w_t>0\), a rational quota share \(q\) with \(0<q<\sum_t\mu_t w_t\), and a rational \(\lambda\in[0,1]\). For each integer \(K\) such that every \(K\mu_t\) is integral, form the finite weighted game with \(K\mu_t\) voters of type \(t\), each of weight \(w_t\), and quota \(Kq\). Let \(B_t(K)\) and \(G_t(K)\) be the paper’s raw Banzhaf and Public Good scores per voter of type \(t\). The score of that type is
\[ p_t(K)=\lambda B_t(K)+(1-\lambda)G_t(K). \]
The question is whether there is a \(K_0\) such that for every admissible \(K\ge K_0\), \(w_t\ge w_s\) implies \(p_t(K)\ge p_s(K)\). A yes answer needs a proof valid for all sufficiently fine replications; a no answer needs a violating pair of types and violations for infinitely many \(K\). Equivalently, one can study the eventual minimum Banzhaf share needed for local monotonicity.

The model preserves the paper’s raw indices: in each finite refinement, Banzhaf still counts swing coalitions and Public Good still counts minimal winning coalitions. For example, a type-\(t\) Banzhaf score can be computed by summing, over coalition count vectors \(x\) for the other voters, the number \(\prod_s {K\mu_s-\mathbf 1[s=t]\choose x_s}\) of coalitions satisfying \(\sum_s w_sx_s<Kq\le\sum_s w_sx_s+w_t\). The continuous input is the population distribution; the finite refinements define what it means for these coalition-count indices to have an eventual type-level comparison. This keeps the question computational and population-continuous without changing the outcomes into lotteries or fractional decisions.

My anchor is Lemma 7, proved in this paper. It says that for a collection of the paper’s raw indices, a multiplier vector \(\alpha\), and a game class \(\mathcal G\), one can decide whether the convex combination is locally monotonic on all \(n\)-player games in \(\mathcal G\), provided the incidence vectors of those games form a polyhedron. The paper supplies ILP formulations for the relevant game classes and indices; the computation tests adjacent-player score differences and returns a violating game if one exists. This is an algorithmic anchor for the underlying LM verification problem, not a polynomial-time guarantee: the lemma gives no runtime bound in \(n\) or the encoding length. I use its Banzhaf/Public Good case; the mirror does not cover all six indices or every result in the paper.

I expect the fixed-support version to be tractable, tentatively, perhaps with support size \(\tau\) as a parameter: both indices can be expressed through type-count vectors rather than named voters. But the challenge is to handle every replication size \(K\) from its binary-encoded mass data without iterating through all \(K\) voters. If that arithmetic cannot be compressed, the problem could instead be continuum-specifically hard. The paper establishes neither outcome, and it contains no hardness result that could transfer. Further questions are whether the eventual threshold is computable exactly, whether it has a finite symbolic certificate, and how complexity changes when \(\tau\) grows.

The case is plausible because the authors’ problem remains recognizable: weighted voting games, coalition-based power indices, and the same per-voter comparison between voting weight and power. It also has a natural high multiplicity regime: many members but few weight classes. The paper’s broader asymptotic results on the cost of local monotonicity make population growth especially relevant.

The weak point is fundamental: the paper defines power by counting coalitions, and a nonatomic coalition does not have a canonical counting measure. This mirror therefore defines type power through finite refinements; it does not claim to have found a unique intrinsic continuum power index. Also, the paper’s asymptotic obstructions can use a unique heavy voter whose population share vanishes as the electorate grows. Those examples motivate the question but do not answer it in the fixed-positive-mass type regime above. That limit on the claim is also why I would present this as a credible, focused mirror rather than a continuization of the whole paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Lemma 7 is a real algorithmic anchor, so I would not claim the paper has no computational result. But its task is specific: for a fixed \(\alpha\), test local monotonicity across every \(n\)-player game in a class, using a finite incidence encoding with a variable for each coalition. The proposed mirror instead asks whether one rational type distribution has an eventually monotone sequence of finite replications. That is a new asymptotic problem, not a direct continuization of Lemma 7. A closer mirror would need a continuous version of the power indices and then test monotonicity across a class of continuous societies.

That is where the fundamental difficulty lies. In an atomless society, removing one person changes no coalition mass, so individual Banzhaf decisiveness vanishes. Minimal winning coalitions can lie on the quota boundary, but the Public Good index counts them, and a continuum of agents supplies no canonical way to count those coalitions. A mass measure on coalition vectors, a random coalition model, or a boundary-density rule could define new indices; each adds a choice the paper’s definitions do not make. Local monotonicity of a convex combination then depends on those choices.

Exact replication is the strongest repair, and it is a coherent high-multiplicity question. But it asks about raw counts along a sequence of finite games, where lattice and lower-order effects can determine the comparison. It does not produce a power index on the continuous society itself. An entropy or boundary-density formulation might be a worthwhile new study, but it would require specifying a new index or coalition-selection model before Lemma 7’s computation could be mirrored.

So the negative case is limited: it defeats an *intrinsic* continuous counterpart of Lemma 7, but it cannot show that the proposed replication question is unworthy. The fixed positive masses avoid the paper’s vanishing-singleton examples, and the replication problem is mathematically well-posed. Under ChoCo’s high-multiplicity standard, that leaves this anchor plausibly alive.
