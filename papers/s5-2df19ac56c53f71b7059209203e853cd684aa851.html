The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results establishes a problem as hard, tractable, or parameterized-tractable. Its propositions and lemmas characterize optimal persuasion and voter welfare. So, under your anchor rule, there is no fully qualifying positive case.

The closest structural anchor is Proposition 4.2, proved in this paper. For exchangeable, affiliated payoff states, it says there is an optimal individual-persuasion policy in which the probability of recommending approval in the low state rises with leniency: \(\pi_i(L)\leq\pi_{i+1}(L)\). It also says that if voter \(R_i\)’s approval constraint binds, every more demanding voter \(R_j\), \(j<i\), has a strictly lower low-state approval probability: \(\pi_j(L)<\pi_i(L)\). This is a result about the shape of an optimum, not its computational complexity.

The strongest nearby mirror I can make is a high-multiplicity limit of individual persuasion. Imagine a large membership body whose many delegates must unanimously approve a project. Delegates have one of \(\tau\) doubt-threshold types \(t\), with threshold \(\ell_t\) and population share \(\mu_t\). Their payoff states are correlated through a common latent project-quality parameter \(q\): draw \(q\) from a finite rational distribution \(G\), then draw each delegate’s state independently, high with probability \(q\). The sender chooses a type-specific individual experiment. By Lemma 4.1, an optimum recommends approval for sure in the high state, so its remaining decision variables are \(p_t=\pi_t(L)\).

For each population size \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) integral, define \(z_t(q)=q+(1-q)p_t\). The sender maximizes

\[ V_N(p)=\int \prod_t z_t(q)^{N\mu_t}\,dG(q) \]

subject, for every type \(t\), to the representative delegate’s approval constraint

\[ \int q\prod_s z_s(q)^{N\mu_s-\mathbf 1_{s=t}}\,dG(q) \;\geq\; \ell_t p_t \int (1-q)\prod_s z_s(q)^{N\mu_s-\mathbf 1_{s=t}}\,dG(q), \qquad 0\leq p_t\leq1. \]

Call this High-Multiplicity Individual Persuasion: given \((T,\mu,(\ell_t),G)\), determine whether the optimal value converges as \(N\) grows, compute that limit, and characterize limiting optimal policies. Proposition 4.2 supplies a concrete prediction for the finite problems: optimal \(p_t\) should be nondecreasing with leniency. This candidate covers the paper’s individual-persuasion results on unanimous adoption; it does not cover the general-versus-independent equivalence or the non-unanimous-rule results.

The regime is plausible: \(N\) delegates can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of threshold types, and the type is a complete description of the delegate’s payoff threshold for this decision. The authors would likely recognize the model as a high-multiplicity version of their individual persuasion problem. The paper’s assumption of distinct thresholds would need relaxing to allow repeated types.

The weakest point is substantial: exact unanimity behaves badly in a literal atomless population, where no individual is pivotal. This formulation therefore keeps the finite unanimous groups and asks for their high-multiplicity limit. That preserves the paper’s strategic question, but makes the mirror less clean than a direct continuum model. And because Proposition 4.2 is not a computational result, I cannot honestly assign this candidate to Class A, B, or C on the paper’s evidence. Further questions include whether the value depends only on \(\mu\) and \(G\), how the strictest types’ \(p_t\) scale with \(N\), and whether the general/independent equivalence survives this limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Proposition 4.2 is a comparative-static characterization: under its assumptions, optimal low-state approval probabilities can be ordered by voter leniency. The other numbered results characterize optimal policies, incentives, and welfare. None states the complexity of a computational problem, gives an algorithm, or identifies an input representation whose complexity could change under continuization. Turning Proposition 4.2 into a monotone policy over a distribution of thresholds would extend its economic characterization, but would not by itself chart the computational landscape ChoCo is meant to study.

The proposed high-multiplicity model is a plausible setting: a large membership body could contain many delegates with the same threshold. But its stated task is to characterize the \(N\to\infty\) limit of finite unanimous games. That is an asymptotic analysis, not an exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithmic question about a society supplied as a distribution. One could instead ask for an algorithm to compute the optimal policy at finite \(N\). That would be a coherent computational follow-up, but it adds an algorithmic problem to the paper; it does not turn Proposition 4.2 into a computational result. And the compact input using threshold types and a latent quality distribution \(G\) adds structure to the paper’s general state law \(f\).

A direct atomless version also faces a deeper problem than a literal change in notation. The paper’s incentives depend on a voter being pivotal when everyone else approves. If adoption means that almost every member approves, an individual has measure zero and cannot change the outcome; the pivotal incentive constraints that drive the results disappear. If unanimity instead means that every individual must approve, the event depends on the entire continuum of actions and is not captured by the mass distribution alone. Replacing unanimity with a positive-mass quota restores a population-level decision, but changes the voting rule and its strategic structure.

So the negative case is strongest against the proposed anchor and its direct continuum interpretation: the former is not a computational result, and the latter loses the pivotality the result characterizes. It is not airtight against a newly formulated finite-\(N\) algorithmic project. Repeated threshold types and a large delegate body make that project plausible; the case against it is that it would be a computational extension of this paper, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper establishes.
