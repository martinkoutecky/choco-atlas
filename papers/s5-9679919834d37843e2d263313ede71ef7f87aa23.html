The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational-complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror: it proves no stated algorithmic upper bound or hardness result for rationalizability.

The closest result is Proposition 8, proved in the paper. For \(n\ge 3\), if the Pareto set is not the set of all allocations, its size is at most \((n-1)!(n-1)\); the equality case also imposes restrictions on which agent-good pair is absent and on the preference profile. The paper uses this to rule out rationalizability for some set sizes. That is a structural necessary condition, not a result that places a computational problem in a complexity class or establishes its hardness.

The introduction notes the enormous number of preference profiles and the desire for a quicker method, but the paper does not give a complexity analysis or algorithm for rationalizability. So I can’t provide a continuous problem *per qualifying anchor* without inventing one. Rationalizability could motivate a future continuous question, but this paper supplies no named computational result on which to build the requested case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed case does not establish a mirror: it identifies no result to mirror. Its only candidate, Proposition 8, is a structural necessary condition on the size of a finite Pareto set. The paper mentions wanting a quicker rationalizability test, but gives no complexity result and no algorithm for deciding rationalizability. That is a serious gap under this programme’s criteria.

The strongest attempt to build on Proposition 8 would be to scale up the population and the goods, then replace assignments by aggregate flows between preference types and good categories. But the proposition’s bound counts permutations of individually named agents and distinct goods. In a mass-flow model, feasible allocations vary continuously, so the Pareto set is generally infinite; the finite cardinality bound has no useful counterpart. Keeping individually named goods and permutation outcomes preserves the proposition’s object, but leaves no fixed finite good-type space as the population grows. Collapsing goods into categories restores a finite-dimensional model while discarding the permutation structure that drives the result.

The cycles do not supply a stronger anchor. They are patterns of swaps among particular agents holding particular goods. In an aggregate model those swaps become flows; if the population distribution is given, preferences are already specified by the types, while inferring an unknown distribution from observed Pareto sets would be a new inverse problem. The paper’s cycle results do not establish a computational claim about that problem.

One could devise a plausible large-cohort setting—students assigned to repeated categories of seats, for example—and ask whether observed aggregate assignments are consistent with some mix of rankings. That may be worth studying on its own. But it requires choosing a new representation for both the observations and the unknown preferences, and Proposition 8’s bound and cycle arguments do not carry over as stated. It is not a continuous mirror this paper supplies.

This does not prove that no adjacent continuous problem could ever be worthwhile. It does make the universal negative hard to prove. The narrower verdict is stronger: this paper offers no named computational result that anchors a worthwhile mirror, and its closest structural result loses its content under the natural mass model.
