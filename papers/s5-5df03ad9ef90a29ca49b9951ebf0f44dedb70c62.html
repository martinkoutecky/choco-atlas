The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), supported voter types \(t\) with rankings \(\pi_t\), swap-cost schedules, and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), choose masses \(x_{t,\pi}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{\pi}x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\). For a designated candidate \(c^\star\) and a natural scoring protocol, find a feasible \(x\) minimizing \(\sum_{t,\pi}d_t(\pi)x_{t,\pi}\), where \(d_t(\pi)\) is the minimum swap cost from \(\pi_t\) to \(\pi\), subject to \(\sum_{t,\pi}s_\pi(c^\star)x_{t,\pi}\geq\sum_{t,\pi}s_\pi(c)x_{t,\pi}\) for every \(c\ne c^\star\).

The model it lives in

A rational LP over type-to-ranking mass transfers: each type is a ranking together with its swap-cost schedule; each unit of mass receives a complete ranking; the objective is total swap cost and the constraints make \(c^\star\) a winner.

The objection that survived

The paper’s algorithm already invokes an optimization oracle for the continuous relaxation, so this mirror may add little computational work; that limits novelty but does not eliminate the distinct fractional-population question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the natural-scoring clause of Theorem 5. It leaves the C1-rule clause and the paper’s other applications aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the LP be solved more efficiently in \(m\) by exploiting the permutation structure instead of listing all \(m!\) rankings?
  • What rounding or integrality-gap bounds relate the continuous optimum to an election with a fixed number of voters?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Continuous Type-Based Scoring Swap Bribery. My anchor is the natural-scoring clause of Theorem 5, proved in this paper: for any natural scoring protocol \(\mathcal R\), \(\mathcal R\)-Swap Bribery can be solved in time \(|C|^{O(|C|^2)}\,O(T^3(\log |V|+\log \sigma_{\max}))\), where \(T\) is the number of voter types and \(\sigma_{\max}\) is the maximum swap cost. This is an algorithmic anchor; the paper gives no comparable named hardness result for this problem.

A type \(t\) is a ranking \(\pi_t\) together with its adjacent-swap cost schedule. The instance gives each supported type a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Choose \(x_{t,\pi}\ge 0\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned its final ranking \(\pi\), subject to \(\sum_{\pi}x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\). Let \(d_t(\pi)\) be the minimum cost of changing \(\pi_t\) to \(\pi\) under that type’s swap costs, and let \(s_\pi(c)\) be candidate \(c\)’s score under \(\pi\). The question is to minimize \(\sum_{t,\pi}d_t(\pi)x_{t,\pi}\), subject to \(\sum_{t,\pi}s_\pi(c^\star)x_{t,\pi}\ge\sum_{t,\pi}s_\pi(c)x_{t,\pi}\) for every \(c\ne c^\star\). A solution is an optimal mass-transfer plan \(x\); each portion of mass still receives a complete, integral ranking.

A plausible regime is a large membership or platform election with hundreds of thousands of voters but far fewer repeated combinations of ranking and outreach-cost schedule. The number of people \(N\) can greatly exceed the number of types \(\tau\). This type definition follows the paper’s own: voters are the same type precisely when both their preferences and swap costs agree. Its input already groups those voters and its running time depends on \(T\) and \(\log |V|\), which is strong evidence that the authors would recognize a mass-based version as their problem. The paper’s variables count voters sent from one type to each final order; the mirror replaces those counts with masses.

I expect this version to be tractable. The displayed formulation is a rational linear program with \(\tau m!\) variables, \(\tau\) mass equations, and \(m-1\) winner inequalities, where \(m=|C|\). Exact LP optimization is therefore fixed-parameter tractable in \(m\) and polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. This is an argument for the continuous problem, not a claim proved by Theorem 5: that theorem optimizes integer counts. A discrete election embeds by setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and restricting transfers to multiples of \(1/N\), while the continuous model allows arbitrary rational splits. Further questions are whether one can exploit the permutation structure for a sharper continuous algorithm, and what rounding or integrality-gap guarantees relate the fluid optimum to an election with fixed \(N\), especially when the winner constraints are tight.

I would cover only the natural-scoring branch of Theorem 5 here, not its C1-rule branch or the paper’s other applications. The soft spot is that the mirror may be mathematically modest: it puts a mass variable on the same type-to-ranking transfers the paper already studies, and the explicit LP is routine once all \(m!\) rankings are listed. Also, the paper’s high-multiplicity integer algorithm already handles large type counts, so this is not a case where the paper’s discrete hardness dissolves. The positive case rests instead on fidelity: the population is the object being redistributed, the paper already identifies the right repeated types, and fractional mass gives a well-posed continuous version of that same bribery question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

My strongest objection is that this particular mirror is already the continuous relaxation used by the paper’s algorithm. If \(N=|V|\), type \(i\) has \(n_i\) voters, and \(x_{i,\pi}\) counts voters of that type sent to ranking \(\pi\), set \(\mu_i=n_i/N\) and \(y_{i,\pi}=x_{i,\pi}/N\). The type constraints become \(\sum_\pi y_{i,\pi}=\mu_i\); the score inequalities and cost objective normalize in the same way. That is exactly the proposed mass-transfer LP. Theorem 3 explicitly calls for optimizing the continuous relaxation, and the swap-bribery formulation has a linear objective and linear constraints. So this formulation’s basic computational answer is already present as a subroutine in the paper.

The direct LP has \(\tau m!\) variables. It gives an exact algorithm polynomial in that expanded size, hence fixed-parameter tractability in \(m\) and polynomial dependence on \(\tau\) and the input bit length. A sharper algorithm that avoids listing rankings would need to solve the associated pricing problem over permutations. That could be a worthwhile research question, as could understanding rounding from the mass optimum to a finite electorate.

I can’t make this into a strong case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The paper’s types already group voters by precisely the relevant complete description—ranking and swap-cost schedule—and large elections with many repeated types are a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The fractional plan remains meaningful, with each portion receiving an integral ranking. The best negative case is therefore narrow: the proponent’s explicit LP mirror is largely a repackaging of a relaxation the paper already uses. The stronger versions they point toward remain open computational questions, so this argument does not defeat the anchor universally.
