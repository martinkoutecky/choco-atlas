What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Proposition 1’s \(Borda^0_k\) ratio case; it leaves the other rule bounds, Theorem 1, and the empirical studies aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The closest defensible anchor is Proposition 1, though there is an important limit: the paper has no named result classifying a problem as in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Proposition 1 is a formal worst-case approximation result, not a complexity theorem. Its full claim is that for a positional scoring vector \(s\), with \(s'_j=s_j-s^*\), the price of top-\(k\) truncation lies between
\[ 1-\frac{s_{k+1}}{s_1} +\frac{s_{k+1}}{s_1}\frac{m s'_1}{\sum_{j=1}^k s'_j} \quad\text{and}\quad 1-\frac{s_{k+1}}{s'_1} +\left(1+\frac{s^*}{s'_1}\right)\frac{m s_{k+1}}{\sum_{j=1}^k s'_j}. \]
The paper proves these bounds and shows they coincide when \(s^*=0\). In particular, it proves that for \(Borda^0_k\), the exact ratio is \(\frac{k}{m-1}+\frac{2m(m-k-1)}{k(2m-k-1)}\). The paper says this matches the optimal ratio from Bentert and Skowron’s Theorem 1; that optimality result is cited from elsewhere.

My lead mirror is the continuous worst-case price of \(Borda^0_k\). Fix \(m\ge 3\), \(2\le k<m\), and a tie-breaking priority. Types are complete rankings \(\pi\in S_m\); a society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those rankings, with \(\mu_\pi\) the fraction of society holding type \(\pi\). Define the full-information score as \(\mathrm{B}_\mu(a)=\sum_\pi\mu_\pi(m-\mathrm{pos}_\pi(a))\), and the truncated score as \(\mathrm{B}^0_{\mu,k}(a)=\sum_{\pi:\mathrm{pos}_\pi(a)\le k}\mu_\pi(m-\mathrm{pos}_\pi(a))\). The instance is \(m,k\) and the tie-breaking priority. The question is to compute the supremum, over all such societies, of the full Borda score of the full-information winner divided by the full Borda score of the \(Borda^0_k\) winner. A solution is the exact supremum; a maximizing distribution is a useful witness. This asks about the quality of the same truncation approximation as the paper, with population fractions replacing voter counts.

A plausible regime is a large membership election with a modest candidate list, where voters fall into a relatively small number of stable preference blocs. For example, many association members might share one of a few complete rankings of finalists. Each ranking is a type, its mass is that bloc’s fraction of members, and the authority observes only each member’s top \(k\). The rule still selects one candidate. The objective still measures the paper’s score distortion. The per-capita scores can be fractional because the society is fractional, while each individual has an ordinary complete ranking.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: the paper’s exact Borda \(Borda^0_k\) ratio is already a closed-form answer, and the finite electorate embeds as the rational distribution \(\mu_\pi=n_\pi/n\). Extending from rational masses to arbitrary real masses does not change the score inequalities that give the bound. Further questions include how the worst-case ratio changes when society is restricted to support size at most \(\tau\), and the smallest \(k\) that ensures a specified distortion bound under that restriction. This mirror covers Proposition 1’s Borda \(Borda^0_k\) case only; it does not cover the empirical winner-match studies, the axiomatic characterization in Theorem 1, or the other rules’ ratio results.

The weak point is that this does not make the paper’s contribution a computational-complexity result. Its central worst-case ratio already ranges over finite profiles of arbitrary size, so the continuous mirror may be judged a natural closure of that analysis rather than a new algorithmic landscape. That limits its value as a ChoCo anchor, but the population mirror itself is direct and recognizable: the complete-ranking types, their masses, the truncation rule, and the distortion objective all come from the paper’s own question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 1 is a distortion theorem, not a computational result in ChoCo’s sense. The paper gives no complexity classification or algorithmic analysis of a computational problem: Theorem 1 is axiomatic, Propositions 1–3 bound approximation quality, and the experiments measure prediction accuracy. A continuous version of Proposition 1 can be well-posed and recognizable without supplying the kind of computational question the programme is looking for.

The proposed Borda\(^{0}_{k}\) mirror is especially close to an exact restatement. A rational mass distribution over rankings is just a finite profile with counts scaled by a common denominator. More strongly, the proof of Proposition 1 uses score inequalities that apply directly to real masses, and its extremal construction has rational multiplicities that can be scaled to a finite profile. So the continuous supremum has the same exact value,
\[ \frac{k}{m-1}+\frac{2m(m-k-1)}{k(2m-k-1)}. \]
There is no new optimization to solve: evaluating a given society’s scores is direct, and the paper already gives the unrestricted worst-case answer.

A large membership organization with a few stable preference blocs is a credible high-multiplicity setting; that is not the weakness. The stronger variant—restricting the worst case to societies with support at most \(\tau\)—is also a sensible follow-up. But it changes the domain of the extremum, which the paper does not study. Its difficulty would come from choosing which rankings appear in the support, a combinatorial restriction also expressible with finite voter profiles. It could motivate a new ChoCo project, but it does not turn this paper’s named distortion result into a continuous computational result.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed anchor, but not airtight against every possible follow-up: mass-based extensions of the paper’s approximation questions are natural. The case rests on the narrower point that the faithful mirror adds neither a new computational task nor a new answer, while the more ambitious variants would be new questions rather than mirrors of the paper’s results.
