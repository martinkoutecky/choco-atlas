The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of provider types \(T\), masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), rates \(\lambda_t<\xi_t\), fixed \(\alpha\in(0,1]\), and a required coalition mass vector \(z\in[0,\mu]\), find the coordinatewise largest minimizer \(y^*\) of \(V_\alpha(y)=\frac{\Lambda(y)^\alpha}{\Xi(y)^\alpha-\Lambda(y)^\alpha}\) over \(z\le y\le\mu\), and return its cost, where \(\Lambda(y)=\sum_t y_t\lambda_t\), \(\Xi(y)=\sum_t y_t\xi_t\), and \(V_\alpha(0)=0\).

The model it lives in

Model many small service providers as a distribution over rate-profile types. A coalition is a mass vector \(y\); its pooled arrival and potential service rates are \(\Lambda(y)\) and \(\Xi(y)\), and its cost is the paper’s expected queue length \(V_\alpha(y)\). Find the largest cost-minimizing coalition containing a required mass \(z\); scaling both aggregate rates by population size leaves this cost unchanged.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s largest best-superset computation and the calculation of \(W(S)\); it leaves the core characterization, the results on nonnegative and negative allocations, and the Shapley-value discussion aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness result or conventional complexity classification. Its strongest computational anchor is Theorem 3, proved here. For any coalition \(S\), the paper defines \(W(S)=\min_{T\supseteq S}V(T)\) and calls the *largest* coalition attaining that minimum the optimal coalition \(\tilde S\). Theorem 3 proves that its Construction Algorithm returns \(\tilde S\), and hence \(W(S)=V(\tilde S)\). The algorithm scans servers once in order of increasing utilization, adding the next server when doing so does not increase the current pooled cost; the paper states that this takes linear time in \(n\). This is an exact algorithmic result, rather than a theorem explicitly classifying a problem as “in P.”

A direct mirror is Continuous Best-Superset Pooling. Let \(T\) be a finite support of provider types. A type \(t\) has arrival rate \(\lambda_t\), potential service rate \(\xi_t>\lambda_t\), and society mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The shared parameter \(\alpha\in(0,1]\) is fixed. An instance also gives a required coalition mass vector \(z\), where \(0\le z_t\le\mu_t\). For any coalition vector \(y\in[0,\mu]\), set \(\Lambda(y)=\sum_t y_t\lambda_t\), \(\Xi(y)=\sum_t y_t\xi_t\), and, for \(y\ne0\),
\[ V_\alpha(y)=\frac{\Lambda(y)^\alpha}{\Xi(y)^\alpha-\Lambda(y)^\alpha}, \]
with \(V_\alpha(0)=0\). The problem is to find the coordinatewise largest \(y^*\) minimizing \(V_\alpha(y)\) over \(z\le y\le\mu\), and return its cost. It asks which additional mass of providers should join a required coalition to make the resulting pooled system as cheap as possible—the same question Theorem 3 answers for a finite set of servers.

This has a credible high-multiplicity regime: thousands of small service hubs or repair centers in a shared dispatch network, with only dozens of recurring demand and staffing profiles. A type records the complete rate profile relevant to the paper’s cost model; mass is the fraction of hubs with that profile. A fractional coalition is sensible when hubs are small relative to the network. The correspondence to the discrete model is especially clean: a coalition with normalized mass \(y\) has actual rates \(n\Lambda(y)\) and \(n\Xi(y)\) among \(n\) providers. The paper’s pooled cost is unchanged when both rates are multiplied by \(n\), so the mass model preserves the objective exactly.

The expected result is Class A. Sort the supported types by \(r_t=\lambda_t/\xi_t\), start at \(z\), and consider the available mass of types from lowest \(r_t\) upward. Add all remaining mass of a type while the current pooled ratio \(\Lambda(y)/\Xi(y)\) is at least \(r_t\); stop at the first type with a larger ratio. The pooled ratio is a weighted average of the type ratios, and \(V_\alpha\) increases with that average. Thus the rule gives a largest minimizing completion, including all tied types at the boundary. It needs \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) time to sort and scan the \(\tau\) supported types, independent of the number \(n\) of providers. For fixed rational \(\alpha\), the selected mass vector is rational; the exact cost can be returned in the displayed form.

The paper’s stochastic arrivals are continuous in time, but its players—the service providers—are finite. That is not a collision with this mirror: the continuous object here is the provider population. Nor does prior work on continuous customer cost allocation answer the same question, since the players and decision are different.

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s optimal-superset computation and the calculation of \(W\), not the paper’s full core characterization or its results on negative allocations and Shapley values. It naturally raises a further problem: characterize the continuous pooling core, whose per-unit type charges \(p_t\) must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t=V_\alpha(\mu)\) and \(\sum_t z_t p_t\le V_\alpha(z)\) for every \(z\in[0,\mu]\). This is the continuous analogue of the paper’s coalition-stability constraints; the finite proof does not by itself settle its complexity or characterize all feasible charges.

The weak point is the regime assumption. Providers are often firms that cannot split, and a fractional coalition is only persuasive when many small, nearly identical units can pool under the paper’s shared-queue model. This would be a poor mirror for a handful of large strategic firms. Also, since the anchor is an algorithmic result rather than a hardness result, this case does not establish that continuization dissolves discrete hardness; the paper offers no hardness anchor to test that question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is that Theorem 3 is an isolated optimization result inside a cooperative queueing paper, not a complexity study. It proves the paper’s greedy construction returns an optimal superset; the linear running time is stated for that construction, but the paper does not classify the complexity of finding core allocations or solving a broader family of computational problems. The core results mainly characterize which cost shares exist. Their continuous extensions would therefore need a computational question that the paper itself does not develop.

That objection does not defeat the proposed mirror, though. Theorem 3 really does compute something, and its best continuous version is coherent: represent many small providers by finitely many rate-profile types and optimize over their mass. Because the queue cost is homogeneous in arrival and service rates, normalized masses preserve the finite model’s cost. A large dispatch network of similar repair providers is a plausible setting. The resulting best-superset problem has a meaningful population interpretation and admits the stated ratio-sorting algorithm. Its simplicity is not a reason to reject it under ChoCo’s criteria.

The full core is less direct. In a continuum model, coalition cost is discontinuous at zero mass: every positive coalition can have a nonzero limiting queue cost even as its mass shrinks. The finite paper’s core characterization therefore cannot simply be carried over as a theorem about a continuum core. But that is a reason the extension needs new work, not evidence that no worthwhile mirror exists.

So I cannot honestly defeat every anchor. The negative case is strongest against the paper’s overall fit as a computational-complexity paper; it does not dislodge the best-superset anchor or rule out a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A universal “no mirror” verdict would overstate what the paper supports.
