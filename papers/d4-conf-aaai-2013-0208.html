But the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition with a computational claim, and Algorithm 1’s reported convergence is empirical rather than a stated guarantee. The computational-result gate therefore fails.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuous population of ranking sources. The paper’s central object is a shared latent ranking inferred from many noisy, possibly incomplete rank lists. Replacing the list of sources by a distribution over list types preserves that problem’s structure.

There is an important limit to the evidence: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. Its only plausible named computational anchor is Algorithm 1, proposed here. Given the comparison matrices, it alternates updates to the low-rank factors, shared matrix, and structured error matrices; updates the Lagrange multipliers and penalty parameter; and stops when the residuals fall below a tolerance. The authors report convergence in fewer than 200 iterations in their experiments, but explicitly give no convergence proof for the nonconvex objective. So this is an algorithmic lead, not a theorem that the problem is in P. If the anchor rule requires a numbered complexity theorem, there are no qualifying anchors.

My lead candidate is Rank Aggregation over a Continuous Source Population with Rank Cap. Fix \(m\) items and rank bound \(r\). An instance gives \(\tau\) types, each a complete partial ranking represented by a comparison matrix \(T^t\) and symmetric observation mask \(W^t\), with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). It also gives a rational \(\lambda>0\). The task is to find a global minimizer \(Z,(E^t)_{t=1}^{\tau}\) of

\[ \min\quad \|Z\|_*+\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\|E^t\|_{2,1} \]

subject to

\[ W^t\odot T^t = W^t\odot\bigl(Z+E^t-(E^t)^\top\bigr) \quad\text{for every }t, \qquad \operatorname{rank}(Z)\le r. \]

The solution is the shared consensus matrix and type-level structured errors; the induced ranking sorts items by the row-sum scores of \(Z\), as in the paper. For a finite population of \(N\) sources with type counts \(n_t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) recovers the paper’s sum over sources after rescaling its regularization parameter by \(N\). Thus mass records the fraction of sources of each complete type, including their ranking and missingness pattern.

A plausible regime is a large service operating many replicated ranking systems on the same catalog or query. Many instances share configurations and produce the same partial rankings, so \(N\) can greatly exceed the number \(\tau\) of distinct outputs. The paper’s experiments, with comparatively small sets of rank lists, do not establish this regime; it is a plausible setting for the mirror rather than their reported one. Still, the authors should recognize the question: it retains their shared \(Z\), masked pairwise observations, antisymmetric structured errors, and explicit rank cap, while changing the input population to type masses.

I would provisionally expect the exact rank-capped problem to be continuum-specifically hard, because the rank constraint leaves a nonconvex global optimization problem even when the population is represented by few types. That is a conjecture, not a result of the paper. A useful follow-up is whether dropping the explicit rank cap and keeping only the trace-norm objective yields a tractable continuous relaxation, and whether its solution preserves the desired rank-\(r\) consensus.

The weakest point is the gap between the anchor and the proposed complexity question: Algorithm 1 is a numerical method with empirical convergence, not a hardness or tractability result. The rank cap that makes the mirror faithful is also what prevents the easy tractability argument for the convex relaxation. So this is a credible continuous research question anchored in the paper’s computed model, but the paper itself does not yet supply a formal complexity result for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the paper gives ChoCo no computational complexity result to mirror. Its numbered contribution is Algorithm 1, an ALM procedure for the rank-constrained, nonconvex formulation. The authors report empirical convergence, but explicitly give no convergence proof; they establish no exactness, approximation guarantee, or worst-case complexity bound. So the algorithm is a possible starting point for a new question, not a published complexity result whose continuous mirror would extend the paper.

The proposed rank-capped source-population model is the best attempt to rescue one. It is mathematically faithful: a complete type must include the observed comparison matrix and its missingness mask. If \(n_t\) sources have the same type, their original penalty terms can share one error matrix \(E^t\), and their total contribution is \(n_t\|E^t\|_{2,1}\). Taking \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and rescaling \(\lambda\) by \(N\) gives the proposed mass-weighted objective. But that exact equivalence also shows the limit of the mirror: it groups duplicate observations. Grouping sources by a coarser label, while their observed rankings differ, would require a new noise or sampling model.

The added global-optimization question—whether the rank-capped objective can be solved exactly or approximated efficiently—could be worthwhile in its own right. It is not, however, a complexity question the paper answers or even analyzes. The paper’s contribution is an empirical matrix-learning method; the proposed question turns that formulation into an exact complexity problem. Dropping the rank cap makes a different, convex weighted model and does not restore a connection to a result in the paper.

This is a narrow negative case, not a proof that no sensible scenario exists. Large groups of annotators or users can share exact partial rankings, so a continuous source population is conceivable, and the weighted model is coherent. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror under every scenario. The strongest case against is that the paper supplies no complexity result to mirror, while the faithful candidate is a new question about the exact optimization problem, rather than a continuization of a computational result the paper establishes.
