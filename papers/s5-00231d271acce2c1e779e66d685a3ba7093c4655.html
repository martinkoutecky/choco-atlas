The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the *population* is a plausible object to continuize. A large online platform could have millions of users but relatively few behavioral and exposure types: users in a type share the same rational-inattention parameters and the same pattern of influence from other types. The mass \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of users in type \(t\). A block-level version of the paper’s belief dynamics could then track how those groups acquire information and update beliefs.

But the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Its named results are proved here, but they are analytic: Theorem 1 gives the Gaussian belief update rule; Theorem 2 says the belief means are mean-stationary at the true state; and Theorem 3 gives a variance recursion. None asserts a complexity classification or analyzes an algorithm for a computational problem. The rational-inattention optimization in equation (1) is solved in closed form in equation (2), but that is not a numbered complexity result either.

So I can’t give a continuous problem “per anchor” without relabeling an analytic theorem as a computational result. A block-population task for computing belief dynamics could be formulated, but it would be a new computational question prompted by the paper, not a mirror anchored in one of its named results. It also requires a block-structured interaction model in place of the paper’s individual network. The positive case is therefore about a plausible high-multiplicity setting, not about a demonstrated computational result whose hardness or tractability might change under continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s central concession is the strongest negative case: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 gives a Bayesian update rule; Theorem 2 establishes mean stationarity; Theorem 3 gives a variance recursion. They are analytic results, not complexity claims or algorithmic analyses. Equation (1) is the closest thing to an optimization problem, but it is a separate scalar choice for each agent, solved explicitly in equation (2). Weighting those choices by population mass does not create the computational question ChoCo is meant to study.

The best attempt to build a mirror from those results still changes the research question. A continuum of agents with the same attention parameters and the same influence pattern across groups could yield block-level belief dynamics. That is a plausible social-learning model, and it may be worth studying. But its natural questions—whether the dynamics converge, and how belief distributions evolve—are analytic questions of the kind already associated with mean-field social learning. The three theorems would become update or propagation statements for that model, not computational results about a problem in the paper.

There is a further modeling cost. In the paper, a person’s position in the directed network determines whose signals they receive and how those signals enter their update. For a generic sparse network, preserving that structure may leave few agents with identical interaction profiles, even if their attention parameters match. A block model restores multiplicity, but it imposes structure on the network to make aggregation possible. Even granting that as the best version of the mirror, it supplies no missing computational target. One could add a new problem—such as choosing an interaction network or allocating attention under constraints—but that would be a new contribution, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.

So the negative case is narrow but strong: a continuous-population formulation may be natural and valuable for social-learning analysis, but the paper offers no computational result for it to mirror. This does not establish that no worthwhile computational problem could ever be built from the model; it does explain why that would be a different research project rather than a continuization of this paper in ChoCo’s sense.
