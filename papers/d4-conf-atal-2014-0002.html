The continuous mirror question

Let a latent event draw a mass vector \(q\in\Delta(\Theta)\) from an unknown prior \(\nu\), and let an atomless unit-mass society have signal types \(\theta\in\Theta\) with masses \(q_\theta\). Is there a mechanism independent of \(\nu\) that uses a measure-preserving peer matching, signal reports, and peer forecasts of \(q\), scored against the aggregate report masses, to make truthful reporting a strict Bayesian equilibrium for almost every agent whenever distinct signals satisfy the corresponding second-order relevance condition? In that condition, for every \(\theta\ne\theta'\), there is a peer signal \(z\) with \(\Pr[z\mid\theta]>0\) such that, if \(\Pr[\theta'\mid z]>0\), then \(\mathcal L(q\mid\theta,z)\ne\mathcal L(q\mid\theta',z)\); otherwise \(\mathcal L(q\mid\theta,z)\ne\mathcal L(q\mid z)\).

The model it lives in

Types are signals \(\theta\in\Theta\) carrying masses \(q_\theta\), with \(q\) drawn from an unknown prior \(\nu\). Agents submit reports \(r\) and forecasts of \(q\); a measure-preserving peer matching links each report to another agent’s forecast. Proper-score payments use the aggregate report masses, and the objective is strict truthful equilibrium for almost every agent without knowledge of \(\nu\).

The objection that survived

In an atomless society, one agent’s report has zero effect on the aggregate mass vector; the proponent mentions a selected peer but does not specify a matching and payment scheme that preserves each agent’s report incentive.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational-complexity anchor in this paper. The numbered theorems establish mechanism possibility and impossibility; they do not classify an underlying problem as NP-hard, polynomial-time solvable, FPT, or similar. I shouldn’t present an equilibrium-existence theorem as a complexity result.

The closest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that, for signal elicitation with at least three agents and an information structure satisfying second-order stochastic relevance, Knowledge-Free Peer Prediction has a strict truthful Perfect Bayesian equilibrium without the mechanism knowing the information structure. The condition requires that, for every agent \(i\) and distinct signals \(\theta_i',\theta_i''\), there be another agent \(j\) and a signal \(\theta_j\) with \(\Pr[\theta_j\mid\theta_i']>0\) such that the distribution of the other agents’ signals conditional on \((\theta_i',\theta_j)\) differs from the distribution conditional on \((\theta_i'',\theta_j)\), when the latter conditioning event has positive probability; if it has probability zero, it must differ from the distribution conditional on \(\theta_j\) alone.

If mechanism-existence results are accepted as near-miss anchors, the strongest continuous question I would propose is Continuum Knowledge-Free Peer Prediction. Consider a large subjective-survey or citizen-science population: many anonymous respondents share a finite signal alphabet \(\Theta\), and their signals are conditionally independent given a common latent event. The event determines a rational mass vector \(q\in\Delta(\Theta)\); in that society, \(q_\theta\) is the fraction of respondents with signal \(\theta\). The mechanism does not know the prior over these mass vectors. Each respondent reports a signal; a randomly selected peer sees that report and forecasts the society’s mass vector. A strictly proper score evaluates the forecast against the mass vector of reports, and rewards both the forecaster and the respondent whose report informed it. The question is whether one structure-blind mechanism can make truthful reporting a strict equilibrium for every such society satisfying the corresponding second-order relevance condition. A solution would be a mechanism and an equilibrium argument, or an impossibility result.

I expect a constructive answer for this restricted regime: proper scoring can reward forecasts of the mass vector, and differences in conditional beliefs about that vector can make truthful reports strictly valuable. But that would be a new mechanism-existence result, not a complexity classification. The mirror is plausible because the society itself is represented by type masses, and a survey with millions of respondents but a small signal alphabet is a credible high-multiplicity setting. Its main weakness is the restriction to exchangeable, conditionally independent populations: the paper allows much more general correlations among named agents. So this is a focused candidate extension, not a mirror of the paper’s full model or a compliant computational-complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case rests on one firm point: Theorem 3 is not a computational result of the kind ChoCo is set up to mirror. It proves that a particular mechanism has a strict truthful equilibrium under second-order stochastic relevance. It gives no complexity classification, algorithmic objective, or running time. Theorem 1 is an impossibility result for elicitation, and Theorem 2 establishes another mechanism’s equilibrium properties; neither supplies a computational anchor either. Recasting Theorem 3 as a continuum equilibrium-existence question would still leave the programme’s computational question unformulated.

The proponent’s crowd-sourcing scenario is credible. A large anonymous population with a small signal alphabet is a natural high-multiplicity regime, and conditional independence given a latent event gives a sensible way to define signal masses. I would not reject it on the grounds that people are individuated, or that a continuum necessarily erases incentives.

The strongest version of the mirror would let each event determine a mass vector \(q^\omega\in\Delta(\Theta)\), then ask whether a structure-blind mechanism can elicit each participant’s signal truthfully when agents can forecast that vector or sampled peers’ signals. That is a coherent continuous mechanism-design question. But if the target is each person’s signal, the mass vector alone is not the target; adding peer forecasts retains a finite-agent Bayesian incentive problem, with the continuum supplying the sampling population. If the target becomes the mass vector itself, the question has shifted to eliciting an aggregate belief. Either route may yield useful theory, but neither mirrors a computational result in this paper.

One could add an algorithmic objective—such as finding a compact mechanism or minimizing payments—but the paper does not specify one, and the population limit does not supply it. That would be a new research question requiring its own case for computational relevance. So the claim that no scenario admits a worthwhile continuous mirror in the broader mechanism-design sense is not established; this paper’s crowd setting makes that claim hard to defend. The stronger negative verdict I can support is narrower: none of the paper’s results gives ChoCo a qualifying computational mirror without first changing the task.
