The continuous mirror question

Given a consultation side with a reason vocabulary \(R\), justifications \(J\) labelled by \(\lambda(j)\subseteq R\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over voter types \(t\) with salient reason sets \(P_t\), a slate budget \(K\), and threshold \(q\), does there exist \(S\subseteq J\), \(|S|\le K\), such that \(\sum_{\ell\in\bigcup_{j\in S}\lambda(j)}\sum_{t:\ell\in P_t}\mu_t\ge q\)?

The model it lives in

A shared slate-selection problem over a continuous society: types are consultation side and salient-reason set, each with rational mass \(\mu_t\); the decision variable is a slate \(S\) of at most \(K\) justifications, and its objective is the total population mass of reasons covered by their labels.

The objection that survived

The objective depends only on reason marginals, so it discards correlations among the concerns held by different types and adds little beyond the paper’s weighted SJP formulation.

fatal: False

The mirror covers Proposition 1’s SJP hardness through population-weighted reason coverage; it leaves the endorsement rule, simulation results, and adversarial analysis unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead—and the only anchor I would use—is Continuous Population-Weighted Subsuming Justification. It mirrors a narrow formal problem in the paper, not the endorsement rule as a whole.

The anchor is Proposition 1, §3.4, proved in the paper: SJP asks, given justifications with reason-label sets, a target reason set \(L\), and a budget \(K\), whether some collection of at most \(K\) justifications covers every reason in \(L\). The proposition says this decision problem is NP-complete. The paper proves membership in NP by using the selected justifications as a certificate; it proves hardness by reducing Set Cover, mapping each universe element to a reason and each set to a justification carrying those labels.

Here is a population version of that question. Consider a large civic consultation on one side of a proposal. Its live reason vocabulary is \(R\), its available justifications are \(J\), and each \(j\in J\) has a label set \(\lambda(j)\subseteq R\). Residents have types \(t\in T\), where a type records the poll side and the set \(P_t\subseteq R\) of reasons its members regard as salient. The society is given by rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\), summing to \(1\); \(\tau=|T|\) is the number of supported types, not the number of residents. Define the population weight of reason \(\ell\) by
\[ \omega_\ell=\sum_{t:\ell\in P_t}\mu_t. \]

An instance also gives a slate budget \(K\) and a rational threshold \(q\). The question is whether there is a slate \(S\subseteq J\), \(|S|\leq K\), such that
\[ \sum_{\ell\in R\cap\bigcup_{j\in S}\lambda(j)}\omega_\ell\geq q. \]
A solution is the slate \(S\), whose covered population-weighted reason mass can be checked from the input. This is weighted SJP with the reason weights derived from a distribution of voter types. Its input size and running time should be expressed in \(|J|\), \(|R|\), \(\tau\), and the binary encoding length \(L\).

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a national or city-wide consultation may have many residents but relatively few recurring combinations of side and salient concerns. Each type’s mass means a fraction of the population. Residents sharing that type are indistinguishable for this problem, as high multiplicity requires.

The hardness transfers directly. For an SJP instance with target \(L\), make one type for each reason in \(L\), each with mass \(1/|L|\) and that single reason as its priority set; set \(q=1\). A slate reaches the threshold exactly when its labels cover all of \(L\). Each type can be replicated by any number of residents without changing the masses, so the reduction persists at arbitrarily large population sizes. The resulting problem is NP-complete for rationally encoded instances: a proposed slate is a polynomial-size certificate, and the reduction is exact. I expect this mirror to be hardness-transferring, with the combinatorics in the reason and justification structure, not in the number of people.

The scope is deliberately just the SJP result and its weighted variant. I would not use the paper’s simulation findings as complexity anchors: they are empirical results, not numbered complexity theorems. Nor does this mirror claim that the paper’s actual endorsement-and-link rule solves the weighted coverage problem.

That distinction is also the weakest point. The paper treats labels as evaluation data and explicitly excludes label-reading greedy coverage from its deployable mechanism. This mirror makes labels central to the optimization objective, so it is a continuous analogue of the paper’s formal SJP, not a replacement for its proposed recommender. I think the authors could recognize it as their problem because they themselves formulate weighted SJP and motivate reason weights through concerns such as minority-held reasons. The next questions are whether useful restrictions on \(K\), \(\tau\), or label frequency yield tractable cases, and which approximation guarantees survive when the weights come from a population distribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor is the strongest one available, and its population story is plausible. Its weakness is that the story adds no distinct computational object. For a slate \(S\), the proposed objective depends on the voter types only through the reason marginals
\[ \omega_\ell=\sum_{t:\ell\in P_t}\mu_t. \]
Once those are calculated, the type distribution can be discarded. Two societies with the same \(\omega_\ell\) for every reason produce the same instance and answer, even if their types and the correlations among their priorities differ. Computing the marginals takes \(O(\tau |R|)\) rational additions; the remaining problem is precisely the paper’s weighted SJP.

That matters because the paper already introduces weighted SJP with a weight for each reason and a coverage threshold, explicitly motivating those weights through concerns such as minority-held reasons. Reading the weights as population shares supplies a persuasive interpretation, but the proposed mirror leaves the formal problem unchanged. The named result is unweighted SJP; the proposed variant uses the weighted SJP already stated in the paper. The population support size \(\tau\) does not survive as a parameter of the selection problem after aggregation. This is the best case against treating the anchor as a new continuous-population mirror: its main claim is already contained in the paper’s own model.

A more population-specific version could score a slate by the mass of types whose entire priority set it covers, for example \(\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[P_t\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in S}\lambda(j)]\). That preserves correlations among each person’s concerns. It also changes the question from covering population-weighted reasons to satisfying whole voter types. The paper defines its coverage benchmark over the shared reason vocabulary; it does not pose that voter-satisfaction objective. Giving each type its own slate would move further from the anchor: without coupling constraints the choices separate by type, and with such constraints the added allocation problem needs its own motivation and analysis.

This does not establish that no related population-level optimization would interest the authors. Minority-weighted coverage makes the proposed interpretation especially credible, and that is where the negative case is weakest. The narrower objection is that this anchor, as stated, does not show a distinct continuization task: it reinterprets the paper’s existing weighted SJP. Preserving type-level information would produce a different problem whose connection to the named result is much less secure.
