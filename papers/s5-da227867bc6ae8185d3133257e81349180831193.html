The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is a large organization or election with two voting periods, where many voters share the same preferred candidate and participation cost. A type would be \((T,t,c)\): preferred candidate \(T\in\{A,B\}\), period \(t\in\{1,2\}\), and cost \(c\). Its mass is the fraction of the electorate with that type. A strategy assigns each type a vote or abstain decision, conditional on the information disclosed; the quantities to compare are per-voter welfare and each candidate’s winning probability under the three disclosure regimes.

That is a recognizable extension of the paper’s setup. But its central mechanism is pivotal voting, and in a nonatomic electorate any one voter has zero effect on the result. With majority determined by the masses of votes, positive-cost voters therefore lose the pivotal incentive that drives the paper’s equilibrium. Keeping the original pivotal mechanism requires retaining individual voter granularity. The paper’s continuous cost distribution also means exact cost is part of a complete type, so cost types would generally not form a small finite support. These are serious obstacles to a mirror the authors would recognize as their problem.

There is no qualifying named computational anchor in the paper. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result. Its substantive findings—such as the two-voter welfare comparison and the numerical comparisons for larger electorates—are not presented as such results. Section 4.3 discusses equilibrium existence and nonuniqueness, but those claims are not numbered computational results. The numbered equations do not supply the required anchors either.

So I cannot honestly give one continuous prover question per anchor, or classify such questions as tractable or hard, without inventing an anchor or changing the paper’s core voting incentives. The distribution-over-voter-types model is a plausible research direction suggested by the paper, but it is not a defensible anchored case for a continuous mirror under the stated standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper offers no computational result to mirror. Its numbered equations describe equilibrium conditions and welfare calculations; §4.3 gives an analytic existence argument and discusses nonuniqueness; the larger-election results are numerical simulations. The paper’s contribution is comparative analysis of disclosure regimes, not a complexity or algorithmic result. A new question about computing or optimizing over disclosure regimes could be interesting, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The best candidate for such a question would be to compute equilibrium participation and compare voter welfare or winning probabilities in a large society with types \((T,t,c)\). The cost coordinate is not fatal: a cost distribution can be represented in the type space. The deeper problem is that the paper’s incentives depend on an individual vote having a positive chance of changing the winner. In a nonatomic society, one voter has zero mass and cannot change the vote margin. With positive participation costs, abstention is then strictly better; a continuous cost distribution gives zero mass to costless voters. Everyone abstains, the election is a coin flip, and the disclosure regimes become indistinguishable. That erases the mechanism the paper studies.

Keeping individual pivotality requires retaining finite voters or positive-mass decision makers. The first keeps the society discrete; the second makes blocs, rather than voters, the strategic agents. Scaling costs with population size or adding a nonvanishing source of aggregate uncertainty might preserve nontrivial behavior, but either changes the paper’s core model and creates a new research question. The authors themselves note that pivotal-cost models fit small elections better because pivotal probabilities fall as electorates grow.

So the positive case does not supply a surviving named anchor, and its strongest proposed mirror degenerates under the population limit. I would not claim that no related continuous model could ever be worthwhile; that universal claim is too strong. The case against is strongest for a faithful, anchored mirror of this paper: there is no computational result to anchor it to, and the obvious large-population version removes the pivotal incentive that drives its findings.
