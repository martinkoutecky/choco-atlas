What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror restates the minimum preference-type question underlying Theorem 1; it leaves the paper's structural discussion without a computational analysis.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s question about how many preferences are needed to generate a Pareto order. But there is no numbered computational-complexity result in the paper that qualifies as an anchor under your rule. The nearest is Theorem 1, proved here: \(\dim(X,P)=n\) if and only if \(P\) is \(n\)-fold, meaning the shortest sequence starting with \(P_1=P\) has \(n\) partial orders, each intermediate \(P_j\) a partial-conjugate of the union before it, and the final order a conjugate of that union. The paper explicitly says this characterization is existential and does not provide an algorithm. Its statement that dimension is NP-complete for fixed \(n\ge3\) is an unnumbered citation to Yannakakis, not a result proved or numbered here.

A candidate continuous problem, tied to the underlying question in Theorem 1, is Minimum-Type Continuous Pareto Representation. Given a finite set \(X\), a partial order \(P\) on \(X\), and an integer \(k\), find a probability distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings of \(X\), with at most \(k\) rankings in its support, such that \(xPy\) exactly when every ranking of positive mass puts \(x\) above \(y\) (with the diagonal included). A solution is the supported rankings and their positive rational masses summing to \(1\). The decision version asks whether such a distribution exists; the optimization version minimizes its support size.

A plausible regime is a large market with a fixed catalogue of alternatives and many consumers in cohorts sharing the same ranking: \(N\gg\tau\), where \(N\) is the number of consumers and \(\tau\) the number of ranking types. The question then asks how many distinct preference types are minimally needed to rationalize an observed unanimous-dominance relation. That is close to the paper’s own question: the authors explicitly observe that duplicating an agent’s preference leaves the Pareto relation unchanged.

The expected outcome is hardness transfers. A distribution with \(\tau\) supported rankings exists exactly when \(P\) has dimension at most \(\tau\): its support is a realizer of \(P\), and any realizer gives a distribution by assigning equal positive mass to each ranking. Thus the mirror reproduces the dimension problem; the introduction reports NP-completeness for each fixed \(n\ge3\), citing Yannakakis. This candidate covers the minimum-agent/Pareto-representation question underlying Theorem 1, not the paper’s full structural discussion as a computational contribution. It also suggests studying approximation or parameterized algorithms for minimum support, and variants that allow an \(\varepsilon\)-fraction of dissenting mass.

The weakest point is that the masses do no computational work: only whether a type has positive mass matters. Equal weights always suffice, so this is essentially a distributional restatement of preference-type support and poset dimension, not a continuous-optimization relaxation. It is a recognizable high-multiplicity object, but a thin positive case—and it does not supply the requested named computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest possible mirror is the minimum-support representation proposed for Theorem 1. But Theorem 1 is a structural characterization of poset dimension, not a computational result: it equates dimension \(n\) with the existence of a shortest sequence of recursive partial-conjugates. The paper itself says the characterization is existential and does not provide an algorithm. The NP-completeness statement in the introduction is background cited to Yannakakis, not a result proved or numbered in this paper. So the proposed optimization problem is adjacent to the theorem, but does not give it the computational anchor the assignment asks for.

Even granting the large-market scenario, the mass variables do no work. For a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, \(xPy\) holds exactly when every ranking with positive mass puts \(x\) above \(y\). Changing the weights while keeping the support fixed cannot change \(P\). Minimizing the number of positive-mass rankings therefore minimizes support size, which is the original minimum-realizer question; equal weights always suffice. A million consumers in cohorts makes the story plausible, but their cohort shares are invisible to the relation and to the proposed objective. The continuous population collapses to a finite support problem.

A mass-sensitive repair, such as allowing a small fraction of voters to disagree with \(P\), could be a worthwhile question. But it changes the exact Pareto relation whose dimension Theorem 1 characterizes. The same is true of moving to a continuum of alternatives: that continuizes the alternative space, not the population. Neither repair supplies a continuous-population mirror of the paper’s named result.

This is not a proof that no related continuous question could interest the authors. The cohort scenario is coherent, and approximate Pareto questions may merit study. The negative case is strongest under the required anchoring standard: the sole candidate is a support-only restatement of the paper’s structural question, and the paper contains no named computational result for it to mirror.
