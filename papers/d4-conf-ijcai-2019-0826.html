The continuous mirror question

Given two candidates \(c_1,c_2\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over voter types, a target \(c_2\), recipient mass \(\kappa\), per-capita budget \(B\), and explicit rational piecewise-linear willingness functions \(f_t\), is there a feasible payment schedule that makes \(c_2\) win in the deterministic nonatomic electorate?

The model it lives in

Each type \(t\) specifies its initial top candidate and willingness function \(f_t\). Represent payments to mass initially voting for \(c_1\) by measures \(\nu_t\) over amounts \(x\ge0\), with \(\nu_t\)'s total mass at most \(\mu_t\), total selected mass \(\kappa\), and total spend \(\sum_t\int x\,d\nu_t(x)\le B\). The converted mass is \(q=\sum_{t\text{ initially votes }c_1}\int f_t(x)\,d\nu_t(x)\); \(c_2\) wins exactly when \(\mu_{c_2}+q\ge\mu_{c_1}-q\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s two-candidate Plurality setting with explicitly represented piecewise-linear willingness functions. It does not preserve finite response fluctuations or the theorem’s inapproximability, and it does not cover Theorems 3–5 or the paper’s other voting-rule questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the finite-election success probability be approximated from this mass model near a zero vote margin?
  • How does the payment-level optimization change for succinct or oracle representations of willingness functions?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that, assuming \(\mathrm{W[1]}\ne\mathrm{FPT}\), there are continuous willingness functions for which Plurality EBEU has no \(g(k)\)-approximation algorithm running in FPT time parameterized by \(k\), for any computable function \(g\), even with only two candidates. The paper proves this by reduction from a cited hardness result for two-dimensional Knapsack (its Theorem 2, citing Kulik and Shachnai). This is the paper’s strongest anchor for asking whether population continuization dissolves a known hardness result.

A plausible regime is a very large online or local election whose voters fall into a modest number of behavioral types. For Plurality, a type \(t\) consists of a voter’s initial top candidate and willingness function \(f_t\); two voters share a type only if both match. Think millions of voters but perhaps dozens or hundreds of types, with type masses measured from the electorate. The attacker has a budget proportional to population size and may target a fraction of the population. In the corresponding finite election with \(N\) voters, mass \(\mu_t\) represents about \(N\mu_t\) voters, the recipient limit is about \(\kappa N\), and the budget is about \(NB\).

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Mean-Field Plurality Bribery with Uncertain Response, the mirror of Theorem 1. There are two candidates, \(c_1\) and the target \(c_2\); type masses \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\) sum to \(1\), and \(c_1\) initially wins. Each type has an explicit rational piecewise-linear willingness function \(f_t:\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\to[0,1]\), eventually constant. The attacker may select mass \(\kappa\), assigning different bribe amounts to different portions of each type, subject to a per-capita budget \(B\). Represent that decision by a nonnegative measure \(\nu_t\) over bribe amounts: \(\nu_t(A)\) is the mass of type \(t\) paid an amount in \(A\). Require \(\nu_t(\mathbb{R}_{\ge0})\le\mu_t\), total selected mass \(\sum_t\nu_t(\mathbb{R}_{\ge0})=\kappa\), and total spend \(\sum_t\int x\,d\nu_t(x)\le B\). Zero-payment selections can pad the exact mass, as in the paper’s exact-\(k\) formulation.

In the nonatomic electorate, a bribed mass \(\nu_t\) produces converted mass \(q_t=\int f_t(x)\,d\nu_t(x)\): individual response uncertainty becomes a deterministic fractional response. The problem is to return a feasible payment schedule maximizing \(q=\sum_{t:\,t\text{ initially votes }c_1}q_t\). The target wins precisely when \(\mu_{c_2}+q\ge\mu_{c_1}-q\), with ties counted as wins. Thus the question, the type information, the bribe action, and the plurality objective all remain recognizable; the population and budget are expressed in mass units.

I expect this explicit-input version to be Class A. For each type, mass assigned across payment levels generates a convex combination of points \((x,f_t(x))\). With piecewise-linear curves, it suffices to use their breakpoints: a variable records how much type mass receives each breakpoint amount. The budget, recipient-mass, and winning-margin constraints are linear, so maximizing converted mass is an LP of size polynomial in the number of types, curve breakpoints, and input bit length. The sharp willingness-function increases used in Theorem 1’s reduction remain representable, but the attacker can split each type’s mass continuously. That removes the reduction’s all-or-nothing item choice.

The weakest point is substantial: this is a mean-field analogue of the paper’s success-probability objective, not an exact preservation of it. As the electorate grows, independent responses concentrate around their expected masses; the finite election’s nontrivial win probability survives mainly near a zero vote margin. Also, Theorem 1’s reduction gives key voters item-specific willingness functions, so the number of distinct types generally grows with the number of items. Its construction therefore does not itself exhibit the large-population, small-support regime.

The next questions are whether a positive-margin continuous solution can be rounded to a finite high-multiplicity election with a useful win-probability guarantee; how to handle the near-tie regime where response fluctuations matter; and whether succinct or oracle representations of willingness functions make the payment-level pricing problem hard. The mirror covers Theorem 1’s two-candidate Plurality EBEU result, with explicit piecewise-linear curves. It does not cover the paper’s results for other voting rules or claim that its finite stochastic objective has an identical continuum limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only strong computational anchor here. Its hardness is built around choosing exactly \(k\) distinct voters, each with an item-specific willingness function, and maximizing the probability of a random vote outcome. That structure does not pass cleanly to a continuous population. If \(k\) stays fixed, those voters have zero mass. If \(k\) scales with the electorate, the selected population becomes divisible: the reduction’s all-or-nothing choice of items disappears. Its key voters encode different knapsack items in their functions, so grouping them into a small number of complete types also erases the distinctions the reduction needs.

The proponent’s stronger mean-field version is coherent: allocate portions of each type to payment levels, and track the expected converted mass. With explicitly represented piecewise-linear willingness functions, the resulting optimization can be written as an LP. But that objective is not the success probability in Theorem 1. Under the usual independent-response interpretation, an atomless population’s aggregate response converges to its expected mass, so the probability of winning becomes \(0\) or \(1\) except at a tie. The LP’s maximization of converted mass is then a proxy; the exact continuous question is a deterministic threshold or minimum-cost problem. Preserving nontrivial win probabilities near the threshold requires finite-population fluctuations and their scale, which a distribution over types alone does not record.

That is the best case against the anchor: the hard result concerns indivisible choices and stochastic election outcomes, while the natural population limit removes both. Yet it does not establish the requested universal negative. The deterministic expected-response problem is still a sensible computational question about a continuous population, and the paper’s authors could plausibly recognize it as a natural limit of their model. It is neither an existing continuous treatment nor an objection based on the answer being easy. So the honest conclusion is that Theorem 1’s particular dichotomy lacks a faithful pure-continuum counterpart; the claim that *no* worthwhile mirror exists is weak.
