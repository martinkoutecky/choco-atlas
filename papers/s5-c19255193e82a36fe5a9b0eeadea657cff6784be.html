What the mirror covers

The proposed extension covers majority aggregation and logical consistency; it does not computationally mirror the paper's representation results or its broader comparison of preference and judgement aggregation.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t provide a case with a qualifying named computational anchor: this paper gives no numbered result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or otherwise computationally complex. Its closest result is Theorem 5.1, proved here: a judgement \(J\) is logically consistent exactly when each associated graph \(G_i(J)\) has a global optimum; if those optima exist, they are all the atomic judgement vector \(J_a\). That is a characterization of consistency, not a complexity result. Theorem 4.4 gives a bijective graph representation, also without a computational classification.

The strongest candidate extension is a Mass-Majority Consistency problem. The instance is a set of atomic propositions and compound propositions defined by Boolean formulas, plus a distribution \(\mu\) over complete, logically consistent judgement types \(t\), with support size \(\tau\) and rational masses. Assume no proposition is tied at half the mass. For each proposition, aggregate by weighted majority; decide whether the resulting judgement is logically consistent, returning a violated formula if it is not. A plausible regime is a large, standardized public consultation on a fixed set of linked propositions, where many respondents share the same complete answer pattern and \(N\gg\tau\). This preserves the doctrinal paradox: the majority judgement may fail to be logical. Theorem 5.1 suggests the graph criterion for checking the result, though applying it to a distribution is an extension, not a result of the paper. With formulas given explicitly, direct aggregation and consistency checking appears polynomial in the support size and input length; that is my inference, not a bound established by the authors.

This candidate covers the paper’s majority-aggregation and logical-consistency thread, not its broader comparison of preference and judgement frameworks. Its weakest point is also decisive for the requested case: the paper studies aggregation structure and paradoxes, not a computational problem. The mass-majority question is a plausible continuous-population extension, but it cannot honestly be presented as a mirror anchored in one of the paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 4.4 gives a bijective representation, and Theorem 5.1 characterizes logical consistency through global optima. Neither analyzes the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s results concern aggregation structure, impossibility, and paradoxes; it specifies no intervention objective, budget, or optimization task.

The proposed Mass-Majority Consistency problem is well-defined, and a large consultation with repeated complete answer patterns is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. So lack of multiplicity is not a persuasive objection. But the distributional input simply supplies weighted counts for the paper’s majority rule. Theorem 5.1 characterizes consistency of the resulting judgement; it does not study a task over a society distribution. This would be a new algorithmic question built around the paper’s subject, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its computational results.

A stronger candidate would ask for the least mass that must change type, at some cost, to make the majority judgement logically consistent. That connects population mass to the paper’s central paradox and could be a worthwhile project. But it adds the intervention costs, allowable conversions, and optimization objective that the paper never defines. Its graphs encode comparisons among possible judgements; they do not specify the cost of changing a respondent’s judgement. A consultation scenario makes the population model plausible, but does not supply that missing problem.

So the strongest negative case is narrow: the proposed anchor does not meet the programme’s stated standard for a mirror, and stronger computational extensions require substantial new modelling choices. It is not an airtight case that no worthwhile continuous question could be built around this paper. A mass-based robustness or intervention problem may be worthwhile on its own; the honest objection is that the paper gives no named computational result for it to mirror.
