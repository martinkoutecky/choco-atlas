The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C=\{c_1,\ldots,c_m\}\), ranking types \(T\) with rankings \(R_t\) and masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), a finite-support permutation-symmetric per-race cardinal-quality law \(\nu\), and \(\lambda>0\), compute \(v_j=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}[u_\lambda(x^{(j)})]\) for \(j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\), then output \(W(\mu,\nu,\lambda)=\arg\max_{c\in C}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t v_{\operatorname{pos}_{R_t}(c)}\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete ordinal ranking types \(t=(R_t)\), and \(\mu_t\) is their mass. The explicit law \(\nu\) supplies the cardinal qualities used to form the positional kernel \(v\); the population computation is \(\operatorname{sc}_{\mu}(c)=\sum_t\mu_t v_{\operatorname{pos}_{R_t}(c)}\), with an ordinary winner set as output.

The objection that survived

The proposed \(\mu\) alone does not determine \(v\); the prover must receive the symmetric cardinal-quality law \(\nu\) explicitly.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6 and the score computations in Example 7 and Appendix D.2, including mass-weighted winner selection; it leaves the axiomatic characterizations, geometric-limit theorems, and empirical explanations aside. Theorem 6's citations are prior art, not a novelty collision.

Open questions for a prover

  • For succinct or implicit \(\nu\), what is the complexity of computing all \(v_j\) and \(W\)?
  • With fixed \(v\), can the minimum mass of ranking types whose changes alter \(W\) be computed?
  • Can \(\lambda\) be optimized jointly with winner robustness while preserving the authors' objective?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified yes, anchored on Theorem 6. I would not claim that this paper contains a named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity theorem: it does not. Its main results are axiomatic and statistical. Theorem 6 is the closest computation-bearing anchor, because the paper uses it to compute optimal scoring vectors from data.

Theorem 6 is cited from Apesteguia, Ballester and Ferrer, Boutilier, and Laplace; it is not proved in this paper. In full, it assumes that the vectors of cardinal qualities \(u_1,\ldots,u_n\) in the races are identically and independently distributed from a permutation-symmetric joint distribution. If \(u_i^{(j)}\) denotes the quality of the athlete finishing in position \(j\) in race \(i\), it defines the scoring vector by

\[ s_j=\mathbb{E}[u_i^{(j)}]. \]

The theorem says that the winner under this scoring rule is the athlete with the highest expected total quality conditional on the observed ordinal rankings:

\[ \arg\max_a \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \sum_{i=1}^n u_i^a \;\middle|\; R_1(u_1),\ldots,R_n(u_n) \right]. \]

Under the additional assumption that the performances of the athletes in each race are themselves identically and independently distributed from a continuous density, the scoring total of an athlete equals that athlete’s expected overall quality. The paper applies this with

\[ u_\lambda(x)= \begin{cases} \lambda^x,&\lambda>1,\\ x,&\lambda=1,\\ -\lambda^x,&0<\lambda<1. \end{cases} \]

My mirror is therefore the following problem.

Continuous Expected-Score Winner. There are \(m\) candidates or athletes \(C=\{c_1,\ldots,c_m\}\), and a finite set \(T\) of complete evaluator types. A type \(t\) contains a strict ranking \(R_t\) of \(C\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the evaluator population having that type, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The population is not a list of named evaluators but the distribution \(\mu\).

The cardinal-quality model is the symmetric model from Theorem 6. Let \(X^{(j)}\) be the quality of the \(j\)-th-ranked candidate for a random evaluator, and let

\[ v_j=\mathbb{E}[u_\lambda(X^{(j)})]. \]

Given \(\mu\), compute the positional scoring rule \(s=(s_1,\ldots,s_m)\) that selects the candidate with maximum expected aggregate quality, and compute the winner set

\[ W(\mu)= \arg\max_{c\in C} \sum_{t\in T} \mu_t\,s_{\operatorname{pos}_{R_t}(c)}. \]

The solution supplied by Theorem 6 is

\[ s_j=v_j, \]

up to positive affine transformation. Thus the continuous score of candidate \(c\) is

\[ \operatorname{sc}_{\mu}(c) = \sum_{t\in T} \mu_t\,v_{\operatorname{pos}_{R_t}(c)}. \]

If the cardinal model has finite support \(\{x^r\}\) with probabilities \(\pi_r\), then

\[ v_j=\sum_r \pi_r\,u_\lambda\!\left(x^{r,(j)}\right), \]

so the rule and winner can be computed by sorting the finite-support quality vectors and taking mass-weighted sums. With explicit \(T\), this is polynomial in \(m\), \(|T|\), the support size, and the encoding length of the rational data. This is a natural Class A mirror.

The high-multiplicity regime is credible in the paper’s rank-aggregation applications, even if it is less compelling for a literal small sports league. Think of a large election, group-recommendation system, or meta-search service with millions of users but a moderate number of recurring preference types. The same applies to a large judging or evaluation population with recurring ranking patterns. A type is a complete description relevant to the problem: two evaluators of the same type have the same ranking and the same parameters used by the scoring model. A finite profile with \(N_t=N\mu_t\) evaluators is recovered exactly: its ordinary total score is \(N\operatorname{sc}_{\mu}(c)\). Thus this is genuinely the paper’s aggregation problem in high-multiplicity form, not merely a probabilistic reinterpretation.

The authors should recognise the mirror because nothing substantive about their objective changes. The candidates, ordinal rankings, positional scores, transformed quality \(u_\lambda\), and expected-quality objective are all retained. Only the repeated evaluator population is represented by masses. Fractional mass does not mean fractional candidates or lotteries: every unit of mass has a complete ranking, and the final winner remains an ordinary candidate. The paper itself explicitly places scoring rules in elections, group recommendation, meta-search, and other collective-ranking settings, so the evaluator-population interpretation is not imported from elsewhere.

This mirror covers Theorem 6 and the computation carried out in Example 7 and Appendix D.2. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s axiomatic results such as Theorem 5. Those could generate a separate question about geometric scoring kernels, but they are not computational anchors and would weaken the case if presented as though they were complexity results.

The main weakness is substantial: Theorem 6 is cited, not proved, and the paper does not establish a discrete hardness result whose fate in the continuum can be tested. Moreover, if one insists that the population must be the athletes rather than the evaluators or voters, the scenario becomes much less natural. Finally, the symmetric iid cardinal model is restrictive; with candidate-specific priors or evaluator-specific quality distributions, a single positional scoring vector may cease to be optimal.

Still, within the paper’s broader rank-aggregation interpretation, this is a credible continuous mirror. It gives a precise mass-distribution problem, an explicit solution, and a polynomial-time algorithm. It also opens the more characteristic ChoCo questions: how much mass must change to alter the winner, whether robustness against deleting evaluator types is tractable, and whether optimizing over the geometric parameter \(p\) remains easy when the type set is implicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case starts with a strict mismatch in what the paper actually proves. Theorem 6 is not a computational-complexity theorem. It is a statistical characterization: under an iid, permutation-symmetric cardinal model, the Bayes-optimal positional score is \(s_j=\mathbb{E}[u_i^{(j)}]\). Example 7 and Appendix D.2 merely evaluate those expectations on data. The paper contains no named theorem about the complexity of computing a winner, optimizing a rule, or solving a decision problem. Thus there is no genuine computational result whose continuous analogue the programme must investigate.

More fundamentally, the proposed \(W(\mu)\) is not determined by \(\mu\). The society \(\mu\) records ordinal rankings, whereas the scores

\[ v_j=\mathbb{E}[u_\lambda(X^{(j)})] \]

come from a separate distribution over latent cardinal performances. Two cardinal models can induce exactly the same distribution over rankings while producing different vectors \(v\). Therefore the proposed “continuous problem” is either under-specified, or it has silently added a second probabilistic model that is doing all the substantive work.

The proponent’s repair is to put the cardinal-quality vector into each type. That makes the input well-defined, but it exposes the weakness of the mirror. With an explicit finite support, the task is just: sort each support vector, compute the \(v_j\)’s, and take weighted positional sums. The same calculation on a discrete profile is obtained by replacing \(\mu_t\) with \(N_t/N\). This is a valid high-multiplicity representation, but not a new computational problem of the sort ChoCo is meant to chart: there is no mass-transfer decision, separation problem, robustness optimization, or nontrivial interaction between the continuum and the rule.

The best attempt to strengthen the mirror would be to ask for more: choose \(\lambda\) or \(p\) from a continuous population, optimize winner robustness under changes to \(\mu\), or allow an implicit/infinite type space. Those could be worthwhile problems, but they are new additions. The first is parameter fitting for an aggregation kernel; the second is a robustness problem absent from the paper; and the third derives its difficulty from the representation of the distribution or from integration, not from the paper’s scoring results. They cannot be presented as computational mirrors of Theorem 6 without importing the programme’s agenda into the paper.

The axiomatic results do not provide a better anchor. Theorem 5 and the later characterizations concern deleting or reversing candidates, majority winners and losers, and invariance under unanimous alternatives. Those operations act on the candidate set, not on the population of races or voters. Replacing integer frequencies by masses merely rewrites the profiles on which the axioms quantify. It does not turn the axioms into computational population problems. Continuumizing the athletes instead is worse: \(F_\lambda\) evaluates an individual athlete’s performance vector across events, and the objective selects a named winner. Collapsing athletes into masses discards precisely the identities the objective compares.

There is a plausible election or recommendation-system story with many repeated preference types. High multiplicity makes that regime sensible, and it should not be dismissed as a collision or as already solved. But it does not rescue this particular anchor. In that scenario the paper supplies a fixed positional kernel \(v\), while the continuous society only supplies weighted evaluation of candidates under that kernel. The result is a bookkeeping reformulation of ordinary scoring, not a computational phenomenon discovered by continuization.

So the negative case is strongest on the absence of a computational result and on the missing cardinal input. It is not airtight: once one grants the paper’s broad rank-aggregation applications and permits a new computational question around robustness or parameter choice, a legitimate continuous-population project can be built. I would therefore not honestly claim that this paper has no worthwhile mirror in any scenario. The more defensible verdict is that the proponent’s stated mirror is too thin to count as a ChoCo computational result, while a stronger mirror would be a new problem rather than a continuization of one proved in the paper.
