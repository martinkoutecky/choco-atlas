The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) interchangeable types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric rational similarity kernel \(c:T\times T\to[0,1]\) with \(c(t,t)=1\), and an integer \(k\), decide whether there is a set \(R\subseteq T\) with \(|R|=k\) such that \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\max_{r\in R}c(r,t)\geq\omega\). Each type’s mass is assigned to its most-similar representative, split evenly among ties.

The model it lives in

A continuous society is a distribution \(\mu\) over preference-and-network-role cohorts; each cohort has a pairwise expected-similarity value \(c(t,u)\). Choose \(k\) cohort representatives to maximize the mass-weighted similarity of each cohort to its nearest representative, with tied mass split evenly.

The objection that survived

Giving the analyst a distribution over complete preference types reveals the population’s preference profile, which weakens the paper’s original information-acquisition motivation; the proponent’s focus on the surrogate objective does not fully resolve that tension.

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with a continuous average representative selection problem. The paper’s Proposition 1(b), proved in its Appendix 8, says that given a constant \(\omega\), it is NP-hard to decide whether there is a set \(M\) of exactly \(k\) nodes with \(\psi(M)\geq\omega\). The same proposition’s part (a) says that, given \(\chi\), it is NP-hard to decide whether there is such a set with \(\rho(M)\geq\chi\). Both parts are proved in this paper by reduction from Dominating Set.

A plausible regime is a large survey or customer-feedback population organized into recurring cohorts: for example, people with the same relevant preference profile and the same social-network role or affinity to every other cohort. There may be thousands of people in each cohort but only \(\tau\) distinct types. A type records the complete preference and network information used by the problem; its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the population. The expected similarity \(c(t,u)\) between types comes from the paper’s expected normalized Kendall–Tau similarity model. This gives the authors’ representatives a natural interpretation: select a few cohort types whose preferences will stand in for the people they represent.

Here are two precise mirrors of Proposition 1. In both, the input is a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types, positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric rational similarity kernel \(c:T\times T\to[0,1]\) with \(c(t,t)=1\), and an integer \(k\leq |T|\). For a selected set \(R\) of \(k\) representative types, write \(c(R,t)=\max_{r\in R}c(r,t)\). Assign each type’s mass to a most-similar representative, splitting it evenly in case of a tie, just as the paper’s rule for \(\Phi\) does.

The lead question, Continuous Average Representative Selection, asks whether there exists \(R\subseteq T\), \(|R|=k\), such that
\[ \psi_\mu(R)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t c(R,t)\geq\omega. \]
A solution is such an \(R\), together with its induced allocation of type masses to representatives. The objective is the expected similarity of a uniformly sampled person to their assigned representative.

The companion, Continuous Robust Representative Selection, asks whether there exists \(R\subseteq T\), \(|R|=k\), such that
\[ \rho_\mu(R)=\min_{t\in T}c(R,t)\geq\chi. \]
A solution is again \(R\) and its induced mass allocation. This objective requires every type with positive mass to be adequately represented, including a small cohort.

Both questions inherit the paper’s NP-hardness on its formal similarity-matrix input. For the reduction, take one type for each vertex of a Dominating Set instance with \(N\) vertices, give each type mass \(1/N\), and set similarity to \(0.9\) across graph edges and \(0.6\) across nonedges, with self-similarity \(1\). Then a \(k\)-type set has \(\rho_\mu(R)\geq0.9\) exactly when it is a dominating set. For the average objective, the corresponding threshold is
\[ \omega=\frac{k+0.9(N-k)}{N}. \]
The same construction also describes a genuinely high-multiplicity population: replicate every type \(H\) times. The number of people becomes \(HN\), while the masses, objectives, and decision remain unchanged. Thus the hardness lives in the number of distinct types, and arbitrary replication of the population does not remove it. I expect both mirrors to be Class B on this formal input domain.

These are close to the paper’s own questions: it defines \(c(S,i)\) by nearest expected similarity, then studies exactly the \(\rho\) and \(\psi\) representative-selection objectives. Replacing the sum over nodes by an integral over population mass is the direct high-multiplicity form of \(\psi\); taking a minimum over the support gives the corresponding worst-case objective. The paper’s representative preferences are weighted by the number of people they represent, which becomes the mass assigned to each representative here.

The scope is the rule-agnostic surrogate selection problems of Proposition 1, which the paper uses to guide its aggregate-preference error objective. It does not extend to the full problem of minimizing \(\mathbb{E}[f(P)\,\Delta\,f(Q')]\), or to learning and validating the preference-spread models. Further questions include whether hardness persists when \(c\) must be realizable by expected Kendall–Tau similarities, and how approximation or parameterized algorithms depend on \(k\), \(\tau\), or additional structure in the similarity kernel.

The weakest point is that the paper’s Dominating Set reduction uses an arbitrary weighted similarity matrix; it does not establish that every matrix in that reduction comes from a realizable stochastic ranking model. The type-cohort regime also needs genuine repeated roles, rather than a distinct network-affinity profile for every person. Those are real limits on the hardness claim for a narrower empirical model. The case for a mirror still holds on the paper’s stated computational abstraction: its proposition takes the similarity matrix as the input to the representative-selection problem, and the continuous questions above preserve that problem while making population mass explicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper studies acquiring information from selected people. Preferences outside the sample are unknown; the network and its expected-similarity model help choose whom to ask. A society given as masses over realized ranking types already reveals the population’s preference profile, so its aggregate can be computed without sampling. The proponent’s type includes preferences as well as network role, which makes that information mismatch sharp.

There is a way to preserve the sampling problem: treat types as observable network cohorts with latent preference behavior, then model what a sampled person reports and how that report changes the estimated aggregate. That could be a question the authors recognize. But it requires an observation and uncertainty model beyond the proposed lift of Proposition 1. The paper’s reduction using an arbitrary similarity matrix does not itself establish the hardness of that richer sampling problem.

The two anchors also differ. For the robust objective, \(\rho_\mu(R)=\min_{t\in T}c(R,t)\), the masses have no effect at all; only the support matters. It remains a sensible worst-case representative-selection question, but its continuous-population interpretation adds little.

For the average objective, \(\psi_\mu(R)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t c(R,t)\), the masses matter. With repeated, interchangeable cohorts, this is a natural weighted version of the paper’s problem, and replicating every cohort preserves the objective. The selection decision still chooses \(k\) types, though, and a literal fractional choice of representative mass risks degeneracy: any positive mass of a type could make that entire type represented. Constraints to prevent that would introduce further modeling choices.

That leaves a real and plausible average-selection mirror. Its algorithmic work may lie mainly in a weighted finite-support problem, and its input may obscure the paper’s information-acquisition motivation, but neither point defeats it under the programme’s criteria. I cannot make an honest universal case that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror; the negative case is weak against the cohort version of the average objective.
