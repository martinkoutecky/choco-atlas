The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point against a mirror is that the paper offers no eligible named result to anchor one. It presents the RTS policy and derives a social-welfare guarantee in Equation (22) and a queue bound, but neither is a named theorem or a complexity classification for a computational problem. Those bounds concern the performance of a Lyapunov-based policy. So the proponent’s case supplies no qualifying anchor to defend.

The best candidate for a mirror would treat workers as masses grouped by capacity, reputation, price, and trust relationships, then ask how to allocate and sub-delegate workload. The obstacle is that the paper’s decisions depend on each worker’s particular trust neighborhood, queue state, deadlines, and delegation chains. A distribution over worker types alone loses the graph structure that determines feasible routing. Including each worker’s full neighborhood in its type preserves that information, but can make the support grow with the network. A repeated trust motif or block-mixing assumption could restore a meaningful mass model, but it would specify a new network regime and a new fluid-control problem; the paper provides no named computational result for that problem to mirror.

That makes the negative case strong against a paper-anchored, named-result mirror, but weak against the user’s universal claim. Repeated worker classes with shared trust structure and homogeneous task classes are plausible, and could yield a worthwhile continuous workload-allocation problem. I can’t honestly rule out every such scenario.
