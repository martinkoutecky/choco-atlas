The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Propositions 1–8 concern whether welfare rules satisfy Condorcet or participation criteria; none asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. Proposition 7 proves that Kemeny satisfies the participation criterion. Proposition 8 proves that it satisfies the Condorcet criterion, using Lemma 12 from Can and Storcken (2013b). Those results make Kemeny the paper’s natural focus, but they are axiomatic results, not computational ones.

The strongest nearby mirror I can offer is Kemeny aggregation over a continuous population. An instance is a fixed alternative set \(A\) and a finite support \(T\subseteq L\) of complete rankings, each with a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). The task is to return a ranking minimizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\delta(t,R) \]
over \(R\in L\). A plausible regime is a large recurring public consultation over fixed policy options: many respondents, but a modest number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings. This is a direct population version of the Kemeny rule the authors define, and Propositions 7 and 8 explain why that rule matters in their paper.

I would forecast hardness transfers: for a finite profile with \(n_t\) voters of each type, setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) leaves the Kemeny minimizers unchanged, since the continuous objective is the discrete distance objective divided by \(n\). The paper itself, however, supplies no hardness result for this forecast to extend, so this is a plausible ChoCo question rather than an anchored complexity claim.

The weakest point is decisive: this mirror shifts the paper’s contribution from axiomatic properties of a rule to the computational difficulty of finding that rule’s output. The authors would likely recognize the model as a natural continuous version of Kemeny aggregation, but the paper’s named results do not establish a computational question about it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper supplies no computational anchor. Propositions 1–8 ask whether four welfare rules satisfy two axioms; none states an algorithmic or complexity result. Kemeny’s participation and Condorcet propositions are the paper’s central results, yet they remain claims about rule properties.

The strongest repaired candidate is weighted Kemeny aggregation: given a finite support of rankings with rational masses, compute a ranking minimizing \(\sum_t \mu_t\delta(t,R)\). A large consultation with many respondents and few distinct rankings makes this a sensible high-multiplicity model. I can’t dismiss it on population grounds. For rational masses, scaling by a common denominator gives an equivalent finite profile, so the task is a continuous-population formulation of Kemeny computation.

That formulation still introduces a computational question the paper never asks. Its answer would concern the cost of computing Kemeny’s output, not the paper’s result that Kemeny satisfies both criteria. A closer continuation of those results would ask whether participation and Condorcet extend to a continuous society. For a single infinitesimal voter, participation becomes vacuous; for a positive-mass cohort, it becomes a meaningful axiom, but that is an axiomatic continuization, outside the programme’s stated computational scope.

So the negative case is strong against calling this a mirror of the paper’s results. It is weak against the broader claim that the Kemeny computation is worth studying as a ChoCo project: that is a credible adjacent question, and I can’t honestly rule it out as worthwhile.
