The continuous mirror question

Given finite man and woman type sets \(M,W\) and a supported distribution \(\mu\) over matched pairs \((m,w)\), with binary-rational masses summing to \(1\), define \(\nu_w=\sum_m\mu_{mw}\). For a quota \(q\in(0,1]\), does there exist \(W'\subseteq W\) with \(\sum_{w\in W'}\nu_w\ge q\) such that restricting \(\mu\) to pairs incident to \(W'\) yields a rationalisable matching? Renormalise the restricted distribution to total mass \(1\); rationalisability is the paper's notion, with strict preferences for each retained type and no blocking pair.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity matching market in which each type is a cohort sharing the relevant preferences, and \(\mu_{mw}\) is the population share matched between types \(m\) and \(w\). Choose indicators \(z_w\in\{0,1\}\) to maximise \(\sum_w\nu_w z_w\), subject to the support graph on selected woman types satisfying the paper's rationalisability criterion; equivalently, ask whether the optimum is at least \(q\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends Theorem 5's one-sided woman-type deletion problem to retained population share; it leaves the rationalisability test, match-deletion results, and empirical analyses unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What approximation guarantees or parameterized algorithms are possible for the mass-quota problem?
  • Which graph restrictions make weighted type deletion tractable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper. It says that MTR (Maximum Type Reduction) is NP-complete: given an aggregate matching \(M=(M,W,X)\) and \(K\in\mathbb N\), decide whether there is a woman-type reduction \(M'=(M,W',X')\), retaining the original matching quantities for every \(w\in W'\), such that \(|W'|\ge K\) and \(M'\) is rationalisable. The authors prove this by reduction from Maximum Independent Set. Their proof uses the rationalisability characterisation attributed to Echenique, Lee, Shum, and Yenmez (2013); the NP-completeness result itself is proved here.

A direct population-level version is Mass-Weighted Type Reduction for Stable Aggregate Matchings. An instance is finite sets of man types \(M\) and woman types \(W\), a supported distribution \(\mu\) over matched type pairs \((m,w)\), with positive binary-rational masses summing to \(1\), and a rational quota \(q\in(0,1]\). Write \(\nu_w=\sum_m\mu_{mw}\) for the population share of woman type \(w\). The question is whether some \(W'\subseteq W\) retains mass \(\sum_{w\in W'}\nu_w\ge q\) and makes the restricted distribution rationalisable. Restriction drops every pair mass incident to a woman type outside \(W'\), then renormalises the remainder. A yes-instance has a witness \(W'\) and strict preference orders for each retained type that make the observed matching stable, in the paper’s sense.

Here a type is a cohort whose members share everything the model uses: their relevant characteristics and their strict ranking of partner types. The mass \(\mu_{mw}\) is the share of matched pairs formed by types \(m\) and \(w\). A plausible regime is a large school-choice market observed across repeated cohorts: many students fall into a much smaller set of complete types, including their common school ranking, while schools are represented by types on the other side. If some student categories are unreliable in the data, the question is how much of the matched population can be retained after excluding whole categories, while restoring rationalisability. That is close to the paper’s own Q2 and its motivation for using type removal as flexibility in an empirical test. Removing only part of a type’s mass would leave its support edges present and so would not change the graph test; whole-type removal is the natural action in this model.

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers. Theorem 5’s instances can be made into valid continuous distributions without changing their support. For each woman type \(w\), let \(d_w\) be its number of incident support edges and assign mass \(\mu_{mw}=1/(|W|d_w)\) to each such edge. Every woman type then has mass \(1/|W|\), and the total mass is \(1\). The graph is unchanged, and the paper’s rationalisability criterion depends on that graph’s support. Thus retaining mass at least \(q=K/|W|\) is equivalent to retaining at least \(K\) woman types in the original MTR instance. This gives NP-hardness for the mass-weighted problem; it is in NP because a proposed subset and preference profile can be checked in polynomial time. The combinatorics survive in the type graph, not in the number of individuals.

This case covers Theorem 5’s type-deletion robustness question, with the objective changed from counting retained types to measuring retained population share. It does not claim that the paper’s other results—the fast rationalisability test or the efficient procedure for deleting matches—need the same mirror. Useful follow-up questions include approximation and parameterized algorithms for the mass quota, whether further graph structure makes weighted deletion tractable, and whether allowing deletion of types on both sides changes the complexity.

The soft spot is that the paper already studies aggregate matching quantities and explicitly notes that \(X(m,w)\) can be real-valued. A referee could therefore see some of the continuous representation as already available in the paper. The proposed question adds a population-share objective and makes the high-multiplicity interpretation explicit, but its hardness proof uses equal-mass cohorts, where that objective reduces exactly to counting types. There is also a substantive modelling condition: if people grouped by observed characteristics do not share the relevant preferences, they are not one type under this programme’s definition. The case survives those caveats because Theorem 5 asks precisely about deleting whole types, and large cohorts with shared preferences make that a meaningful population-level problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is the sole anchor, and the strongest objection is that the paper already admits real-valued aggregate match quantities: footnote 4 says \(X(m,w)\) can be extended to \(\mathbb{R}_+\). Its rationalisability test depends only on which cells have positive mass. So the version that still asks how many woman types can be retained is already covered by the paper’s aggregate model; its NP-hardness reduction uses unit masses and carries over directly.

The proposed mass-weighted version is the best repair. But its feasibility still depends only on the support graph: leaving any positive mass for a type leaves its edges in place. Partial removal changes rationalisability only when a type’s mass reaches zero. The problem therefore becomes selecting a maximum-weight set of woman types whose induced graph satisfies the paper’s component condition. Equal weights recover Theorem 5. The population shares supply weights, while the difficult structure remains a finite type-selection problem.

That makes the continuization contribution thin, but it does not defeat this mirror. Excluding unreliable cohorts and measuring the retained population share is a plausible empirical robustness question, and the paper itself motivates removing types as a way to handle measurement flexibility. A repeated school market with many students sharing complete preference types is a credible high-multiplicity scenario. The answer could be a useful weighted-complexity result even though the underlying feasibility test is support-based.

So the negative case is weak at its hardest point: the mass-weighted problem survives. I can argue that it is closer to a weighted extension of an already aggregate matching problem than a new continuous structure, but I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
