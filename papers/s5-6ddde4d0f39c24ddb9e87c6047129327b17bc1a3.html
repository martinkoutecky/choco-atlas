The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that this paper supplies a very plausible high-multiplicity regime for a later computational question. In club economies or economies with local public goods, many players can share a finite set of payoff-relevant types. If there are \(T\) types and \(N\) players, then \(N\) can grow while \(T\) stays fixed and the type proportions converge to an arbitrary \(\mu\in\Delta\). Here mass means the fraction of players of each type; a type includes every attribute the game uses.

But the paper has no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Its unnumbered THEOREM proves that, under small group effectiveness and \(\lambda\)-QTU, a sequence of games with \(|N^n|\to\infty\) and type proportions converging to an interior \(s^*\) has a vector \(v^*\) such that, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), \((v^*-\varepsilon\mathbf 1)\) is in the \(\varepsilon\)-core of every sufficiently large game in the sequence. The unnumbered Corollary extends this existence result to limiting proportions on the boundary of \(\Delta\). Neither result establishes polynomial time, hardness, or any other complexity classification. Propositions 1–5 provide properties used in the proof, not computational results. The fixed-point existence argument does not by itself give an efficient algorithm.

If an existence result were an eligible anchor, a plausible follow-up would be Continuous Equal-Treatment Approximate-Core Search. An instance would give a finite type set \(T\), a rational society distribution \(\mu\), a rational \(\varepsilon>0\), and a finite encoding of the payoff correspondence \(\mathcal F(\alpha)\) for every coalition mass vector \(0\leq\alpha\leq\mu\). The task would be to output a feasible equal-treatment payoff vector \(v\in\mathcal F(\mu)\) such that there is no nonzero \(\alpha\leq\mu\) and \(y\in\mathcal F(\alpha)\) with \(y_t>v_t+\varepsilon\) for every type with \(\alpha_t>0\). The decision variable is the per-type payoff vector; the objective is to find one satisfying feasibility and the no-blocking condition. This mirrors the approximate-core result, and the authors would likely recognize the underlying question. The paper establishes existence in its setting, but gives no basis for predicting the complexity of this search problem. A finite input encoding for the payoff correspondences would also need to be specified.

So the honest positive case is limited: the scenario is credible, and the paper’s core result identifies a natural object for algorithmic study. Its weakest point is decisive under the requested standard: there is no qualifying named computational anchor, and the paper already studies large populations with converging type distributions. The search problem above is a possible follow-up, not a continuous mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its unnumbered Theorem and Corollary prove existence of approximate equal-treatment core payoffs for large games; the propositions establish facts used in that proof. None classifies the complexity of a search, decision, or optimization problem. The central theorem is therefore a poor fit for a programme whose mirrors ask computational questions.

The population regime itself is unusually credible. The paper studies finitely many player types as the population grows, with type proportions converging to a distribution. Mass is natural here, and the high-multiplicity literature it cites supports that regime. Those are reasons in favor of studying these games, not objections.

The best computational follow-up would be to give type-based games an explicit finite representation and ask for an approximate equal-treatment core payoff, perhaps by describing feasible payoffs for bounded coalition configurations. That could be worthwhile. But it adds the ingredients the paper leaves unspecified: an encoding of coalition payoff sets and effective bounds for its small-group-effectiveness assumption. The paper assumes such bounds exist; it does not provide a computable bound or an algorithm for finding the payoff. Its fixed-point proof is existential. Under the paper’s general assumptions, the complexity would depend on the new representation and restrictions supplied by the follow-up.

So I would reject this as a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper. The proposed search question is a possible new research direction, not an anchor that survives the stated standard. The broader claim that *no* scenario could yield worthwhile computational work is less secure: a structured club or local-public-goods model might support a strong algorithmic question. The negative case is strongest—and only convincing—when limited to the paper as written.
