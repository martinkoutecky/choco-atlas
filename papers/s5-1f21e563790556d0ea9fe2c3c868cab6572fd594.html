What the mirror covers

The mirror covers none of the paper's named results: Theorems 1–5 characterize discussion dynamics and debate equilibria, but provide no computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: when \(X\) is finite and \(p\) is total and transitive, at least one debate exists, and every debate concludes at the compromise position \(x^*\), regardless of the initial status quo. This is a useful characterization of an equilibrium outcome, but it is not a named computational result: the theorem gives no complexity classification or input-to-solution algorithm. Theorem 1–4 likewise concern the dynamics and possible outcomes of discussion. So, under the anchor rule, this paper has no named computational result, and there is no qualifying anchor for a continuous problem.

A nearby extension would take an instance consisting of a finite position set \(X\), two opposing strict preference orders \(\succ_1,\succ_2\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over types, each type being a strict ranking of \(X\) that represents an argument. For each type \(r\), let \(x_r\) be its top-ranked position. Ask for the \(\succ_1\)-best position \(x\) among the supported \(x_r\) such that
\[ \mu(\{r:x_r\succ_1 x\})\le \tfrac12 \quad\text{and}\quad \mu(\{r:x_r\succ_2 x\})\le \tfrac12. \]
This is a weighted compromise-selection problem, expected to be tractable by sorting and cumulative masses. It resembles Theorem 5’s compromise rule, which counts arguments whose top positions each participant prefers to \(x^*\).

That extension does not make the society continuous: its mass is over argument types, not agents. Theorem 5 also analyzes a finite, unweighted debate game; it does not establish that a debate with divisible argument mass has the stated outcome. Defining that game and proving its equilibrium properties would be new work. The honest positive case is therefore a plausible neighboring model, but not an anchor-compliant ChoCo mirror of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is the paper’s strongest candidate, but it characterizes an equilibrium outcome; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1–4 likewise describe discussion dynamics and possible outcomes. Given explicit finite rankings, one can compute the Theorem 5 compromise by finding each argument’s top position and counting which of those positions each participant prefers to a candidate. Turning that calculation into a complexity question would require extra choices about the input representation or the task. Those choices would supply the computational problem; the paper does not.

The proposed weighted extension makes that gap clearer. Its measure \(\mu\) is over argument rankings, not voter types. In the paper, an argument is a shared reason or criterion, while the two participants are the strategic players with opposing utilities. Treating arguments as citizens changes what the mass represents and how it affects the game. Treating citizens as speakers instead changes the two-player game itself: who speaks, what they can argue, and how their preferences enter the outcome. Either could be an interesting new model, but neither is a population version already present in the paper.

There is also no evident high-multiplicity regime in the source problem. A debate might occur before a large electorate, but the electorate does not enter the formal model: the relevant agents remain two participants, and their identities and alternating moves matter. Giving them constituencies does not make those constituencies interchangeable units of mass in the theorem.

So the negative case is strong for a ChoCo mirror of this paper, though it cannot rule out every worthwhile project inspired by deliberation. A weighted-argument debate or a many-speaker deliberation model could be worth studying on its own. But that would introduce the population object and computational question rather than continuize a named computational result in this paper.
