The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow high multiplicity version of Chwe’s anonymous mechanism design problem. There is one qualification up front: the paper has no named theorem asserting NP-hardness or membership in P. Its closest computational anchor is a constructive optimization result.

That anchor is Proposition 3, proved in the paper. For odd \(n\ge 3\), common signal accuracy \(q\in(1/2,1)\), and at least one person with \(\phi_i(b)>q\) and one with \(\phi_j(a)>q\), it says that the supermajority penalty procedure \(f_{SP}\) uniquely maximizes an unbiased person’s expected utility among all anonymous incentive compatible procedures. Its rule is determined by \(y=q/(1-q)\) and the unique \(k\in\{0,\ldots,(n-3)/2\}\) for which the following \(z\) lies in \((0,1]\):
\[ D_k=\frac{k!(n-k)!}{(((n-1)/2)!)^2},\qquad z=\frac{D_k y^{(n-1)/2}-k(y^{k-1}+y^{n-k})} {(n-k)(y^k+y^{n-1-k})-k(y^{k-1}+y^{n-k})}. \]
If \(j\) people report \(a\), the probability \(\gamma_{SP}(j)\) of choosing \(a\) is \(1\) for \(j<k\), \(z\) for \(j=k\), \(0\) for \(k<j\le(n-1)/2\), \(1\) for \((n+1)/2\le j<n-k\), \(1-z\) for \(j=n-k\), and \(0\) for \(j>n-k\). The paper proves this result and the formula defining \(k,z\) in Lemma 2.

A concrete high multiplicity mirror is this. The instance has an odd population size \(N\), common rational signal accuracy \(q\), and a finite set \(T\) of bias types. Each type \(t\) has normalized bias \(\phi_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is a binary rational, with \(N\mu_t\) an integer. Thus \(N\) can be large while the support size \(\tau=|T|\) is small. Require positive mass on both sides of the paper’s significant-bias thresholds: some type has \(\phi_t(a)>q\), and some type has \(\phi_t(b)>q\). Type means the complete prior and preferences relevant to the paper’s normalized utility; agents of that type share those parameters and the common \(q\).

The decision variable is an anonymous randomized rule \(\gamma:\{0,\ldots,N\}\to[0,1]\), where \(\gamma(j)\) is the probability of choosing \(a\) after \(j\) reports for \(a\). Find the rule maximizing
\[ U^0(\gamma)=\tfrac12 P_\gamma(a\mid D=a,\text{ all report truthfully}) +\tfrac12 P_\gamma(b\mid D=b,\text{ all report truthfully}), \]
subject to truthful reporting being at least as good for every positive-mass type as always reporting \(a\) or always reporting \(b\), with everyone else truthful. If the population’s average bias is \(\tfrac12\), this objective is also its average normalized welfare.

I expect this problem to be tractable for explicit \(N\), with parameters \(N,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. Proposition 3 gives the optimizer directly; scanning the \(O(N)\) possible values of \(k\) and evaluating the rational formula yields an explicit rule. That is an output-sensitive construction, since the rule itself has \(N+1\) entries. The paper does not state this as a P result or analyze bit complexity, so that classification is a consequence one would establish for the mirror, not a claim to attribute to Chwe.

The regime could be a large anonymous panel assessing the same binary question: many assessors, drawn from a few standardized prior and preference classes, each receiving an independent signal of common accuracy. This preserves Chwe’s problem—anonymous decisions, private binary evidence, individual incentives, and truth-telling—and makes the society a distribution over types rather than a list of identities. The proposed mirror covers the anonymous optimal-procedure result, not the paper’s broader discussion of nonanonymous mechanisms or continuous signals.

The weakest point is also the boundary of the case: the procedure’s incentive constraints depend on an individual changing the report count by one. If agents become literally atomless, one person’s report changes no aggregate mass, so those constraints vanish. This mirror must retain \(N\) as the scale that gives each person mass \(1/N\); it is a high multiplicity representation of the population, not a fully atomless game. That is why I would present it as a plausible, limited continuous-population mirror rather than claim that Proposition 3 already solves a genuinely atomless limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 3 is the paper’s strongest mirror candidate: it constructs a unique optimal anonymous rule, though the paper gives no complexity classification. A large panel with a few standardized bias types is plausible. The difficulty is that the result depends on each person changing the report count by one. The type distribution \(\mu\) does not enter the IC constraints except through whether the two extreme-bias types are present. Aggregating people by type therefore adds no optimization over \(\tau\); \(N\) and the one-vote step do the work.

In an atomless version, an individual’s report changes no aggregate report mass, so every rule makes that person indifferent between truthful and false reports. The paper’s IC condition becomes vacuous. Under the usual law-of-large-numbers limit, truthful reports yield an \(a\)-share of \(q\) when \(a\) is correct and \(1-q\) when \(b\) is correct. A majority threshold then chooses correctly with certainty. Many rules attain the same welfare, while the supermajority penalties that make \(f_{SP}\) distinctive have zero probability under truthful aggregation. Truth-telling can remain an equilibrium, but IC no longer elicits it: other reporting patterns are equilibria too.

Keeping a finite pivotality scale preserves Chwe’s question, but it also keeps the finite-population mechanism: deviations change the count by one, and the rule remains indexed by \(N\). Proposition 4 says that as \(N\) grows, the SP rule’s welfare approaches majority rule; the penalty regions become rare. A local derivative condition could retain some first-order incentive information, but it would be a new marginal-incentive criterion, and the finite rule’s rare-event optimization would not survive in the ordinary limit. Recovering that behavior requires retaining the report-count granularity or its large-deviation information.

So the proponent’s panel scenario makes high multiplicity sensible, but it does not rescue this anchor as a continuous-population computational problem. The honest limit of the negative case is that a local or coalition-based variant might interest the authors; it would ask a neighboring question with a different incentive notion.
