That is an existence theorem, not an algorithm or a complexity classification. The other named results are also about equilibrium structure, continuity, or core selection.

A related high-multiplicity problem is easy to formulate, but it would be a follow-up question, not a mirror anchored in a computational result from this paper. Call it High-Multiplicity No-Delay Equilibrium Search. An instance has a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) legislator types. A type specifies an ideal point \(a_t\), quadratic utility \(u_t(x)=-(x-a_t)^2\) on \(X=[0,1]\), and a proposer-recognition propensity \(\lambda_t\). The instance also gives a status quo \(q\), discount factor \(\delta\), and passage threshold \(\alpha\). A type-contingent stationary strategy specifies a proposal \(p_t\) for each proposer type and an acceptance set \(A_t\) for each voter type. A proposal passes when at least an \(\alpha\) fraction of the society accepts it. The task is to return an exact description of a no-delay equilibrium: acceptance follows the type’s continuation-value threshold, each proposer chooses an optimal passing policy, and the induced proposals determine continuation values consistently.

A possible regime is a large party convention or similar assembly: many delegates, but only a modest number \(\tau\) of distinct preference and recognition types, with \(n\gg\tau\). The type-level mass is the fraction of delegates of each type; the action is a policy proposal or vote, and the equilibrium conditions—not a social welfare objective—determine the solution. This keeps the population continuous while preserving the paper’s bargaining question. However, in the continuum limit an individual delegate may be too small to affect passage, changing the strategic role of voting. The authors might recognize the connection, but it is not plainly the same game.

I would not assign that search problem to Class A, B, or C from this paper: Theorem 1 proves existence without giving a constructive method, and the paper supplies no hardness result to transfer. The relevant follow-up questions would be whether the equilibrium exists in this population model, how to compute it from rational type masses, and how its solutions relate to those of large finite assemblies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is decisive at the level of the proposed anchor: this paper states no computational result. Theorem 1 proves existence of a no-delay stationary equilibrium, using a fixed-point argument over mixed proposals. The other numbered results concern purity, continuity, delay, and core selection. The continuous policy space \(X\) and distributions over proposals are part of the *outcome and strategy* model; they do not make the population continuous.

The proposed high-multiplicity equilibrium search would be a new problem, not a computational version of Theorem 1. To state it, one must choose a population distribution over types and replace the paper’s arbitrary decisive-coalition rule \(D\) with something that aggregates by mass, such as a quota \(\alpha\). A large delegate convention with repeated preference types is a plausible setting for that choice. But it changes a model whose legislators have named identities and whose voting rules can include veto players, houses, and arbitrary coalitions.

The deeper change is strategic. In the finite game, a legislator’s vote can affect whether a proposal passes, and weak dominance pins acceptance to the comparison between the proposal and continuation value. In an atomless population, one voter has zero mass and cannot change passage; accepting and rejecting have the same consequence for that voter. A mass threshold can still define a coherent aggregate game, but the paper’s individual voting logic no longer supplies it. Treating factions as pivotal blocks restores that logic only by making the blocks strategic players rather than a continuous population.

One could investigate computation in such a newly specified bargaining game, and a large assembly may make its population regime credible. The negative case is therefore not that no related project could be worthwhile. It is that the paper gives no computational claim to mirror, and its existence theorem does not settle the computational question the proposed extension would ask. The convention scenario is the strongest point for the proponent; it supports a possible follow-up, but not a continuous computational mirror of this paper’s results.
