The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of claimant types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), per-person claim amounts \(a_t\geq 0\), relative strengths \(w_t>0\) normalized by \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t w_t=1\), and estate \(e>0\) per claimant with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t\geq e\), compute the per-person awards \(x_t=\min\{\lambda_* w_ta_t,a_t\}\), where \(\lambda_*\) is the least nonnegative value satisfying \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\min\{\lambda_* w_ta_t,a_t\}=e\).

The model it lives in

A distribution of claimants over finitely many complete types; each type has common per-person claims and strength, and receives a common per-person award. Compute the rule-defined allocation \(x_t\) that exhausts the per-capita estate while respecting claims and proportionally treating claimants who are not fully reimbursed. The rule supplies the selection criterion; no separate scalar welfare objective is needed.

The objection that survived

Proposition 4 proves equivalence with an iterative construction but gives no complexity bound; polynomial bit complexity is the proponent’s inference.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers computing the absolute priority allocation \(P_y\) in Proposition 4. It leaves the paper’s fairness characterizations, strategy-proofness results, comparative priority rule \(P_z\), and comparison with bankruptcy rules unmirrored.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: make the claimant population continuous and compute the paper’s absolute priority allocation \(P_y\). That keeps the paper’s division problem and fairness rule intact; it does not rely merely on the fact that the estate is divisible.

My lead anchor is Proposition 4, proved in this paper. It establishes that the two definitions of \(P_y\) are equivalent: the allocation \(P_y(B)_i=\min\{\lambda s_i a_i,a_i\}\), with \(\lambda>0\) chosen so the awards sum to the estate, is the same as the rule that repeatedly applies the weighted proportional rule \(P\), fully reimburses claimants whose proportional award exceeds their claim, removes them and their claims, and repeats on the remainder. The paper gives this as an algorithm and uses it to compute examples. This is an algorithmic anchor, though it does not state a standard complexity result such as membership in P; the paper gives no comparable named hardness result.

A continuous version is Mass Absolute Priority Allocation. An instance is a finite support \(T\) of claimant types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) has a per-person claim amount \(a_t\geq 0\) and relative claim strength \(w_t>0\), normalized so \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t w_t=1\). The estate is \(e>0\) per claimant, with \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t\geq e\). A type is complete for this rule: its members share both their claim amount and strength. Mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of claimants of that type.

The question is to compute the per-person allocation \(x_t\) for every type. A solution is the unique allocation given by
\[ x_t=\min\{\lambda_* w_ta_t,a_t\},\qquad \lambda_*=\min\left\{\lambda\geq 0: \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\min\{\lambda w_ta_t,a_t\}\geq e\right\}. \]
Thus each type receives the same per-person treatment, the aggregate allocation is efficient, and no one receives more than their claim. The rule’s selection criterion is absolute priority with proportional satisfaction among claimants who are not fully reimbursed; the paper does not posit a separate scalar welfare objective.

This has a credible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large compensation or insolvency process with many claimants but relatively few combinations of standardized claim amounts and statutory priority weights. With \(n\) claimants and \(n_t/n=\mu_t\), take total estate \(ne\) and individual strength \(w_t/n\) for each claimant of type \(t\); the continuous allocation then matches the finite \(P_y\) allocation exactly. The expected result is tractability: sort the at most \(\tau=|T|\) breakpoints \(1/w_t\), locate the interval containing \(\lambda_*\), and solve its linear equation. For binary rational input this gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\) and bit length \(L\). That algorithmic expectation is my inference for the mirror, not a complexity theorem claimed by the authors.

The mirror covers Proposition 4’s computation of \(P_y\), not the paper’s full axiomatic programme. Its strongest point is that \(P_y\) already depends on claim amounts and strengths, so repeated claimant types can be aggregated without changing what the rule asks. The estate remains a scalar amount and the allocations remain per-person receipts; the continuization is specifically the distribution of claimants over types. The weighted bankruptcy work discussed in the paper is useful neighboring evidence that these weighted claim problems are meaningful, though it is not itself a continuous-population result.

The weak point is substantial: this is a philosophy-and-economics paper, not a complexity paper, and Proposition 4 does not report a running-time bound or a hardness result. The proposed mirror therefore supplies a clean high-multiplicity computation, but offers little prospect of showing that a hard discrete problem becomes easy—the paper’s finite rule is already algorithmic. It also raises a natural next question: how should the mirror be represented and computed when claimant types have infinite or continuous support, rather than the finite support required by the programme’s input model?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central problem is that the paper gives no named computational result to continuize. Its propositions concern fairness axioms, impossibility, and characterizations of division rules. Proposition 4 is the closest thing to an algorithm: it proves that \(P_y\)’s formula agrees with a repeated proportional-allocation procedure. But it does not formulate a computational problem or analyze its complexity. Proposition 9’s strategy-proofness characterization is likewise an axiomatic result, not a complexity result.

The proposed mass version is coherent, and a population with many claimants sharing a few claim-and-strength types is plausible. The negative case should not rest on denying that. Rather, computing \(P_y\) for such a distribution would be a new computational extension of the paper’s rule, not a continuous mirror of a computational result the paper establishes. The proposed breakpoint algorithm may be useful, but it is the proponent’s new result. I do not count the weighted-bankruptcy literature as a collision or as evidence against the mirror; it supports the plausibility of the claimant model.

A broader attempt to mirror Proposition 9 runs into a different issue. Its strategy-proofness properties compare outcomes before and after individuals split or merge claims. In a population measure, an individual has no mass: splitting or merging identities either becomes an extra invariance condition on representations, or requires retaining individual atoms. That does not prevent defining such a condition, but it makes it a new axiom to study rather than a computational question supplied by this paper.

So the strongest negative case is about fit with ChoCo’s computational-gap aim, not about whether a continuous claimant model can be written down. It is strong if the programme requires a paper to contribute a computational result; it is weaker if the programme also welcomes new computational analyses built from rules in axiomatic papers.
