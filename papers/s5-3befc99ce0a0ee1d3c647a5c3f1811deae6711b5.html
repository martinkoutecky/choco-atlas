What the mirror covers

The proposed version concerns only Theorem 4, restricted to repeated independent single-person service; it leaves Theorems 1–3 and 5 and the general multi-agent framework aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow, and there is an anchor limitation: the paper states no named result in a complexity class such as P or NP-hard. Its theorems characterize implementation; they do not establish a complexity bound. The closest useful anchor is Theorem 4, which is a precise characterization but not a complexity result in the strict sense of your anchor requirement.

Theorem 4, proved in this paper, says that an SCF \(f:\Theta\to X\) is obviously-strategyproof (OSP) implementable if and only if its canonical operator \(\gamma_{\mathrm{OD}-f}\) is achievable. The operator groups states through chains of pairwise obvious-incentive conflicts: at a state in \(E\), a conflict arises when some type prefers the best outcome reachable by a false report to the worst outcome under truthful play, allowing opponents’ types to range over \(E_{-i}\). Achievability means that for some finite number of iterations \(N\), the resulting set is a singleton for every state: \(\gamma_{\mathrm{OD}-f}^{(N)}[\Theta,\theta]=\{\theta\}\). The theorem is proved here via Proposition 2; it is not merely a result cited from earlier work.

A plausible continuous mirror is Population OSP Menu Implementation. Imagine a large public service or non-rival consumer programme in which each person independently completes the same sequential application or choice protocol. A type \(t\in T\) is the person’s complete relevant description—preferences, costs, and other attributes used by the mechanism. The society is a mass distribution \(\mu\) over a fixed finite \(T\), with rational type shares and support size \(\tau\); millions of people may therefore be represented by a modest number of types. A prescribed rule \(f:T\to X\) assigns each person an individual outcome. The society-level outcome is the mass distribution \(\nu_\mu(x)=\sum_{t:f(t)=x}\mu_t\), or its average allocation when outcomes are bundles.

The decision variable is a common finite sequential protocol and a truthful strategy for each type. The question is whether the protocol gives every type its prescribed outcome and makes truthful play obviously dominant for each person. A solution is the protocol, its type-contingent strategies, and the resulting individual outcomes; the aggregate is the corresponding outcome distribution. Individual bundles can remain integral even when the population’s average bundle is fractional. The continuum here is the actual recipient population, not a prior over states or a continuous outcome space.

For this regime, I expect a tractable answer: with one decision maker per independent service instance, the OSP condition reduces to checking \(u^t(f(t))\ge u^t(f(t'))\) for all \(t,t'\). That takes \(O(\tau^2)\) utility comparisons for explicit finite input. This is my deduction for the restricted mirror, not a running-time result claimed by the paper. Theorem 4 supplies the characterization behind the question, but does not itself prove this complexity bound.

The scope is just Theorem 4’s OSP-implementation question, in a repeated individual-service setting. I would not claim a mirror for its general multi-agent strategic interactions, its PBE result, or its revelation principles more broadly. A natural next question is whether an operator characterization survives when agents compete for scarce resources and the allocation to one person depends on the population’s type distribution.

The soft spot is that independent service instances remove the cross-agent strategic interaction that makes much of the paper’s mechanism-design problem distinctive. An author or referee might regard this as a continuum of repeated single-agent problems, rather than a continuous version of the paper’s central multi-agent setting. The case survives only as a limited mirror: each person is a genuine strategic agent with a complete type, the population is represented by mass, and the OSP implementation question remains intact for that setting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the wrong kind of anchor for this programme: it characterizes OSP implementability, but makes no computational claim. It says that \(f\) is OSP-implementable exactly when \(\gamma_{\mathrm{OD}-f}\) is achievable. It supplies no finite input encoding, size measure, or algorithmic bound; indeed, the paper allows \(\Theta\) to be infinite. The other named results are also characterizations, not complexity results. The proposed \(O(\tau^2)\) bound is a new claim about the analyst’s model, not a computational result in the paper.

The proposed independent-service mirror changes the object from a social choice function \(f:\prod_i\Theta_i\to X\) to a one-person rule \(f:T\to X\), then repeats it across a population. For OSP implementation, the type shares \(\mu_t\) do not affect the incentive comparisons: they reduce to checking conditions such as \(u^t(f(t))\ge u^t(f(t'))\). The shares can describe the aggregate outcome distribution, but they do not change whether the rule is implementable. That is a sensible mass description of service users, but the population is incidental to the computational question. It does not mirror Theorem 4’s operator, which tracks how a sequential mechanism separates joint states through strategic interactions among agents.

The stronger candidate would make allocations depend on type masses—for example, by introducing scarce resources or competition. But if the mechanism’s relevant outcome depends only on \(\mu\), a single person’s misreport changes no mass in an atomless population. The unilateral incentive comparisons at the heart of OSP then lose their aggregate effect. One can preserve them by retaining a finite population and its \(1/n\) changes to the type shares, or by adding individual allocations that depend on each person’s report. The first retains a pivotality scale that the exact continuum omits; the second makes the question partly an individual menu problem in an aggregate environment. Treating each type’s entire mass as a strategic player is another possible rescue, but it adds coordinated group action and changes the paper’s individual-agent solution concept.

I would not count high multiplicity itself as an objection: many repeated agents with common relevant types are a plausible regime. A finite high-multiplicity OSP problem could be worth studying. The difficulty is that this paper provides no computational result to carry into that problem, and its continuous independent-service version makes the mass dimension irrelevant to implementation. A non-atomic OSP theory with market effects might also be worthwhile, but it would need a new model of individual deviations, outcomes, and sequential protocols before there is a computational question to mirror.

So the negative case is strongest against this anchor and this proposed mirror, not as a proof that no related research could be valuable. Its weak point is precisely that a carefully designed non-atomic mechanism model might make population masses and incentives interact meaningfully. That would be a new computational mechanism-design direction, rather than a continuous mirror grounded in Theorem 4.
