The continuous mirror question

Given posts \(P\), supported types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), each type’s complete weak preference order over eligible posts and its own last resort \(\ell_t\), and rational per-capita capacity bounds \(b_p \leq u_p\), does there exist a capacity vector \(q\) and assignment masses \(\alpha_{t,p}\) such that \(b_p \leq q_p \leq u_p\), \(\sum_{p}\alpha_{t,p}=\mu_t\), and \(\sum_t\alpha_{t,p}\leq q_p\) for every real post \(p\), with last resorts unlimited? Require that no feasible reassignment \(\beta_{t,p,r}\), respecting the same capacities, has positive vote margin \(\sum_{t,p,r}\beta_{t,p,r}\bigl(\mathbf{1}[r\succ_t p]-\mathbf{1}[p\succ_t r]\bigr)>0\).

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over complete applicant preference and eligibility types; per-capita post capacities \(q_p\); assignment masses \(\alpha_{t,p}\); and feasibility as the objective, with popularity tested against all capacity-feasible reassignment masses \(\beta_{t,p,r}\). Each unit of mass is assigned one actual post or its type’s last resort.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Section IV’s FIXINGCOPIES decision and search problem; it leaves Section III’s weighted optimal-popular-matching algorithm and the finite-instance experimental comparisons outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of continuous Popular Capacity Expansion in \(|P|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length?
  • Does fractional capacity expansion preserve the known FIXINGCOPIES hardness, or can it bypass the discrete reduction?
  • Can the paper’s safe dominance rule be replaced by a useful rule for weighted type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest scenario is a large annual graduate or training-program intake. Applicants fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of complete preference types—identical ranked program lists and eligibility details—so hundreds of types might describe tens of thousands of applicants. Mass is the fraction of the cohort of each type. This is close to the paper’s own training-program example: increasing a program’s capacity by a fraction of the cohort is meaningful when capacities and intake scale together.

There is an anchor limitation. The paper has no numbered theorem that states a complexity classification. Its NP-completeness claim for FIXINGCOPIES appears in prose and is attributed to earlier work; its polynomial-time claim for optimal popular matching with ties is also unnumbered. The closest original numbered result for the copy problem is Theorem 4.2, proved here. It says: if \(I\) has no popular matching and \(p_i\in F_I\), where \(F_I\) consists of posts that are neither \(f\)-posts nor \(s\)-posts, then any instance obtained by copying posts that *does* admit a popular matching still admits one when \(p_i\) is left at its original copy count. This is a computationally useful dominance result, but it is not a hardness or tractability theorem. So it is a structural anchor for the case below, not a qualifying complexity anchor under the strict rule in the prompt.

The concrete mirror is Popular Capacity Expansion over a Continuum. An instance has a finite set of posts \(P\), supported preference types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational lower and upper capacity bounds \(b_p\leq u_p\). Each type has a complete weak ranking over the posts and an unlimited, type-specific last resort \(\ell_t\). The question is whether there are capacities \(q_p\in[b_p,u_p]\) and assignment masses \(\alpha_{t,p}\geq0\) such that
\[ \sum_p\alpha_{t,p}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t\alpha_{t,p}\leq q_p, \]
and the resulting assignment is popular. A solution is the chosen capacity vector and assignment. Popularity means that no feasible reassignment makes strictly more population mass prefer the new assignment than the current one.

That definition can be made finite and precise. For each type \(t\), let \(z_{t,p,r}\) be the mass currently assigned to \(p\) that a challenger reassigns to \(r\). Require \(\sum_r z_{t,p,r}=\alpha_{t,p}\) and that the challenger respects the same capacities. For every feasible \(z\), its vote margin must satisfy
\[ \sum_{t,p,r} z_{t,p,r} \bigl(\mathbf{1}[r\succ_t p]-\mathbf{1}[p\succ_t r]\bigr)\leq 0. \]
The population and capacity choices are continuous; each unit of mass still receives one actual post. This mirrors the paper’s capacity-expansion problem, rather than making outcomes or preferences probabilistic.

I would tentatively expect hardness to transfer, because the choices over posts and their capacity roles appear central to the copy problem, and the paper notes hardness even with a shared master preference order. That is only a hypothesis: fractional capacity increases could bypass the discrete reduction, so a transfer proof would need to establish a gap or another reason fractional choices do not help. Theorem 4.2 also motivates asking whether an appropriate mass-based analogue of \(F_I\) gives safe pruning; its finite Gallai–Edmonds labels do not automatically extend to weighted populations. Further questions include the complexity in \((|P|,\tau,L)\), whether popular assignments exist for every fixed capacity vector, and how closely continuous solutions approximate discrete ones.

This covers Section IV’s post-copy problem, not the paper’s optimal-popular-matching result in Section III. Its softest point is the missing numbered complexity anchor—and, mathematically, the possibility that fractional seat expansion removes the very integrality responsible for the known hardness. The scenario and model remain plausible: capacity expansion for a large homogeneous intake is already the paper’s problem, expressed in cohort shares.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s stated anchor is weak: Theorem 4.2 is a pruning rule for the finite copy-search problem, not a complexity result. The NP-completeness claim for FIXINGCOPIES is attributed to earlier work, while the paper’s own polynomial-time result for optimal popular matching is stated in prose rather than as a numbered theorem. So the case does not supply the kind of named computational result the prompt asks it to mirror. That is a weakness in the anchor, though, not evidence that the underlying question is unworthy.

The stronger version of their mirror survives my objections. In a large training intake, applicants can be grouped by complete preference and eligibility types, and program capacity can scale with the cohort. For type mass \(\mu_t\), popularity has a direct meaning: no feasible reassignment may produce a positive net mass of applicants who prefer the reassignment. Fractional aggregate assignments still represent people receiving whole posts, and changing capacity to a per-capita quantity is a natural co-modification of the population model. Neither identity dependence nor a vanishing majority comparison blocks it.

Theorem 4.2 itself does not establish that a mass-based dominance rule exists. Its \(F_I\) condition and the \(E,O,U\) labels come from the finite first-rank graph, so a weighted-flow version would need its own definition and proof. But that is a research question, not a fundamental mismatch. And setting aside the proponent’s anchor, the paper’s optimal-popular-matching algorithm is an even clearer computational target: one can ask for weighted-rank optimal popular assignments over a rational type distribution and per-capita capacities. Its running-time claim is unnumbered, but it is plainly a computational result in the paper.

Adjacent work on popular mixed and fractional matchings concerns fractional outcomes on a finite graph; it does not, by itself, make the population continuous or settle this mirror.[Huang and Kavitha, “Popularity, Mixed Matchings, and Self-duality”](https://doi.org/10.1137/1.9781611974782.151)

So I can disqualify Theorem 4.2 as a complexity anchor as presented, but I cannot honestly make the broader case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The training-cohort model remains plausible, and the strongest negative case is therefore weak.
