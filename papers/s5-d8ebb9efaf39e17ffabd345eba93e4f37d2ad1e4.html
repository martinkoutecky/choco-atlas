The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large city choosing a public-good level, where many residents share the same complete preference ordering and only a modest number \(\tau\) of preference types occur. A type would be a full single-peaked preference on \([0,1]\), or the completely indifferent preference \(R_0\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents with that type. This is a sensible population regime for the paper’s public-decision setting.

The paper’s named results do not provide a qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says that for \(n\ge3\), a rule \(f:S_0^n\to[0,1]\) is onto and pairwise strategy-proof if and only if it is a target rule with a default: for profiles with at least one single-peaked agent, it chooses target \(x\) when \(x\) lies between the smallest and largest reported peaks, and otherwise chooses the nearer endpoint of that interval; at the all-indifferent profile it chooses default \(y\). This is an axiomatic characterization, not a result about complexity, algorithms, or a computational problem. Lemma 2 and Proposition 1 also concern incentive and efficiency properties, not computational complexity. So the paper has no named computational result to serve as an anchor.

A possible follow-on, rather than an anchor-backed mirror, is *Target-Rule Evaluation for a High-Multiplicity Public-Good Society*. Its instance is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete preference types, with rational masses summing to \(1\), plus rational target \(x\) and default \(y\). Its task is to return the chosen level. If every positive-mass type is indifferent, return \(y\); otherwise let \(p_{\min}\) and \(p_{\max}\) be the smallest and largest peaks among the positive-mass non-indifferent types, and return
\[ \min\bigl(\max(x,p_{\min}),p_{\max}\bigr). \]
The expected answer is tractable: scan the support to find the two extreme peaks, in \(O(\tau)\) comparisons. This is a precise population-continuous computational question, and the public-good interpretation is plausible. But the paper neither poses nor analyzes it, and its simplicity comes from evaluating a characterized rule. I would not present it as a computational result mirrored from this paper.

The main weakness is decisive for the requested case: the paper studies a finite society and characterizes rules through axioms; its interval is the outcome space. A continuous extension of the characterization would remain axiomatic. Moreover, individual misreports in an atomless population change zero mass, so extending pairwise strategy-proofness would require a new definition of coalition deviations. The population mirror is conceivable, but there is no honest anchor-specific ChoCo problem to hand a prover from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes rules by onto-ness and pairwise strategy-proofness; Proposition 1 and Lemma 2 concern incentive and efficiency properties. None defines an input, computational task, or complexity question. The condition \(n\ge3\) is a qualification on the characterization, not a complexity result. That rules out an anchor-backed ChoCo mirror.

The proposed evaluation task is well-defined, but it does not fill that gap. For a target rule, the output depends on the smallest and largest peaks in the positive-mass support, or on the default if that support has no non-indifferent type. The masses’ magnitudes do not matter. Since the paper’s finite-society rule already uses those same extremes, a list of peaks gives the same computation without a continuous-population model. And because preferences are complete orderings over \([0,1]\), an input encoding needs specification; for evaluation, the theorem’s tops-only property lets one avoid that issue by recording just peaks. This makes the evaluator a concise restatement of a characterized rule, rather than a computational question the paper poses.

The best direct extension of the theorem would put a distribution over preference types and ask which measurable rules are onto and strategy-proof. That could be a sensible axiomatic question, but it remains axiomatic. In an atomless society, deviations by one or two individuals have zero mass and do not change the distribution; replacing them with positive-mass coalition deviations may be natural, but it changes the incentive axiom without creating a computational task. More generally, the rule responds to whether a peak is present in the support, not how much mass it carries, so the continuous population’s proportions contribute little to the result.

A plausible algorithmic follow-on would add costs for changing residents’ preferences and ask for the least-cost mass transfer that changes the selected level. That could be worth studying in its own right. But the cost model and intervention problem are additions: the paper supplies neither, and any complexity would come from those additions rather than from its characterization. A simple version may reduce to summing the costs of removing types from one side of the target; a richer version would need further structure.

So the negative case is strong against a *mirror of this paper’s results*: there is no computational anchor, and the most immediate population reformulation adds no population computation. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists. A city-wide public-good decision with a carefully specified, cost-bearing intervention model is plausible; I cannot honestly rule out a valuable continuous algorithmic project built from it. That would be a new problem prompted by the setting, not a computational mirror of this paper.
