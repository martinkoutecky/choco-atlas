The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant positive case: the paper has no named result asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic classification.

The closest hook is Theorem 2.1, proved in the paper: weights minimizing the expected squared gap between the popular vote and the council vote are \(w_\nu=\tfrac12\mathbb{E}| \sum_i X_{\nu i}|\). But that is an analytic characterization of the optimum, not a result that the underlying problem is in P or has another computational classification. Theorems 2.2, 3.3, and 5.1 give asymptotic expected-margin laws under independent, common-belief, and mean-field voting models; they likewise do not analyze computational complexity.

The paper’s electorate remains finite: its random outcome is a vote vector in \(\{-1,1\}^N\). The common-belief variable \(Z\) and its distribution model uncertainty about proposals and correlated votes; they are not a continuous distribution of voter types. Taking \(N\) large does not itself supply a continuous-population computational result.

A continuum version of the fair-weight optimization could be posed, but it would be a new analytic reformulation rather than a mirror anchored by a named computational result in this paper. Under the stated anchor rule, there is no per-anchor question to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed case for in effect concedes the strongest negative point: it identifies no theorem about computational complexity or algorithms. Theorem 2.1 characterizes optimal weights, and Theorems 2.2, 3.3, and 5.1 derive their asymptotic behavior under particular probability models. None asks for an exact, approximate, or parameterized algorithm. On the programme’s anchor test, there is no computational result here to mirror.

The best candidate is Theorem 2.1. It gives \(w_\nu=\tfrac12\mathbb{E}|S_\nu|\), where \(S_\nu\) is the state’s total vote, so perhaps one could replace each finite electorate with a continuous mass and ask for optimal weights. But that carries over an expected-margin formula, not a computational problem: the theorem has already reduced the optimization to a separate expectation for each state. In the natural models the paper studies, the later theorems evaluate that quantity or its limit. To make the task computationally difficult, one would need to supply a more complicated encoding of the joint vote distribution. Then the algorithmic question would concern inference in that encoding, rather than computation over a continuous society.

There is a deeper obstacle to the most direct mass model. The population’s type proportions alone do not determine the quantity in Theorem 2.1; the within-state dependence does. Two states can have the same marginal proportion of voters favoring either side, yet one have independent votes and the other a shared belief. Their expected absolute margins can scale as \(\sqrt{N}\) and \(N\), respectively. Preserving the difference requires carrying a joint law, common-belief variable, or interaction structure alongside the population masses. If the types are expanded to contain that information, the continuous input is effectively a representation of the dependence model, and the difficulty lies in that representation.

The other suggested candidates do not provide a better anchor. Theorems 2.2, 3.3, and 5.1 are analytic statements about how expected margins scale as population size grows. A continuum limit could restate those scaling questions, but it would still be analysis of probability models, not a computational classification. The paper’s common-belief variable \(\mu\) is already a distribution over proposal environments, not a distribution of voters; treating it as a continuous society would change what is being continuized.

Large electorates are plainly a sensible high-multiplicity regime for voting, and this is not an argument against applying that regime to voting. The weakness is narrower: this paper gives no computational question for that regime. One could invent a new problem about evaluating fair weights from a succinctly encoded correlation model, but its complexity would depend on choices of encoding the paper does not make, and it would not mirror any of its named results. That leaves a strong case against a worthwhile continuous *computational mirror of this paper*, though not proof that no adjacent computational project could be devised.
