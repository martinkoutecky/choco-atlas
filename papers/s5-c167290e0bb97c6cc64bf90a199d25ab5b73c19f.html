The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-horizon MDP, a feasible policy set \(\Pi\), a finite support \(T\) of stakeholder reward types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), bounded per-type rewards, an aggregation rule \(\ell\in\{1,2\}\), and \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), construct a portfolio \(\Pi'\subseteq\Pi\) such that for every \(p\le1\), some \(\pi\in\Pi'\) has \(v_\mu^{(\ell)}(\pi,p)\ge\alpha\max_{\rho\in\Pi}v_\mu^{(\ell)}(\rho,p)\). Here \(v_\mu^{(1)}(\pi,p)=\mathbb{E}[f_\mu(G(\xi),p)]\), \(v_\mu^{(2)}(\pi,p)=f_\mu(\mathbb{E}[G(\xi)],p)\), and \(f_\mu\) is the mass-weighted generalized \(p\)-mean, including its \(p=0\) and \(p=-\infty\) cases.

The model it lives in

A shared finite-horizon MDP and policy set, with stakeholder types \(t\in T\), masses \(\mu_t\), and per-type rewards; the decision is a portfolio of policies, evaluated by population-weighted generalized \(p\)-means. The stated guarantee is oracle-relative: fixed-\(p\) weighted welfare optimization is available as an oracle.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1’s portfolio guarantee and Proposition 4.4’s warm-start bound; it leaves the heuristic and experimental results without a separate mirror, and the paper supplies no hardness anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the weighted fixed-\(p\) policy oracle be implemented in time polynomial in the MDP description, support size \(|T|\), mass bit length, and other input parameters?
  • Can the portfolio and warm-start bounds be proved directly for weighted inputs with explicit dependence on the smallest positive type mass, without expanding to a population of size \(Q\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a continuous stakeholder welfare portfolio problem. The continuum here is the population of stakeholders, not the paper’s already-continuous parameter \(p\). Imagine a public health agency choosing a recurring intervention policy for a very large beneficiary population. Beneficiaries who have the same reward model and the same decision-relevant circumstances form a type; a type’s mass is its fraction of the population. A plausible regime is millions of beneficiaries but only dozens of recurring reward-model types. Members of a type are interchangeable for evaluating the policy, while the policy still acts in the paper’s shared MDP and faces its same constraints.

Formally, an instance gives a finite-horizon MDP, a feasible policy set \(\Pi\), a finite support \(T\) of types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-type reward functions bounded by \(L\) and \(U\), an aggregation rule \(\ell\in\{1,2\}\), and an approximation factor \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). For a trajectory \(\xi\), let \(G_t(\xi)\) be the cumulative per-person reward for type \(t\). Replace the paper’s equally weighted mean by the population-weighted one: for \(p\notin\{0,-\infty\}\), \(f_\mu(x,p)=(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t^p)^{1/p}\); at \(p=0\), \(f_\mu(x,0)=\exp(\sum_t\mu_t\ln x_t)\); and at \(p=-\infty\), \(f_\mu(x,-\infty)=\min_{t\in T}x_t\). Define \(v_\mu^{(1)}(\pi,p)=\mathbb E[f_\mu(G(\xi),p)]\) and \(v_\mu^{(2)}(\pi,p)=f_\mu(\mathbb E[G(\xi)],p)\).

The lead question, Continuous Stakeholder Welfare Portfolio, is: given that instance and access to an oracle maximizing \(v_\mu^{(\ell)}(\pi,p)\) over \(\Pi\) for a specified \(p\), construct a set \(\Pi'\subseteq\Pi\) such that for every \(p\le1\), some \(\pi\in\Pi'\) satisfies \(v_\mu^{(\ell)}(\pi,p)\ge\alpha\max_{\pi'\in\Pi}v_\mu^{(\ell)}(\pi',p)\). A solution is the portfolio of policies itself; it need not minimize the portfolio’s cardinality.

The anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper (Appendix A.1). In full, for an MDP with \(N\) reward functions and condition number \(\kappa=U/L\), a feasible policy set, an aggregation rule \(\ell\in\{1,2\}\), and \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), \(p\)-MeanPortfolio returns an \(\alpha\)-approximate portfolio for every \(p\le1\). Its portfolio size is \(O(\ln\kappa/\ln(1/\alpha))\), and its number of calls to the fixed-\(p\) welfare-optimization oracle is \(\widetilde O((\ln\kappa)^2\ln\ln N/\ln(1/\alpha))\), with the tilde hiding lower-order terms.

There is a particularly direct dictionary to this mirror. If the rational masses have common denominator \(Q\), replace each type \(t\) by \(Q\mu_t\) identical stakeholders. The paper’s ordinary unweighted \(p\)-mean on those \(Q\) reward coordinates is exactly \(f_\mu\), including the cases \(p=0\) and \(p=-\infty\). Thus the portfolio theorem applies to the virtual discrete population; the continuous input simply stores repeated types by their masses. That is a faithful high-multiplicity version of the same policy-selection question, not an outcome-space relaxation. The expected direction is tractable in the paper’s oracle model. For a succinct implementation, the remaining question is whether the weighted fixed-\(p\) policy oracle can be solved efficiently from the MDP and the \(\tau=|T|\) type records.

A second, narrower mirror is Continuous Welfare Warm-Start Sensitivity. Its instance is the same typed society and MDP, together with \(p<q\le1\), a choice of \(\ell\), and a policy \(\pi_p\) optimal at \(p\). The task is to certify how far that policy can be from optimal at \(q\); the answer is the additive bound \((q-p)UH\kappa\ln\kappa\), where \(H\) is the horizon and \(\kappa=U/L\). Its anchor is Proposition 4.4, also proved here (Appendix A.2): for the paper’s finite stakeholder model, if \(q>p\) and \(\pi_p\) maximizes \(v^{(\ell)}(\cdot,p)\), then \(\lvert v_*^{(\ell)}(q)-v^{(\ell)}(\pi_p,q)\rvert\le(q-p)UH\kappa\ln\kappa\), for both \(\ell=1\) and \(\ell=2\). The same rational-mass replication gives this sensitivity question for the continuous society. It is a useful supporting result because the bound does not grow with the number of virtual stakeholders. The expected direction is a direct sensitivity guarantee, though the cost of finding \(\pi_p\) still rests on the same oracle.

These mirrors cover the paper’s portfolio guarantee and its warm-start bound; they do not claim to continuize the paper’s heuristic or its experimental results. The paper has no numbered NP-hardness or other hardness-classification anchor, so this case has no discrete hardness result whose disappearance in the continuum can be tested. Further questions include whether one can minimize portfolio size, obtain total running-time guarantees beyond oracle calls, and handle approximate welfare oracles and uncertain type masses.

The weakest point is that this is an extension of an algorithmic model, not a new tractability theorem established by the paper: the authors study a finite list of stakeholders, and the weighted formulation is not written down there. Rational-mass replication shows why the mirror is mathematically faithful, but it does not prove that the scalarized policy oracle is efficient on a compressed, typed MDP. Still, the population interpretation is natural for the paper’s own multi-stakeholder applications, and it preserves the central question: how to compute a small portfolio of policies that performs well across welfare criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is a scope concern. In the paper, \(N\) indexes reward functions: it counts the welfare dimensions being aggregated, not necessarily people. In the healthcare experiment, the 59 rewards encode different subgroup priorities, while the 2,100 beneficiaries are arms in the RMAB. Giving those 59 dimensions population masses would change how the paper’s objectives are weighted. The experiment itself therefore does not establish that its stakeholder coordinates are a population with many interchangeable members of each type.

A more faithful beneficiary-level mirror would need to model the population’s type-specific rewards and how the intervention policy affects them. That could be a worthwhile problem, but it is a new population model; Theorem 4.1 does not by itself establish its total running time. The theorem bounds calls to a fixed-\(p\) policy-optimization oracle, and the paper acknowledges that each call can be expensive. Proposition 4.4 is weaker as an independent anchor: it gives sensitivity to \(p\), rather than a complexity classification for a continuous society.

That is the best skeptical case, but it does not defeat the stronger mirror the proponent proposed. A large population of stakeholders sharing a small number of reward types is coherent. With rational type masses, the weighted \(p\)-mean is exactly the paper’s ordinary \(p\)-mean on a replicated population; the same construction applies to both anchors. This replication is evidence for a sensible high-multiplicity regime, not a novelty objection under the programme’s standards.

So the negative case is weak on the central claim. The paper’s experiments do not directly supply a population model, and its oracle-call bounds leave a real computational question about compressed inputs. But those are limits on what the paper already proves, not reasons that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. I cannot honestly make the universal negative stick.
