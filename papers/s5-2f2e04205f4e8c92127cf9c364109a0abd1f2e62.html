The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation types with rational monotone submodular valuations \(v_t\) over \(m\) good types, rational population masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), does there exist a rational assignment \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) of type mass to integral bundles \(S\subseteq [m]\), with \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}=q_g\), such that for every \(t,u\in T\) and bundles \(A,B\) with \(x_{t,A}>0\) and \(x_{u,B}>0\), \(v_t(B\setminus\{g\})\le v_t(A)\) for every \(g\in B\)?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity course-allocation model: \(\tau\) complete student valuation types carry masses \(\mu_t\); each student receives an indivisible bundle of course types, while course supplies scale per capita as \(q_g\). The variables \(x_{t,S}\) assign mass to bundles, and the question is whether a feasible assignment satisfies EFX across every bundle in its support.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's constructible EFX nonexistence family; it leaves Theorems 1 and 3, and the paper's encoding and formal-verification results, aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether such an EFX mass assignment exists, in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give a case with a qualifying computational anchor: the paper names no result saying a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest result is Theorem 2, proved here. It says that for every \(n\ge 3\) and \(m\ge n+5\), there is a fair division instance with submodular valuations for which no EFX allocation exists. The authors find the three-agent, eight-good counterexample using SAT, then extend it to larger \(n,m\). That is a computationally obtained existence result, but it is not a complexity result.

The most plausible follow-up mirror would be high-multiplicity EFX feasibility. An instance would give a finite set of complete valuation types \(T\), each with a monotone submodular valuation \(v_t\) over a catalog of \(m\) good types; rational population masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one; and rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\) for the goods. Each person receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq [m]\), with at most one copy of each good type. The decision variable \(x_{t,S}\ge 0\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\). It must satisfy \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}=q_g\). The assignment is EFX if, whenever type \(t\) has positive mass assigned bundle \(A\) and any agent has positive mass assigned bundle \(B\), \(v_t(B\setminus\{g\})\le v_t(A)\) for every \(g\in B\). A solution is such a rational mass assignment \(x\).

A plausible regime is a large course-allocation system: many students share a small number \(\tau\) of complete course-bundle preference types, while course-seat supplies grow with enrollment. The mirror keeps each student’s bundle indivisible and applies EFX across assigned bundles; only the population and supplies are represented by masses. Its input size would depend on \(m,\tau\), and the valuation-table bit length.

For this proposed problem, fixing which type-bundle pairs have positive mass leaves linear feasibility constraints. Choosing that support is the remaining combinatorial step, so my tentative prediction is continuum-specific hardness from support selection. Theorem 2 does not establish that prediction or imply that its obstruction survives: splitting each valuation type across many bundles may make a continuous EFX assignment possible.

That is the weak point and the decisive one for the requested grading. The mirror is a concrete, plausible research question, but Theorem 2 is not the kind of named computational result the anchor rule requires. Strictly applying that rule, the paper offers no qualifying anchor for a positive continuous-complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against begins with the anchor. Theorems 1 and 2 concern EFX existence for fixed numbers of agents and goods; Theorem 3 guarantees one of two relaxations. The SAT and SMT experiments establish those mathematical claims, but the paper gives no complexity classification for an allocation problem across a family of inputs. In particular, Theorem 2 is a counterexample to a fairness guarantee, not a result about an algorithm’s complexity. Its use of SAT does not make it one.

Theorem 2 is the proponent’s only anchor. Their proposed high-multiplicity EFX problem is coherent, but it changes the object being studied: it adds a typed population, per-capita supplies of replicated good types, and a search over mass assignments to bundles. The theorem itself concerns a fixed finite set of goods and a particular valuation profile. It supplies no result about the proposed regime with small \(\tau\), nor a bridge from its counterexample to that regime. So the proposal is better understood as a new fair-division problem inspired by the paper than as a continuous mirror of a computational result in it.

The best version of their model survives the usual objections. It can keep each person’s bundle integral, define types by complete preferences, and scale course-seat supplies with enrollment. A large course-allocation system with repeated preference types is a plausible setting. I cannot honestly argue that this model is incoherent, already answered, or necessarily trivial.

That leaves a narrow but solid negative conclusion: the paper offers no qualifying computational anchor for the proposed mirror. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario is weak; the course-cohort model may be worthwhile on its own. Rejecting it would require an argument the paper does not provide.
