The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a population version of the paper’s PSLQ mechanism. There is an important limit to that case: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result—no theorem classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized tractable. Its named results concern mechanism properties. So I cannot supply a qualifying anchor or claim this is a continuous mirror of a complexity result.

The closest result is Theorem 5.12, proved in this paper: with integer quotas, PSLQ is weakly strategy-proof. That means a student cannot misreport and obtain an assignment strictly better in stochastic-dominance terms than under truthful reporting; if the misreport assignment stochastically dominates the truthful one, the two assignments must be equal. This makes PSLQ a natural mechanism to mirror, but the theorem does not establish a computational complexity bound.

A plausible regime is a large project-placement cycle with many students and a relatively small set of shared, complete preference rankings—for example, thousands of students and dozens of ranking types. A type is a strict ranking of the projects; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students with ranking \(t\). Normalize each project’s integer lower and upper quotas by the population size, giving per-capita quotas \(\ell_p\) and \(u_p\). Each student still receives one indivisible project in a realized assignment; type-level entries describe the assignment probability or fraction of that type assigned to each project.

One concrete prospective problem is Continuous-PSLQ Evaluation. Its input is a project set \(P\), a support \(T\) of ranking types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and rational per-capita quotas satisfying \(\ell_p\le u_p\) and \(\sum_p\ell_p\le1\le\sum_pu_p\). At time \(s\), let \(y_p(s)\) be the mass already assigned to project \(p\). Each type eats at unit speed from its highest-ranked active project. Following the paper’s rule, \(p\) is active if \(y_p(s)<\ell_p\), or if \(\ell_p\le y_p(s)<u_p\) and
\[ 1-s>\sum_{q\in P}(\ell_q-y_q(s))_+. \]
The requested solution is the exact type-by-project output matrix \(q_{tp}\) produced when eating ends. This is a mechanism-evaluation problem, with no separate welfare objective.

I would expect this evaluation problem to be tractable: the paper’s eating procedure has at most \(|P|\) phases in which the active set shrinks, and each phase advances to a quota or critical-time event. With rational inputs, those event times can be computed by rational arithmetic. That is an expected bound for the proposed mirror, not a result proved in the paper. The mirror is recognizable because, when a rational instance is replicated to a sufficiently large finite population, the type masses and per-capita quotas scale back to integer counts and quotas, and the normalized eating process reproduces the paper’s PSLQ process. This covers PSLQ and its output; it does not cover RPLQ or the paper’s full contribution.

The weakest point is the missing computational anchor. Theorem 5.12 establishes an incentive property, not an algorithmic classification, and evaluating this mechanism may be a modest aggregation result rather than a substantive complexity contribution. The paper also shows in Proposition 5.13, proved here, that for finite markets with at least two students and three projects, no mechanism can satisfy ordinal efficiency, envy-freeness, and weak strategy-proofness for all markets when quotas are allowed to be real. That result cautions against assuming the axioms transfer automatically under quota normalization; it is not itself a complexity result, and it does not settle the atomless population version. The continuous-mass evaluation problem is a credible population mirror, but it cannot serve as an anchored case for continuizing a named complexity result from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

On the programme’s stated gate, this paper has no qualifying anchor. Theorem 5.12 proves weak strategy-proofness; Propositions 5.10 and 4.4 prove other mechanism properties; Proposition 5.13 gives an impossibility result for combining axioms. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Calling PSLQ an “eating algorithm” does not supply a complexity result to continuize.

The proponent’s strongest version is a society \(\mu\) over a small support of ranking types, with quotas expressed per capita, and a task to compute the continuous eating process’s output. That is a coherent population model. Large student cohorts with recurring preference rankings are plausible, so I would not argue that multiplicity is meaningless here. But output evaluation is a newly posed computational question, not a continuous mirror of a named result in this paper. The paper proves properties of the mechanism; it does not establish a complexity claim about evaluating it.

Theorem 5.12 is also difficult to carry over as an individual-incentive result. In an atomless society, one student’s misreport does not change \(\mu\), so the aggregate process cannot by itself express the effect of that student’s deviation on the outcome they receive. A test would need a tagged individual with a separately defined allocation, or a positive-mass deviation. The former adds new semantics to the mechanism; the latter changes the question from unilateral strategy-proofness. Proposition 5.13 sharpens the sensitivity: the paper’s positive theorem relies on integer quotas, while normalizing quotas by population size generally gives real per-capita quotas. That sensitivity is a modeling challenge, though the impossibility result itself is not a computational objection.

So the case against is strong for the programme’s computational-complexity remit: there is no named computational result here to mirror, and the proposed evaluation problem is a new question. It is weaker as the universal claim that no continuous treatment could be worthwhile. A high-multiplicity assignment setting is plausible, and a continuous mechanism-design study could still interest its authors; the supplied material does not establish that such work would be worthless.
