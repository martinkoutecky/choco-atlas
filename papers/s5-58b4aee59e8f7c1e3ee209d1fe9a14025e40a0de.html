The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I can make is a narrow one about recovering what top-choice population shares are compatible with pairwise data. The relevant result is Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper: for a pairwise preference function \(P\), let \(W(P)\) be the set of top-choice distributions \(w\) that can arise from some ranking profile inducing \(P\), and define
\[ u_i=\min_{y\ne y_i}P(y_i\succ y). \]
The theorem says \(W(P)\subseteq\{w\in\Delta(Y):w_i\le u_i\ \forall i\}\). This gives the paper’s algorithm a tractable outer approximation of the feasible population shares. It is a useful computational anchor, though it is not a complexity classification.

A concrete follow-on problem is Exact Feasible Top-Choice Optimization. An instance consists of \(M\) alternatives, a rational pairwise preference matrix \(P\) known to be realizable by strict rankings, and rational weights \(a_1,\ldots,a_M\). Each type is a complete ranking \(r\); its mass \(\mu_r\) is the fraction of evaluators with that ranking. The question is to compute
\[ \max_{\mu\in\Delta(S)} \sum_{i=1}^M a_i\sum_{r:\,r(y_i)=1}\mu_r \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{r:\,r(y_i)<r(y_j)}\mu_r=P(y_i\succ y_j) \quad\forall i<j. \]
A solution gives the optimum and a ranking distribution \(\mu\) attaining it. Equivalently, it computes the support function of \(W(P)\) in direction \(a\). Theorem 4.4 bounds this feasible set but does not compute its exact support function; the paper explicitly motivates the outer approximation by the difficulty of characterizing \(W(P)\).

The regime is a large survey or evaluator panel repeatedly ranking a fixed slate of options. With millions of responses but a small, fixed \(M\), there can be many more people than distinct complete rankings, so ranking types and their fractional masses are a sensible high-multiplicity description. The authors would likely recognize this question: it uses their exact notion of feasible population distributions and asks what their outer approximation leaves unresolved. I would expect any hard part to come from the number of alternatives and the ranking space, rather than the number of evaluators. Whether that warrants a Class B verdict would require a discrete embedding; the paper establishes neither that reduction nor an exact algorithm. Natural follow-ups are exact membership in \(W(P)\), computing the whole feasible polytope, and comparing its answers with finite-electorate instances.

The qualification is decisive for calling this a *mirror*: the paper already takes the evaluator population to be a distribution over rankings and studies algorithms on that continuous object. Also, it contains no named result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, or similar. Theorem 4.4 is the closest computational anchor, but the exact optimization problem above is a follow-on to the paper’s model, not a continuization of one of its discrete computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed optimization is well-posed, but it is not a continuization of Theorem 4.4. The paper already takes the evaluator population to be a distribution \(\sigma\in\Delta(S)\) over rankings. Its pairwise data are \(P_\sigma\), and its feasible top-choice distributions \(W(P)\) are defined using those same population distributions. The proposed objective optimizes over \(\mu\in\Delta(S)\) with constraints \(P_\mu=P\). It asks for more information about the paper’s existing continuous model; it does not turn a finite-population problem from the paper into a continuous one. Theorem 4.4 is also an inclusion, not a complexity result: the paper contains no named result classifying a discrete computational problem that this optimization could mirror.

The strongest repair is to start with a finite panel of \(n\) evaluators, represent rankings by integer counts, and compare that problem with the fractional optimization over \(\mu\). One could study the gap between the integer and fractional optima or how the feasible sets behave as \(n\) grows. That could be worthwhile. But it adds a finite-panel problem and a discrete-to-continuous comparison absent from the paper. Every finite panel’s empirical ranking distribution is already a point of \(\Delta(S)\), so the paper’s theorem applies to it directly; an integrality or sampling analysis would be new work around the paper’s model, not a mirror of a discrete result in it.

I would not base the negative case on a lack of plausible multiplicity: a large survey panel ranking a fixed slate is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. The case is narrower: the anchor identifies an unresolved computation inside a paper whose population model is already continuous. That may justify a follow-on project, but it does not establish a worthwhile *continuous mirror* of this paper.
