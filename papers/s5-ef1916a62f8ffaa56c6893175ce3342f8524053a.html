The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) over the six strict rankings of \(\{a,b,c\}\) and a target candidate \(x\), does there exist a sequence of finite electorates with integer type counts \(N_t^{(r)}\), totals \(n_r\to\infty\), and \(N_t^{(r)}/n_r\to\mu_t\), such that \(x\in S_B(N^{(r)})\) for every \(r\), where \(S_B\) is the paper’s Borda solution after iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies?

The model it lives in

The six strict rankings are types with masses \(\mu_t\). Instances are rational distributions over those types; the decision variables in the limiting definition are finite integer count vectors \(N^{(r)}\) whose normalized shares converge to \(\mu\). The question is membership of \(x\) in the attainable limiting winner set; there is no optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

Covers the limiting winner correspondence based on Theorem 1. It leaves the paper’s rule characterizations, monotonicity results, and impossibility results without separate continuous mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give a necessary and sufficient condition for \(x\) to belong to the limiting winner set, including boundary cases where finite-count offsets and integer effects matter.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest population mirror is an asymptotic Borda outcome problem. A voter type is one of the six strict rankings of \(\{a,b,c\}\); \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the electorate. A plausible regime is a very large election in which many voters share each ranking, so the number of voters is large while the support has at most six types. This is a particularly clean high-multiplicity setting: type captures exactly the preferences relevant to the paper’s game.

The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It characterizes the Borda Rule’s solution after iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies: a candidate \(x\) is the unique solution exactly when, for some labelling \(x,y,z\), at least one of these holds, writing \(n_{uvw}=|I_{uvw}|\):

\[ n_{xyz}>n_{xzy}+n_{yxz}+2n_{yzx}+2n_{zxy}+2n_{zyx}; \]

\[ n_{xyz}>n_{xzy}+n_{yxz}+2n_{yzx}+2n_{zxy}+2n_{zyx}-1 \quad\text{and}\quad n_{zxy}>n_{yxz}; \]

or

\[ n_{xyz}>n_{xzy}+n_{yxz}+2n_{yzx}+2n_{zxy}+2n_{zyx}-2 \quad\text{and}\quad n_{xzy}>0. \]

Otherwise the solution is all of \(\{a,b,c\}\). This is a useful anchor for a population-limit question because its conditions are stated in the six type counts. But it is not an eligible computational-complexity anchor under the requested criterion: it asserts no result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. The paper’s other main results likewise characterize scoring rules by axioms; they do not give computational-complexity classifications.

Here is a precise follow-on question, rather than a claim that the paper already answers it. Given rational masses \(\mu\), consider sequences of finite electorates with integer type counts \(N_t^{(r)}\), totals \(n_r\to\infty\), and \(N_t^{(r)}/n_r\to\mu_t\). In each finite electorate, voters use the paper’s Borda ballots and its tie-breaking and iterated-undominance solution. Define

\[ L_B(\mu)=\left\{x:\text{some such sequence has }x\in S_B(N^{(r)})\text{ for every }r\right\}. \]

The decision question is: given \((\mu,x)\), is \(x\in L_B(\mu)\)? A witness can be given by the convergent sequence of type-by-ballot shares. There is no optimization objective here; the output is the set of possible limiting winners. Theorem 1 is the reason to ask this: after dividing its inequalities by electorate size, the constant offsets disappear away from the boundaries, while boundary cases retain the finite-count effects. For this fixed three-candidate, six-type problem, I would expect the limit question to be tractable by analyzing the resulting finite system of linear inequalities and its boundary cases. Further questions include whether uniqueness is stable under all approximations of \(\mu\), and what changes when the number of candidates grows.

The weakest point is also central: in a genuinely atomless voting game, one voter’s ballot has zero effect on aggregate scores, so the paper’s pivotality-based iterated-dominance analysis does not carry over directly. Defining \(L_B\) through limits of finite games preserves the paper’s solution concept, but makes this an asymptotic limit problem rather than a standalone atomless game. So there is a plausible continuous-population research question here, but no named computational result in this paper to anchor it as a ChoCo complexity mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchor. Theorem 1 is a named result, but it characterizes the Borda outcome correspondence; it makes no claim about an algorithm, complexity, or optimization problem. Its conditions are explicit inequalities in the six ranking counts. The paper’s other main results likewise characterize rules by axioms or establish impossibilities. There is no computational result here whose complexity over a continuous society needs charting.

The proposed \(L_B(\mu)\) is a coherent follow-on question, but it asks about limits of the paper’s already characterized finite outcomes. Writing \(p_t=N_t/n\), the inequalities in Theorem 1 become linear conditions on \(p\), with the constant offsets vanishing away from their boundaries. The sequence question amounts to asking which outcome regions approach \(\mu\), including their boundary behavior. That may be useful asymptotic voting theory; it does not supply the missing computational problem from the paper.

There is also a substantive obstacle to a direct continuous game. With an atomless population, an individual voter has zero mass, so changing their ballot cannot change the outcome. All ballots are equivalent against every opposing profile, weak dominance removes nothing, and any candidate can win when everyone casts a ballot favoring that candidate. The solution is therefore the full candidate set for every \(\mu\). Treating each ranking type as a player who controls its mass would restore influence by making each type a coordinated bloc, changing the paper’s individual strategic problem. The sequence construction retains individual pivotality through \(1/n\)-scale effects, so its meaningful strategic content comes from finite electorates approaching a limit.

This negative case has a clear limit: large electorates over six rankings are a natural high-multiplicity regime, and the asymptotic stability question could be worthwhile on its own. The case against is strongest for the narrower claim that this paper offers a worthwhile ChoCo computational-complexity mirror.
