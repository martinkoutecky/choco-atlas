The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of roommate-preference types \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a complete weak order over roommate types for each \(t\), compute a Pareto-optimal assignment of the population to rooms of size three. Represent each room by a multiset \(q\) of three types and let \(x_q\) be the fraction of rooms with composition \(q\). Require \(\sum_q x_q=1\) and \(\sum_q n_t(q)x_q=3\mu_t\) for each \(t\), where \(n_t(q)\) is the number of type-\(t\) occupants. Pareto dominance compares occupants' worst-roommate outcomes, allowing a type-preserving pairing between the original and reassigned occupants.

The model it lives in

Use one variable \(x_q\ge 0\) for each multiset \(q\) of three types, subject to \(\sum_q x_q=1\) and \(\sum_q n_t(q)x_q=3\mu_t\). Let \(\ell_t(q)\) be the least-preferred roommate type for a type-\(t\) occupant in composition \(q\), and let \(u_t\) strictly increase across that type's preference tiers. Maximize \(\sum_t\sum_q n_t(q)x_q u_t(\ell_t(q))\); any optimizer is Pareto optimal. There are \(\binom{\tau+2}{3}\) room compositions, so this is a polynomial-size LP in \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the complete weak-preference, three-person-room hardness results in Theorems 7 and 8. It leaves aside the paper's strict identity-specific rankings, incomplete-preference feasibility results, and other room capacities.

Open questions for a prover

  • For finite cohorts with \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), what guarantees allow an LP-optimal room distribution to be rounded to an exactly feasible discrete assignment while controlling changes in type-level outcomes?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s three-person room problem. Think of a large incoming dorm cohort: students fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of preference types, each type has many members, and members of a type share a complete weak ranking of the types they might room with. Ties within a category are natural here, and weakly ordered preferences are already part of the paper’s model. The regime is \(N\gg\tau\), where \(N\) is the number of students.

This keeps the paper’s central question intact: partition everyone into rooms of size three so that no feasible reassignment makes anyone better off and nobody worse off. The co-modification is that the assignment is described by masses of room compositions. A room still contains exactly three students; it is the mass of rooms of each composition that can be fractional.

My lead anchor is Theorem 8, proved in this paper. It says that, with complete weak preferences, rooms of capacity three, and comparisons based on the worst roommate, computing a Pareto optimal assignment is at least as hard as triangle cover. The reduction gives rank 1 to each graph neighbor and rank 2 to each non-neighbor; a triangle cover corresponds to a Pareto optimal assignment in which every player gets two first-ranked roommates. The paper’s preceding all-or-none argument makes that property useful for recognizing the hard instances. Triangle cover’s NP-completeness is cited to Garey and Johnson.

The continuous mirror is Continuous Worst-Roommate POA for Three-Person Rooms. An instance has a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to 1, and a complete weak order \(\succsim_t\) over roommate types for each \(t\). For each multiset \(q\in T^3\), let \(n_t(q)\) be the number of type-\(t\) occupants. The requested solution is a vector \(x_q\ge 0\), where \(x_q\) is the fraction of rooms with composition \(q\), satisfying
\[ \sum_{q\in T^3}x_q=1,\qquad \sum_{q\in T^3}n_t(q)x_q=3\mu_t\quad\text{for every }t. \]
In a room of composition \(q\), a type-\(t\) occupant’s outcome is their least-preferred roommate type in that room. Call \(x\) Pareto optimal if there is no other feasible \(y\) whose outcome distribution for every type can be paired with \(x\)’s so that nobody is worse off, while a positive mass of students is strictly better off. The pairing allows members of the same type to be reassigned among rooms, as their shared type description makes them interchangeable.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). There are \(\binom{\tau+2}{3}\) possible room compositions. Give each type’s preference tiers strictly increasing ordinal utility values and maximize total utility over the feasible \(x\). The result is Pareto optimal: any Pareto improvement would raise this objective. This is a linear program of size polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. It gives a concrete instance where the discrete triangle-cover hardness dissolves under a plausible high-multiplicity model.

A second, closely related anchor is Theorem 7, also proved here. It says that computing a Pareto optimal assignment is at least as hard as directed triangle cover when preferences are complete and weak, rooms have capacity three, and the best roommate determines comparisons. In its reduction, each player ranks outgoing neighbors first and everyone else second; the directed graph has a triangle cover exactly when there is an assignment in which every player has a first-ranked roommate. The paper proves directed triangle cover NP-complete in Section 2.2, by reduction from 3-dimensional hypergraph matching.

Its mirror is Continuous Best-Roommate POA for Three-Person Rooms, with the same type masses and room-composition variables. Here each type-\(t\) occupant’s outcome in \(q\) is their most-preferred roommate type. Ask for a feasible \(x\) with no Pareto improvement under those outcomes. The same linear-programming argument predicts a polynomial-time solution, again Class A. Theorem 8 is my lead because the triangle-cover reduction and its worst-roommate criterion give the more direct anchor; Theorem 7 independently tests the corresponding best-roommate variant.

These mirrors cover the paper’s two hardness results for finding a Pareto optimal assignment with complete weak lists and three-person rooms. They do not claim to mirror its strict, identity-specific rankings or its incomplete-list feasibility results. In the finite paper, a student’s ranking can distinguish named individuals; in this scenario, students of one type tie members of the same roommate category. That restriction is the substantive bet: it captures large cohorts whose preferences are category-based, but loses the identity-level distinctions used by the reductions.

The room-composition LP also suggests further questions: can continuous Pareto optimality be verified by an efficient dominance test; what changes with incomplete preferences or variable room capacities; and how closely can a fractional room distribution be rounded to a finite cohort assignment? For a finite instance with proportions \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), every discrete assignment maps to a continuous one, but the continuous solution need not lie on the finite cohort’s room-count grid. Exact recovery or rounding guarantees would make the discrete-to-continuous bridge sharper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror gets its tractability by changing what a preference describes. The paper gives each player a ranking over *individual people*. The proposed type model gives each player a ranking over categories: two roommates of the same type are interchangeable. That is a natural model for some settings, but it drops the identity-specific comparisons that let the paper encode arbitrary graphs. In a large dorm cohort where students rank named peers, the number of distinct preference types may grow with the cohort, so the proposed compression may not apply.

That objection does not defeat either anchor, though. The paper also studies weak preferences, and category rankings can be represented as ties among individuals in the same category. A large cohort whose roommate preferences are genuinely category-based is plausible, and its members are indistinguishable in the respects the model uses. That makes it a legitimate high-multiplicity regime, even if it is narrower than the paper’s general model.

Nor does the Pareto objective break the continuous formulation. For either Theorem 7’s best-roommate comparison or Theorem 8’s worst-roommate comparison, each room composition gives each type a rank outcome. With three-person rooms and \(\tau\) types, there are only \(\binom{\tau+2}{3}\) compositions. Assign each type’s rank outcomes strictly increasing utilities and maximize total utility over feasible room-composition masses. Any Pareto improvement would increase that objective, so an optimizer is Pareto optimal. This directly answers both mirrors as polynomial-size LPs.

So the negative case rests on the limited reach of the category-based regime: it may poorly describe settings where students care about particular people. But it cannot establish that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. Theorem 7 and Theorem 8 both survive for plausible category-based cohorts, and their continuous mirrors appear well-posed. The honest negative case is weak here.
