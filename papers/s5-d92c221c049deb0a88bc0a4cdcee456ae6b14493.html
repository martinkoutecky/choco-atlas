The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational-complexity result of the required kind: no theorem establishes an algorithmic upper bound, NP-hardness, parameterized complexity, or a similar classification. The strongest named result for a mirror is therefore Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper, but it is a structural incompatibility result rather than a complexity anchor.

Theorem 3.1 says that weighted PROPX and Pareto optimality can be incompatible with strictly positive additive costs even for two agents and four chores. The agents’ shares are \(3/10\) and \(7/10\), and their cost vectors over \(a,b,c,d\) are \((12,1,10,10)\) and \((11,6,1,6)\). Their total costs are \(33\) and \(24\), giving weighted proportionality thresholds \(99/10\) and \(84/5\). The unique weighted PROPX allocation is \((\{a\},\{b,c,d\})\), with costs \((12,13)\); \((\{b,d\},\{a,c\})\), with costs \((11,12)\), Pareto dominates it. So no allocation satisfies both requirements.

The mirror I would put to the authors is High-Multiplicity Weighted PROPX–PO Feasibility. Consider a large workforce with two types, each occupying mass \(\mu_t=1/2\). A type is a complete per-worker cost vector and entitlement weight. There are four recurring chore categories \(E=\{a,b,c,d\}\), with the cost vectors and relative weights from Theorem 3.1. Each category has supply \(q_e=1/2\) units per worker: in a finite population of \(2k\) workers, this means \(k\) indivisible occurrences of each category. A mass of workers may receive the same bundle, but each worker’s bundle remains integral.

Write \(x_{t,b}\) for the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{E}\). Require \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_e=q_e\) for each category \(e\). With per-worker relative weights \(w_1=3/10\), \(w_2=7/10\), the type-\(t\) weighted proportionality threshold is
\[ \theta_t=\frac{w_t}{\sum_u\mu_u w_u}\sum_e q_e c_t(e). \]
These choices give \(\theta_1=99/10\) and \(\theta_2=84/5\), matching the finite example’s thresholds. A bundle is PROPX for type \(t\) when, after deleting any one occurrence it contains, the worker’s remaining cost is at most \(\theta_t\).

The question is: given rational type masses, chore supplies, costs, and weights, does there exist a mass assignment \(x\) satisfying weighted PROPX that is Pareto optimal? Pareto optimality means there is no reassignment of the same workers to integral bundles, preserving every chore supply, that weakly lowers almost every worker’s cost and strictly lowers the cost for a positive mass of workers. A yes-solution is such an assignment \(x\); a no-instance has none.

This is a plausible mirror because the paper’s question—whether weighted fairness can coexist with efficiency—remains intact. The scenario is a large organization assigning recurring, standardized tasks to workers in a small number of job profiles, with profiles carrying different responsibility weights. Here \(2k\) workers and \(4k\) task occurrences grow together while the number of worker types stays at two. The repeated categories are a deliberate co-modification of the supply model to express a large population; they do not make any worker’s bundle divisible.

For this restricted two-type, four-category question, I would provisionally expect tractability: the PROPX condition leaves a finite set of admissible integral bundles, and the mass-assignment constraints are linear. The hard part is finding a fair assignment that is Pareto optimal among all assignments, including unfair ones; the paper gives no result that resolves that part. Further questions include whether Theorem 3.1’s incompatibility survives in this high-multiplicity regime, whether compatibility returns as type masses grow, and how complexity changes when the number of types or chore categories is part of the input.

This mirror covers only Theorem 3.1’s strict-positive-cost, unequal-share incompatibility phenomenon. It does not claim to mirror the item-minimality propositions or the near-equal-share family in Theorem 3.2 and Corollary 3.1.

The weakest point is substantial: Theorem 3.1 concerns four one-off chores assigned to two named agents. It does not show that incompatibility persists when each chore category has many occurrences and workers of the same type can split across different bundles. The continuous question may have a compatible allocation even though the finite example does not. That gap is the research question this mirror makes precise, but it is also why Theorem 3.1 cannot honestly be presented as a computational-complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to continue. Theorem 3.1 proves an incompatibility in one finite instance; Theorem 3.2 extends that structural result to a family; Propositions 3.1 and 3.2 give compatibility bounds. None classifies the complexity of finding a weighted PROPX and Pareto optimal allocation. That matters for this programme: its target is the computational landscape that appears when the population becomes continuous.

Theorem 3.1 is the proponent’s only anchor. Its proposed workforce model is coherent, and the obvious repair is to define Pareto improvements through a coupling between workers’ old and new bundles, so that no worker is made worse off. But this does not turn the theorem into a complexity result. It poses a new input problem over type masses, per-capita chore supplies, and distributions over integral bundles. The finite theorem’s unique PROPX allocation does not carry over automatically when workers of the same type can be split among different bundles. Establishing whether fairness and efficiency coexist in that new regime could be worthwhile, but it would be a new structural question rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

The item-minimality results and the near-equal-share construction do not supply a stronger anchor. Their content is also structural: they identify which finite numbers of agents and chores permit or prevent compatibility. Scaling the chores and population together is an admissible co-modification, but it creates a new family of allocation problems; it does not reveal a computational claim that the paper made and a continuous model can classify.

That is the best case against treating this paper as a ChoCo project: it lacks the programme’s required computational anchor. The case is weak against the broader claim that some continuous-population question would be worthwhile. Repeated workers with common cost profiles and recurring chores are plausible, and the resulting fairness-and-efficiency problem is well posed. I cannot honestly argue that no such scenario could merit study; I can argue that its motivation would be a new research direction, not the computational continuization of this paper’s results.
