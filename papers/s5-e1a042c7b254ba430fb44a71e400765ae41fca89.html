What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror targets Theorem 3’s two-agent SPNE construction; it leaves Theorem 1’s branch-count formula and Theorem 2’s backward-induction claim untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as NP-hard or in P. Its best algorithmic anchor is Theorem 3, proved in the paper: with two agents, complete information, and additive utilities, the strategy pair in which each agent reports items in the reverse of the other agent’s preference order is a subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium of parallel allocation. The theorem gives an equilibrium construction, though its statement does not establish uniqueness or a complexity class. The paper’s abstract and algorithm describe a linear-time method, but I would not treat that as a separate numbered complexity result.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large training platform assigning many copies of course seats. There may be \(N\) students but only \(\tau\) distinct preference-and-value types, with \(N\gg\tau\); the number of students and seats in each course both grow, while the set of course types stays comparatively small. A type \(t\) is a strict ranking and additive utility vector \(u_t\), and \(\mu_t\) is that type’s fraction of the student population. This is a reasonable regime for asking about the same strategic question the paper studies: how people choose among remaining items when claims are simultaneous and contested items are assigned by lottery.

I would ask the following continuous problem, which I’ll call Parallel Allocation Continuum-SPNE. An instance has a finite set \(J\) of item classes, a rational type distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) types, rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\) of each item class, additive utilities \(u_t(j)\), and a finite horizon \(K\). In each round, every agent reports one currently available item class. A type-\(t\) strategy may randomize, splitting its mass among available classes. If \(d_j\) is the mass claiming class \(j\), and \(s_j\) its remaining per-capita supply, then a claimant receives one indivisible copy with probability \(\min\{1,s_j/d_j\}\) when \(d_j>0\). Equivalently, if supply is short, a uniform lottery selects the winning mass of claimants; if supply is ample, every claimant gets a copy and some supply remains. Winners and losers both continue, as in the paper’s repeated-pick setting. After \(K\) rounds, the game ends.

The task is to compute a subgame-perfect equilibrium: a type-contingent strategy at every reachable public state of remaining supplies, together with the induced per-type allocation flows and expected utilities. Individual goods remain indivisible; fractional quantities describe expected allocations across a mass of agents. For exact output, a prover could be asked for a finite semialgebraic description of the strategy and allocation map. The computational parameters are \(m=|J|\), \(\tau\), \(K\), and the bit length \(L\) of the rational data.

This keeps the parallel reports, random resolution of contested items, additive utility, and equilibrium objective. It also makes the population—not the outcome space—the continuous object. The proportional lottery is the natural many-copy limit of random assignment among claimants, though it is a co-modification needed to express the mechanism at population scale. I would tentatively expect the general problem to be Class C, hard for continuum-specific reasons: type-level crowding creates endogenous rationing probabilities, and the paper’s reverse-order construction does not directly solve the resulting equilibrium conditions. That is a forecast, not a result. Useful follow-up questions include whether finite high-multiplicity instances converge to these equilibria, and whether special cases such as small \(\tau\) recover a tractable structure.

The weakest point is substantial: Theorem 3 concerns exactly two strategic agents, whereas this mirror has a large population of individually strategic agents. The theorem does not imply that its reversed-preference construction survives this change, and the paper does not analyze many-agent equilibria. The case for the mirror rests instead on the unchanged underlying allocation question and a credible regime—many people in relatively few complete preference-and-value types. I would not extend this mirror to the paper’s game-tree branch-count formula or claim it covers the paper’s other results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives a two-agent SPNE construction, and the paper claims a linear-time method for computing it. But the strategic unit in that result is exactly two individuals. The growing parameter is the number of items, \(m=|O|\), not the population. Taking more items or more item classes as the limit would continuize the outcome side, which is outside ChoCo’s scope.

That creates a hard choice for a mirror of Theorem 3. Keep two strategic agents, and there is no high-multiplicity population. Or make the agents a population of many people with a small number of complete preference-and-utility types. Then no individual controls a positive share of the mass: in the limit, a unilateral report does not change aggregate demand or the lottery odds. The resulting nonatomic game may be worth studying, but its deviation incentives are different from those in the two-player SPNE theorem. That is a change in the strategic object, not merely a continuous representation of the same instance.

The course-seat proposal handles the obvious objection that fixed item supplies would vanish per capita by scaling supply with population. That co-modification is reasonable. A careful version would also track the distribution of agents across types and past allocations, since agents who have received different items can face different continuation choices. Even with those repairs, the model asks for equilibria of a many-agent rationing game. It does not directly mirror the paper’s two-agent reversal result; it extends the paper’s mechanism and equilibrium question to a different population regime.

One could preserve the two-agent interaction by considering many independent two-agent allocation problems and taking a distribution over those pairs. But then the continuum is over instances or markets, not over the society sharing one allocation problem. The result would aggregate solutions to separate games rather than continuize the population studied by the paper.

This is the strongest case against the proposed anchor, but it does not sustain the universal claim. The paper explicitly identifies algorithms for three or more agents as future work, so a large-population equilibrium model is plausibly within the authors’ research agenda. A well-specified continuum game could therefore be a worthwhile extension, even though it is not a faithful high-multiplicity mirror of Theorem 3. The honest negative verdict is narrow: the paper offers little support for a continuous mirror of its named two-agent result. I cannot honestly conclude that no continuous-population scenario would be worth ChoCo’s time.
