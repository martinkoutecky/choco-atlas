What the mirror covers

The proportionality guarantees and per-batch bounds are mathematical results without a computational anchor; angular-mean computation is a follow-up on the paper’s existing weighted profiles.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible scenario is a large deployment that repeatedly ranks items using one shared linear scoring rule: for example, an AI service prioritizing requests for users whose preferences are captured by learned scoring vectors. Many users may share the same complete vector, so \(N\) can be very large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct vectors is much smaller. A type is a unit scoring vector \(\theta_j\); its mass \(\alpha_j\) is its fraction of the user population. The collective decision is a single scoring vector used across batches.

That suggests a concrete follow-up, Angular-Mean Computation. Given \(d\), a finite weighted profile \(\{(\theta_j,\alpha_j)\}_{j=1}^{\tau}\) with \(\sum_j\alpha_j=1\), and an accuracy parameter \(\varepsilon>0\), find a unit vector \(\hat\theta\) whose value is within \(\varepsilon\) of the global minimum of
\[ F(\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\alpha_j\,\angle(\theta,\theta_j)^2. \]
This computes the paper’s angular-mean aggregator itself, so the authors should recognize it as their problem. I would expect approximation to be manageable when \(d\) is fixed; for variable \(d\), the paper gives no basis to classify global optimization as tractable or hard. It notes that the objective need not be convex and that gradient methods do not guarantee convergence. Any claim of continuum-specific hardness would need a new reduction.

However, this is an unanchored candidate under your requirements. The paper’s named results establish proportionality guarantees, a per-batch impossibility, and convergence bounds; none classifies a computational problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. For instance, the theorem that the angular mean achieves long-run proportionality establishes a fairness guarantee, not an algorithmic complexity result. The per-batch impossibility is likewise a mathematical nonexistence result, not a complexity classification. So there is no eligible anchor to quote, and I cannot present this as an anchor-compliant case.

The deeper weakness is that the paper already represents its electorate by voter types and fractional shares \(\alpha^{(i)}\). In ChoCo terms, its profiles are already finite-support population distributions. A high-multiplicity deployment story is plausible and supports the model, but mirroring that model as a new continuous population problem would largely restate the paper. An atomless distribution over scoring vectors would be a genuine extension, but individual types would then have zero mass, making the paper’s individual proportionality entitlement vacuous without a new group-level fairness definition.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no numbered computational result here to anchor a ChoCo mirror. The named theorems establish proportionality guarantees, an impossibility, and convergence bounds; none classifies an algorithmic problem. The proponent’s Angular-Mean Computation is a reasonable algorithmic follow-up, but it is not a continuous-population mirror waiting to be formed.

In fact, its population input is already a finite-support distribution: \(\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha^{(i)}\delta_{\theta^{(i)}}\). Here \(n\) counts distinct preference types, and \(\alpha^{(i)}\) is each type’s population share. There is no separate headcount variable to take to a high-multiplicity limit. Computing the angular mean minimizes the paper’s own objective \(F(\theta)=\sum_i\alpha^{(i)}\angle(\theta,\theta^{(i)})^2\) on this already mass-weighted profile. Duplicating the electorate without changing its type shares leaves the instance unchanged. A complexity study of this optimization could be worthwhile on its own, but it would analyze the paper’s existing model, not create a continuous mirror. The paper itself also flags angular-mean computation and points to existing work on it.

The best genuinely different version would allow an atomless distribution over scoring vectors. But then every exact type has mass zero, so the paper’s individual entitlement vanishes. A coalition-level entitlement could avoid that, but the paper gives no coalition fairness criterion: choosing which groups count and how their agreement is averaged would add a new fairness question, rather than continuize the theorem as stated. The per-batch bound does not supply that missing bridge; its type-count-free dimension bound controls deviations uniformly over scoring directions, while its individual normalization still depends on positive type masses.

So the negative case is strong for the programme’s stated gap: this paper already uses population shares, and its results are not computational classifications. It is not an airtight claim that no related research could be valuable. A new coalition-fairness model over diffuse preference distributions might be. But that would be a new problem, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of these results.
