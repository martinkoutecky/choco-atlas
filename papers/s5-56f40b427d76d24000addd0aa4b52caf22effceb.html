The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for a prospective computational extension, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. It contains no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing polynomial-time solvability, hardness, or a parameterized complexity result. Theorem 4.3, proved here, says \(f^{np}\) is the unique power index satisfying efficiency, the null-player property, symmetry, and \(f^{np}\)-minimal monotonicity: when \(W_i^m(v)\subseteq W_i^m(w)\), it requires
\[ f_i(N,v)\lvert W^{np}(v)\rvert\le f_i(N,w)\lvert W^{np}(w)\rvert. \]
Theorem 4.4, also proved here, gives the corresponding uniqueness characterization for \(g^{np}\), replacing that last condition with \(g^{np}\)-minimal monotonicity, which multiplies each power by \(\sum_j\lvert W_j^{np}\rvert\). Both are axiomatic characterizations, not results about computing the indices. The paper itself presents computation as future work. So there is no qualifying anchor and, strictly, no per-anchor prover question to give.

There is nevertheless a plausible high-multiplicity setting for a follow-up. Imagine a large voting body with \(q\) members divided among a small number \(\tau\) of voting-rights or role types. Members of a type are interchangeable in the simple game; the input records each type’s population fraction \(\mu_t\), rather than listing all \(q\) members. The meaningful output is each type’s aggregate power, the sum of its members’ individual index values. That preserves total power as the population grows, even though an individual member’s share may vanish.

One precise, but unanchored, candidate is Entropy-Limit Type-Power Computation. An instance gives rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a rational monotone polytope \(W\subseteq[0,\mu]\) describing which coalition-mass vectors win, and a rational accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\). For each admissible scale \(q\), make \(q\mu_t\) interchangeable members of type \(t\), and let a coalition win exactly when its vector of type counts divided by \(q\) lies in \(W\). Set the coordinates for null types to zero when forming the null-player-free winning region \(W^{np}\). The task is to output, within \(\varepsilon\), the limiting aggregate \(f^{np}\) and \(g^{np}\) power of each type as \(q\) grows.

The natural candidate for that limit is derived from the finite indices’ coalition counts. A type-mass vector \(x\) corresponds to \(\prod_t {q\mu_t\choose qx_t}\) coalitions, so uniform counting of coalitions concentrates at the entropy-maximizing vector
\[ x^*=\arg\max_{x\in W^{np}}\ \sum_t\mu_t\,h(x_t/\mu_t), \qquad h(z)=-z\log z-(1-z)\log(1-z). \]
For a rational polytope, this objective is strictly concave and the maximizer is unique. The proposed limit for either index’s aggregate type power is \(x_t^*/\sum_s x_s^*\). This gives the mirror a concrete finite-to-continuous bridge: it is the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s own null-player-free indices, not merely a new interpretation of “power.” I would expect this restricted problem to be tractable by convex optimization, while leaving a bit-complexity analysis and finite-\(q\) convergence bounds to prove.

The weak point is substantial: this does not mirror Theorems 4.3 or 4.4 themselves. It narrows the games to role-symmetric rules with a concise mass-space representation, aggregates individual power by type, and proposes a new asymptotic computation because the paper does not study computational complexity. Whether the authors would accept that as a continuous form of their future computation agenda is plausible; whether it satisfies the requested anchor standard is not.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchors is that neither is a computational result. Theorems 4.3 and 4.4 characterize \(f^{np}\) and \(g^{np}\) by axioms; continuizing those characterizations would continuize an axiomatic question, which is outside ChoCo’s scope. The paper’s future interest in computing the indices does not turn those theorems into complexity results.

The entropy-limit proposal is a plausible follow-up, but it does not rescue either anchor: it changes the target from characterizing an index to computing an asymptotic, type-aggregated value. Its restriction to games represented by a monotone polytope matters. A general type-symmetric simple game is an upward-closed set of lattice points, and replacing that set with a polytope can change which coalitions win. Supplying the polytope makes the proposed optimization concrete, but leaves open whether it represents a broad enough part of the paper’s computation agenda to be a worthwhile mirror.

There is also a continuum-degeneration concern. Under the proposal’s concentration assumption, both indices converge to the same type-power vector, \(x_t^*/\sum_s x_s^*\). The paper’s two separate characterizations thus become indistinguishable at leading order. Studying finer asymptotic corrections might preserve their differences, but that would be a further modeling choice, not a mirror of the stated limit problem.

That is the strongest negative case I can make, but it does not support the universal conclusion that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. Repeated role types in a large voting body are a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper itself identifies computation as future work. The honest verdict is narrower: this paper supplies no in-scope computational anchor, while its proposed continuous computation problem is adjacent and not yet shown to be a sufficiently faithful or consequential extension.
