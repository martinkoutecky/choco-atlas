The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s closest candidate, Theorem 1, does not anchor a continuous *computational* mirror. It proves group strategy-proofness of the minimal RPE mechanism on \(\tilde{\mathcal R}\), and the paper shows that this domain contains almost all finite preference profiles. Example 1 supplies a manipulation on the full domain. These are incentive and domain results: the paper gives no complexity classification or algorithmic theorem for a computational problem.

The proposed large housing or student-placement market is a sensible high-multiplicity setting. But asking how to compute a minimal RPE there would introduce a new computational problem. The paper does not specify a finite encoding for its arbitrary continuous preferences, or analyze the running time of finding an RPE. One could make the new question precise by choosing rational, finitely represented preferences, scaling supplies, and defining the output. Those choices may lead to worthwhile work, but they do not turn Theorem 1 into a result about computational complexity. Likewise, asking whether a positive mass can manipulate would require choosing a new strategic unit and incentive notion. An infinitesimal agent has zero population mass; a positive-mass coalition changes the question.

So the strongest case against a mirror is that there is no computational result here to mirror, even under a better high-multiplicity scenario. The paper may be a useful starting point for a separate computational study of rationing-price equilibria or mass manipulation. I cannot honestly conclude that such a study would not be worthwhile; the negative case is strong about this paper’s fit with ChoCo’s continuization programme, and weaker as a universal claim about every research direction one could build from its model.
