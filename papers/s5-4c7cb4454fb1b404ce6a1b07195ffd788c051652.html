The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem establishing NP-hardness or membership in a complexity class. Its strongest computationally useful result is Theorem 2, which gives an exact existence condition and identifies the stable set; I’m using it as a broad algorithmic anchor, not calling it a complexity classification.

Theorem 2, proved in this paper, concerns the asymmetric three-agent majority pillage game with \(v_a\ge v_b\ge v_c\ge0\) and empty core, meaning \(v_a<v_b+v_c-1\). Let \(s^{ij}\) give half the resource to agents \(i,j\) and none to the third. The theorem says that \(S=\{s^{ab},s^{ac},s^{bc}\}\) is internally stable exactly when \(v_a\le \tfrac12+v_c\). Under the empty-core condition it is externally stable too, and it is the unique stable set; if the inequality fails, no stable set exists. The paper proves this as a generalization of the symmetric result of Jordan and Obadia (2015).

My lead mirror is Atomless Three-Type Majority Pillage: Stable-Set Existence. Its scenario is a very large association or federation of local units, with three types of units that differ in their shared baseline leverage. The number of units \(N\) is large while the number of types is \(\tau=3\); the mass \(\mu_t\) of type \(t\) is its population fraction. Members of a type share the same intrinsic power \(v_t\), and coalitions can form from any measurable subset of the population. A common resource is allocated among them, with total supply scaling with \(N\), so normalize the mean per-capita allocation to one.

Precisely, the input is rational masses \(\mu_a,\mu_b,\mu_c>0\) summing to one and rational intrinsic powers \(v_a\ge v_b\ge v_c\ge0\), encoded in binary. Let \(\Omega=[0,1]\) carry Lebesgue measure, partitioned into type sets of measures \(\mu_t\). A feasible allocation is a measurable \(x:\Omega\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\) with \(\int_\Omega x\,d\lambda=1\). For a measurable coalition \(A\), define its power at \(x\) by
\[ \Pi(A,x)=\int_A\bigl(x(\omega)+v_{\tau(\omega)}\bigr)\,d\lambda. \]
For feasible \(x,y\), let \(W=\{\omega:y(\omega)>x(\omega)\}\) and \(L=\{\omega:x(\omega)>y(\omega)\}\). As in the paper, \(y\) dominates \(x\) when \(\Pi(W,x)>\Pi(L,x)\). The decision problem is: on inputs whose atomless game has empty core, does a stable set exist? A solution to this decision version is yes or no; a constructive follow-up would give an exact description of the stable set when one exists.

I think the authors would recognize this as a continuum-agent extension of their problem: it keeps the same resource-based additive power, gainers-versus-losers dominance, and stable-set concept. It continuizes the population itself, rather than relying on the paper’s already-divisible allocation space. The type masses and per-capita resource supply are natural co-modifications in the high-multiplicity regime.

For this mirror I would investigate Class C, meaning hardness specific to the continuum, while treating that as a research prior rather than a conclusion. The theorem’s three-point stable set does not tell us what happens when coalitions can split a type and allocations can vary within it. The main follow-up is whether those functional allocations can be reduced to finitely many type-level statistics; if so, the problem may instead admit a tractable structural characterization. Other useful questions are whether the atomless core-empty regime is itself characterizable from \((\mu,v)\), and whether finite replications of the type masses converge to the atomless stable-set answer.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s theorem is about three strategic agents, whereas this mirror has atomless coalitions and allocation functions. That changes the coalition structure, so Theorem 2 is motivation for the question, not evidence that its threshold or its three-element answer survives. And under a strict requirement that an anchor explicitly state a complexity-class result, this paper supplies no qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, and it misses ChoCo’s computational gate. It gives an exact structural characterization of stable-set existence for a game with three strategic agents and a continuous resource simplex. It states no complexity bound, algorithm, or computational problem. The paper’s continuous object is the allocation; the proposed mirror adds a continuous population. That could motivate a new question, but it does not continuize a computational result from this paper. The proponent acknowledges this gap.

The proposed atomless model also changes the coalition structure that Theorem 2 analyzes. In the paper, a coalition is a subset of three agents. In the mirror, a coalition can be any measurable subset of a type, and allocations can vary across individuals. The paper’s three-point stable-set result depends on its three-agent structure; it supplies no algorithmic or complexity result for this much larger space of coalitions and allocations.

There is a concrete sign of the change. Write \(q(\omega)=x(\omega)+v_{\tau(\omega)}\) and \(E=\{\omega:x(\omega)>0\}\). Since \(\int_E q\ge1\) and the population is atomless, one can choose a positive-measure \(L\subset E\) with \(\int_L q<\frac12\int_E q\). Then \(W=E\setminus L\) has greater power than \(L\) at \(x\). Transfer a small positive amount of resource from \(L\) to \(W\); the resulting allocation dominates \(x\). Thus every feasible allocation is dominated, for every parameter choice. The paper’s distinctions between empty and non-empty core regimes disappear in this model. This does not settle stable-set existence, but it shows why Theorem 2’s regime and proof cannot simply carry over.

A better mirror could ask for the complexity of deciding stable-set existence in high-multiplicity pillage games. But that would need to specify how populations, resources, and intrinsic power scale, and whether members of a type can join coalitions separately. If types must act as indivisible blocs, population mass does not create the atomless coalition problem; if members can split, the model moves into the functional setting above. Either choice may yield worthwhile research, but the paper gives no computational anchor for it.

So the negative case is strong against this paper as a direct ChoCo anchor: its results are structural game-theoretic characterizations, not complexity results. It is weaker against the universal claim. A plausible high-multiplicity power-contest model could still be worth studying; I cannot honestly conclude that no scenario produces a worthwhile continuous mirror.
