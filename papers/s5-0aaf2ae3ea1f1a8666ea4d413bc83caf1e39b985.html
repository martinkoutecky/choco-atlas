The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric nonnegative rational contact kernel \(W\), and an integer \(k\), let \(q_i^r\in[0,1]\) be the share of type \(i\) holding opinion \(1\) at round \(r\). Define \(D_i=\sum_j W_{ij}\mu_j\), and update \(q_i^{r+1}\) to \(1\) when \(\sum_j W_{ij}\mu_jq_j^r>D_i/2\), to \(0\) when it is less, and to \(q_i^r\) on a tie. Decide whether there exists an initial state \(q^0\in[0,1]^{|T|}\) whose first two-periodic time, \(\min\{r:q^{r+2}=q^r\}\), is at least \(k\).

The model it lives in

An atomless population partitioned into \(|T|\) contact types with rational masses \(\mu_i\) and symmetric kernel \(W\); the state \(q_i\) is each type's opinion-\(1\) share, and the computational objective is to maximize the first two-periodic time over initial states \(q^0\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.1's threshold question about worst-case voting time. It leaves aside Theorem 1.2's family-contraction bound and the paper's other graph-specific bounds and properties.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can long trajectories from fractional initial shares be ruled out or characterized in terms of binary initial states?
  • Can the paper's reduction gadgets control mixed shares well enough to establish hardness for this model?
  • What is the decision problem's complexity in the number of types, rational bit length, and threshold \(k\)?
  • Does the symmetric-kernel update always reach a two-periodic state under the stated tie rule?

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible mirror keeps the paper’s object intact: binary opinions, synchronous local majority updates, and the worst-case time to reach a two-periodic state. The population becomes continuous. I would use a large community with many members but relatively few interaction roles—for example, a platform whose members fall into recurring groups with the same contact rates toward each group. A type is one such role, including its contact profile and update rule; \(\mu_i\) is its share of the population. The number of people can be enormous while the number \(\tau\) of types stays small.

Here is a precise candidate, Block-Mean-Field Voting-Time. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to one, a symmetric nonnegative rational contact kernel \(W_{ij}\), and an integer \(k\). Each person still has opinion \(0\) or \(1\). Let \(q_i\in[0,1]\) be the share of type \(i\) holding opinion \(1\). A person in type \(i\) receives contact exposure \(W_{ij}\mu_j\) from type \(j\). Write \(D_i=\sum_j W_{ij}\mu_j\). In each round, all members of type \(i\) adopt \(1\) if \(\sum_j W_{ij}\mu_jq_j>D_i/2\), adopt \(0\) if it is less than \(D_i/2\), and retain their individual opinions in a tie. Thus a type’s next share is \(1\), \(0\), or its current \(q_i\), respectively. The decision question is whether some initial opinion profile—equivalently, some \(q^0\in[0,1]^\tau\)—has voting time at least \(k\), where voting time is the least \(r\) for which the opinion profile at \(r+2\) equals the profile at \(r\), up to sets of measure zero. A witness is such an initial profile. Opinions and time remain discrete; it is the population mass within each type that is continuous.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1.1, proved in this paper: for a general simple graph \(G\) and integer \(k\), deciding whether some initial binary opinion assignment makes the deterministic majority process take at least \(k\) rounds to reach a two-periodic state is NP-complete. The authors prove this by reducing from 3SAT. In the proposed mirror, a rational block kernel specifies the interaction architecture, and \(q_i\) lets a type contain a fractional share of each opinion. A graph \(G\) embeds as a special case by taking one type per vertex, equal masses, and \(W_{ij}=1\) exactly on edges; binary \(q_i\)’s then reproduce its trajectories exactly.

I would provisionally expect hardness to transfer (Class B): the paper’s reduction puts its combinatorics in the interaction architecture, which the kernel can still encode, while the population in each role can be large. That expectation needs a real reduction. The graph embedding proves only that every discrete yes-instance remains a continuous yes-instance; it does not rule out extra long trajectories arising from fractional initial shares. Useful next questions are whether a worst-case trajectory can always be represented with \(q_i\in\{0,1\}\), whether the paper’s gadgets can control mixed shares, and what the exact complexity is in \(\tau\), the rational bit length, and \(k\). The symmetric-kernel restriction also gives the natural setting to verify that the two-periodic convergence argument carries over.

This case covers Theorem 1.1, not the paper’s family-contraction upper bound in Theorem 1.2. Its weakest point is that the paper studies exact neighborhoods in a finite graph, while the mirror replaces them with role-based contact exposure. That co-modification seems natural for a replicated population, and preserves the underlying question about computational worst-case voting time, but the continuous hardness claim remains to be established.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1.1 is a genuine computational anchor: it proves NP-completeness for deciding whether some initial opinion assignment makes the process run for at least \(k\) rounds. So “there is no computational result to mirror” cannot carry the negative case.

The strongest objection is that the paper’s hard instances put their information in the graph’s exact adjacency pattern. The reduction uses specific literal cliques, representative vertices, gates, and a path whose activation order carries the formula’s evaluation. If a type must include an agent’s full interaction pattern, those roles are mostly distinct. A continuous population with one type per vertex preserves the graph’s computation, but its type count grows with the graph; the extra mass is then largely redundant.

The stronger proposed mirror replaces exact neighborhoods with a block contact kernel. That is a plausible model for a large population organized into recurring interaction roles, and binary type shares reproduce a finite graph’s trajectory under the proposed embedding. But the embedding establishes only that graph yes-instances remain yes-instances. Fractional initial shares could create long trajectories even when the original graph is a no-instance, so the paper’s reduction does not establish hardness for the continuous decision problem.

That gap does not defeat the mirror. It identifies a proof obligation. The deeper concern is that the two ways to retain the paper’s structure pull apart: exact local neighborhoods preserve the original dynamics but make the type space grow with the graph; aggregate contact rates give a natural high-multiplicity population but replace the graph’s local process with a block mean-field one. A richer continuum of local environments might bridge that gap, but it would need a finite input representation that retains the relevant adjacency information. The paper supplies no such representation.

This is a reason to question whether continuization adds much here: the population size itself is not where the paper’s computational structure sits. But it is not a decisive objection. A platform or organization with repeated interaction roles could make the block model a sensible object, and the worst-case convergence-time question would still be computational. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario; the case against is weak precisely at that point.
