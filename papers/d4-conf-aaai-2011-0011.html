The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive observation is that the paper studies groups of users whose preferences are drawn from a shared distribution. A plausible population type would be a complete utility function \(u\), with mass \(\mu_u\) representing the fraction of households with that utility. This gives a natural cohort setting, but the paper processes users individually; it does not take a mass distribution as a computational input.

There is no eligible named complexity anchor. The paper’s only numbered proposition is Proposition 1: if the hypothesis set \(H\) contains the true distribution \(H^\ast\), then, for any decision \(d\), \(r(d)\leq \mathrm{PrMR}(d,H\mid C,p)\) with probability at least \(p\). The paper states this guarantee but omits its proof. It is a statistical regret guarantee, not a result that classifies an underlying problem as tractable or hard. The query-count tables are experimental results, not named complexity results.

So I can’t honestly state a continuous problem *per qualifying anchor* or assign one of the programme’s three complexity outcomes. The weak point in the positive case is that a cohort-level mirror would turn the paper’s statistical guarantee into a new optimization problem about allocating queries across masses of users. The group setting supports that as a research direction, but the paper’s named result does not establish it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no eligible complexity anchor. Proposition 1 is the closest candidate: if \(H\) contains the true distribution \(H^\ast\), then \(r(d)\leq \mathrm{PrMR}(d,H\mid C,p)\) with probability at least \(p\). That is a statistical guarantee, not a result about the complexity of a computational problem. The query-count tables are experiments; the PMR linear program and the KS test are tools used by the method, not complexity classifications.

A stronger mirror would model a large household cohort by a measure over complete utility types, then choose elicitation policies across that mass. But the paper’s key group assumption already is that users are drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution over utilities. For a known distribution, each user’s elicitation problem can be handled separately; the cohort adds no computational coupling. If the distribution remains unknown, a continuum with exact masses removes the finite-sample uncertainty that drives hypothesis rejection. Keeping that uncertainty means keeping the sequential sample and its individual query histories. Adding shared query capacity or an aggregate objective could create a coupled optimization problem, but those additions would define a new problem rather than continue one of the paper’s named results.

The cohort setting is therefore plausible, as the paper’s household example suggests, but it does not rescue the proposed anchor: Proposition 1 already reasons over a population distribution, and it supplies no complexity question to mirror. My negative case is strongest on that point. I cannot rule out a useful new project on batch preference elicitation; the paper alone does not make it a worthwhile continuous mirror for ChoCo.
