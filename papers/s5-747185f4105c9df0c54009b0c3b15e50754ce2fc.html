What the mirror covers

No result yields a discrete-to-continuous mirror; Theorem 3 is the strongest constructive candidate, while Theorems 1–6 analyze equilibria and efficiency within the paper’s distributional population model.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible computational mirror would take a finite set of issues and a large electorate represented by a rational mass distribution over \(\tau\) complete voter types. A type records everything relevant to its utility, such as its ideal position on each issue and its issue weights. The candidates’ actions are their binary platforms on pledgeable issues, with their fixed positions held fixed. Given the distribution of voter types—and, if included, a distribution over states—the question could be to find a pure-strategy equilibrium of the platform game, or report that none exists. Each candidate’s objective is the probability of winning.

A plausible regime is a large electorate with relatively few recurring preference types: many voters share the same policy preferences and issue weights, while their type masses differ. That is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario, and the authors would likely recognize equilibrium computation as a question about their platform game. But there is a substantial weakness: the paper already represents voters through a measure \(\mu\) and state-dependent distributions \(\mu_\tau\). The population is already continuous in the paper’s model. A computational study would add a new question to that model; it would not continuize a discrete population problem established by the paper.

More decisively, the paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Theorems 1–6, proved in the paper, characterize equilibria, majority-efficiency, and competition-efficiency under various assumptions. For example, Theorem 1 characterizes equilibria when the candidates have identical feasible policy sets; Theorem 3 establishes existence of a majority-efficient policy under independent preference coordinates. Neither states that a computational problem is in P, hard, or has a parameterized complexity classification. The paper’s informal claims that some equilibria are straightforward to compute or that the framework is tractable do not supply a numbered computational result.

So there is a credible computational follow-up—finding equilibria of a platform game with a finite-support electorate—but no eligible anchor, and thus no anchored continuous problem or expected complexity class to give under your criteria. The honest positive case is that the paper’s model is a promising starting point for computational work. It does not provide the named computational result needed to make that work a continuous mirror of one of its results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed equilibrium computation is the strongest possible follow-up here, but it is not an anchor for continuization. Theorem 1 characterizes equilibria when the candidates have identical feasible sets, and Theorem 3 gives a condition for a majority-efficient policy to exist. Neither establishes tractability, hardness, or a parameterized complexity result. The paper’s remarks that some cases are “straightforward to compute” do not specify an algorithmic problem and complexity classification to mirror.

More fundamentally, the paper already models the population by a measure: voter preferences have distribution \(\mu\), and election outcomes compare the masses supporting each candidate. Restricting that model to a finite support of \(\tau\) complete types with rational masses is an input representation of a population the paper already permits. The binary platforms and potentially infinite issue set do not make the population discrete. Thus the proponent’s better version—fix finitely many pledgeable issues and ask whether a pure equilibrium exists or compute one from those rational masses—could be worthwhile computational work, but it studies an already continuous-population game. It extends the paper’s analysis; it does not continuize a discrete computational result.

The negative case is strongest on that boundary. It cannot show that equilibrium computation in this model would be uninteresting; it can show that the paper supplies neither a qualifying computational result nor a discrete population problem for a continuous mirror to transform.
