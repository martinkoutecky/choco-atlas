The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of worker types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), employer types \(J\) with rational per-capita capacities, and a rational initial assignment \(x'\) that is individually rational and worker quasi-stable, compute a stable mass assignment \(x^*\) that firms weakly prefer to \(x'\) and that is firm-worst among all such stable assignments. Each worker type ranks employer types, employer choices rank worker types and accept preferred mass up to capacity, and \(x_{tj}\) records assigned mass; each individual worker still receives at most one employer.

The model it lives in

A large placement market with finite support \(T\) of applicant types, where each type has a complete ranking of employer or position types and mass \(\mu_t\). Employer types have per-capita capacities and responsive choices over worker-type mass. The initial assignment \(x'\) records each type’s mass at each employer type or unemployment; unemployed mass makes offers, and employers retain their preferred feasible mass. Capacities scale with the represented population, while mass may be split across workers of the same type.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is mass restabilization after worker entry or position closures. One qualification up front: the paper contains no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or similar complexity classification. Its closest computational anchors are correctness and outcome theorems for the Acceptance Firing algorithm (AF). So the mirror below proposes a computational question prompted by those results; it does not extend a complexity result the paper already proves.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 21, proved in this paper. For a worker quasi-stable input matching \(\mu'\), AF returns the meet, in Blair’s firm order, of the stable matchings that firms weakly prefer to \(\mu'\). Equivalently, the returned matching is stable, firms weakly prefer it to the input, and it is the firms-worst matching among all stable matchings with that property. The supporting Theorem 13 is attributed to Cantala [4], rather than proved here: if AF starts from a worker quasi-stable matching, all intermediate matchings are worker quasi-stable and its output is stable. Theorem 21 is the stronger anchor because it identifies which stable outcome the algorithm selects.

Call the proposed mirror Continuous AF Restabilization. A type \(t\) is a complete worker description: a strict ranking of a finite set \(F\) of employer or position types, plus any characteristics relevant to firms’ preferences. The input gives a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and an initial assignment \(x'_{tf}\) of each type’s mass among positions and unemployment. Employer types have rational per-capita capacities and substitutable, separable choices over worker types—for a concrete version, each employer type ranks worker types and hires its most preferred acceptable mass up to capacity. The initial assignment is individually rational and worker quasi-stable. The action variable is a mass assignment \(x_{tf}\), with each type’s mass assigned across positions or unemployment. There is no cost objective in the paper’s problem; the canonical target is the firm-worst stable assignment among stable assignments firms weakly prefer to \(x'\).

The algorithmic question is: given that instance, compute that target assignment. The mass version of AF has unemployed mass offer to its most-preferred acceptable position not yet tried; each position retains its preferred feasible mass from current workers and new offers, and rejected or displaced mass continues. Because identical workers can be split fractionally, the implementation would track portions of each type by offer history. The requested solution is a mass assignment \(x^*\) that is stable, firms weakly prefer it to \(x'\), and every other such stable assignment is weakly preferred by firms to \(x^*\). This is exactly the outcome characterization in Theorem 21, stated for mass allocations.

A plausible regime is a large placement market for entry-level jobs: many applicants, but relatively few distinct preference and qualification types, and positions pooled into employer categories with the same capacity and hiring preferences. Applicants of the same type share their full ranking and relevant characteristics. Worker mass greatly exceeds the number \(\tau=|T|\) of types; capacities scale with the population, as they would when representing pooled positions. Entry cohorts and closures give the same kind of disruption the paper studies. This is a narrower model than the paper’s full preference domain, but the population regime is credible, and the mirror keeps its central question: how AF selects a stable outcome after disruption.

I expect this restricted version to be tractable, since it has finite type support, rational mass variables, and explicit substitutable employer choices. That is a conjecture for the mirror, not a complexity result of this paper. A prover could be asked whether continuous AF terminates and equals the firm-worst stable mass assignment for this model, with running time in \(|F|\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. Further questions are whether the theorem survives for general substitutable, \(q\)-separable type-level choices; whether the mass outcome is the limit of discrete AF as each type is replicated; and whether arbitrary choice representations introduce continuum-specific hardness. The discrete-to-continuous correspondence matters: continuous assignments permit fractional type mass, so equality with discrete AF should be proved, not assumed.

This mirror covers Theorems 13 and 21’s AF restabilization result, not the paper’s entire lattice theory. Its weak point is the move from named workers and positions to pooled types and scalable capacities, together with fractional splitting during offers. The authors would likely recognize the question as a continuous version of their AF outcome problem, but accepting that specific continuum model—especially for employer choice behavior—would need justification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper contributes no numbered complexity result. Theorem 13 is Cantala’s earlier correctness result, cited here; Theorem 21 characterizes AF’s output by its position in a lattice. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. So Continuous AF would introduce a new computational question—how to compute a mass version of the selected matching—rather than continuize a complexity result of this paper.

There is a deeper mismatch in the proposed type model. The paper assumes each firm has a strict, complete preference over subsets of named workers. If two workers are genuinely the same type, every firm must treat them alike; but strict preferences over individual subsets distinguish subsets that swap one for the other. Keeping the paper’s strict preferences can therefore make workers’ firm-side priority information part of their types, potentially leaving little repeated mass. Pooling workers instead requires anonymous, weak preferences within types, plus a mass-based stability and choice model. That may be a sensible matching problem, but Theorem 21’s lattice characterization does not automatically carry over to it.

A better mirror than a literal mass copy would use many applicants and positions across repeated preference and priority classes, with capacities scaling by class. It would ask whether an anonymous mass version of AF selects the firm-worst stable assignment. That is coherent and the proponent’s placement-market scenario is plausible. But it is a new theorem about a modified preference domain, not a straightforward continuous counterpart of the paper’s result. Under responsive type priorities, the computation also looks like a compressed deferred-acceptance process; under richer choices, the cost of representing and optimizing firms’ choices becomes the central issue, not a complexity question exposed by this paper.

That is the best negative case, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proposed mass matching remains plausible, and a tractable compressed algorithm or a proof that the lattice outcome survives anonymous ties could be valuable. I would argue that this paper is a weak ChoCo anchor, not that every continuous matching mirror should be rejected.
