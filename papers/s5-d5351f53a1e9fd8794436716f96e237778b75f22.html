The continuous mirror question

Given finite sets of \(A\)-types and \(B\)-types with rational masses \(\alpha_i,\beta_j\), rankings with ties, acceptable pairs \(E\), and nonnegative rational weights \(w_{ij}\), choose matched masses \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) subject to \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\beta_j\), maximizing \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}x_{ij}\). For each acceptable pair, define \(S_i(j)=\alpha_i-\sum_k x_{ik}+\sum_{k:j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\) and \(W_i(j)=\alpha_i-\sum_k x_{ik}+\sum_{k:j\succeq_i k}x_{ik}\), with \(S_j(i),W_j(i)\) defined symmetrically; require that it is not the case that \(S_i(j)>0\) and \(W_j(i)>0\), or that \(W_i(j)>0\) and \(S_j(i)>0\). Find an exact maximum-weight feasible assignment or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

An atomless bipartite society in which agents with identical rankings, acceptable partners, and edge weights form types. The variables \(x_{ij}\) are masses paired across acceptable type pairs, bounded by type supplies; they describe actual pairings of individuals, and strong stability forbids any acceptable pair of types from having positive masses on both sides that could block.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers maximum-weight strongly stable matching over type masses; it leaves the paper's individual-vertex polytope identity and rotation-based runtime bounds untransferred.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can exact maximum-weight mass-stable matching be solved in time polynomial in the number of supported types and the binary input length, or is the blocking disjunction a source of continuum-specific hardness?
  • How does the optimum relate to finite clone markets with rational type multiplicities?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is Maximum Weight Strongly Stable Mass Matching. It keeps the paper’s matching problem intact: agents have incomplete lists with ties, a matching is rejected if a pair can jointly block it with at least one strict improvement, and the objective is maximum total edge weight. The change is that agents with identical problem-relevant data form a type, and the society is described by masses of those types.

A plausible regime is a large, centralized placement market with standardized cohorts: for example, applicants grouped by credential and region, and positions grouped by employer category. Within a type, every agent has the same acceptable counterpart types, the same ranking and ties over them, and the same edge-weight schedule. Those details define the type; grouping agents who differ on them would change the problem. A market with hundreds of thousands of agents but tens or hundreds of such templates has \(N\) large and support size \(\tau\) much smaller. The paper itself motivates matching through labor markets, so this is a recognizable setting for its authors.

The anchor is Theorem 13, proved in this paper. For an instance graph, \(P_{\mathrm{SSM}}\) has a nonnegative variable \(x_{ij}\) for each possible match, capacity constraints \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le 1\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le 1\), and, for each acceptable edge \((i,j)\), two inequalities ruling out a blocking pair. Each inequality requires that the mass of partners one endpoint strictly prefers, plus the mass of partners the other endpoint strictly prefers, plus one endpoint’s mass tied with the proposed partner, is at least \(1\); the second inequality uses the other endpoint’s tie mass. The theorem says that \(P_{\mathrm{SSM}}\) is exactly the convex hull of incidence vectors of strongly stable matchings. Thus maximizing the paper’s linear edge-weight objective over this polytope returns a maximum-weight strongly stable matching. This is the paper’s named polyhedral result, and the paper uses it to compute the weighted problem; I do not rely on an unnumbered algorithm statement as a separate anchor.

Here is the continuous question. The input is finite sets of \(A\)-types and \(B\)-types, with positive rational masses \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_j\) summing to \(1\) across both sides; acceptable type pairs \(E\); each type’s ranking with ties over acceptable types on the other side; and a nonnegative rational weight \(w_{ij}\) for each acceptable pair. Masses and weights are given in binary. The type pair’s weight is the same for every pair of individuals drawn from those two types.

A solution is a matrix \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) of matched mass, with \(\sum_jx_{ij}\le\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_ix_{ij}\le\beta_j\). It is an actual mass pairing: in an atomless type group, agents can be partitioned and paired so that exactly \(x_{ij}\) mass of type \(i\) is matched to type \(j\). It does not give any individual a fractional partner. Its weight is \(\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}x_{ij}\).

Require strong stability at the mass level. Let \(u_i=\alpha_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\) and \(v_j=\beta_j-\sum_ix_{ij}\) be unmatched masses. For an acceptable pair \((i,j)\), define
\[ S_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:j\succ_i k}x_{ik}, \qquad W_i(j)=u_i+\sum_{k:j\succeq_i k}x_{ik}, \]
and define \(S_j(i)\) and \(W_j(i)\) symmetrically on side \(B\). These are the masses of agents who strictly or weakly prefer the proposed type to their current assignment; an unmatched agent prefers any acceptable neighbor to being unmatched. The pair is forbidden if either \(S_i(j)>0\) and \(W_j(i)>0\), or \(W_i(j)>0\) and \(S_j(i)>0\). In other words, no positive masses from the two types can be paired to form a blocking pair. The problem asks for an exact maximum-weight stable \(x\), or a report that none exists.

I expect this question to be Class A, tentatively: an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\), the binary input length \(L\), and the weight bit length, perhaps using a type-compressed polytope or separation procedure. Theorem 13 makes the polyhedral route credible, but does not prove that the individual-level polytope compresses this way. Further questions include whether the mass-feasible region has a polynomial-size exact description, and whether its optimum approximates the normalized optimum of large finite markets with the same types.

The mirror covers the weighted strongly stable matching problem and its stability condition, not the paper’s entire rotation structure or its results about other notions of stability. Fractional \(x_{ij}\) is a description of how much of each population type receives each type of partner; the outcome for each individual remains an ordinary match or no match. That makes population mass the continuous object.

The weakest point is also the central technical question. Theorem 13 applies to named individual vertices. After aggregating clones into types, “no blocking pair” means certain strict and weak status masses cannot both be positive; that is a disjunctive condition. Simply replacing individual capacities with type masses in the paper’s inequalities need not preserve it. So the theorem is evidence for a tractable mirror, not a proof of one. Even if type compression turns out to be hard, this remains the authors’ problem with the society continuized and asks exactly where their polyhedral tractability stops. The paper’s discrete weighted problem is already tractable, so this mirror is principally a question about support-sensitive computation, not about dissolving discrete hardness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 13 does not establish the proposed mass model’s tractability. It proves integrality for a graph of named individuals; the paper’s maximum weight algorithm then uses that structure on the individual-level instance. Neither result says that aggregating interchangeable agents preserves strong stability.

A small example shows the gap. Let each of two types on each side have mass \(1/2\), with strict preferences
\[ a_1:b_1\succ b_2,\quad a_2:b_2\succ b_1,\quad b_1:a_2\succ a_1,\quad b_2:a_1\succ a_2. \]
Both the diagonal matching and the off-diagonal matching are strongly stable. Their average assigns mass \(1/4\) to every type pair. That point satisfies the direct type-level scaling of the paper’s polytope constraints: for example, on \((a_1,b_1)\), the better-partner mass plus the tied-pair mass is \(1/4+1/4=1/2\), the required capacity. But it is not strongly stable as a mass pairing: \(S_{a_1}(b_1)=1/4\) and \(W_{b_1}(a_1)=1/4\), so positive masses can block. The convex combination that Theorem 13 permits is a lottery over whole matchings; implementing its average by splitting the population creates cross-group blocking pairs.

The better mirror therefore needs the exact positive-mass blocking condition, not just the aggregate LP. For each type pair it imposes disjunctions such as \(S_i(j)=0\) or \(W_j(i)=0\). That is a coherent population model, but Theorem 13’s polyhedral result does not characterize its feasible set. Defining \(x\) as a lottery over whole-market matchings would recover the paper’s convexification, but it would change the question to outcome uncertainty rather than population continuization.

This weakens the claimed bridge to the paper’s main result, but it does not defeat the better mirror. Cohort-based matching can be plausible, and the exact mass-stability question remains well-posed. Since ChoCo explicitly treats continuum-specific hardness as a result worth finding, the disjunction is not itself a reason to dismiss the question. The honest negative case is limited: the paper’s integrality theorem is poor evidence that the continuous version will inherit its algorithm. I cannot support the stronger claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror.
