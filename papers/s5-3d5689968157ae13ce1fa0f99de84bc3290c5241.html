The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is about the paper’s population and delegation process, not its complexity results. A plausible regime is a very large membership organization or public consultation in which many people share the same policy preferences, starting voting power, and delegation constraints. Let type \(t\) have mass \(\mu_t\), so \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of participants in that cohort. At each round, \(x^r_{tt'}\) is the mass of voting power delegated from cohort \(t\) to cohort \(t'\), with total outgoing power bounded by what \(t\) holds. Delegated power is multiplied by \(1-c\), as in the paper. To make committee selection meaningful when there are infinitely many citizens, replace fixed \(k\) with a fixed committee share \(\kappa\): the committee is the top \(\kappa\) mass by per-capita power. Corrections and votes use the paper’s proposal rule and power-weighted majority, with sums replaced by integrals. The type description includes preferences over proposals, starting power, and delegation constraints; \(\tau\), the number of represented types, can be small compared with the population.

The one possible anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper, not cited from elsewhere. It asserts that the deliberative process stops after finitely many iterations under the paper’s stopping assumptions: stopping if the proposal is unchanged across consecutive iterations, if committee membership and its members’ voting power are unchanged across consecutive iterations, or if everyone has equal voting power. The theorem’s proof argues that repeated redistributions dilute units, and that with finitely many people and units this eventually leaves everyone with zero power. This is a termination result, but the paper contains no named computational-complexity result asserting tractability, hardness, or an algorithm for a computational problem.

A continuous mirror would be Nonterminating Mass Deliberation. Its instance is a finite support of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational initial per-capita powers, dilution \(c\), committee share \(\kappa\), type preferences over \([0,1]^s\), and delegation constraints; take the correction-size function to be explicitly encoded, for example as a rational piecewise-linear function. The committee quota and cohort-level actions are part of the stated mass model. The question is whether there exists a legal infinite sequence of delegation flows and accepted corrections that avoids every stopping condition. A yes-solution is an infinite legal trajectory; a no-solution is a proof that every legal trajectory reaches a stopping condition in finite time. I would expect some continuous instances to admit nonterminating play: ever smaller positive mass can move, while multiplicative dilution gives \((1-c)^r>0\) after every finite number \(r\) of transfers. If forced into the programme’s trichotomy, I would call this a Class C candidate, but I have no hardness argument; more accurately, it is a conjecture that the finite-termination guarantee fails in the continuum, not a complexity classification.

This mirror retains the paper’s distinctive structure—delegation, dilution, a power-selected committee, proposal changes, and power-weighted vetoes—and scaling the committee with society size is a defensible co-modification. The weakest point is substantial: the fixed committee of \(k\) named individuals becomes a positive-mass committee with cohort-level actions, which changes the institution. Also, the theorem’s printed proof does not establish its key finite-time step under the stated rule \(u\mapsto(1-c)u\): a positive unit remains positive after any finite number of mutations. That does not by itself disprove Theorem 4.1, since another stopping condition might intervene, but it makes this a fragile anchor. So the paper offers a recognizable continuous-process question, but not a strong ChoCo computational-complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 4.1, and it is not a computational result. It says the paper’s deliberative process eventually stops; it gives no algorithm, complexity bound, or computational problem to classify. The proposed “Nonterminating Mass Deliberation” question would add one: encode instances and moves, then decide whether an infinite legal trajectory exists. That could be a worthwhile problem, but it is a new verification question built around the paper’s model, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

A stronger version could preserve the population more carefully than the proponent’s committee-share model. A diffuse electorate could delegate power to a fixed set of representative agents, preserving the paper’s \(k\)-person committee; alternatively, preference cohorts could act as collective blocs. But either version still needs a finite, effective description of the process. The paper allows arbitrary total orders over \([0,1]^s\), leaves the correction-size function general, and does not specify a finite action or strategy representation. Rational masses and a piecewise-linear correction-size function alone do not resolve those choices. Supplying them could yield a precise computational problem, but its complexity would depend on substantial modeling decisions the paper does not make.

The fixed-\(k\) committee and per-person correction rights also resist a simple type-mass interpretation. Keeping individual representatives as special agents may be a sensible hybrid model; making cohorts exercise correction rights collectively changes the institution. Neither choice is forbidden by the programme, but both underscore that the proposed question needs more than taking the population to a continuum.

So the best negative case is about fit: this paper supplies no computational-complexity result for a continuous mirror to chart. I cannot honestly make the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous research question could be built from its deliberation model. A carefully specified termination or reachability problem might be worth studying; the case against is that it would be a new computational agenda, not a mirror of this paper’s results.
