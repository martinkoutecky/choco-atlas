The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is limited by a basic fact: this paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Theorems 1–10 are structural, characterization, aggregation, or strategy-proofness results. Even the constructive Theorem 5 does not state a running time or complexity class. Thus, under the programme’s strict anchor rule, there is no compliant NP-hardness, tractability, FPT, or parameterized-complexity anchor here.

The closest defensible lead is Theorem 6, proved in the paper, although it is a structural rather than computational anchor. It states that a domain has the representative-voter property exactly when it is either a single-crossing domain or a closed Condorcet domain with exactly four orders whose associated graph is a \(4\)-cycle. The single-crossing sufficiency is credited to Roth [91], while the converse and the \(4\)-cycle exception are established through the paper’s median-graph analysis.

A natural continuous mirror would be:

Continuous Representative-Type. An instance consists of a finite alternative set \(X\), a single-crossing domain \(\mathcal D=\{R_1,\ldots,R_\tau\}\) arranged along its associated chain, and a rational mass distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb Q^\tau\), where \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of society with ranking \(R_i\). Assume that no pair of alternatives has exactly half the mass on each side. Define the weighted majority order by

\[ x P_\mu y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{i:xR_i y}\mu_i>\frac12. \]

The task is to output a type \(R_j\) with \(\mu_j>0\) such that, for every pair \(x,y\),

\[ xR_j y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad xP_\mu y. \]

The solution is therefore a positive-mass representative type, together with its top-ranked alternative.

This is a credible population mirror. The agents are voters in a large electorate repeatedly submitting one of a small number of complete rankings; \(\mu_i\) records the fraction using each ranking, with \(\tau\ll N\). The population, rather than the outcome space, is continuous. This is exactly the high-multiplicity interpretation of the paper’s profiles: agents of the same type are indistinguishable for the problem.

The expected classification is Class A. On a single-crossing chain, the representative type is a weighted median of the type distribution. It can be found by cumulative masses, while all pairwise majority comparisons can be checked in polynomial time, for example in \(O(\tau m^2)\) arithmetic operations. Theorem 2 supports the same construction more generally: for a closed Condorcet domain, weighted majority with strict margins produces an order in the domain, even when that order need not itself have positive mass. Theorem 6 identifies precisely when it must be a representative type.

The mirror is plausible because the paper’s central objects are already profiles, majority relations, representative voters, and distributions of rankings in all but name. It does not continuize alternatives, preferences, or the social choice rule. It replaces multiplicities of identical rankings by their masses.

Its weakness is substantial: the continuous version is almost an immediate weighted reformulation of the finite-profile result. The paper does not study an optimization problem, an input-size issue, or a complexity barrier, and the resulting computation is elementary. Moreover, with a nonatomic population, individual strategic manipulation would become vacuous, so Theorem 10 should not be mirrored without adding a nontrivial block or mass-manipulation model.

Accordingly, I would present this as a genuine but modest continuous-population extension of Theorem 6—not as a full ChoCo computational mirror. Under the programme’s strict requirement that every anchor be a named complexity result, the correct conclusion is that this paper supplies no qualifying anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive negative point is the one the proponent concedes: this paper contains no qualifying computational result. Theorem 6 is a structural characterization of domains with the representative-voter property. It has no encoded computational instance, optimization or decision problem, running-time claim, hardness claim, or parameterized statement. The \(O(\tau m^2)\) procedure appears only after adding a new input object, the mass vector \(\mu\). It is therefore a computational consequence of a newly invented formulation, not a continuous mirror of a computational theorem in the paper.

The proposed Continuous Representative-Type problem is nevertheless a legitimate high-multiplicity model. Voters with the same complete ranking can sensibly be grouped, and a rational mass vector is exactly the normalized multiplicity vector of a finite profile. That concession matters: the objection is not that population mass is unnatural, nor that the result is “already continuous.” The problem is that Theorem 6 already quantifies over every odd profile. On a single-crossing chain, replacing repeated voters by masses merely turns the representative voter into a weighted-median type. The paper supplies the structural fact; the mass formulation supplies no new computational object for ChoCo to study.

The strongest repair would remove the supplied chain and ask, given a succinct domain and \(\mu\), whether a positive-mass type represents the weighted majority order. But then the substantive difficulty lies in recognizing or representing the domain, not in continuizing the population. If the domain is explicitly listed, the question is just weighted aggregation followed by a support check. If it is implicitly represented by a median graph, a lattice, or a system of pairwise constraints, the resulting complexity problem is new and depends on that encoding. The paper proves no corresponding computational statement. Likewise, asking for the minimum mass perturbation that creates a representative type, or for robustness of the representative type, could produce worthwhile ChoCo problems, but those are new intervention and robustness problems rather than mirrors of Theorem 6.

The other apparent rescue routes are weaker. Theorems 2–5 concern closure, betweenness, and median-graph structure; adding \(\mu\) to those structural objects does not make population mass part of the theorem. Theorem 9’s maximality is a property of which rankings belong to the domain, independent of how much population carries each ranking. Theorem 10 is axiomatic rather than computational, and its individual strategy-proofness interpretation becomes vacuous for measure-zero agents in an atomless population. Allowing positive-mass coalitions to manipulate would require a new coalition or block-deviation model.

The universal claim that no imaginable downstream optimization could ever be worthwhile is too strong to prove: one could attach bribery, robustness, or domain-design costs to these structures and obtain an interesting new paper. But that would be a new computational-social-choice problem inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results. Under the programme’s anchor rule, the paper should therefore receive the negative verdict: the proposed mirror is mathematically sound but computationally non-qualifying, and no stronger mirror is latent in the paper without importing a new problem whose essential content is not theirs.
