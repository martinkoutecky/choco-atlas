The continuous mirror question

Given \(\epsilon\in(0,1/4)\) and a polynomial-time common preference function over three house categories satisfying the paper's assumptions, take types \(a\in\{1,2,3\}\) of mass \(\mu_a=1/3\), each endowed with category \(a\), and supply \(1/3\) of each category. Find \(p\in\Sigma_3\) and a nonnegative mass flow \(x\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\) with every row and column sum equal to \(1/3\), such that \(x_{aj}>0\) only if \(\operatorname{dist}_1(p,P_j)\le\epsilon\).

The model it lives in

The society has three endowed-category types with \(\mu_a=1/3\), and three categories with supply \(1/3\) each. Its common demand function determines preference sets \(P_j\); the decision variables are prices \(p\) and aggregate flows \(x_{aj}\), with no optimization objective. Each household still receives one indivisible house; the flow records aggregate assignments.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the three-agent case of Corollary 4.11 with identical preferences; it does not cover the paper's other market sizes or its query-complexity results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the three-cohort PPAD-hardness persist when the common demand correspondence is induced by utility functions with monotonicity in money and no externalities?
  • Does PPAD-hardness extend to unequal rational cohort masses and corresponding per-capita house supplies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the Three-cohort HousingMarket\(_\infty\). Take a large housing exchange with three standardized house categories. A fraction \(1/3\) of households is endowed with each category, and each category has supply \(1/3\) of the population. Households share the same demand function over the three categories and money; their complete types differ only by their endowed house category. Thus the population can be arbitrarily large while the support has just three types. Each person still receives one indivisible house; fractional quantities describe only the aggregate mass assigned between types and categories.

The instance consists of \(\epsilon\in(0,1/4)\) and a polynomial-time demand function \(f\) for the three house categories, satisfying the paper’s closedness, bounded-price, and boundary-cover assumptions. The solution is a price vector \(p\in\Sigma_3\) and a mass-flow matrix \(x\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{3\times3}\) with
\[ \sum_j x_{aj}=\tfrac13 \quad\text{for every cohort }a, \qquad \sum_a x_{aj}=\tfrac13 \quad\text{for every category }j, \]
such that \(x_{aj}>0\) only if \(p\) is within \(\epsilon\) of the shared preference set \(P_j\). There is no optimization objective: the question is to find an approximate competitive equilibrium, with prices clearing the aggregate supplies and every assigned mass receiving a demanded category.

My anchor is Corollary 4.11, proved in this paper: for every \(n\ge3\), \(n\)-HousingMarket and \(n\)-RainbowKKM are PPAD-hard in the white-box model. I rely on its \(n=3\) case. The paper’s reduction for that case uses three identical copies of one KKM covering (Theorem 4.10), and its introduction states that hardness holds even for three agents with identical preferences. The underlying PPAD-completeness of 2D-Sperner is cited from Chen and Deng (2009); the reductions establishing the housing-market result are proved here.

The mass-flow formulation preserves that hard question exactly in this restricted case. Multiply \(x\) by \(3\): the result is a doubly stochastic matrix. By the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem, its positive support contains a permutation. Every edge in that permutation is \(\epsilon\)-demanded at \(p\), so it gives an \(\epsilon\)-equilibrium assignment for the paper’s three-agent market. Conversely, any such finite assignment yields a mass flow by assigning mass \(1/3\) along each of its three pairs. Thus fractional type-level allocation does not make this instance easier by allowing a split allocation that has no discrete counterpart.

I expect this mirror to be hardness-transferring (Class B): the paper’s PPAD-hard three-agent market embeds in the continuous population problem, and a continuous solution recovers a finite assignment. It covers the three-agent identical-preference hardness result, not the paper’s full range of markets or its query-complexity results. The authors can plausibly recognize it as their problem with repeated, interchangeable households and houses: the price-and-demand equilibrium condition is unchanged, while supplies scale with population.

The weakest point is that the hard instance already has only three agents. Replicating each into a large cohort gives a coherent high-multiplicity regime, but the hardness is not caused by population size; it comes from the encoded geometry of the preference functions. The scenario is also deliberately narrow: three standardized cohorts with shared demand may fit a planned housing exchange better than a general housing market. That limits the scope of the claim, but not the equivalence for this named anchor.

This mirror also raises useful follow-ups: does the hardness survive with utility functions, monotonicity in money, or no externalities, as the paper asks? For unequal cohort masses, when does a feasible mass flow still yield a finite matching? And do bounds parameterized by the number of types become possible for broader versions of the market?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a convincing case against the three-cohort anchor. The strongest objection is that it is a restricted cloning extension of the paper’s market: the paper has distinct houses, while the mirror treats houses within each category as interchangeable and fixes equal cohort and supply shares. That would limit what the mirror says about identity-sensitive preferences or unequal endowments.

But the anchor chooses the paper’s own three-agent, identical-preference hardness result, where that objection loses force. Endowment category is part of a complete type, and a market with recurring cohorts and category supplies is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The allocation condition also survives exactly: for \(N\) divisible by \(3\), scale the mass-flow matrix by \(N\). The result is an integer transportation matrix, which can be decomposed into assignments using only demanded edges. Thus a continuous solution yields a finite assignment, and a finite assignment yields a mass flow.

The hardness is already present with three agents, so replication does not show that a larger population causes the difficulty or that continuization makes it easier. That limits the mirror’s reach, but the programme explicitly treats hardness transfer as useful evidence about where continuization stops helping. Nor is this an existing continuous treatment: the paper’s prices are continuous, but its population is finite.

So the negative case is weak at its central point. The mirror is narrow, and its population dimension may add little computational leverage; neither point defeats a well-posed mirror of Corollary 4.11. Under the stated grading rule, that anchor survives.
