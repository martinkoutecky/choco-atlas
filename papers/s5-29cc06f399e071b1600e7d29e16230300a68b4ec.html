The continuous mirror question

Given a finite school set \(S\), finite type set \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), type data \((v_\theta,\Lambda_\theta,\omega_\theta,\pi_\theta)\), homogeneous school priorities, and an explicit lottery rule when capacity cuts through a priority block, compute a pure nonatomic DSPDA reporting equilibrium \(\sigma:\Theta\to\mathcal R\). An infinitesimal agent of each type must weakly prefer its report to every alternative under the induced lottery, and the output must include the allocation masses \(x_{\theta,s}\) or report nonexistence.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity school-choice market in which types carry preferences, priority information, loss dominance, and tie-breaking data; \(\mu\) gives type masses, \(\kappa_s\) gives per-capita school capacities, reports are rank-ordered lists, and DSPDA induces type-level matching lotteries \(F_\theta\). A representative type-\(\theta\) agent maximizes \(U_\theta(F)=\sum_s f_{\theta,s}v_{\theta,s}-\Lambda_\theta\sum_{s<r}f_{\theta,s}f_{\theta,r}(v_{\theta,s}-v_{\theta,r})\).

The objection that survived

The proposed notation leaves unclear whether a deviation is by one infinitesimal agent or by the whole mass \(\mu_\theta\); only the former gives a genuine nonatomic equilibrium, and it removes the finite game’s aggregate strategic effect.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3’s pure-CBNE existence and essential-uniqueness construction. It leaves the individual CPE characterizations, Proposition 4’s fixed-capacity asymptotics, Proposition 5’s static-mechanism impossibility, and the sequential-remedy results untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the homogeneous-priority nonatomic game always have a pure equilibrium, and when is it unique?
  • Can the equilibrium be computed in time polynomial in \(|\Theta|\), \(m\), and the input length, avoiding enumeration of all \(m!\) reports?
  • Which boundary-lottery or common-shock specification best preserves the paper’s EBLA uncertainty while remaining a genuine high-multiplicity model?

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no qualifying computational-complexity anchor: no named theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserts NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, W[1]-hardness, or a comparable result. It is an equilibrium and mechanism-design paper, not a computational-social-choice paper. So, literally, it cannot support a standard ChoCo anchor. The strongest honest positive case is nevertheless a single continuous mirror of its central equilibrium result.

The natural regime is a large centralized school-admission system: millions of applicants, a small number of schools, and relatively few complete applicant profiles. A type is

\[ \theta=(v_\theta,\omega_\theta,\Lambda_\theta,\pi_\theta), \]

where \(v_\theta\) gives cardinal school values and the true ranking, \(\omega_\theta\) gives the common priority score, \(\Lambda_\theta\) is loss dominance, and \(\pi_\theta\) records any common tie-breaking or priority uncertainty relevant to the applicant. The type must include every feature affecting the decision. The society is a mass vector \(\mu\) over finitely many such types; \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of applicants of type \(\theta\). School capacities are per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), with \(\sum_s\kappa_s\le 1\). This describes, for example, recurring cohorts with common preference templates, priority categories, and behavioural parameters, where \(N\) is very large but \(|\Theta|\) is modest.

The action of a type is a reported rank-ordered list, possibly truncated. DSPDA is run on masses: each type sends its mass to the first listed school, schools retain mass according to the common priority order, and rejected mass continues. If a capacity cuts through a priority block, a specified lottery or tie-breaking rule gives type \(\theta\) a matching lottery \(F_\theta=(f_{\theta,s})_s\). Its objective is the paper’s CPE utility,

\[ U_\theta(F) = \sum_s f_{\theta,s}v_{\theta,s} - \Lambda_\theta \sum_{s<r} f_{\theta,s}f_{\theta,r} \bigl(v_{\theta,s}-v_{\theta,r}\bigr), \]

after schools are indexed by decreasing true value. The output is a report policy \(\sigma:\Theta\to\mathcal R\), together with the induced allocation masses \(x_{\theta,s}\).

My lead anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper: under finite type spaces, homogeneous school preferences, and a publicly known deterministic tie-breaking rule, there exists an essentially unique choice-acclimating Bayesian Nash equilibrium in pure strategies. “Essentially unique” means unique once the public rule for resolving indifference is fixed. The proof constructs the equilibrium iteratively, beginning with the highest-priority types and proceeding downward.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Mass-CBNE-DSPDA. Given \(S\), rational capacities \(\kappa_s\), a finite type set \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), type data \((v_\theta,\omega_\theta,\Lambda_\theta,\pi_\theta)\), and the school priority rule, output the essentially unique pure report policy \(\sigma\) satisfying

\[ U_\theta(F_\theta^\sigma) \ge U_\theta(F_\theta^{r,\sigma_{-\theta}}) \qquad \text{for every }\theta\in\Theta \text{ and every report }r\in\mathcal R, \]

and output the induced mass allocation \(x_{\theta,s}\). If no such policy exists outside the homogeneous-priority regime, output NO.

This is recognisably the authors’ problem: the mechanism is still DSPDA, the action is still strategic reporting of school rankings, the behavioural objective is still EBLA/CPE utility, and the output is still the equilibrium allocation. Only named students have been replaced by masses of indistinguishable applicants, while capacities scale with the cohort. It is not a tractable caricature obtained by deleting loss aversion.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A in the finite-school regime, at least parameterized by \(m=|S|\). Proposition 3’s construction processes priority types in order; for each type, one can evaluate its finitely many possible reports and choose a best response. This gives an immediate \(O(\lvert\Theta\rvert m!\operatorname{poly}(L))\) algorithm, hence fixed-parameter tractability in \(m\) and polynomial time for fixed \(m\). The interesting computational question is whether the \(m!\) dependence can be removed. With unrestricted heterogeneous school priorities, equilibrium computation may instead become continuum-specific hard, but this paper supplies no hardness result.

I would not use Proposition 4 as a second anchor. It is proved here and is highly suggestive—the paper shows that the probability of instability and the expected number of students exposed to justified envy remain bounded away from zero as \(n\) grows—but its limit keeps elite capacity \(q\) fixed while \(n\) increases. That is a singular vanishing-capacity regime, not the ordinary per-capita-capacity continuum. It is better treated as a follow-up question: does the continuum model with \(\kappa_s>0\) preserve any nonzero instability, and can the fixed-\(q\) asymptotics be recovered as a boundary-layer limit?

The weakest point is substantial: in an exact deterministic continuum, aggregate uncertainty disappears by the law of large numbers, so many CPE lotteries collapse to degenerate outcomes and loss aversion loses its force. The proposed mirror must therefore retain an explicit type-level priority lottery, boundary randomization, or uncertainty about the aggregate state. If the authors reject that co-modification, this is not a convincing mirror of their behavioural mechanism. Subject to that qualification, Mass-CBNE-DSPDA is a genuine high-multiplicity version of their central problem and a plausible Class A computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper supplies no computational-complexity anchor. Proposition 3 is an existence-and-uniqueness theorem for a finite Bayesian game, not a theorem about complexity, an algorithm, or even an equilibrium-computation problem. Turning it into Mass-CBNE-DSPDA would therefore not continuize a computational result from the paper; it would invent a new computational question around an equilibrium claim. That can sometimes be worthwhile, but here the proposed limit does not preserve the object Proposition 3 analyses.

The finite proof relies on an individual-by-individual priority order. A student’s report affects the lotteries faced by lower-priority named students, and the proof constructs equilibrium by processing those students from highest to lowest score. In a nonatomic society, an individual deviation has measure zero and does not change any aggregate school load or rejection probability. The descending induction disappears. Every type instead best-responds to a fixed mass allocation.

The proponent’s inequality silently conflates two different deviations. If one infinitesimal applicant of type \(\theta\) deviates, \(\sigma_{-\theta}\) is unchanged and there is no finite-market strategic interaction. If the entire mass \(\mu_\theta\) deviates, the allocation changes, but that is a coalition or type-block deviation, not the CBNE notion in the paper. A mass equilibrium based on the latter would be a new equilibrium concept, and Proposition 3 gives no reason to expect existence, essential uniqueness, or the claimed \(O(|\Theta|m!\operatorname{poly}(L))\) algorithm for it.

The problem is sharper because the paper’s uncertainty is precisely what the law of large numbers removes. In the finite model, an applicant is uncertain about the other students’ scores, preferences, and reports. With a fixed society distribution \(\mu\), the aggregate mass of every type is known exactly. Under homogeneous priorities and positive per-capita capacities, admission cutoffs become deterministic. For a representative type whose outcome is deterministic, \(F_\theta\) is a point mass and the loss-aversion term vanishes:

\[ \sum_{s<r} f_{\theta,s}f_{\theta,r} \bigl(v_{\theta,s}-v_{\theta,r}\bigr)=0. \]

Thus \(\Lambda_\theta\) remains in the notation but no longer drives behaviour. The central phenomenon of the paper—students deliberately choosing stochastically dominated reports to avoid disappointment—has disappeared.

The proposed repairs do not follow from continuization. Randomizing a tie at a capacity boundary can preserve a nondegenerate \(F_\theta\), but it replaces the paper’s deterministic priority rule with a new randomized mechanism and makes loss aversion active only for types cut by that boundary. Encoding the paper’s deterministic tie-breaking order in the type, on the other hand, makes each priority position part of the type. Then agents with different positions are not identical, so the supposed high-multiplicity regime disappears. Replacing those positions by anonymous random tie-breaking is a substantive model change, not a consequence of taking a population limit.

A common aggregate shock could also preserve uncertainty: applicants might be uncertain which priority environment or cohort state will materialize. But then the society is a random distribution \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\), not the single mass vector \(\mu\) in the proposed problem. One is studying a new Bayesian mean-field matching model with common shocks. That may be an interesting project, but it is not the continuous mirror of Proposition 3, whose uncertainty comes from finite sampling of other agents.

Proposition 4 does not provide an escape route. Its nonvanishing conclusion keeps elite capacity \(q\) fixed while \(n\) grows, so the affected fraction is vanishing. A normalized continuum instead uses \(q/n\to\kappa\); with \(\kappa>0\), aggregate uncertainty collapses, while \(\kappa=0\) is a singular boundary-layer regime. Retaining the paper’s conclusion requires tracking an \(O(1)\) number of exceptional students or introducing a special rescaling, neither of which is the programme’s continuous society.

The universal claim cannot be proved in a modelling domain: one could construct a new stochastic mean-field version with common shocks, randomized priorities, and a block-deviation equilibrium. But that concession reinforces the negative verdict. The paper itself offers no computational result to mirror, and its only plausible anchor loses either its strategic interaction, its endogenous uncertainty, or its high-multiplicity interpretation in the continuum. The resulting research question would be a new stochastic matching programme, not a worthwhile ChoCo mirror of this paper.
