What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Theorem 3’s minimum-maximal-circulation hardness; it leaves the strong-maximum algorithm and the Kemeny connection unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: the Minmax Circulation decision problem is NP-complete. Its input is a directed multigraph \(G\) and an integer \(K\); it asks whether there is a circulation whose removal leaves an acyclic graph and uses at most \(K\) arc units. The paper proves this by reduction from Feedback Arc Set.

A direct population setting is a large pairwise-preference survey. Each respondent is assigned one comparison between alternatives and reports one direction. A type \(t=(i,j)\) means “assigned the comparison of \(i\) with \(j\), and reports \(i\succ j\).” Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of respondents of that type. With \(n\) alternatives, there are at most \(n(n-1)\) such types, even when the survey has millions of respondents. This is close to the paper’s vote-graph model: each type contributes mass to exactly one directed arc.

Call the resulting decision problem Continuous Minmax Circulation. An instance consists of alternatives \(V\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over supported pairwise-comparison types, and a rational budget \(B\). Set \(q_{ij}=\mu_{(i,j)}\). The question is whether there is a real-valued flow \(x\) satisfying \(0\le x_{ij}\le q_{ij}\), flow conservation at every alternative, \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\le B\), and acyclicity of the residual graph containing exactly those arcs with \(q_{ij}-x_{ij}>0\). A solution is such a flow; its residual graph induces the reported partial order by transitive closure. Equivalently, the optimization version minimizes the total mass removed, \(\sum_{i,j}x_{ij}\).

I expect hardness to transfer (Class B). Given the paper’s integer-capacity graph with total arc multiplicity \(Q\), make each arc unit a respondent assigned to that comparison, combine parallel arcs into one type, and set each type’s mass to its multiplicity divided by \(Q\). Set the continuous budget to \(K/Q\). Fractional flows do not create an escape route on these instances: for any feasible fractional \(x\), take a topological order of its residual graph. Every arc pointing backward in that order must be fully removed. Fixing those backward-arc flows at capacity leaves a minimum-cost circulation problem with integral bounds, which has an integral optimum by total unimodularity. That optimum costs no more than \(x\) and leaves only forward arcs. Thus the normalized continuous instance has a feasible solution exactly when the original integer instance does. The decision version is also in NP: a topological order and a polynomial-bit rational flow certify feasibility. So this formulation is NP-complete.

The mirror covers only Theorem 3’s minimum-maximal-circulation hardness; it does not claim to mirror the strong-maximum algorithm or the Kemeny connection. Its weakest point is that the paper already permits nonintegral arc capacities and defines the same minimum-maximal-circulation objective over them. For the one-comparison-per-respondent scenario, the mass model is therefore almost exactly a population interpretation of a model the paper already has. Also, if one insists that every respondent submits a complete ranking, the reduction above may not produce realizable profiles; hardness for that restricted regime would need a separate proof. A useful follow-up is whether the hardness persists for complete-ranking populations, or whether that restriction admits algorithms parameterized by the number of alternatives or supported ranking types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the proponent’s only anchor, and its proposed pairwise-survey mirror appears to collide with the paper’s own model. The paper allows nonintegral capacities \(q_{ij}\), real-valued circulations, and fractional residual votes; it even says its results generalize to initially fractional capacities. For respondents each assigned one pairwise comparison, setting \(q_{ij}\) to that type’s mass gives the same problem: minimize removed mass subject to flow conservation and an acyclic residual graph. Normalizing the paper’s integer instances by their total mass gives the proponent’s distribution-and-budget formulation. The resulting hardness is the same Theorem 3 on rescaled inputs, not a missing continuous-population result.

A complete-ranking version does not clearly escape this. If the operation still removes pairwise-comparison mass, the ranking distribution is compressed into \(q\), which is all the circulation problem uses. If the operation must instead remove or transfer whole voters while preserving their rankings, it changes the problem: a circulation can remove one comparison incidence without removing that voter’s other comparisons. Theorem 3 does not anchor that voter-level task. Restricting capacities to those realizable by complete-ranking populations could be an interesting separate promise problem, but it would need its own justification and would not supply a distinct continuization of the result as stated.

So the strongest negative case is narrow but substantial: the natural one-comparison-per-agent mirror is already present in the paper’s fractional-capacity formulation. I can’t rule out every useful ranking-constrained extension; I don’t see one that both avoids this overlap and remains a mirror of Theorem 3.
