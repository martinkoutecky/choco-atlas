The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is the weighted-majority part of the pessimistic, status-quo-precedence model. The anchor is Theorem 3.4, proved in the paper. It says that dictatorships, 3-oligarchies, and all quasi-dictatorships are self-maintaining under every common belief with SQP tie-breaking; within the domains of simple games and weighted majority games, these are exactly the self-maintaining rules. [Theorem 3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.22051v3)

This is a structural classification, not a theorem claiming P, NP-hardness, or another complexity class. The paper supplies no named result of that kind. I’d use Theorem 3.4 as the strongest available anchor because it gives an exact answer to a recognition question: which rules in a natural domain withstand every belief and every permitted replacement?

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large DAO, cooperative, or membership organization whose constitution divides people into a small number \(\tau\) of formal classes, with many members in each class. A type records the member’s constitutional class, per-capita voting weight, and relevant belief model; members of a type are otherwise interchangeable for the question. For \(N\) members, type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), with \(N\gg\tau\). At a future decision, let \(x_t\in[0,1]\) be the fraction of type \(t\) supporting it. The society is the distribution \(\mu\); the paper’s common belief becomes a distribution \(F\) over these mass-share states. The decision itself remains binary.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover:

Robust Type-Weighted Constitutional Stability. The instance is rational type masses \(\mu\) and an incumbent rule \(f\) from the type-weighted majority domain
\[ \mathcal W_\mu=\left\{h_{a,Q}(x)=\mathbf 1\!\left[\sum_t\mu_t a_t x_t>Q\right]: a_t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0},\ \sum_t\mu_ta_t>0,\ \frac12\sum_t\mu_ta_t\le Q<\sum_t\mu_ta_t\right\}. \]
For any rule \(h\) and common belief \(F\) on \([0,1]^\tau\), define a type member’s expected probability of agreement as
\[ u_t(h,F)=\int\!\left(x_t\mathbf 1[h(x)=1]+(1-x_t)\mathbf 1[h(x)=0]\right)\,dF(x). \]
For each proposed replacement \(g\in\mathcal W_\mu\), type \(t\) votes for replacement exactly when \(u_t(g,F)>u_t(f,F)\); equality means voting to retain the incumbent. Let \(c_t\in\{0,1\}\) be those type-level votes. The question is whether \(f(c)=0\) for every replacement \(g\) and every common belief \(F\). A solution is a yes/no classification of the incumbent; a negative answer exhibits a belief and replacement that pass under \(f\).

This preserves the paper’s central question—whether the incumbent institution can be replaced under its own rule—and its utility, belief, replacement-domain, and tie-breaking assumptions. The type-weighted rule is the necessary co-modification for mass to matter: on the finite grid \(x_t=k_t/n_t\), it reproduces the corresponding weighted vote among members of each class. The mirror covers Theorem 3.4’s weighted-majority slice; it does not attempt to continuize the paper’s other belief models or extensions.

I would initially investigate this as Class A, plausibly fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\) and polynomial in the rational input length. For a fixed pair of rules, the payoff-difference vectors lie in a finite-dimensional convex hull, so a small-support belief witness is plausible; the threshold regions are polyhedral. The difficult step is handling the search over replacement rules, so this is a research direction rather than a consequence of Theorem 3.4. Further questions are whether the theorem’s dictatorship/oligarchy/quasi-dictatorship classification survives with fractional mass states, and whether every failure has a finitely supported rational belief witness.

The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 3.4 uses individual-level minimal winning coalitions, while the mirror adds interior mass profiles and restricts replacement rules to those that treat members of a type alike. The characterization may change, and authors might reject the restricted replacement domain if individualized weight assignments are essential to their question. That is why the regime must be an institution whose voting rules actually operate on a small set of formal classes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the proponent’s anchor, Theorem 3.4, is that its classification is built around named pivotal voters. Dictatorships and quasi-dictatorships depend on particular people, and the proof uses individual minimal winning coalitions. In the proposed mirror, the rule acts on \(\tau\) type-level votes \(c_t\). At that stage, \(\mu_t a_t\) is just the weight of a type-level voter, so the population size has disappeared from the rule’s input. The theorem’s classification therefore does not itself supply a high-multiplicity algorithm or show that the continuous problem inherits its structure.

That objection does not defeat the better version of the mirror. A large organization could plausibly have many interchangeable members in a small number of formal classes, with constitutional rules that assign power by class. Its stability question would use class-homogeneous replacement rules and beliefs over fractional support profiles. That is a coherent population-continuous model, and the paper’s exact characterization does not answer it: the fractional profiles change the utility comparisons that determine how each class votes. The authors could reasonably recognize this as their self-maintenance problem under a high-multiplicity regime.

There is a genuine modeling choice here. With common beliefs and identical members within each type, everyone in a type makes the same replacement choice, so the stability vote has only \(\tau\) coordinates even when each type contains many people. That limits the mirror’s claim to new computational structure. But the distribution of future support remains continuous, and the resulting robust classification or complexity question is still well-posed once its input representation is specified. The finite-dimensional vote alone is not enough to show that question is uninteresting.

So the negative case is weak where it matters: I can challenge whether this particular proposal yields a new computational landscape, but I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in the class-based institutional scenario. The observation that the paper states characterizations rather than complexity bounds is also insufficient: deciding or characterizing self-maintenance is still a computational question. Theorem 3.4 survives as a viable anchor, even though its continuous analogue may turn out to be less novel than hoped.
