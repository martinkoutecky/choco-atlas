The continuous mirror question

Given a finite rational distribution \(\mu\) over commute-interval types \([x_j,y_j]\), does the least point where right-side mass is at most left-side mass maximize \(W_\mu(t)=-\sum_j\mu_j(|t-x_j|+|t-y_j|)\), and does the resulting direct mechanism remain group strategy-proof when a coalition may split type mass among alternate reports?

The model it lives in

The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete commute types \([x_j,y_j]\), with rational masses summing to \(1\). The decision variable is a facility point \(t\in\mathbb{R}\); the objective is to maximize \(W_\mu(t)=-\sum_j\mu_j(|t-x_j|+|t-y_j|)\). Deviations may reassign portions of a type's mass to other interval reports, and group strategy-proofness requires that no such coalition make every deviating portion strictly better off under its true type's benefit.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the pit-stop welfare result of Theorem 2 and its incentive companion, Theorem 1; Theorem 4 supplies a separate flexible-shuttle analogue. It leaves the fixed-length and utility-measure results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the least mass-balanced point remain group strategy-proof when a coalition can split the mass of a single commute type across reports?
  • How do the weighted mechanism and its incentive guarantee relate formally to finite high-multiplicity instances?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s facility-mechanism problems: keep the line, reports, payoffs, and strategic question, but represent the commuters by masses of complete commute types.

The paper has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or parameterized. Its relevant named results instead give explicit mechanisms and prove their properties. I’ll use those as constructive anchors, without presenting them as complexity-class results.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for the pit-stop facility game on a line, when each player’s benefit is
\[ \beta_i(x_i,y_i,t)=-\bigl(|t-x_i|+|t-y_i|\bigr), \]
the lowest balanced mechanism maximizes social benefit. The adjacent Theorem 1, also proved here, establishes that the lowest balanced mechanism is group strategy-proof for the pit-stop game with interval-peaked benefit functions. Theorem 1 supplies the incentive guarantee for the particular benefit function in Theorem 2.

The corresponding question, Mass-Pit-Stop Welfare–GSP, is: given a finite support of commute types \([x_j,y_j]\), with rational masses \(\mu_j>0\) summing to \(1\), design a deterministic direct mechanism \(F\) on such distributions. Its output is a point \(t\in\mathbb R\). For every truthful distribution \(\mu\), it must maximize
\[ W_\mu(t)=-\sum_j\mu_j\bigl(|t-x_j|+|t-y_j|\bigr) \]
and satisfy weighted group strategy-proofness: for any coalition that takes mass \(a_j\leq\mu_j\) from each type and jointly changes its reports, some positive mass in that coalition must not strictly improve under its true type’s benefit. The weighted analogue of the lowest balanced rule is a candidate solution: choose the least \(p\) for which the mass of intervals wholly to the right of \(p\) is at most the mass wholly to its left. This is a single-station version of the paper’s problem, not a replacement of its strategic question with welfare optimization alone.

A second, more direct shuttle anchor is Theorem 4, proved here: for the flexible-length shuttle game, the respective median mechanism is group strategy-proof when
\[ \beta_i([x_i,y_i],[p,q]) =-\min\bigl(y_i-x_i,\ |x_i-p|+|y_i-q|\bigr). \]
The corresponding question, Mass-Flexible-Shuttle GSP, has the same kind of finite rational support over trip intervals. Its mechanism must return a facility interval \([p,q]\), with \(p\leq q\), and prevent any coalition from making every member strictly better off by changing its reports. The natural candidate is to take weighted medians of the left and right trip endpoints and return the interval they define. The output and utility retain the paper’s flexible facility and walking-distance payoff.

A plausible regime is a large metro or regional transit system with many commuters but comparatively few origin–destination zone pairs. A type is one complete trip interval: riders with that interval have the same preferences and report space in this model. The population can be millions while the supported set of commute types is much smaller. This is a natural high-multiplicity instance regime; it does not require treating a seven-person committee as a limit object.

I expect both questions to be tractable (Class A). Sorting the \(\tau\) supported endpoints and doing weighted selection or balance checks gives an exact outcome in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). The paper does not prove the weighted results: extending its arguments requires replacing counts by mass, including when a coalition splits the mass of one type across reports. Further questions are whether those extensions hold for the full stated deviation model, how the weighted and finite-agent variants relate, and what welfare guarantees the flexible-shuttle rule has.

The weakest point is the main one for ChoCo: these anchors are mechanism theorems, not hardness results. The paper neither identifies a discrete hardness barrier nor shows that continuization changes the complexity landscape. The proposed mirrors look sensible and plausibly easy to solve, but they may yield a useful high-multiplicity mechanism formulation without delivering the programme’s hoped-for example of discrete hardness dissolving in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that the paper proves mechanism properties, not computational results of the kind ChoCo is set up to mirror. Theorems 1 and 4 establish group strategy-proofness for explicit mechanisms; Theorem 2 establishes group strategy-proofness and welfare optimality for the pit-stop rule. None gives a complexity classification, an algorithmic bound, or a hardness result for a computational problem. The paper’s central question is which mechanism satisfies the incentive and welfare conditions.

The mass pit-stop version is nevertheless coherent. A transit authority serving many commuters on a small set of origin–destination corridors is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and \(\mu\) over complete trip types preserves the relevant preferences. One can ask whether the weighted lowest-balanced rule maximizes \(W_\mu(t)\) and resists coalitions that may split type mass across reports. That would be a legitimate weighted mechanism-design question. But it replaces the paper’s axioms about finite-player coalitions with mass-weighted axioms; it does not turn Theorems 1 and 2 into results about computational complexity. Adding costs and asking for the least-cost mass transfer that changes the facility outcome could make a computational problem, but that would be a new intervention problem rather than a mirror of those theorems.

The flexible-shuttle anchor has the same limitation. With repeated commute intervals, the weighted endpoint-median rule is a natural continuous version of the respective median mechanism, and a positive-mass coalition definition gives a sensible way to avoid vacuous individual deviations in an atomless population. This is a plausible extension of Theorem 4, but its central claim is still an incentive property of a rule, not the complexity of computing or manipulating one.

So the negative case is strongest as a claim about fit with ChoCo’s computational remit, not as a claim that no useful continuous facility-game model exists. I cannot honestly argue that the proposed population regime is implausible: it is one of the paper’s strongest features. A weighted group-strategyproofness study could be worthwhile in mechanism design. The case against is that this paper supplies no computational result for ChoCo to continuize.
