The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: the paper’s central computation—aggregating partial rankings with Borda scores—has a natural continuous-population form. Imagine a large consultation or election in which many voters submit the same truncated ballot. A type is one distinct partial ranking; its mass is the fraction of voters submitting it. With few ballot types relative to the electorate, that is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the authors would likely recognize the resulting winner computation as their problem.

One could define Continuous Partial-Borda Winner Determination as follows. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of partial rankings, and binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). Under the paper’s stated convention that unranked candidates receive the lowest score, let \(b_t(c)\) be the Borda score of candidate \(c\) on type \(t\), with unranked candidates scoring \(0\). The task is to output each candidate’s exact score
\[ \mathrm{sc}_{\mu}(c)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t b_t(c) \]
and the full winner set \(\arg\max_{c\in C}\mathrm{sc}_{\mu}(c)\). A solution is that score vector and winner set. This is computable by direct summation in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. If \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), it gives the discrete election’s scores scaled by \(1/n\), so the winner set is unchanged. I would expect this mirror to be tractable, in Class A.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a qualifying mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to anchor one to. The proposed continuous Borda calculation is a sensible model: in a large election or a massive course, many people could submit the same partial ranking, so treating each ranking as a type with a fractional mass is natural. Its scores would be \(\mathrm{sc}_{\mu}(c)=\sum_t\mu_t b_t(c)\). That the calculation is easy, or that it preserves winners for discrete proportions, is not a reason to reject it. The problem for the proponent is that this is a continuous formulation of the paper’s central computation, not a mirror of a named result by the paper. The examples do not supply a substitute: they are not formal guarantees, and some announce a winner despite tied scores without specifying a tie-breaking rule.

A stronger proposal, such as asking how much mass must change before the Borda winner changes, would pose a more substantive computational question. But the paper does not study winner robustness, ballot changes, or optimization over possible completions of unranked candidates. Those would be new questions suggested by the topic, not continuous mirrors of results in this paper.
