The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchored case for a continuous computational mirror: the paper contains no named result about an algorithm, runtime, or computational hardness.

The closest candidates are existence results. For example, Theorem 3.2, proved in this paper, says that an EFX allocation exists for a set-monotonic valuation profile under specified near-equal-bundle conditions: if \(m\bmod n\leq 1\), \(n-1\) agents must have \(\langle\lfloor m/n\rfloor-1,\lfloor m/n\rfloor\rangle\)-size-monotonic valuations; if \(1<m\bmod n\leq n-1\), the corresponding conditions apply to \(m\bmod n\) agents at sizes \(\lfloor m/n\rfloor,\lfloor m/n\rfloor+1\), and a different \(n-1-(m\bmod n)\) agents at the preceding size range. Theorem 3.3 and Theorems 4.1–4.2 also assert existence under valuation restrictions. None states that finding an allocation is in P or gives any other complexity classification.

The proofs construct allocations using maximization over bundles, but the paper gives no valuation representation, oracle model, or runtime analysis. Treating those existence theorems as computational anchors would overstate what they establish. A continuous-population EFX existence question might be independently worth posing, but it would not be a mirror anchored in a named computational result from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is also the strongest case against a mirror: every result they name is an existence theorem, not a complexity result. Theorem 3.2 gives EFX existence under conditions involving \(m \bmod n\) and valuation restrictions; Theorem 3.3 and Theorems 4.1–4.2 give related existence claims. None defines a computational problem, specifies an input encoding, or establishes a runtime or hardness bound. A continuous yes/no question under those same hypotheses would already have the answer “yes.” Asking how to find an allocation is a reasonable next question, but it is a new computational target rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proof’s bundle maximizations make that target more plausible, but they do not settle its complexity. With valuations given as complete tables, those maximizations can be done by scanning the table; with a value oracle or a compact representation, the cost is different. The paper chooses no representation and makes no complexity claim. Any continuous computational result would therefore depend on choices that the paper leaves open.

The strongest repaired proposal is a high-multiplicity EFX allocation problem: represent agents by valuation types with masses, scale the supply of goods per capita, and describe an allocation by the mass of each type receiving each integral bundle. Theorem 3.2 suggests a limit with proportions \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\) in the two adjacent size-monotonicity classes, where \(m/n\) tends to \(q+\alpha\). That is a coherent model, and it preserves indivisible bundles.

But it does not arise canonically from the paper’s model. If the finite set \(E\) stays fixed as the population grows, eventually \(m\leq n\), and Observation 1 makes EFX immediate. Keeping a nonzero per-capita supply instead requires scaling the goods too. For arbitrary set-monotonic valuations, extending valuations to replicated goods requires additional assumptions. The one or two unrestricted agents in several theorems also become zero-mass types in the limit, unless the model retains named atoms. Those choices may produce a worthwhile computational fair-division project, but they supply much of the problem the paper itself does not formulate.

So the negative case is strong that this paper has no continuous *computational mirror anchored in its named results*. It is weaker as a claim that no continuous EFX computation could be worth studying: the type-mass model above is plausible. I would call that a new research direction prompted by the paper, not a computational mirror of its contributions.
