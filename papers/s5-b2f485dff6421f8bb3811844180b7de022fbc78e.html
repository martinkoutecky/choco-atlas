The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no continuous question tied to a result, so there is no anchor to defeat. Their strongest point is decisive under the stated standard: the paper’s central formula, \(q_{\mathrm{av}}(M)\approx \tfrac12+1/\sqrt{\pi M}\), is an analytic approximation, not a complexity or algorithmic result. The paper reports computing Banzhaf indices for examples, but it does not state a computational result about that task.

The best candidate for a mirror would give citizens continuous masses of “yes” and “no” voters within each state, then ask how those masses affect Banzhaf power or the optimal quota. But Banzhaf power here comes from the probability that a *discrete representative* is pivotal among \(M\) representatives. If voter masses determine each representative’s vote, the random ballot model disappears; if the random ballot model is retained, the representative’s power depends on finite-population pivotal events, whose probabilities shrink at scale \(1/\sqrt{N_i}\). The upper-tier computation still concerns the same \(M\) discrete representatives. A mass-based question about how many citizens must change their view to flip a Council decision could be well posed, but it changes the paper’s objective from ex ante voting power to robustness or manipulation.

A continuum of state types is a stronger alternative: one could study the distribution of Banzhaf power across many states of different sizes. Yet each state’s individual power vanishes in the atomless limit. The quota correction the paper studies also vanishes: \(q_\ast-\tfrac12\) is of order \(M^{-1/2}\). Rescaling to preserve that correction leads back to a finite-size Gaussian or local-limit calculation—the paper’s analytic territory, not a new complexity landscape. And its Dirichlet simplex is a prior over population *sizes* for a fixed \(M\)-state body, not a continuous society of voter types.

So the case against a mirror of this paper is strong: none of its results supplies a computational anchor, and the closest continuous variants either change the question or wash out the pivotality the paper measures. The universal claim still has a limit: a high-multiplicity weighted-voting problem could be worthwhile in its own right. The case here is that it would be a new project, not a continuous mirror of this paper.
