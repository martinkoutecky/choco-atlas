The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a plausible population model for this paper, but I can’t make a compliant computational-mirror case anchored in one of its named results. None of the numbered theorems, propositions, or corollaries classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard. For example, Corollary 3, proved here, says that every SSB preference profile satisfying strict maximality admits an efficient and envy-free random assignment. The \(\alpha\)-envy-free theorem likewise proves existence. These are substantive results, but their fixed-point proofs establish existence, not an algorithm or complexity bound. Theorem 1, cited from ABB14b, characterizes efficiency using positive welfare weights; it is not a complexity result either.

The paper does report PPAD-hardness results in §7, attributed to CCPY22a and Tröbst–Vazirani. But those are unnumbered results from other works, not computational anchors of this paper. So there are no eligible anchors here, and I cannot honestly assign a complexity class to a per-anchor mirror.

The strongest *candidate* model is a large centralized school or housing assignment. Let a type be a complete ranking of \(m\) school or housing categories, extended to lotteries by the paper’s pairwise-comparison preferences. A large cohort could have unit mass, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) for its \(\tau\) ranking types, where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and the number of applicants is much larger than \(\tau\). Let \(x_t\in\Delta(O)\) be the lottery assigned to each member of type \(t\). Since the original model gives \(n\) objects to \(n\) agents, the continuous version would scale capacity with population: each category has supply \(1/m\), so feasibility requires
\[ \sum_t \mu_t x_t(o)=\frac{1}{m} \quad\text{for every }o\in O. \]
A natural search task would be to find a feasible type allocation that is pairwise-comparison envy-free, \(\phi_t(x_s,x_t)\le 0\) for every pair of types, and efficient: no other feasible allocation weakly improves every positive-mass type and strictly improves at least one.

That is a recognizable extension of the paper’s assignment problem: population masses actively constrain the allocation, and full rankings make type members interchangeable. The authors might accept it as a continuous analogue of their ordinal assignment setting, with capacity scaling as part of the model. But it is only a proposed new search problem. Corollary 3 supplies a nearby existence guarantee, not a result about computing this allocation; whether computation is tractable or hard remains open here.

That is the weakest point, and it is decisive for the requested anchor-based case: the paper’s results are about existence and fairness, while the computational claims it mentions belong to cited works. The positive case supports a plausible population model, but not a computational mirror tied to a named result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is an anchor mismatch. This paper’s named results establish existence or impossibility of assignments with fairness and efficiency properties; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Corollary 3 and the \(\alpha\)-envy-free theorem are genuine results, but turning either into “compute an assignment with these properties” adds an algorithmic question the paper does not answer. Theorem 1 is cited from earlier work, and the PPAD-hardness results in the conclusion are also attributed to other papers. Under ChoCo’s anchor requirement, none supplies a computational result here to continuize.

The strongest proposed mirror is still clear: give a large school-assignment cohort as rational masses over \(\tau\) complete ranking types, scale each school’s capacity with the population, and ask for an assignment by type that is pairwise-comparison envy-free and efficient. One could instead ask for a pseudo-market equilibrium, or for an efficient \(\alpha\)-envy-free assignment for SSB types. These are coherent population models. Fractional lotteries in the paper’s outcome space do not themselves make the population continuous, but the mass model does; scaling capacities is a reasonable part of that model.

That formulation does not, however, rescue the anchor-based case. It makes computing a witness for an existence theorem a new research task, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. It also requires a chosen type-level allocation model: with pairwise-comparison preferences, averaging the lotteries of agents with the same ranking need not preserve their comparisons, since those comparisons are bilinear. That is a modeling choice to resolve, not a proof that no sensible mirror exists.

So the negative case is strong only under the programme’s stated anchor discipline: this paper offers no named computational result to mirror. It is weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous computational question arises. The proposed school-assignment search problem may well be worth studying; the honest case against it is that it is newly attached to the paper’s existence results, not that the population model is inherently unsound.
