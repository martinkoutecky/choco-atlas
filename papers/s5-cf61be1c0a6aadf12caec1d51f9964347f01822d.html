The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest anchored case for a continuous-population mirror. The paper has no named computational result: its theorems concern market-design guarantees and convex analysis, not complexity or algorithms for a stated computational problem.

The closest foothold is Theorem 2 (proved here). It characterizes when a sudden-revelation switch can simultaneously preserve conditional prices and excess utility while reducing uninformed traders’ utility to zero: this is possible exactly when the convex roof of \(R^{\hat b}\) agrees with \(R^{\hat b}\) on the conditional price spaces. The paper then constructs the new cost function from that roof. Theorem 3 proves price preservation and scheduled utility decrease for gradual LCMMs; Theorems 12–13 prove worst-case-loss bounds. These are substantive results, but none asserts a computational complexity or algorithmic result of the kind required for an anchor.

The tempting positive move—give traders private-information types with rational masses, aggregate their trades, and ask for an optimal switching policy—would introduce a new population-optimization problem. The paper’s switch construction depends on market state and payoff geometry, not on a distribution of traders; making the market’s outcome, price, or trade space continuous would also miss the programme’s population-continuization scope. So there is no precise continuous problem per qualifying anchor to state here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s central point is the strongest one: none of the paper’s numbered results states a computational complexity claim. Theorems 2 and 3 characterize market design guarantees; Theorems 12 and 13 bound worst-case loss. They concern whether a cost function can preserve specified incentives, prices, or loss bounds. Their main mathematical objects are the payoff geometry, convex conjugates, and market state. The paper uses continuous trade quantities and, in one setting, continuous time, but neither makes the society a continuous population.

Theorem 2 offers the closest possible computational mirror: given \(C\), \(X\), and a state \(\s\), decide whether a switch preserving \(\CondPrice\) and \(\CondVal\) while achieving \(\ZeroVal\) exists, and construct one when it does. The paper gives a convex-roof characterization and sufficient conditions. That could motivate an algorithmic question about convex geometry, but the input is the market’s payoff and cost structure. Traders enter only through the aggregate state \(\s\); the guarantees quantify over beliefs and events without weighting them by how many traders hold them. Adding a distribution over trader types therefore supplies no population-dependent quantity to this question. Making type masses affect the objective would require a new objective, such as expected welfare over types, which the paper does not formulate.

Theorem 3 admits a more tempting scenario: many traders with repeated or similar information trade while the market’s utility for information declines. But the result’s update depends on the current state, the LCMM constraints, and the schedules \(\beta_g\). Its guarantees already cover arbitrary trade histories. The unknown number of trades is not a population-size parameter: traders may submit multiple trades, and the analysis attaches no budgets, capacities, or behavior to named individuals. A continuum of trader types could be used to specify demand or equilibrium behavior, but the paper supplies no demand model for that calculation. It would create a different market-dynamics problem rather than continue the result about preserving prices and reducing information utility.

Theorems 12 and 13 are no firmer anchors. Their loss bounds take a supremum over outcomes and trades, and, for gradual decrease, over arbitrarily long trade sequences. Splitting a trade among many traders, or grouping traders who submit the same trades, leaves the cumulative market state and the bound unchanged. A population-weighted loss or per-capita trading constraint could make masses matter, but those additions change what is being optimized and what trades are allowed.

Many traders of similar types are entirely plausible in prediction markets, so I would not base the negative case on a claim that multiplicity is unrealistic. The stronger point is that the paper’s results have no population-sensitive computational question for that plausible regime: trader identities and counts are absent from their statements, while the state aggregates trades exactly. A new population model could be interesting, but it would need its own demand, constraints, or welfare objective before there is a computational mirror to study. I cannot rule out every such extension; the case is strongest for the paper as written, where none of the three cited result families gives the continuization programme a population-complexity problem.
