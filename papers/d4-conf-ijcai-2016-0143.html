The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Section 5 reports empirical EGTA findings, but those are simulation results for a particular finite model, not named complexity results. For example, it reports different equilibrium strategy weights for informed traders under single-entry and multi-entry settings. I can’t turn that into a numbered anchor without inventing one, so there is no anchor-indexed continuous problem to hand to a prover.

The strongest unanchored candidate would be a continuum version of the paper’s equilibrium analysis. Its instance would give a distribution \(\mu\) over trader classes, where a class specifies information-generation process, budget, and entry mode; an instance would also specify the ground-truth process, trading horizon, and LMSR parameters. With market depth and aggregate budgets scaled with population mass, each class chooses the paper’s weighted-belief strategy parameter \(\theta\), or a distribution over such choices. The problem would be to compute an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium and its induced price path, trader payoffs, market-maker payoff, and price-discovery error. A plausible regime is a large prediction market with many traders in a small number of information and budget classes.

That candidate is recognizably related to the paper’s question, but its weakest point is substantial: the reported findings come from a 42-player game, a restricted strategy set, and a particular simulation procedure. In an atomless market each trader’s price impact vanishes, which changes the strategic interaction those findings measure. So this is a possible research question, not a result-backed positive case; I can’t honestly assign it a complexity class from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named result to anchor a continuous mirror to: the paper states no theorem, lemma, or corollary about computational complexity or algorithms. Its equilibrium findings are empirical results from a particular simulation pipeline: 42 strategic traders, 14 noise traders, a restricted strategy set, and a role reduction chosen to make the game manageable. So there is no computational result here whose continuous counterpart could sharpen or overturn a published bound.

A large market with many traders in a few information and budget classes is plausible, and the proponent’s candidate is the strongest version of a mirror. One could scale budgets and LMSR liquidity with population size, represent each class’s private-signal process in the type distribution, and ask for an approximate equilibrium and its price-discovery error. The difficulty is that this constructs a new game rather than taking a high-multiplicity version of the game the paper analyzed. In an atomless population, one trader’s price impact disappears; retaining nontrivial aggregate price movement requires choosing new scaling assumptions. The resulting target is a dynamic mean-field equilibrium problem, whereas the paper’s EGTA computed equilibria of a finite, role-symmetric game with strategic price impact. A convergence or error-bound question between those models might be useful, but it is a new result to pursue, not a mirror of a result in this paper.

Making that candidate into a computational-complexity problem would require another choice: for example, asking for the complexity of computing an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium, or of choosing liquidity and spread to optimize price discovery. The paper motivates such questions, but neither formulates them nor supplies a named computational result to carry over. The continuous population is sensible; the case that this paper contains a worthwhile continuous *mirror* is therefore weak.
