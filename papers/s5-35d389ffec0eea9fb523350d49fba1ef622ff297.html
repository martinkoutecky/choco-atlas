The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) binary policy choices and a distribution \(\mu\) over the supported three-variable parity-approval types \(t=(i_1,i_2,i_3,b)\), each with binary-rational mass, maximize \(V_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[x_{i_1}+x_{i_2}+x_{i_3}=b\pmod 2]\). For each fixed \(\delta>0\), is it NP-hard to find an assignment with \(V_\mu(x)\ge\tfrac12+\delta\) whenever \(\max_x V_\mu(x)\ge1-\delta\)?

The model it lives in

Each type is a stakeholder’s complete parity requirement over the shared binary policy choices, and its mass is the fraction of the population holding that requirement. The decision is a binary assignment \(x\); its approval score is \(V_\mu(x)\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror I see is a continuous high-multiplicity version of the paper’s Max-3Lin problem, interpreted as collective approval of a binary policy. It is a focused case: it mirrors one computational result, while leaving the paper’s analytic results about noise stability and Gaussian geometry outside the claim.

My anchor is Theorem 2.3, which the survey cites to Håstad [Has97, Has01] rather than proving itself. The theorem says that for every \(\delta>0\), it is NP-hard to \((\frac12+\delta,1-\delta)\)-approximate Max-3Lin. In the paper’s approximation convention, this means that it is NP-hard to guarantee an assignment satisfying at least a \(\frac12+\delta\) fraction of the equations on every instance whose optimum satisfies at least a \(1-\delta\) fraction.

Here is the mirror. There are \(n\) binary policy choices, \(x\in\{0,1\}^n\). A voter type is an equation \(t=(i_1,i_2,i_3,b)\) of the form
\[ x_{i_1}+x_{i_2}+x_{i_3}=b \pmod 2. \]
That type approves exactly the policies satisfying its equation. An instance is a distribution \(\mu\) over the possible equation types, given by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and their binary-rational masses, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The decision is a single policy \(x\); its approval score is
\[ V_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[x_{i_1}+x_{i_2}+x_{i_3}=b\pmod 2\right]. \]
Call the problem Continuous Max-3Lin Approval: find \(x\) maximizing \(V_\mu(x)\). Its approximation version asks, for fixed \(\delta>0\), for an \(x\) with \(V_\mu(x)\ge\frac12+\delta\) whenever \(\max_x V_\mu(x)\ge1-\delta\). A solution is the binary policy assignment itself.

A plausible regime is a large organization, public program, or shared technical deployment with many stakeholders and a common set of \(n\) binary choices. Each stakeholder submits a standardized parity requirement over three choices. The type is the entire requirement, including its variables and right-hand side, so people of the same type are indistinguishable for this problem. If there are \(N\) stakeholders but only \(\tau\) distinct requirements, then \(\mu_t\) is the fraction holding requirement \(t\); in the regime of interest, \(N\) can be much larger than \(\tau\). The setting is specialized, but the population interpretation preserves the underlying optimization exactly: equation types are approval ballots, and the winning policy maximizes the population’s satisfied mass.

I expect hardness to transfer. Given any discrete Max-3Lin instance with \(M\) equations, set \(\mu_t\) to the number of occurrences of equation \(t\), divided by \(M\). Every assignment then has exactly the same objective value as in the original instance. The construction has at most \(M\) support types and polynomial-size binary rational masses. Thus the continuous approximation question includes the instances covered by Theorem 2.3. Its hardness comes from choosing among \(n\) Boolean decisions, not from the number of people. That makes this a useful Class B mirror, even though it does not claim that continuization makes the problem tractable.

I think the authors and a referee could recognize this as their problem’s continuous-population analogue because the objective and feasible decisions remain unchanged; only the frequencies of repeated equation types become masses. The key co-modification is that a voter’s type is an approval constraint rather than a ranking. The paper presents Max-3Lin as a CSP, not as a voting problem, so this is an approval-based collective-choice interpretation of its underlying optimization. The strongest objection is that parity approval ballots may feel tailored to the CSP rather than like a familiar electorate. That limits the scenario’s breadth, but it does not weaken the exact correspondence to the named result.

This mirror covers Theorem 2.3 alone. It leaves open whether useful tractable regimes arise when the support or incidence structure is restricted, whether the problem admits parameterized algorithms in \(\tau\) or structural parameters, and how its approximation landscape changes under such restrictions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 2.3, and it is a computational result: NP-hardness of approximating Max-3Lin. I can’t defeat it by saying the hardness survives the change of representation; the programme explicitly counts a useful hardness transfer as a valid mirror.

The strongest objection is about whether the proposed society is real enough to be the authors’ problem. In the paper, an equation is a constraint, not an agent with preferences. The mirror turns every equation into a voter, every satisfying assignment into an approved policy, and the fraction of satisfied equations into approval score. That is an exact mathematical encoding, but its social-choice interpretation is specialized: voters approve policies according to parity constraints. A large technical organization whose stakeholders share requirements about configurations could make this plausible. Without such a setting, the approval-voting language risks being a label placed on a CSP rather than a population model the authors would recognize.

That objection survives the stronger version too. Let voters have complete dichotomous preferences over binary policy assignments, with each type approving exactly the assignments satisfying one parity equation, and let their binary-rational type masses define the society. This is a coherent continuous approval problem, and it mirrors Theorem 2.3 exactly. But once that policy-choice setting is accepted, the objection collapses: equation types are complete descriptions for the problem, the mass objective is the voters’ approval score, and the discrete instances embed as empirical distributions. The fact that the resulting problem is weighted Max-3Lin, or that its hardness transfers, is not a valid reason to reject the mirror under the programme’s criteria.

So I can make a case for caution at the author-recognition gate, but not an honest universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The anchor survives if a parity-based policy setting is accepted as a plausible population regime.
