The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), an addable pool \(D\), a target \(p\in C\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) ranking types on \(C\cup D\), where each type has positive binary-rational mass and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), does some subset \(D'\subseteq D\) make \(p\) a Copeland\(_\alpha\) winner in \(C\cup D'\)? For each pair \(x,y\), the type masses preferring each candidate determine whether the pairwise result is a win, loss, or tie; a tie awards \(\alpha\) points to each candidate.

The model it lives in

Each type is a complete ranking, and \(\mu_t\) is its population fraction. The decision variable is the integral subset \(D'\subseteq D\); the question is whether \(p\)'s Copeland\(_\alpha\) score is maximal after adding it. Describe complexity in \(m=|C|+|D|\), \(\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.7's constructive control by adding an unlimited number of candidates for fixed rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\); it leaves the paper's other control, bribery, and parameterized results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the parameterized complexity in \(\tau\) when \(m\) varies?
  • What is the complexity of minimizing \(|D'|\) subject to making \(p\) a winner?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with Theorem 3.7, proved in this paper by a reduction from Vertex Cover. It says that for every fixed rational \(\alpha\) with \(0<\alpha<1\), constructive control by adding an unlimited number of candidates for Copeland\(_\alpha\) is resistant: the chair can sometimes make the preferred candidate win, and deciding whether they can is NP-hard. The paper’s result covers both unique-winner and nonunique-winner models, and both rational and irrational voter representations. I would use the ordinary Copeland case, \(\alpha=\tfrac12\), as the clearest lead.

Call the mirror Continuous Copeland-CCACu. Fix \(\alpha\in\mathbb Q\cap(0,1)\). An instance has an initial candidate set \(C\), a finite pool \(D\) of candidates who may be added, a preferred candidate \(p\in C\), and a list of \(\tau\) ranking types over \(C\cup D\). Each type \(t\) has positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For candidates \(x,y\), let
\[ w_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:\,x\succ_t y}\mu_t. \]
In any selected candidate set \(S\), \(x\) gets one point against \(y\) if \(w_\mu(x,y)>\tfrac12\), zero if \(w_\mu(x,y)<\tfrac12\), and \(\alpha\) if \(w_\mu(x,y)=\tfrac12\). The question is whether there is a subset \(D'\subseteq D\) for which \(p\) has maximum Copeland score in \(C\cup D'\). A solution is such a subset \(D'\); as in the paper’s unlimited-control problem, there is no bound on how many candidates may be added. The natural input parameters are \(m=|C|+|D|\), \(\tau\), and the total mass bit length \(L\).

A plausible regime is a large ranked-ballot election in which an agenda setter can admit candidates from a proposed slate. Millions of voters may be distributed over hundreds or thousands of shared, complete rankings, so \(N\), the electorate size, is much larger than \(\tau\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the electorate with ranking \(t\). Voters of the same type are indistinguishable for this problem. This is a standard high-multiplicity scenario, and the paper itself treats succinct multiplicities in its bounded-case results. The authors should recognize the mirror: it retains their Copeland pairwise comparisons, their chair’s candidate-addition action, and their winner condition, while making the population a distribution over voter types.

I expect Class B: hardness transfers. Given a finite election with \(n_t\) voters of each ranking type and \(N\) voters total, set \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). For every candidate subset considered, the pairwise majority outcomes, Copeland scores, and winner status are unchanged by this normalization. Thus the discrete instance is an instance of the continuous question with exactly the same successful candidate subsets; Theorem 3.7’s hardness carries over. A proposed subset is also a polynomial-size certificate, and its scores can be computed with rational arithmetic, so the fixed-\(\alpha\) problem is in NP as well. This mirror therefore gives an NP-complete high-multiplicity formulation for each fixed rational \(\alpha\in(0,1)\).

The mirror covers Theorem 3.7’s CCACu result only. It does not claim to continuize bribery, voter control, partition control, or the rest of the paper. Useful questions it raises include whether the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in the support size \(\tau\) when \(m\) varies, and what the complexity and approximation landscape looks like for the related objective of minimizing \(|D'|\).

The weak point is that this anchor does not give a fractional action: the chair still chooses an integral subset of candidates. Further, every rational distribution can be represented by a finite electorate after choosing a common denominator. So this is chiefly a scale-free, high-multiplicity formulation, and the source of hardness remains the candidate-selection combinatorics. That limits the mirror’s novelty. Its fit remains strong under the programme’s stated scope, which continuizes the population and treats high multiplicity as supporting evidence for a sensible regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot honestly defeat Theorem 3.7 as a continuous mirror. It is a named computational result, and a large electorate with many voters sharing a relatively small set of rankings is a sensible high multiplicity regime. Voters are anonymous in the problem, so replacing counts by type masses loses no identity information the result needs.

The strongest negative case is that Copeland-CCACu barely uses the population as a continuous object. For each pair \(x,y\), the outcome depends only on whether \(\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t\) is above, below, or equal to \(1/2\). The decision is therefore constant within regions of the mass simplex cut out by those majority thresholds, and the control action remains choosing an integral subset of candidates. The reduction’s combinatorics live in those candidate choices. A better mirror, with binary rational masses and complexity measured in \(m,\tau,L\), does not remove this feature.

That is a limitation on the mirror’s novelty, but it does not defeat it under ChoCo’s stated standard. The question still concerns a society given as a distribution over voter types; the fact that the result may be hardness-preserving is not grounds against asking it. Converting rational masses to integer counts may require a very large explicit electorate, which is precisely why the high multiplicity formulation can matter. Existing high multiplicity work supports the regime rather than showing this computational question has already been answered continuously.

So the adverse case is weak: it can argue that this anchor offers little opening for continuous optimization technology and may yield a straightforward transfer of the known hardness. It cannot establish that the mirror is ill-posed, irrelevant to a plausible population regime, or already answered in the continuous-population model. The anchor survives.
