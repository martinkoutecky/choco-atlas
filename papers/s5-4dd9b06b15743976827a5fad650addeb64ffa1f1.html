The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of attributes with finite domains and a support of \(\tau\) complete agent types, each specified by a GAI utility \(u_t\) on a shared cardinal scale and a binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), find a complete assignment \(o^\star\) maximizing \(U_\mu(o)=\sum_t \mu_t u_t(o)\).

The model it lives in

A continuous population is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete GAI utility types; each type has mass \(\mu_t\), and the decision remains a whole assignment from the finite combinatorial domain. The objective is the mass-weighted GAI utility \(U_\mu(o)=\sum_t\mu_tu_t(o)\), with utilities on a common cardinal scale.

The objection that survived

The paper’s GAI utilities represent ordinal preferences, so averaging them across types can change under positive rescaling; a shared cardinal scale is an additional assumption not supplied by the paper.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the undominated-extraction hardness in Proposition 18. It leaves the proposition’s cut-extraction results and the paper’s other queries, transformations, expressiveness results, and compilation map outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does complexity depend on \(\tau\), GAI scope size, and interaction-graph treewidth, and which structural restrictions permit efficient optimization?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is to the paper’s GAI optimization result, Proposition 18. It says that for \(GAI_k\) with \(k\ge2\), and for GAI generally, both \(\succeq\)-cut extraction and \(\succ\)-cut extraction are NP-complete; undominated checking is coNP-complete; and undominated extraction is NP-hard. The authors prove these claims here by reduction from 3-Coloring. Their construction uses one attribute per graph vertex, with domain \(\{r,g,b,d\}\), and a pairwise utility for each edge. For the undominated-extraction reduction, the score makes any undominated assignment either the default all-\(d\) assignment or a valid 3-coloring, so extracting one decides whether the graph is 3-colorable. I would mirror specifically the undominated-extraction part of this proposition. ([Proposition 18 and its proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.04107))

The continuous problem is Continuous GAI Population Optimum. An instance has a finite set of decision attributes with finite domains, and a support of \(\tau\) complete agent types. Each type \(t\) is a GAI utility over the whole combinatorial decision space, represented by its scopes and rational utility tables; its mass \(\mu_t\) is a binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The social value of a complete assignment \(o\) is \(U_\mu(o)=\sum_t\mu_t u_t(o)\). The task is to return any \(o^\star\in\arg\max_o U_\mu(o)\). Since this aggregate utility defines a complete preorder, its maximizers are exactly its undominated alternatives. The decision variable is the selected whole assignment; bundles and outcomes remain discrete.

A plausible regime is a large public-service or infrastructure planning body choosing a joint plan across many linked projects. Many thousands of stakeholders may evaluate plans through a small set of standardized preference profiles, with pairwise GAI terms capturing interactions between linked choices. The type includes the person’s full preference representation, so repeated agents are genuinely indistinguishable for the problem. One can have \(N\) in the hundreds of thousands and \(\tau\) in the dozens; the hardness witness already sits at \(\tau=1\), with an arbitrarily large cohort sharing one type.

That one-type slice is the main reason I believe the mirror holds. Set \(\mu_t=1\) for a type whose utility is the paper’s hard GAI instance. The population objective is then exactly the GAI utility being optimized in Proposition 18. Replicating that type any number of times leaves the answer unchanged, and the reduction’s combinatorics remain in the growing attribute set and its pairwise interactions. I therefore expect hardness to transfer: the continuous problem is NP-hard even with one supported type and pairwise factors. This is not an algorithmic result about population multiplicity dissolving hardness; it marks a case where it does not.

The authors should recognize this as their undominated-alternative problem applied to a utilitarian aggregate that is still a GAI. The mirror covers that optimization query, not the paper’s other queries or its compilation map as a whole. It generates natural follow-up questions about the role of the factor graph’s treewidth, approximation guarantees for aggregate welfare, and how complexity changes when \(\tau\), arity, and interaction structure are parameterized.

The soft spot is that the paper studies preferences of a single decision maker, not population aggregation. The continuous model adds a common cardinal welfare interpretation for the GAI tables, so the authors might regard the population wrapper as an application rather than their own central problem. The case survives because the stated task preserves their underlying GAI optimization query exactly, including the hard one-type instances.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The GAI optimization result is a real computational anchor, but the proposed mirror needs a social objective that the paper does not supply. Its GAI functions represent one decision maker’s ordinal preferences. Replacing \(u_t\) by any strictly increasing transformation preserves that type’s preference, yet can change the maximizer of \(U_\mu(o)=\sum_t\mu_tu_t(o)\). So a distribution over the paper’s preference types does not, by itself, determine the proposed collective choice. The “common cardinal welfare” assumption repairs that problem only by adding a substantive social welfare model absent from the paper.

That repair is the strongest version of the mirror: stakeholders choose among shared plans, their GAI utilities use a common scale, and the society maximizes total welfare. But then \(U_\mu\) is itself a GAI: for each scope \(Z\), its factor is \(\tilde g_Z=\sum_t\mu_tg_{t,Z}\). Forming those factors reduces the task to the paper’s existing GAI optimization query. The one-type case is exactly its hardness result; multiple types add weighted aggregation, not a new population-specific query. A rank-based aggregation could avoid the cardinal-scale assumption, but it would change the objective and no longer mirror the paper’s undominated-extraction result.

That leaves a coherent application of the paper’s GAI machinery, but a weak case that this paper contains a worthwhile continuous mirror: the population interpretation comes from adding a collective welfare rule, while the computation collapses to the existing single-preference problem. The negative case is not airtight, since the common-scale planning scenario is plausible and the authors might welcome it as an application.
