Why no mirror

Theorems 1 and 2 rule out fairness guarantees on particular instances; neither says that computing an allocation is hard or gives an algorithmic guarantee. The proposed high-multiplicity feasibility problem is coherent, but it cannot supply the missing computational character required for a green grade.

What the mirror covers

The proposed extension uses the fixed three-type, six-category profiles from Theorems 1 and 2 to ask whether their obstructions persist with replicated supply; it does not turn the paper's impossibility proofs into complexity results.

Open questions for a prover

  • For the fixed profile from Theorem 1 with \(\mu_i=q_e=1/3\), does the no-α-EFX obstruction persist for every \(\alpha<2^{1/3}\) as a mass distribution over bundles?
  • What is the complexity of feasibility for general explicitly represented type costs and rational masses and supplies, and how does it depend on \(|T|\) and \(|M|\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity EFX feasibility problem. There is an important limit to that case: this paper has no named result about running-time complexity—nothing asserting NP-hardness, polynomial-time solvability, or a parameterized bound. Its named theorems are allocation-guarantee impossibility results. They can motivate a continuous question, but they do not establish its complexity.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for three agents and six chores there are normalized, monotone subadditive cost functions for which no \(\alpha\)-EFX allocation exists for any \(1\leq\alpha<2^{1/3}\). The construction compresses a four-level ordinal obstruction into costs \(1/2, 2^{-2/3}, 2^{-1/3}, 1\). This is a genuine guarantee-impossibility result, not a claim that finding an allocation is computationally hard.

A second, narrower anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. It says that there is a three-agent, six-chore instance with monotone weighted-coverage costs for which no \(\alpha\)-EFX allocation exists for any \(1\leq\alpha<20/19\). The paper proves this using a weighted-coverage realization of the same ordinal obstruction. That is useful support for the mirror because weighted coverage gives the costs a familiar structure, though its inapproximability factor is weaker.

Here is the continuous problem I would ask for Theorem 1, and treat as the lead. Call it *High-Multiplicity Subadditive \(\alpha\)-EFX Feasibility*. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of agent types, a finite set \(M\) of chore categories, a normalized monotone subadditive cost function \(c_t:2^M\to\mathbb Q_{\geq0}\) for each type \(t\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and rational per-capita supplies \(q_e\in[0,1]\) for each chore category \(e\). A person receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\), with at most one chore of each category; only the share of people receiving each bundle is continuous.

A solution is a mass distribution \(x_{t,S}\geq0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni e}x_{t,S}=q_e \quad\text{for every }e\in M. \]
It must also satisfy EFX across every pair of bundles used by positive mass: whenever \(x_{t,S}>0\), \(x_{u,R}>0\), and \(e\in S\),
\[ c_t(S\setminus\{e\})\leq \alpha c_t(R). \]
Given the instance and \(\alpha\), the question is whether such a distribution exists; a witness is the distribution \(x\). This preserves the paper’s comparison between one person’s integral bundle and another’s. It makes the population continuous, while allowing chore supply to scale with it.

The paper’s six-chore construction gives a concrete instance to study: take its three cost types with \(\mu_i=1/3\), and set \(q_e=1/3\) for each of the six categories. At population size \(N\) divisible by three, this describes \(N/3\) people of each type and \(N/3\) copies of each chore category. At \(N=3\), it is the original instance. The new question is whether its obstruction survives as the population and chore supply grow, or whether distributing mass across more people restores \(\alpha\)-EFX. Theorem 1 does not answer that question.

For Theorem 2, the corresponding problem is the same feasibility question restricted to weighted-coverage cost functions. Its concrete instance uses the paper’s three weighted-coverage cost types, again with \(\mu_i=q_e=1/3\), and asks whether the mass distribution is \(\alpha\)-EFX below \(20/19\). Keeping this as a separate, narrower question tests whether the obstruction survives in that cost class; Theorem 2 does not establish that it survives replication either.

A plausible regime is a large workforce receiving recurring assignments from a small set of chore categories. For example, millions of workers might fall into three complete workload-cost types, with repeated task slots in six categories allocated at a fixed per-capita rate. The number of people can be large while the number of cost types and chore categories stays small. That makes high multiplicity credible as an instance regime; it does not require treating named individuals with different costs as interchangeable.

I expect the fixed three-type, six-category feasibility question to be tractable: there are only finitely many integral bundles, and one can enumerate candidate bundle supports and check mass feasibility. For the general version, I would tentatively expect continuum-specific hardness from choosing compatible supports as the number of types and categories grows. Neither expectation follows from the paper. Its theorems give no runtime classification, and their three-agent nonexistence examples are not hardness reductions.

The main weakness is that EFX is sensitive to indivisible bundles, and the paper’s gap may disappear when each chore category has many copies. The authors could reasonably recognize this as a high-multiplicity extension of their allocation problem, since it retains complete cost types, integral individual bundles, and EFX comparisons. They could also object that the replicated-supply question is not what their fixed three-agent theorems prove. So this is a concrete, plausible mirror to investigate—not a continuous consequence of the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to continue. Its “inapproximability” theorems say that certain instances admit no allocation meeting a fairness guarantee; they do not show that finding an allocation is hard, or give an algorithm for doing so. That distinction matters for ChoCo: the paper studies which fairness guarantees are possible, not the computational landscape of a named problem.

Theorem 1 is the strongest proposed anchor, but its faithful high-multiplicity version remains a small fixed instance: three cost types, six chore categories, and 64 possible bundles per type. One could enumerate compatible supports and check the resulting mass constraints. That gives a concrete extension of the existence question, but no substantial complexity landscape. To obtain one, the mirror must let the number of types or categories grow. Then it becomes a broader computational problem about EFX allocation; this paper’s three-agent impossibility result does not anchor its complexity.

Theorem 2 does not provide a separate route around that problem. It realizes the same ordinal obstruction with weighted-coverage costs and a different approximation gap. Repeating the types and chore categories gives a plausible high-multiplicity weighted-coverage feasibility problem, but the theorem still says nothing about its computational difficulty. Allowing the instance dimensions to grow again turns it into a broader problem not settled by this paper.

The proposed model is coherent: a large workforce with recurring task categories and a few complete cost types is a plausible regime. So I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists. The negative case is narrower: these results motivate a new high-multiplicity EFX feasibility problem, but they do not establish a computational question whose continuous mirror the paper itself answers or classifies. That is a real gap in the case for this paper as a ChoCo computational anchor, though it does not rule out the mirror as a worthwhile follow-on project.
