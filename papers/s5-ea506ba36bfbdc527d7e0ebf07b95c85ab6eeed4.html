The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of acyclic more-or-less CP-net types \(N_t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and outcomes \(o,o'\), compute \(D_\mu(o,o')=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[N_t\models o'\succ o]\), or decide whether \(D_\mu(o,o')\ge q\). Determine the complexity when every supported type has a tree structure.

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution over complete individual CP-net preference types. Each supported type carries rational mass; the decision is the share of types whose network entails the same strict dominance comparison. Apply the paper's critical-flipping algorithm to each type and sum the weighted answers.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's dominance-testing result for a distribution over more-or-less CP-net types; it leaves outcome optimization, ordering queries, and learning CP-nets aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the support be represented implicitly while retaining efficient computation of the weighted dominance mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Population Dominance Mass. Consider a large service whose users compare configurations such as meeting times and locations. The service records each user’s conditional preferences as an acyclic more-or-less CP-net over the same variables and domains. A user’s type is their complete CP-net, including its graph and conditional preference tables; users with identical networks are one type. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) supported types, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of users of type \(t\). A plausible regime is millions of users but dozens or hundreds of recurring preference templates.

Given that distribution and two outcomes \(o,o'\), ask for the exact mass
\[ D_\mu(o,o')=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[N_t \models o' \succ o]. \]
Here \(N_t \models o' \succ o\) means that \(o'\) is strictly preferred to \(o\) under every preference ranking consistent with type \(t\)’s CP-net. The answer is a rational number; the associated decision question is whether \(D_\mu(o,o')\ge q\), for a supplied threshold \(q\). The outcomes remain assignments to discrete attributes: the continuous object is the population of users.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that if dominance testing for binary CP-nets of a given graph structure is in complexity class \(C\), then dominance testing for more-or-less CP-nets of the same graph structure is also in \(C\). The structures the paper names are trees, singly connected graphs, and multiply connected graphs. For the tree case, the paper cites the \(O(n^2)\) binary algorithm of Boutilier et al. [5]; its critical-flipping construction preserves that complexity class for more-or-less CP-nets, with only \(O(n)\) additional work in the described algorithm. The tree-restricted population problem therefore takes polynomial time by answering the original dominance query once per supported type and summing the weighted answers. Its running time is polynomial in the encoded networks, \(\tau\), and the mass bit lengths.

This is a reasonable population-level extension of the paper’s problem: it asks how much of a user base can certify one configuration as better than another using precisely the dominance relation the authors study. A finite census with \(n_t\) users of each type gives \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), so the mass has the direct interpretation of population share and connects to a high-multiplicity representation. The result is about a common practical question for a platform or service: what fraction of users have this robust preference, given their conditional preference models?

The scope is deliberately narrow. This mirrors the paper’s dominance result, not its outcome-optimization or ordering tasks, and it does not turn outcomes or time into continuous objects. Its expected direction is tractability for tree-structured types. It also suggests threshold queries for a population share, comparisons across outcome pairs, and the question of whether the same method can handle a distribution that is represented compactly rather than by an explicit support list. The paper gives no named hardness theorem that would anchor a continuum-specific hardness claim.

The weak point is that the population layer aggregates many instances of the authors’ query; it does not make a single dominance query easier. The paper studies one CP-net, not collective preference analysis, and a referee could regard “the share of users whose networks entail dominance” as an application of its algorithm rather than its central problem. I think the mirror is still defensible because the types are complete agent preferences and the output is a genuine population statistic, while the query inside each type remains exactly the paper’s dominance question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a genuine computational result, so the strongest objection is not that the paper lacks an anchor. Nor can I rule out a high-multiplicity regime: a service could have many users sharing a finite set of CP-net templates.

The concern is that the proposed mirror changes the object being studied. In this paper, a CP-net describes one decision maker’s conditional preferences over outcomes; Theorem 1 concerns dominance search *inside that network*. Its nodes are attributes, not people. The quantity \(D_\mu(o,o')\) instead asks what share of a population’s separately modeled users robustly endorse one comparison. That may be useful, but it is a new population audit built around the theorem’s query, rather than a population version of the query itself.

The stronger rescue would make the audit drive a collective choice: define a relation by the share of users whose CP-nets entail each comparison, then ask for a majority-dominant or Condorcet outcome. That is a plausible group decision problem. But it requires adding an aggregation rule and deciding what to do with the mass whose CP-nets leave a comparison unresolved. Those choices supply the social-choice question; the paper supplies an oracle for evaluating each user’s preference. The result may be worthwhile, but its case rests on extending the paper into collective preference aggregation, not on a population axis latent in Theorem 1.

This is a limited negative case, not a decisive defeat. The “robustly prefers” share has a clear interpretation, and the proponent’s tree-restricted version is well-posed. I cannot honestly argue that no plausible scenario makes it worthwhile; the stronger claim is only that this is a less direct and less compelling continuization target than the case for suggests.
