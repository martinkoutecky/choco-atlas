The continuous mirror question

Given finite type sets \(L\) and \(R\), rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) and \(\mu_j>0\) with \(\sum_{i\in L}\mu_i=\sum_{j\in R}\mu_j=1\), a compatibility relation, and a strict partial order for each type over compatible types on the other side, choose rational matched masses \(x_{ij}\ge 0\). Require \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\mu_j\). For each compatible pair \((i,j)\), define \(D_i(j;x)=\mu_i-\sum_{k:\neg(j\succ_i k)}x_{ik}\), and define \(D_j(i;x)\) symmetrically. Require \(D_i(j;x)=0\) or \(D_j(i;x)=0\) for every compatible pair, then maximize \(\sum_{(i,j)}x_{ij}\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic two-sided placement market in which each type specifies a complete compatibility and preference profile, \(x_{ij}\) is matched mass, type masses bound the assignments, and positive willing mass on both sides of a type pair is forbidden.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s maximum stable marriage with partial orders. It keeps the paper’s one-to-one matching objective and its strict-preference definition of blocking, while making the population the continuous object.

Let \(L\) and \(R\) be finite sets of types on the two sides, with positive rational masses \(\mu_v\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(v\) specifies its compatibility neighborhood and a strict partial order \(\succ_v\) over compatible types on the other side. Agents of a type share that entire profile. For every compatible pair \((i,j)\), choose a matched mass \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), subject to \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\mu_j\).

Define the mass of type \(i\) that would strictly prefer type \(j\) to its current assignment by
\[ D_i(j;x)=\mu_i-\sum_{k:\,\neg(j\succ_i k)}x_{ik}. \]
This counts unmatched mass and mass assigned to partners strictly worse than \(j\); an incomparable current partner does not count as worse. The flow is stable when, for every compatible pair, \(D_i(j;x)=0\) or \(D_j(i;x)=0\). Otherwise positive masses on both sides want to match across that pair, so some positive mass can block. The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{(i,j)}x_{ij}\). A solution is a rational flow satisfying the capacity and stability conditions.

This gives two precise questions, one for each anchor. Their common input size is naturally measured in the number of supported types \(\tau=|L|+|R|\), the number of compatible type pairs, and the bit length \(L\) of the rational masses.

Lead: \(\mathrm{CMSM\text{-}PO}\). Use arbitrary strict partial orders on the type neighborhoods. This mirrors the maximum stable marriage problem with partial orders.

Theorem 3, proved in this paper in Section 4.2 using a hardness result of Bansal and Khot [5], says that assuming UGC, for every \(\delta>0\), it is NP-hard to approximate max-smpo within a factor of \(2-\delta\).

Restricted question: \(\mathrm{CMSM\text{-}IO}\). Use the same instance and solution, but require each type’s partial order to be an interval order. This mirrors the stable-marriage subcase of the paper’s interval-order result.

Proposition 2, derived in the paper from the cited Askalidis et al. result [2], says that assuming UGC, for every \(\delta>0\), it is NP-hard to approximate max-kernel-po with interval orders within \(3/2-\delta\); the same hardness already holds for maximum stable marriage with interval orders.

A plausible regime is a large centralized placement market: many applicants and individual positions, grouped by qualifications, location, and shared preference or priority profiles. The mass of a type records its fraction of the market. Positions can be treated as individual agents, so each unit of mass on either side receives at most one match. In such a market, \(N\) actual agents can greatly exceed \(\tau\), while the compatibility graph and preference orders across types retain the combinatorial structure. The model does make assignments divisible at the type level; that is the natural co-modification needed to describe a continuum population.

There is also a useful high-multiplicity bridge. Given rational \(\mu\) and a rational flow \(x\), clear their denominators by some common multiplier \(q\). Make \(q\mu_v\) identical agents of each type, connect all compatible type classes, and realize \(qx_{ij}\) matches between each pair of classes. Under the mass-blocking condition above, this is stable exactly when the resulting clone matching has no blocking pair. Thus rational mass solutions correspond to stable matchings in suitable finite clone markets. This makes the mirror recognizable as the paper’s problem on replicated profiles, rather than a change to outcome-space continuity.

My initial expectation for both questions is Class B, hardness transfers: the hard structure appears to lie in compatibility and preference patterns among types, rather than in the number of individual agents. That is a prediction, not a consequence of either theorem. The paper’s results concern finite individual-agent matchings and do not establish that their approximation gaps survive the mass-flow version. For \(\mathrm{CMSM\text{-}PO}\), the immediate follow-up is whether the UGC gap can be preserved against rational flows, or whether an integrality or rounding result relates an optimal mass flow to a discrete stable matching. For \(\mathrm{CMSM\text{-}IO}\), the corresponding question is whether mass splitting retains the \(3/2\) barrier or permits a better approximation.

The soft spot is exactly that transfer. The paper’s hardness reduction uses individual vertices and edges; putting several agents into a type requires them to share a compatibility profile, which makes the type-to-type connections complete. A fractional stable flow may then split assignments in ways unavailable to the original matching and defeat the reduction’s soundness. The mirror itself remains a precise, plausible continuous problem, but its hardness classification needs new work.

This case covers Theorem 3 and the stable-marriage subcase of Proposition 2. It does not claim to extend the paper’s matroid-kernel algorithm or its interval-order approximation guarantee.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that neither hardness anchor gives evidence about a genuinely high-multiplicity regime. Theorem 3’s reduction encodes an arbitrary graph’s adjacency and vertex-specific preference structure in the agents. To preserve that instance exactly, the type description must include each agent’s compatibility neighborhood and preferences; in the hard instances, that can leave essentially one type per agent. Then \(\tau\) grows with the original instance, so the result offers no reason to expect a useful compression. Replicating those types instead creates complete connections between type classes and allows assignments to split. That is a sensible new flow problem, but the reduction’s soundness no longer follows.

The same weakness applies to Proposition 2’s stable-marriage case. Its hardness comes through socially stable matching, where the acquaintance pattern and preferences are encoded in local neighborhoods. Preserving those details can again require many distinct types. A market with repeated applicant and job profiles is plausible, but it restricts the compatibility structure that drives the hardness result. The paper’s \(3/2\) lower bound therefore does not establish a barrier—or even a prediction—for that mass-flow problem.

There is a further novelty check, though not a decisive objection: computational work already studies fractional stable matching and optimization objectives in marriage markets, including the number of fully matched agents. That literature does not settle this proposed objective or the paper’s interval-order and arbitrary-poset domains, but it means “allow fractional assignments” alone is not a new research direction. [Chen, Roy, and Sorge, *Fractional Matchings under Preferences: Stability and Optimality*](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2021/13).

Still, I cannot honestly turn these points into a universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The paper has clear computational results, and a large market with repeated complete agent profiles is a coherent setting for a type-level flow model. The negative case is that the proponent’s anchors do not substantiate its value: their hard instances depend on structure that type compression may remove, while the resulting fractional-stability problem has close prior neighbors. That makes this a weaker ChoCo target than the case for claims, but it does not rule out a worthwhile mirror.
