What the mirror covers

The proposed mass-flow extension concerns the mechanism properties in Theorems 1 and 3; it leaves the empirical efficiency comparisons and the broader characterization of truthful online auctions aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • How should ties and individual assignments within a type mass be defined so that a measure-zero report deviation has a well-defined utility and truthfulness condition?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a large online exchange for standardized jobs, cloud tasks, or short-term labor. Each agent wants to trade one unit and has a side, an arrival period, a departure period, and a value or cost. In a high-volume regime, many agents share those attributes—perhaps after grouping times and values into practical bands—so the society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete types \(q=(r,a,d,w)\). The mass \(\mu_q\) is the fraction of the market with type \(q\). Each person still receives or sells an integral unit; the aggregate matching can use fractional mass.

The nearest named result is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: the price-ranked double auction \(M_{\mathrm{pr}}(f)\) is truthful when its price schedule satisfies (B1)–(B3). In full, the price to an agent must not depend on that agent’s arrival, departure, or reported value; it must not depend on future arrivals; and, while an agent’s price permits it to remain active, that price must be independent of the other currently active bids that also remain affordable, with an unaffordable price staying above the agent’s value under changes to those bids. The proof is given here using lemmas and a price-based characterization cited from Hajiaghayi et al. [9]. Theorem 1, also proved here, adds that \(M_{\mathrm{pr}}(f)\) is feasible and runs at no deficit.

These are useful mechanism-design results, but they are not named computational-complexity results of the kind your anchor rule requires. The paper states no numbered result that places a problem in P, proves it hard, or gives a parameterized complexity classification. Section 5’s offline integer program is computed with CPLEX, but it is an unnumbered empirical benchmark. So I cannot honestly offer Theorem 3 as a qualifying complexity anchor.

The most credible *candidate mirror*, if a mechanism theorem is allowed to motivate the question, is Continuous Price-Ranked Double-Auction Implementation. An instance gives a finite horizon \(H\), patience bound \(K\), a finite support \(Q\) of types \(q=(r,a,d,w)\), rational masses \(\mu_q\), and an efficiently evaluable online price schedule satisfying the continuous versions of (B1)–(B3). The task is to compute an online allocation and payment policy: at each period, specify the mass \(x_{q,q',t}\) matched between active buyer and seller types, along with the per-agent payments. A solution must respect type-mass limits and the paper’s timing, feasibility, and no-deficit conditions, and make truthful reporting a dominant strategy for each representative individual. The paper’s mechanism seeks those properties; it does not maximize welfare, so adding a welfare objective would change the question.

I would expect this implementation problem to be tractable when the schedule has a polynomial-time evaluator: each period has finitely many active types, and matching sorted mass on the two sides can be performed directly. That expectation is an extrapolation, not a result established in the paper. It is plausible as their problem because it preserves the online market, strategic reports, patience windows, and price-based matching. Further questions include whether the discrete mechanism’s guarantees converge to the mass version and whether all truthful, feasible, no-deficit mass mechanisms admit a useful characterization.

The soft spot is substantial: with atomless agents, one person’s report does not change aggregate prices, which may make truthfulness easier than in the paper’s finite-agent setting. And because the paper supplies no qualifying complexity anchor, this is a plausible population mirror, not an anchor-backed case for a continuization result in ChoCo’s complexity programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 proves feasibility and no deficit; Theorem 3 proves truthfulness under conditions on the price schedule. Neither states a running time, complexity classification, or algorithmic bound. Theorem 2 is a characterization cited from prior work. Section 5’s integer program is an empirical benchmark, not a complexity result. So Theorem 3 cannot serve as the anchor the ChoCo comparison requires.

Their proposed implementation is a sensible population model, but it does little computational work. With finite support and a polynomial-time price evaluator, each period can compute prices, sort active buyer and seller types, and match mass until the feasibility and budget conditions bind. A finite horizon gives only polynomially many such operations in the input size. Theorem 1’s guarantees carry over directly to mass matching. For Theorem 3, a single agent’s report has zero effect on aggregate prices in an atomless society. The individual incentive condition can still matter for that agent’s allocation and payment, but the theorem’s distinctive concern about active bids influencing one another’s prices largely disappears.

A stronger follow-up would optimize price schedules for expected surplus under online, truthfulness, feasibility, and no-deficit constraints. The authors’ discussion of tuning and meta-schemes gives that question a plausible connection to their work. It also requires specifying a market distribution, an objective, and a schedule representation. Its computational core would be a new mechanism-optimization problem, rather than a continuous version of a named result in this paper.

I would therefore argue against a ChoCo continuization on the evidence offered: the only named anchor is noncomputational, and the direct mass implementation is essentially execution of an already specified mechanism. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous research question exists is much harder to defend. A high-volume market with repeated complete types is credible here, and schedule optimization could be worthwhile. The negative case is strong at the programme’s computational-anchor gate, but weak as a universal claim about every possible continuous mirror.
