The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of goods \(G\), additive valuation types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and an allocation \(x_{t,B}\ge 0\) of type mass to integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\), with \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), decide whether there are common prices \(p_g\in[0,1]\) such that every bundle \(B\) with \(x_{t,B}>0\) maximizes type \(t\)'s additive utility among all integral bundles affordable at budget \(1\). The per-capita supply of good \(g\) is the amount allocated, \(q_g=\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}\).

The model it lives in

A course-allocation market with many students in a small number of complete additive valuation types. Each student receives an integral bundle of seats; the allocation records fractional type mass \(x_{t,B}\) assigned to each bundle, and a common price vector must support every bundle assigned positive mass.

The objection that survived

For a fixed allocation, the price conditions depend on which type-bundle pairs have positive mass, not on their mass values; this makes the population proportions irrelevant to the recognition answer.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead is \(\textsc{CEEI-Test}_\infty\): given a continuous population and a proposed allocation of indivisible bundles, decide whether some common price vector makes that allocation a competitive equilibrium from equal incomes.

The anchor is the paper’s citation to [12, Theorem 49], proved in Brânzei’s thesis rather than in this paper. It says that, given additive valuations for indivisible goods and a fixed allocation, deciding whether any price vector makes that allocation a market equilibrium is coNP-hard. The cited reduction starts from SUBSET-SUM with positive integers \(w_1,\ldots,w_m\) and target \(K\). It uses two buyers and \(m+2\) goods, and fixes the allocation \(x_1=\{m+1,m+2\}\), \(x_2=\{1,\ldots,m\}\). Buyer 1 values goods \(j\le m\) at \(w_j\), good \(m+1\) at \(K-1\), and good \(m+2\) at \(4(\sum_j w_j)^2\); buyer 2 values goods \(j\le m\) at \(w_j\), good \(m+1\) at \(K+1\), and good \(m+2\) at \(0\). The fixed allocation admits equilibrium prices exactly when no subset of the \(w_j\) sums to \(K\). The paper invokes this hardness for its CEEI test. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.01734) · [Brânzei’s thesis](https://pure.au.dk/ws/files/85229011/siminas_thesis.pdf)

Here is the continuous question. An instance gives a finite catalog \(G\), additive valuation types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational nonnegative values \(v_{tg}\), and a proposed allocation \(x_{t,B}\) for each type \(t\) and integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\). Require \(\sum_{B\subseteq G}x_{t,B}=\mu_t\); the per-capita supply of good \(g\) is \(q_g=\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}\), so the proposed allocation clears the market. Each person receives an ordinary subset of goods; only the mass of a type assigned to each bundle is fractional. The question is whether there are prices \(p_g\in[0,1]\) such that, for every \(t,B\) with \(x_{t,B}>0\), \(B\) maximizes \(\sum_{g\in B}v_{tg}\) among all bundles \(S\subseteq G\) with \(\sum_{g\in S}p_g\le1\). A price vector satisfying those conditions is a solution.

A natural regime is a large course-allocation market: many students share one of a small number of complete additive valuation types, course capacities grow with enrollment, and each student can receive at most one seat in each course. For the reduction above, take two types with mass \(1/2\) each, give them the two buyers’ values, and set \(x_{1,\{m+1,m+2\}}=1/2\) and \(x_{2,\{1,\ldots,m\}}=1/2\). Every course has per-capita supply \(1/2\). Each type has positive mass, so the continuous price test imposes exactly the two buyers’ demand conditions from the reduction. Thus the continuous test is coNP-hard already at \(\tau=2\). The expected direction is hardness transfers: the reduction’s combinatorics lie in the number of goods and their binary-encoded values, not in population size. In particular, parameterizing by \(\tau\) alone cannot make the exact test fixed-parameter tractable unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{coNP}\).

This is a close mirror of the paper’s CEEI recognition problem: it keeps equal budgets, additive preferences, integral bundles for each person, price support, and market clearing. The paper itself uses course allocation as an application, and its appendix develops an exact feasibility method for testing CEEI. The mirror covers that recognition task; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s picking-sequence results.

The weak point is the supply co-modification. The paper’s example has unique goods, while this high-multiplicity regime treats repeated seats as one good class with a shared price and a one-seat-per-person cap. That is a natural course-market model, but a referee who insists on the paper’s literal one-copy-per-object setting may regard it as a variant. The hardness anchor is also cited from the thesis rather than proved in the article.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed test’s population is mathematically inert. There is also a provenance issue: the coNP-hardness is Theorem 49 of Brânzei’s thesis, cited by this paper; the paper uses it to motivate an exact CEEI test rather than proving that hardness itself.

For a continuous allocation \(x\), let \(S_t=\{B:x_{t,B}>0\}\). The test asks for prices such that every \(B\in S_t\) is utility-maximizing for type \(t\). Its truth value depends on which types and bundles have positive mass, not on the values of the masses. Changing their proportions while preserving those supports leaves every price constraint unchanged. In the proposed reduction, the two types each receive one bundle, so the half-and-half population contributes no condition that the two original buyers did not already impose. The large course market is a credible high-multiplicity setting, but for this fixed-allocation recognition question the continuous input collapses to its finite support. That makes this a weak fit for a programme whose object is the population distribution.

The objection has a real limit. The course-market co-modification is sensible, and the reduction appears to lift correctly. A stronger candidate would ask whether exogenous per-capita course capacities admit *any* CEEI allocation; then type masses would constrain market clearing. That is a meaningful continuous-market question, but it changes the paper’s task from testing a proposed allocation to deciding equilibrium existence, and the paper’s CEEI test and sequenceability results do not settle its complexity. So the negative case is strongest against this particular anchor. It does not establish that no worthwhile CEEI mirror can be formulated.
