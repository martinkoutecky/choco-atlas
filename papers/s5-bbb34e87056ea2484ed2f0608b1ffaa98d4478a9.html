What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 proves a complete \(1\)-out-of-\(5\) allocation exists for four agents, and Corollary 7 characterizes the universal guarantee for \(d\ge5\). The paper provides no computational guarantee for finding such an allocation.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a defensible positive case under the programme’s anchor requirement: this paper has no named computational result. Theorem 1, proved here, shows that every four-agent instance with nonnegative additive valuations has a complete \(1\)-out-of-\(5\) MMS allocation. Corollary 7, also proved here using an exact-MMS counterexample cited as [6], says a complete \(1\)-out-of-\(d\) allocation is guaranteed for every four-agent instance exactly when \(d\ge5\). These are existence results, not claims about an algorithm or a complexity class; the paper explicitly says its proof is existential and does not address computational complexity. Lean verification does not change that.

The population is fixed at four agents. Treating four valuation classes as masses would not make the theorem a high-multiplicity result: the paper allocates bundles to four agents, and says nothing about fairness to many individuals in those classes. A continuous cohort-allocation question could be posed, but this theorem would not establish it, and I can’t honestly present it as the paper’s continuous mirror.

So there is no qualifying anchor, and no continuous problem I can give as the requested positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against a ChoCo mirror is unusually strong: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Corollary 7 are existence statements about finite allocations. The paper explicitly says its proof is existential and does not address computational complexity. Lean verification certifies the proof; it does not give a runtime or an algorithmic result. The proposed positive case offers no affirmative anchors, and on this point its concession is sound.

The best alternative would be a high-multiplicity fair-division problem: represent people by complete additive-valuation types, scale the supply per capita, and ask whether each type’s members can receive integral bundles meeting a specified maximin-share threshold. One could represent the assignment by masses of each type receiving each bundle. That is a coherent continuous computational question, but the threshold is the sticking point. The paper’s \(1\)-out-of-\(5\) benchmark is proved for four recipients. With a growing population, one must choose a new benchmark, such as one based on the number of recipients or a fixed five-way per-capita partition. Either choice defines a new problem; neither is supplied by the theorem.

Keeping the theorem’s exact target does not solve this. Four valuation classes can be repeated into large cohorts, but then the cohorts’ members—not the four classes—are the recipients, and the guarantee must be restated for the larger population. Keeping exactly four recipients avoids that change, but gives no population multiplicity. The paper’s discussion also makes clear how specific its proof is to four agents: its anchors and collision repair do not directly supply the general-population version.

So I would reject this paper as a qualifying continuous mirror under ChoCo’s computational-result anchor requirement. The limit of that conclusion is important: a type-mass allocation problem could still be a worthwhile research direction in computational fair division. What this paper lacks is a computational result that makes that direction its mirror.
