The continuous mirror question

Given τ roles with rational masses μ_a>0 summing to 1, a symmetric nonnegative rational influence-density matrix J, type-specific thresholds θ_a^-, θ_a^+ and periodic schedules ℓ_a,ρ_a, and rational initial opinion shares x_a(0)∈[0,1], determine whether the population ever reaches a state after which no positive mass changes opinion at any future update. At time k, role a receives positive influence P_a(x(k))=∑_b μ_b J_{ab}x_b(k). When it listens, its mass holding opinion 1 changes to opinion 0 in amount x_a(k) if P_a<θ_a^+, and its mass holding opinion 0 changes to opinion 1 in amount 1-x_a(k) if P_a≥θ_a^-; otherwise the corresponding flow is zero. The equilibrium condition is zero transition flow, not merely an unchanged share vector.

The model it lives in

A society of τ homogeneous roles with masses μ_a, where each member of role a has the same thresholds and schedule and receives influence from role b according to the density J_{ab}; the state is each role's opinion share x_a, and the decision is whether the induced transition flows eventually cease.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 1's reachability hardness for undirected unsigned weighted periodic asynchronous threshold dynamics. It leaves the synchronous pseudo-polynomial orbit bound in Theorem 3 and Corollary 3, and the signed-graph simulation in Theorem 1, unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity when τ is fixed, and how does it depend on the role schedules?
  • What new orbit behavior or complexity arises from fractional initial shares?
  • Can PSPACE-hardness hold with bounded influence densities and suitably restricted mass and threshold encodings?

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible mirror is a role-structured population: many people occupy each of a finite number of social roles, and everyone in a role has the same thresholds, update schedule, and pattern of influence on the other roles. For example, these could be recurring cohorts in a large platform whose members see the same communities with the same strengths of trust or distrust. The number of people \(N\) can be much larger than the number of roles \(\tau\); the role graph can still carry substantial computational structure.

Call the decision problem Continuum Periodic-Async Equilibrium Reachability. An instance has a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) roles, rational masses \(\mu_a>0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric nonnegative influence-density matrix \(J_{ab}\), thresholds \(\theta_a^-,\theta_a^+\), periodic listening parameters \(\ell_a,\rho_a\), and rational initial opinion shares \(x_a(0)\in[0,1]\). A type is the complete structural role: its row of interaction densities and its thresholds and schedule. The share \(x_a(k)\) is the fraction of role \(a\)’s mass holding opinion \(1\) at time \(k\). With unsigned relations, the positive influence on a member of role \(a\) is
\[ P_a(x(k))=\sum_{b\in T}\mu_bJ_{ab}x_b(k). \]
At times \(k=\ell_a+i(\rho_a+1)\), role \(a\) listens and its opinion share updates by
\[ x_a(k+1)=x_a(k)\mathbf{1}[P_a(x(k))\ge\theta_a^+] +(1-x_a(k))\mathbf{1}[P_a(x(k))\ge\theta_a^-]. \]
At other times it stays fixed. The question is whether the resulting society ever reaches an equilibrium: a time after which no member changes opinion again. A solution is the yes/no answer; a yes instance can be witnessed by a finite trajectory to that point, and a no instance by a repeated augmented state showing that the trajectory cycles without reaching one.

My anchor is Corollary 1, proved in this paper: deciding whether an undirected unsigned weighted graph, from a specified opinion configuration and under periodic asynchronous threshold-based dynamics, ever reaches an equilibrium is strong PSPACE-complete. The paper derives it using its proved Theorem 2, which simulates synchronous threshold dynamics on directed unweighted graphs by periodic asynchronous dynamics on undirected weighted graphs. The underlying PSPACE-completeness result for directed deterministic majority dynamics is cited from [26]. Thus the paper proves the corollary’s transfer to its graph model, while the source hardness result is prior work.

The same decision question embeds in the role-structured population. Given an anchor instance on \(n\) vertices, make each vertex a role, set \(\mu_a=1/n\), copy its thresholds and schedule, set \(x_a(0)\) to its initial bit, and set \(J_{ab}=n\pi(a,b)\) on each edge, where \(\pi\) is its edge weight. Then \(P_a\) exactly equals the vertex’s weighted positive influence in the graph. Every role’s opinion share stays in \(\{0,1\}\), so the continuous trajectory reproduces the graph trajectory step by step and reaches equilibrium in exactly the same cases.

This has a high-multiplicity interpretation: each role can contain \(M\) identical agents, giving \(N=nM\) people and \(\tau=n\) roles. Connect each pair of agents in adjacent roles with the corresponding interaction scaled by \(1/M\); equivalently, scale all edge weights and thresholds together to use integers. The aggregate influence on each person remains the same. The mirror therefore retains the paper’s underlying threshold and reachability question while making population shares legitimate state variables. I expect hardness to transfer (Class B): the reduction’s combinatorics remain in the role network and update schedules, even when each role represents a large population.

There is a qualification to the anchor’s “strong” label. With the per-unit-mass density \(J\) as the input coefficient, this reduction makes \(J\) scale with \(n\). It establishes PSPACE-hardness for the rational-input mirror, but I would not claim strong PSPACE-completeness under an encoding that treats those density magnitudes as the numerical parameters. The paper’s bounded numerical values are preserved by the equivalent role-level total-influence weights.

This case covers Corollary 1, not the paper’s synchronous polynomial orbit bound or its signed-graph simulation result. The main weakness is the regime assumption: exact roles with uniform exposure to whole cohorts are plausible in some large, organized networks, but much real social interaction is individualized and may not aggregate this way. I think the mirror survives that objection because the authors’ problem is still recognizable—deterministic threshold opinion change over a weighted network—and the type definition records the full interaction role rather than erasing it. The next questions are whether the mirror is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\) or in role-network structure, and how orbit lengths behave when initial opinion shares are fractional rather than unanimous.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Corollary 1, and the strongest skeptical point is that its hardness does not use population size. In the proposed embedding, each graph vertex becomes a role, so \(\tau=n\), and every initial share is \(0\) or \(1\). Cloning each role \(M\) times and scaling pairwise influences by \(1/M\) preserves the graph trajectory, but the clones add no new computational structure: the hard instance still sits in the role network. That makes the mirror a block-population encoding of the original graph problem, rather than evidence that taking a population to a continuum simplifies or reshapes the computation.

This leaves a real question about how representative the hard instances are. Their roles encode the vertices’ different positions in an arbitrary graph. A large population with only a few recurring social roles may be plausible, but Corollary 1’s reduction does not establish hardness for that narrower regime. Its \(x_a\in\{0,1\}\) instances also say nothing about the behavior specific to fractional opinion shares.

Those points do not defeat the mirror under ChoCo’s stated criteria. The program counts complexity in \(\tau\), allows \(\tau\) to grow, and treats an exact high-multiplicity version as a sensible object when repeated agents share the same full interaction profile. The cohort story is plausible, and the reduction gives a legitimate Class B result. The fact that its hard instances lie at the corners of the share space is a limit on what the anchor establishes, not a collision with an existing continuous computational treatment.

There is a modeling detail to handle carefully: equilibrium must mean that no positive mass changes opinion, not merely that the share vector stops moving. At \(x_a=1/2\), for example, equal masses could swap opinions while leaving the share unchanged. The role model can define and check equilibrium using the induced flows, so this is repairable rather than a fundamental obstacle. My best case against is therefore weak: the anchor may offer less insight into continuum-specific behavior than its framing suggests, but I cannot make the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The Corollary 1 mirror survives.
