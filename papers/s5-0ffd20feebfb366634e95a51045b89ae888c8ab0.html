The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot identify a qualifying anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are existence theorems, not computational results: for example, Theorem 4.3 proves a fair, almost equicardinal splitting exists with \((r-1)n\) cuts when \(r\) is a prime power; Theorems 5.1 and 5.9 prove discrete and continuous binary splittings exist with that cut bound when \(r\) is a power of two; and Theorem 6.9 proves an envy-free fair splitting exists under its preference assumptions, with an optimal cut bound. None classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. Proposition 5.7 proves a connectivity property, not a complexity result.

The nearest positive foothold for a follow-on would be a large group of recipients with a small set of complete preference types: type mass would represent the fraction of recipients of each type, and one could study fair resource divisions that keep each type’s assignments among its preferred shares. But the paper’s theorems give one-to-one allocations among finitely many thieves. Replacing those recipients with type masses changes the allocation problem, and would create a new computational question rather than mirror a named computational result here.

The paper’s existing continuity is in the resource: its measures describe how bead types are distributed along the necklace. The number of thieves remains finite. So the honest positive case is limited: the paper offers a possible starting point for a population-continuous research question, but no named computational anchor from which to formulate the requested continuous problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 4.3 proves almost equicardinal fair splitting exists; Theorems 5.1 and 5.9 prove binary splittings exist; Theorem 6.9 and its extensions prove envy-free existence; Proposition 5.7 proves connectivity. None asks for an algorithm or classifies a computational problem. The discrete and continuous versions of the necklace theorem do not change that: the paper’s continuity is in the resource measures, while the thieves remain finitely many recipients.

The best repair would be to pose a search problem for these divisions, perhaps with rational piecewise-constant measures and finitely described preferences. But that requires choosing an input representation and an algorithmic task the paper does not specify. In particular, Theorem 6.9 permits arbitrary closed preference sets on a configuration space, with no finite encoding or effective access assumed. Restricting those preferences to computable valuations could yield a worthwhile fair-division problem, but it would be a new computational formulation, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proponent’s high-multiplicity foothold has a similar gap. Grouping repeated thieves by complete preference type is a sensible regime to investigate. Yet the results allocate shares among individual thieves, with envy-freeness expressed through a permutation of recipients and shares. Extending that to positive masses of recipients requires specifying how a type’s mass consumes or divides its share. If each person keeps an individual share, its fair quota shrinks toward zero as the population grows; if the group receives an aggregate share, the allocation and envy conditions need a new formulation. Either could motivate separate research, but neither supplies the missing computational anchor here.

So I find the negative case strong against a *mirror of this paper*, but weaker as a universal claim that no related continuous computational fair-division question could be worthwhile. The paper supports that broader direction; it does not itself identify the computational question or result that ChoCo is meant to continuize.
