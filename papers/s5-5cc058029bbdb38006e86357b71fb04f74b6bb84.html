What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered; Theorem 1 and Lemma 1 characterize aggregation rules, while the proposed intervention task draws on the paper's discussion of the Condorcet effect but is not one of its results.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible population regime is a large public consultation on a fixed set of logically connected policy claims. A type would be a participant’s complete admissible judgment vector \(x\in X\); \(\mu_x\) would be the fraction of participants with that vector, and \(\tau\) the number of distinct vectors present. That is a sensible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s setting, and its authors would likely recognize it as a population version of logical aggregation.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor the required mirror. The closest result is Theorem 1, attributed to Guilbaud (1952) and given a reconstructed proof here: for an agenda with at least three propositions, a systematic aggregation rule that preserves every admissible valuation set \(X\subseteq\{0,1\}^P\) is dictatorial, and every dictatorial rule has that property. This is an impossibility theorem about aggregation rules, not a result that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The other named results, including Lemma 1’s characterization by proper and strong simple games, do not supply a complexity anchor either.

So the honest positive case is limited: the population model is plausible, but this paper provides no eligible anchor and therefore no per-anchor computational question to hand a prover. The weak point is that translating coalitions into masses would impose anonymity, while Theorem 1 concerns general systematic rules; the paper’s discussion of infinite electorates and nonprincipal ultrafilters also cautions against treating the continuous case as an automatic extension.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The lack of a computational theorem is a real limitation, but it does not rule out a worthwhile mirror. Theorem 1 identifies the central event in the paper: a collective judgment can violate the logical constraints obeyed by every individual. A computational question can measure how much intervention it takes to prevent that “Condorcet effect” in a particular large consultation.

For example, let \(X\) be the admissible judgment vectors and let \(\mu\) describe the fractions of participants holding each vector. Fix issue-wise majority and a coherent target judgment \(y\in X\). Given costs \(\gamma(x,x')\) for persuading a participant of type \(x\) to adopt type \(x'\), ask for the minimum-cost mass transfer that makes the majority outcome equal \(y\). With variables \(q_{x,x'}\) for transferred mass, this is an LP when the reachable types and costs are listed; when the logical agenda is implicit, the pricing problem over admissible judgments becomes the complexity question. That is a population-based bribery or outcome-robustness problem, directly tied to the paper’s account of logical inconsistency.

This is a narrower mirror than a continuous version of the dictatorship characterization, and it adds an intervention objective the paper does not study. But large consultations with repeated judgment patterns are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s discussion of majority, admissibility, and the Condorcet effect gives the computational question a clear home. Its account of infinite electorates is axiomatic, not an existing computational treatment. So the proponent is right that the paper supplies no complexity result; the stronger claim that it supplies no worthwhile computational mirror does not follow.
