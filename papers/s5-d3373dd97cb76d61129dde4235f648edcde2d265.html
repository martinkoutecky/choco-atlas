The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), a rational population distribution \(\mu\), symmetric valuation matrices \(v,v'\) with entries in \(\{-1,0,1\}\) differing on one unordered pair of distinct types, a starting NS partition \(\mathcal P^0=(q^1,\ldots,q^r;\sigma^0)\) under \(v\), and a rational threshold \(\kappa\), does there exist a finite rational post-update NS partition \(\mathcal P'=(p^1,\ldots,p^s;\sigma')\) under \(v'\) with distance at most \(\kappa\)? Each coalition vector is in \(\mathbb Q_{\ge0}^{|T|}\), and the vectors and singleton pool sum to \(\mu\). With \(U_t(q)=\sum_{u\in T}v'_{tu}q_u\), every type \(t\) present in an occupied coalition \(q\) must satisfy \(U_t(q)\ge0\) and \(U_t(q)\ge U_t(r)\) for every occupied coalition \(r\); if \(\sigma'_t>0\), then \(U_t(q)\le0\) for every occupied \(q\). Define distance by \(d_\mu=1-\sum_t\min(\sigma^0_t,\sigma'_t)-\max_{M}\sum_{(i,j)\in M}\sum_{t\in T}\min(q^i_t,p^j_t)\), where \(M\) ranges over partial one-to-one matchings of old and new occupied coalitions.

The model it lives in

An atomless population of complete types with rational masses, symmetric type-to-type valuations, finite rational coalition-mass vectors, and a singleton mass pool. Stability compares each type’s per-capita utility across occupied coalitions; the objective is to minimize the population mass whose coalition changes, with partitions matched to maximize retained mass.

What the mirror covers

Covers the symmetric NS nearby-partition question for a type-pair cohort update. It leaves the IS, CIS, and CNS results, the other valuation restrictions, and the sequence-of-updates results aside; nonatomic deviations also remove the finite-agent consent effects relevant to the other stability notions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every feasible stable mass partition have a finite-support rational representative with a useful bound in \(|T|\) and the input length?
  • Can the stability constraints and minimum-change objective be solved in time polynomial in \(|T|\), the starting partition support, and the rational input length?
  • What rounding or additive-approximation guarantees relate this mass problem to finite populations with repeated types?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s Theorem 1, specifically its symmetric Nash stability case.

Theorem 1, proved in this paper by reduction from Set Cover, says: for positive, polynomial-time computable rational functions \(\alpha,\beta\), both \(X\)-1-1-ALTERED and \(X\)-1-1-SYM-ALTERED are NP-complete for \(X\in\{\mathrm{IS},\mathrm{CIS}\}\) when valuations lie in \(\{-\beta(n),0,\alpha(n)\}\); the same holds for \(X\in\{\mathrm{NS},\mathrm{CNS}\}\) when \(\alpha(n)\le\beta(n)\). These are decision problems: given an \(X\)-stable starting partition, an allowed valuation update and a distance bound \(k\), decide whether a new \(X\)-stable partition exists within distance \(k\). I use its symmetric NS result with \(\alpha=\beta=1\), so the valuations are in \(\{-1,0,1\}\). The theorem and its set-cover proof appear in the [published paper](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2026/0380.pdf).

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover. Call it Symmetric NS Cohort Update\(_\infty\). An instance has a finite set \(T\) of complete agent types, a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), and symmetric valuation matrices \(v,v'\) over type pairs. The old matrix \(v\) and a starting partition are given; the starting partition is NS under \(v\). The update to \(v'\) changes one unordered pair of types, with values in \(\{-1,0,1\}\). The decision version also gives a rational threshold \(\kappa\in[0,1]\).

A type is a full role-and-preference description: agents of type \(t\) have the same valuation toward every type \(s\), and respond identically to the update. Think of a large fleet of robots arranged in work cells, with many robots in each of a small number of capability or firmware types. A software change could alter how one type values working alongside another. The regime is \(n\) large with \(\tau=|T|\ll n\). The paper already studies updates affecting groups of agents, including the symmetric case, so a cohort-wide update fits its model family, though it changes the scale of the particular one-pair update in Theorem 1. [The paper’s update model and symmetric group-update setting](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2026/0380.pdf)

Represent a coalition by a rational vector \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{\tau}\), where \(q_t\) is the fraction of the whole population of type \(t\) in that coalition. A partition is a finite list of positive-mass coalition vectors, plus a singleton pool \(\sigma\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{\tau}\) for agents whose coalitions have zero population mass; the vectors and singleton mass sum to \(\mu\). These fractional entries describe the distribution of people across teams: every actual person still belongs to one team. The starting partition and the returned partition have finite rational descriptions.

Under \(v'\), a type-\(t\) agent in coalition \(q\) has population-normalized utility \(U_t(q)=\sum_{s\in T}v'_{ts}q_s\). The partition is NS if every type present in a positive-mass coalition \(q\) gets at least zero and at least as much utility there as in any other occupied coalition. If \(\sigma_t>0\), singleton agents of type \(t\) must not gain by joining an occupied coalition, so \(U_t(q)\le0\) for every such \(q\).

The objective is to minimize the fraction of people who must change coalitions. To define distance when coalition names are irrelevant, match old and new positive-mass coalitions one-to-one to maximize retained mass, and retain singleton agents by type. If the old vectors are \(q^i\), the new ones are \(p^j\), and the singleton pools are \(\sigma^0,\sigma'\), this gives
\[ d_\mu(\mathcal P^0,\mathcal P')= 1-\sum_t\min(\sigma^0_t,\sigma'_t) -\max_{\mathcal M}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal M}\sum_t\min(q^i_t,p^j_t), \]
where \(\mathcal M\) ranges over partial one-to-one matchings of old and new coalitions. The question is whether a post-update NS partition with \(d_\mu\le\kappa\) exists; the optimization version asks for one minimizing \(d_\mu\).

I would expect this mirror to be Class A in the many-agents/few-types regime: splitting a cohort across coalitions can turn the reduction’s choice of whole set-agents into fractional mass allocation, suggesting LP or column-generation methods. That is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 1. A prover would need to establish a useful finite-support bound and control the stability conditions while generating coalition profiles. I would target a running time polynomial in \(\tau\), the starting partition’s support, and the rational input length \(L\). The further questions are whether such a support bound exists, whether the relevant pricing problem is tractable, and whether an algorithm yields rounding guarantees for finite populations.

The scenario is credible, and the problem is precise. The authors ask for a nearby stable partition after valuation updates, and they explicitly consider group updates. The mirror continuizes the population: time remains one update, coalitions still receive people as indivisible members, and the objective becomes moved population mass. It does not rely on continuous outcomes or on probabilities over finite profiles.

My weakest point is that a single agent has zero mass in this model. Her deviation does not change anyone else’s utility, so the consent conditions behind IS, CIS and CNS collapse in the limit; this mirror properly covers only the NS slice of Theorem 1. Also, the theorem’s Set Cover reduction uses many distinct element and set roles, so its hardness does not directly transfer to a regime with many agents per type. Those limits leave a worthwhile question: does the discrete hardness come from indivisible agents, or from the combinatorics among types that remain after continuization?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The direct mirror of the symmetric NS result loses its perturbation in the limit. In the paper, one update changes the valuation on one edge. For an endpoint, that changes utility by at most \(1\); after population normalization, the change is \(1/n\). The endpoints also have mass \(1/n\). As \(n\) grows, the update disappears from the positive mass type matrix, and the endpoints cannot move enough mass to affect anyone else’s incentives. The original mass partition therefore remains NS at distance \(0\). Keeping the endpoints as named atoms would preserve the update, but then its computational content sits in those exceptional individuals rather than in a continuous population.

The proponent’s repair—changing the valuation between two whole types—can have a nonzero effect: a type \(t\) agent’s normalized utility can change by an amount proportional to \(\mu_s\). But that changes \(\Theta(n_t n_s)\) pairwise valuations, rather than one. The paper does study group updates, so this is a plausible question for the broader model. It is not, however, a continuous version of the cited one-edge theorem, and that theorem’s Set Cover reduction provides no evidence that its hardness or structure carries over.

The reduction also depends on agents with different roles and valuation neighborhoods. In the relevant instances, elements and sets generally need distinct complete types, so \(\tau\) grows with the instance. That does not invalidate a mirror, but it undercuts the proposed picture of fractional allocation among a small number of cohorts as a direct relaxation of the reduction. Nor does fractionality itself give an LP: NS constraints compare every type’s payoff across every occupied coalition, while the number and composition of coalitions are part of the solution. A useful algorithm would first need a support bound and a finite-to-continuum correspondence.

That is a strong objection to the *specific* Theorem 1 mirror and to its suggested LP route. It does not rule out the more substantial cohort-update question. A large robot fleet with repeated capability types and a policy change affecting all interactions between two types is credible, and it belongs to the paper’s group-update family. The honest negative case is therefore weak against the user’s universal claim: the exact one-pair anchor degenerates, but the cohort-scale problem remains well-posed and could still be worth studying.
