That reconstruction runs into a basic mismatch. In the European Council, the players are member states, and the index measures how often each state can swing a coalition under the Council’s voting rules. The relevant society has 27 or 28 distinct states. A full type would include the state’s population and its other role in the game, leaving no meaningful multiplicity. Splitting a state into fractional states changes the institution’s membership and its state-count threshold. Treating citizens as the population changes who participates in the Council vote. Varying population shares while keeping the 27 state players gives a continuous-parameter study of a finite voting game, rather than a continuous population.

A more general setting with many repeated, weighted voters seems more promising. But in an atomless limit, a single voter has zero mass and cannot swing the result. Under independent random coalition formation, coalition mass concentrates at its expectation; away from a threshold, the winning outcome becomes deterministic. Any nonzero limiting power therefore needs finite-population fluctuations, a specified scaling limit, or a rule for coordinated blocks. Those additions supply the substance of the power calculation; the type-mass distribution alone does not. They lead toward an asymptotic random-coalition or noise model, rather than a continuous-population computational problem of the kind this programme targets.

The vulnerable point in this negative case is that an asymptotic, type-level Banzhaf measure for large replicated councils could be a legitimate research question. But it would need extra assumptions to preserve pivotality, and the paper supplies no computational result anchoring that extension.
