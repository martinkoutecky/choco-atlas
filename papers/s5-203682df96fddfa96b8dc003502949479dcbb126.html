The case FOR (proponent)

The best raw material for a population mirror is clear: in a large election, take a complete ranking (or chamber of \(\mathcal A\)) as a voter type and \(\mu_c\) as the fraction of society with type \(c\). The output could be a social ranking. That gives a plausible high-multiplicity setting, with many voters and relatively few distinct rankings.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a continuous computational problem. Its central candidate, Theorem 3.1, says that for \(|\mathcal A|\ge 3\), every admissible map \(\Phi:Ch(\mathcal A)^m\to Ch(\mathcal A)\) is a projection onto one voter’s component. The paper gives a topological proof of this theorem, which it attributes to Terao; it is an axiomatic classification result, not a result about complexity, algorithms, or optimization. Proposition 2.1 is likewise Arrow’s axiomatic theorem. The homology propositions and lemmas support the proof, rather than establish computational classifications.

One could propose classifying maps \(F:\Delta(Ch(\mathcal A))\to Ch(\mathcal A)\) under distribution-level versions of IIA and Pareto. That would be an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s scope. It also needs a new account of dictatorship: a distribution records type masses, not voter identities, so the paper’s projection onto one named voter does not pass directly to a society of masses.

So I cannot give a continuous computational problem per eligible anchor. The population setting is plausible, but this paper supplies no computational anchor for the requested mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the level of the paper’s actual results: none gives ChoCo a computational problem to continuize. Theorem 3.1 is a classification of admissible aggregation maps: under IIA and Pareto, the map must be a projection onto one voter. Proposition 2.1 is Arrow’s corresponding axiomatic result. Neither studies the complexity of finding an outcome, changing an instance, or deciding a computational property. Proposition 8.1 and the surrounding homology results establish steps in the proof; they do not supply separate algorithmic anchors. The paper’s contribution is a new proof of an axiomatic theorem, not a computational result whose population axis can be replaced.

The proponent’s proposed setting—an election represented by masses over rankings—is plausible as a high-multiplicity society. But it does not preserve the central object in the theorem. A projection chooses a *named voter*. A distribution over rankings records how much mass has each type, not which individual supplies a coordinate to project onto. If types are just rankings, aggregation forgets the identity the conclusion singles out. If types include identity so that the dictator is representable, the type space grows with the electorate and the compression that makes the high-multiplicity regime useful disappears. Replacing an individual dictator with a dictatorial mass block could be a sensible new axiom, but it changes the question into an axiomatic classification over distributions.

The best computational reformulation I can see is to ask whether an explicitly or succinctly represented aggregation map satisfies IIA and Pareto, or to identify its dictator. With an explicit truth table, this is a finite scan; with a succinct circuit representation, complexity may depend on that representation. Either way, the interesting computational object is the encoding of the map and its dependence on voter coordinates, not a society given as masses over types. Likewise, asking for the least mass transfer that changes the social ranking requires choosing an aggregator and a transfer cost that this paper does not provide. Those could be worthwhile problems, but they would be added around the theorem rather than mirrors of a named result in it.

So the population scenario survives as a plausible setting for social choice generally, but it does not rescue a continuous *computational* mirror of this paper. The honest limitation of this negative case is that one could build adjacent computational problems from the axioms, or develop a distribution-level Arrow theorem. The former depend on extra modeling choices; the latter are axiomatic and outside ChoCo’s scope. Neither gives this paper a strong computational anchor.
