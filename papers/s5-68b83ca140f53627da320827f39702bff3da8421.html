The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no eligible result: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational complexity classification. The closest result is the unnumbered best-response algorithm in §3.4.1. It considers one user with weights \(w_i\) over \(n\) machines, other users’ aggregate bids \(y_i\), and budget \(X\), and seeks bids \(x_i\ge 0\) maximizing \(\sum_i w_i x_i/(x_i+y_i)\) subject to \(\sum_i x_i=X\). The paper gives an \(O(n\log n)\) algorithm, but it is not a numbered result, and \(n\) there is the number of machines, not the population. So there is no lead anchor, and no valid per-anchor mirror question.

The strongest positive case I can make is an unanchored, plausible population limit of Tycoon’s market. Imagine a large compute service with many users running repeated kinds of CPU-bound workloads. Users of a type share the same host preferences, per-user credit budget, and relevant usage profile; there are \(N\) users but only \(\tau\) such types, with \(N\gg\tau\). Let \(\mu_t\) be type \(t\)’s fraction of users. Group equivalent host markets into \(H\), with per-user resource supply \(\rho_h\). This is a high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s CPU-bound workload experiments make full use of allocated CPU a plausible setting.

A concrete candidate is the Tycoon Fisher-Market Limit. The input is rational \(\mu_t,\rho_h\), per-user budgets \(X_t\), and per-unit resource values \(w_{th}\). Find prices \(p_h\ge0\) and per-user allocations \(a_{th}\ge0\) such that each type chooses a utility-maximizing affordable allocation,

\[ a_t\in\arg\max_{\substack{z_h\ge0\\\sum_h p_hz_h\le X_t}} \sum_h w_{th}z_h, \]

and markets clear,

\[ \sum_t\mu_t a_{th}\le\rho_h,\qquad p_h\left(\rho_h-\sum_t\mu_t a_{th}\right)=0. \]

These prices and allocations are a solution. They connect directly to Tycoon’s proportional-share rule: bids \(b_{th}=p_ha_{th}\) give type \(t\) its corresponding share of each market’s supply. I would expect this problem to be tractable through a convex market-clearing formulation, for example the Eisenberg–Gale objective \(\max \sum_t\mu_tX_t\log(\sum_h w_{th}a_{th})\), subject to the supply constraints.

Tycoon’s persistent bids are continuous in time, and its allocations can be fractional, but neither fact continuizes the population. The proposed mirror does: users become a distribution over complete types. I think the authors could recognize it as a serious limit of their market and agent model. Its weakness is that it asks for a price-taking market equilibrium, while §3.4.1 computes an individual best response in a finite-user market. That change may make it a related extension rather than the continuous form of their result. Further questions include approximation and bit complexity, and whether the tractability persists with multiple resource constraints, non-use of allocated resources, or strategic users with non-negligible market impact.

So the positive case is plausible at the model level, but it cannot satisfy the requested named-result anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper offers no clear population-complexity result to mirror. Its closest candidate is the best-response algorithm in §3.4.1. Even granting it as a named result, it optimizes one user’s bids across \(H\) hosts, given other users’ aggregate bids \(y_h\), and runs in \(O(H\log H)\). The number of users does not appear: their effect is already summarized by \(y_h\). With \(\tau\) user types, applying the same algorithm to each type gives \(O(\tau H\log H)\), with no new population-sensitive question. The auctioneer’s proportional allocation rule likewise uses aggregate bids directly. The paper reports a system design and its performance; it does not classify the complexity of a problem whose difficulty turns on population size.

The proposed Fisher-market model is coherent, but it changes the computational task. In a static setting where users consume their full allocations, Tycoon’s proportional-share rule can be expressed with a host price \(p_h\): a user spending rate \(b_{th}\) obtains allocation \(a_{th}\) with \(b_{th}=p_h a_{th}\). That makes a price-taking equilibrium a plausible limit. Tycoon’s agent algorithm, however, computes one user’s best response to fixed bids. It does not compute a market equilibrium. The Fisher formulation adds price-taking behavior, a shared budget across hosts, and market clearing. The paper instead emphasizes independent local markets, usage-based charging, and allocations that change with demand and actual use. The equilibrium question could be a valuable extension, but it is not a continuous version of the algorithm in §3.4.1.

A stronger mirror would retain Tycoon’s proportional-share mechanism and model many users as a distribution over complete types: preferences, budgets, workloads, and usage patterns. At a given snapshot, aggregate bids by type and apply the paper’s agent and auctioneer rules. That preserves more of Tycoon, but the snapshot calculation still reduces to sums and per-type best responses. Asking for a stationary equilibrium or for behavior under changing jobs and balances adds the substantive problem; it also adds equilibrium or dynamic assumptions that the paper does not formulate. Preserving the full service model may require tracking evolving account and workload states, which moves the question toward a new population-game model rather than a mirror of the reported result.

That is the limit of the negative case. A large service running many repeated CPU workloads, with a small set of shared preference and budget profiles, is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A static Fisher limit could be recognized as a serious extension of Tycoon’s market model. So the paper is a weak source of a named population-complexity anchor, but the supplied material does not support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The Fisher-market proposal remains the strongest counterexample to that claim.
