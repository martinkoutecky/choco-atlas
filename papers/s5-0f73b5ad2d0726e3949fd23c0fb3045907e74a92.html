The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(A\), committee size \(k\), finite voter-type set \(\mathcal T\), and rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal T\), compute an integral committee \(W\in\binom{A}{k}\) with no integral committee \(U\in\binom{A}{k}\) such that \(U\succeq_t^{\mathcal B}W\) for every \(t\) with \(\mu_t>0\) and \(U\succ_t^{\mathcal B}W\) for some such \(t\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete weak orders over \(A\), \(\mu_t\) is their population mass, and the decision variable is an integral committee \(W\in\binom{A}{k}\). The objective is to find any \(\mathcal B\)-Pareto-undominated committee, with dominance quantified over positive-mass types rather than weighted by \(\mu\).

The objection that survived

The proposed problems collapse societies with different positive masses to the same support, so denominator clearing creates repeated agents without recovering population-proportion information or mass-sensitive computational structure.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers computation of \(\mathcal B\)-efficient committees and verification of \(RS\)-efficient committees; it leaves the other set extensions, strategyproofness results, and their algorithms outside the proposed mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does \(\mathcal B\)-efficiency become tractable when \(\lvert\mathcal T\rvert\) is fixed or the type domain is restricted?
  • What is the complexity of a mass-sensitive variant requiring strict improvement on at least \(\rho\) of the population while preserving integral committee selection?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is Continuous-\(\mathcal B\)-Efficient Committee, anchored in Theorem 7, proved in the paper: *computing a \(\mathcal B\)-efficient committee is NP-hard, even for dichotomous preferences*. I use the numbering of the supplied extended version. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.06644)

An instance consists of a finite candidate set \(A\), a committee size \(k\), and a finite support of voter types \(\mathcal T\). A type \(t\in\mathcal T\) is a complete weak order over \(A\), and \(\mu_t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\) is its population mass, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The committee remains an integral \(k\)-subset \(W\in\binom{A}{k}\). Under the best extension, \(U\succeq_t^{\mathcal B}W\) when the most-preferred member of \(U\) is at least as good for type \(t\) as the most-preferred member of \(W\). The question is to output a committee \(W\) for which there is no \(U\in\binom{A}{k}\) such that \(U\succeq_t^{\mathcal B}W\) for every type with \(\mu_t>0\), and \(U\succ_t^{\mathcal B}W\) for at least one positive-mass type.

The natural regime is a large electorate selecting a small committee of projects, representatives, or hires: millions of agents, but only \(\tau\) recurring preference profiles induced by departments, regions, professional roles, or standardized stakeholder groups, with \(\tau\ll N\). This is exactly high multiplicity: agents of the same type are indistinguishable for the problem. The action is committee selection; neither candidates nor committee membership are fractionalized.

Theorem 7 transfers directly. Its Hitting Set reduction creates, for every set \(C_i\), a dichotomous type ranking \(C_i\) above \(X\setminus C_i\). Give every such type positive mass, for example \(\mu_i=1/\ell\). If a hitting set of size at most \(k\) exists, padding it to size \(k\) gives a committee that weakly improves every type and strictly improves every type whose current committee misses \(C_i\). Hence every \(\mathcal B\)-efficient committee must hit every \(C_i\). Conversely, an efficient committee that hits every \(C_i\) certifies a hitting set. Replacing each type by \(R\) identical agents produces a population of \(R\ell\) agents with the same \(\tau=\ell\) types, so the mirror genuinely includes arbitrarily high-multiplicity societies. I expect this problem to be Class B: the hardness lives in the candidate-side hitting-set structure, so continuization should not remove it.

A second, independent mirror is Continuous-RS Pareto Verification, anchored in Theorem 2, also proved here: checking whether a committee is Pareto optimal under the responsive extension is coNP-complete, even for dichotomous preferences with \(Topwidth(\pref)\ge 3\), or for strict preferences.

Its instance is \((A,k,\mathcal T,\mu,D)\), where \(D\in\binom{A}{k}\) is the status quo. For each type \(t\), \(U\succeq_t^{RS}D\) iff there is an injection from \(D\) to \(U\) mapping every member of \(D\) to an alternative weakly preferred by \(t\). The question is whether there exists an integral committee \(U\) that weakly improves every positive-mass type and strictly improves at least one positive-mass type. Equivalently, decide whether \(D\) is continuous-population \(RS\)-efficient.

The paper’s Vertex Cover reduction survives normalization. For a graph \(G=(V,E)\) and integer \(k\), it creates \(k\) agents for every edge, alternatives \(V\cup\{d_1,\ldots,d_k\}\), and status quo \(D=\{d_1,\ldots,d_k\}\). For an edge \(e=[u,v]\), the corresponding type has top class \(\{u,v,d_i\}\); the special type has top class \(\{x,y\}\) for a fixed edge \([x,y]\). The paper proves that \(D\) is not \(RS\)-efficient exactly when \(G\) has a vertex cover of size at most \(k\); it also gives the strict-preference variant. Assigning positive rational mass to each constructed type gives precisely the continuous problem. Thus this mirror is also Class B, with coNP-hardness transferring rather than dissolving.

The weakest point is that exact Pareto efficiency uses \(\mu\) only through its support: the numerical masses do not matter once they are positive. This makes the mirror less mass-sensitive than continuous bribery or robustness, and it may look like a compressed anonymous-profile representation rather than a new optimization geometry. Still, it is a faithful high-multiplicity mirror: rational \(\mu\) can be expanded into a finite profile by clearing denominators, and every finite profile maps back to its type distribution. The further questions are whether these problems become tractable for fixed \(\tau\), whether a mass-sensitive \(\rho\)-Pareto variant is useful, and whether restricted type domains such as single-peaked or bounded-top-width preferences change the Class B picture.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both mirrors is that Pareto efficiency does not actually use population mass. Let

\[ \operatorname{supp}(\mu)=\{t:\mu_t>0\}. \]

For every set extension considered in the paper,

\[ U\succeq_\mu W \]

depends only on whether each type has positive mass, not on the value of that mass. Consequently, any two societies with the same support have exactly the same efficient committees. A society in which one type constitutes \(99.9\%\) of the electorate and one in which it constitutes \(0.001\%\) are indistinguishable to the problem.

That is a genuine continuum degeneracy. The proposed mirrors do not compute over a population distribution; they compute over a set of represented preference types. Clearing denominators and expanding types into repeated agents gives a valid encoding, but it is not a two-way computational bridge: many genuinely different distributions collapse to the same instance. Replicating every type \(R\) times only pads the population without changing the problem.

This undermines the proposed \(\mathcal B\)-mirror. Theorem 7 can indeed be encoded with positive masses: a type corresponding to \(C_i\) ranks \(C_i\) above \(X\setminus C_i\), and an efficient committee must hit every \(C_i\) whenever such a hitting set exists. But the reduction works identically with one representative of each type. The masses contribute no constraint, objective coefficient, approximation scale, or high-multiplicity phenomenon. Calling this Class B is formally defensible, but the hardness is a property of an integral committee problem indexed by a support set, not of a continuous society.

The best repair would be to introduce a mass-sensitive notion: for example, require an improvement on at least \(\rho\) of the population, or maximize the mass of types receiving a strict improvement. That would be a worthwhile continuous problem in its own right, but it is no longer the paper’s \(\mathcal B\)-efficiency problem. A positive mass attached to a Pareto coordinate cannot affect coordinatewise dominance; weighting the coordinates by \(\mu_t\) changes nothing. To make masses matter one must replace Pareto efficiency by a threshold, welfare, or coverage objective, and the paper supplies no canonical choice.

The same objection applies to Continuous-RS Pareto Verification. The Vertex Cover construction can be assigned positive masses, and duplicating every constructed type produces arbitrarily large electorates. But again, this proves only that the support version is hard. Moreover, the \(k\) agents \(e^1,\ldots,e^k\) created for one edge are not copies of one type: their rankings differ through the dummy alternatives \(d_i\). The reduction therefore has no intrinsic repeated-agent structure. Replication can manufacture high multiplicity afterwards, but the reduction and the efficiency test are completely insensitive to it.

There is also a discontinuity that is especially unattractive as a population model. A type with mass \(10^{-100}\) constrains Pareto efficiency just as strongly as a type with mass \(1/2\); reducing that mass to zero removes the constraint entirely. In an ordinary large electorate this is not how “importance in the population” behaves. It is a support test with a measure-zero boundary, not a computational question about population proportions.

Thus the proponent has not shown a mass-sensitive continuous mirror of either theorem. Any stronger proposal—mass thresholds, aggregate welfare, or approximate Pareto dominance—could be interesting, but it would be a new quantitative committee problem rather than a continuization of the paper’s named results.

This negative case is not airtight. Under the programme’s broad standard, a high-multiplicity support problem may itself count as a sensible object, and the authors would certainly recognize both proposed questions as their problems. If support-only high-multiplicity models are admitted, both anchors survive. The honest conclusion is therefore that the anti-case identifies a substantial modelling weakness, but cannot establish the requested universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
