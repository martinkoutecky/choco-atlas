The continuous mirror question

Given candidates partitioned into groups \(G_1,\ldots,G_g\), parameters \(\bar\alpha,\bar\beta\), \(k\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) of support size \(\tau\) over complete rankings \(t\) of the candidates, find a top-\(k\) ranking \(\rho\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_t\left(\sum_{a\in D_\rho}|t(a)-\rho(a)|+\sum_{a\notin D_\rho}|t(a)-(k+1)|\right)\), subject to \(\lfloor\alpha_i k\rfloor\le |D_\rho\cap G_i|\le\lceil\beta_i k\rceil\) for every group \(G_i\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings of the fixed candidate set, with mass \(\mu_t\); the decision assigns each candidate to at most one of the top \(k\) positions using integral variables \(x_{a,j}\in\{0,1\}\). Minimize expected generalized Spearman footrule cost, keeping the paper’s candidate-group quotas. The LP has mass-weighted cost coefficients and the same feasible-region structure, giving a polynomial-time exact algorithm in \(d,\tau,g\), and the input bit length \(L\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1 as exact fair top-\(k\) aggregation and Theorem 4.1 as a \(2\)-approximation for fair full ranking. It does not cover the auxiliary matching hardness result in Theorem D.2, the experiments, or the Kendall–tau claims.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the exact fair top-\(k\) problem. Here a type is a complete ranking \(t\in\mathcal S_d\) of the same \(d\) candidates. A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those rankings, with support size \(\tau\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of rankers with type \(t\). Think of a large recommendation or public consultation platform where many people rank a fixed shortlist, and preference archetypes recur. The population can be much larger than \(\tau\). The groups \(G_1,\ldots,G_g\) are groups of candidates, as in the paper—not groups of rankers.

Call the continuous problem Fair Top-\(k\) Rank Aggregation over a Ranking Distribution. Its input is \(d\), a partition of the candidates into \(G_1,\ldots,G_g\), \(k\in[d]\), rational vectors \(\bar\alpha,\bar\beta\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) given by its \(\tau\) supported rankings. Find a top-\(k\) ranking \(\rho\), with domain \(D_\rho\) of size \(k\), minimizing
\[ \sum_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_t\left( \sum_{a\in D_\rho}|t(a)-\rho(a)| +\sum_{a\notin D_\rho}|t(a)-(k+1)| \right) \]
subject to
\[ \lfloor\alpha_i k\rfloor\le |D_\rho\cap G_i|\le \lceil\beta_i k\rceil \quad\text{for every }i\in[g]. \]
A solution is an optimal, integral top-\(k\) list satisfying those quotas.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper: given a set of rankings over \(d\) candidates, their partition into \(g\) groups, \(\bar\alpha,\bar\beta\), and \(k\), a polynomial-time algorithm finds an optimal \((\bar\alpha,\bar\beta)\)-\(k\)-fair top-\(k\) aggregate ranking. In the mirror, the objective becomes an expectation over ranking types. The LP’s costs change to mass-weighted costs, while its fairness constraints and integral output remain the same. The paper’s total-unimodularity argument is therefore the natural route to an exact polynomial-time algorithm, with running time polynomial in \(d,\tau,g\), and the input bit length \(L\). This is Class A. The mirror asks for the same consensus problem over a population distribution; it does not make the output fractional or relax the fairness quotas.

For a finite electorate with \(n_t\) rankers of each type, setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) turns the expected objective into the paper’s sum objective divided by \(n\). The optimal ranking is unchanged. The paper writes its input as a set \(S\subseteq\mathcal S_d\); this mirror treats repeated rankings as weights, the natural high-multiplicity form of its sum over input rankings. If the authors insist on literal set semantics, that is a weighted extension rather than a literal restatement.

A second, worthwhile mirror is Fair Full Rank Aggregation over a Ranking Distribution. Use the same input, and seek a full permutation \(\sigma\) whose top \(k\) positions satisfy the same quotas and whose expected full Spearman footrule cost
\[ \sum_{t\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_t\sum_{a=1}^{d}|t(a)-\sigma(a)| \]
is at most twice the minimum cost of any fair full ranking. The solution is that fair permutation together with the \(2\)-approximation guarantee.

Its anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved here: for a set of rankings over \(d\) candidates, candidate groups, \(\bar\alpha,\bar\beta\), and \(k\), a polynomial-time algorithm finds a \(2\)-approximate fair aggregate full ranking. The paper’s algorithm uses a fair top-\(k\) list followed by a minimum-cost completion matching. Replacing sums over input rankings with mass-weighted sums gives the corresponding distributional costs in both steps, and the approximation proof is linear in those costs. This gives a credible Class A \(2\)-approximation mirror, with complexity polynomial in \(d,\tau,g,L\). Whether the continuous full-ranking problem admits an exact polynomial-time algorithm remains a separate question; Theorem 4.1 does not settle it.

The paper’s Theorem D.2 says that Fair Colorful Weighted Perfect Matching is NP-hard. I would not use it as a hardness anchor for either aggregation problem: it is an auxiliary matching problem, and the paper’s reduction from rank aggregation to matching does not transfer matching hardness back to rank aggregation. A continuous version of that matching problem would also make the matching structure—not the ranker population—the continuous object.

The scope here is the paper’s exact top-\(k\) theorem and its \(2\)-approximation for full ranking. It does not cover the experiments or claim anything for Kendall–tau. The weakest point is that the paper’s examples have only a handful of rankers, and both anchored aggregation results are already polynomial-time results. So this case is not evidence that continuization dissolves hardness. Its honest strength is that a large population of ranking contributors is a sensible regime for the paper’s aggregation objective, and the resulting expected-cost problems preserve the paper’s actual fairness and ranking decisions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is a mismatch in the paper’s literal input model. Both Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 4.1 take a set \(S\subseteq\mathcal S_d\), so each distinct ranking contributes once. They do not encode how many people hold each ranking. The proposed distribution \(\mu\) therefore changes the objective: two electorates with the same support but very different frequencies are indistinguishable to the paper’s set-based model, but can have different continuous optima. The reported data—seven movie raters and 25 football experts—also look more like small panels of distinct sources than a high-multiplicity population.

That objection does not defeat the better mirror, though. Rank aggregation is naturally used with repeated submissions and frequency weights, and a large population ranking a fixed slate is plausible. Distributional rank aggregation already treats rankings as a distribution and uses expected loss, though its cited treatment is axiomatic rather than the fair computational problem here ([Prasad et al., 2015](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/prasad15.pdf)). The fairness constraints concern candidates, not rankers, but that does not prevent the ranker population from supplying the objective weights. The paper’s set convention is a real caveat about fidelity, not a fundamental barrier to a model its authors would likely recognize.

The two anchors survive for the same reason. In Theorem 3.1, replacing unit counts by masses changes the LP’s cost coefficients while leaving its feasible region and integral solutions intact. In Theorem 4.1, the approximation analysis is linear in the aggregation costs, and the completion matching can use mass-weighted edge costs. Neither output tracks individual contributors, so there is no identity information for the continuum to lose.

I can’t honestly make the requested universal negative case. The literal set-based formulation gives a narrow objection to claiming these are exact mirrors of the paper as written. But the weighted population versions are coherent, plausible, and close enough to standard rank aggregation that they pass the programme’s modelling gate. Theorem 3.1 alone is enough to leave a worthwhile continuous mirror standing; Theorem 4.1 stands as well.
