The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of complete agent types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), contract preferences, precedence-ordered slot classes with per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), and priorities over type-contract categories, find a feasible mass assignment \(y_{t,x,s}\) that is slot-stable under sequential slot choice with fractional rationing at priority cutoffs and weakly rank-dominates every other slot-stable mass assignment for each type. Determine whether such an assignment always exists and how to compute it in terms of support size and input bit length.

The model it lives in

A continuum population over complete preference and priority types, with mass split across whole contracts and per-capita slot-class capacities; sequential slot choice and cutoff rationing define stability, and typewise rank dominance defines agent-optimality.

The objection that survived

The stated dominance criterion ranges over every stable mass assignment, while Theorem 1 compares only slot-stable outcomes; the paper’s Examples 3 and 4 show that this distinction matters.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s cumulative-offer selection among slot-stable outcomes. It does not transfer the paper’s separate strategy-proofness, group strategy-proofness, or unambiguous-improvement results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does an agent-optimal slot-stable mass assignment always exist under fractional cutoff rationing?
  • Can one be computed in time polynomial in support size and input bit length?
  • How does the mass outcome relate to finite cohorts with repeated types?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a narrow positive case, anchored on Theorem 1, which the authors prove. It says that the agent-optimal slot-stable outcome in the associated agent–slot market, projected back to the original market, is the outcome of the cumulative offer process. That is a genuine algorithmic result: it identifies what the process computes. The qualification is that the paper states no complexity-class result—no theorem that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or similar. If the anchor rule requires an explicit complexity classification, this paper has no qualifying anchor. Theorem 1 is the closest defensible computational anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a district-wide school assignment for a large annual cohort: tens of thousands of students, but perhaps hundreds or a few thousand recurring combinations of ranked program lists and priority categories. A type would include the student’s preferences over contracts, acceptable options, and priority category at every slot. The interesting regime is \(N\gg\tau\): many students, relatively few complete types.

Here is the lead mirror: Mass Slot-Stable Selection. Its input is a finite set of types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); contracts available to each type and its ranking over them; precedence-ordered slot classes at each branch; rational per-capita capacity \(\kappa_s\) for each slot class; and each slot’s priority order over type–contract categories. Its output is a mass assignment \(y_{t,x,s}\), with unmatched mass \(u_t\), satisfying \(\sum_{x,s}y_{t,x,s}+u_t=\mu_t\) and the slot capacities. Each unit of mass receives at most one contract; the assignment records how many members of a cohort receive each whole contract.

Extend the paper’s sequential slot-choice rule to mass: slots fill in precedence order, take mass by priority, and may split a tied priority class at the cutoff. Require stability in the paper’s sense, with sets replaced by measures: there must be no nonzero mass of agents who prefer a proposed contract and whom the branch’s sequential choice rule would accept. The selection objective is an agent-optimal mass outcome: for every type \(t\) and every preference-rank cutoff \(r\), the output’s mass receiving one of \(t\)’s top \(r\) options must be at least as large as in any other stable mass assignment. In symbols, if \(x_{t,r}\) is the option at rank \(r\), require \(F_y(t,r)\ge F_z(t,r)\) for every stable assignment \(z\), where \(F_y(t,r)=\sum_{x\succeq_t x_{t,r}}\sum_s y_{t,x,s}\). The task is to return such an assignment, or report that no stable assignment with this dominance property exists.

I would expect this problem to be Class A, with an algorithm based on mass deferred acceptance or a finite optimization formulation. Theorem 1 gives a concrete reason to ask whether the agent–slot reduction survives aggregation; it does not prove that it does. The useful follow-up questions are whether an agent-optimal stable mass assignment always exists, whether it can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and how its answer relates to finite cohorts with \(N_t\) students of each type.

This mirrors the paper’s cumulative-offer result: a stable selection with the same slot precedence and priority structure, and an aggregate counterpart to agent-optimality. It does not claim to mirror Theorem 4’s group strategy-proofness; incentives for a positive-mass cohort are a separate question.

The weak point is the priority model. The paper assumes strict linear priorities over individual contracts, so individual tie-break positions can make almost every student a distinct type. A moderate-\(\tau\) mirror needs priority classes and mass rationing at a tie cutoff, as in a large-lottery limit. That is a substantive co-modification, though it preserves the paper’s central slot-specific priority and precedence structure. If authors or referees would reject that extension, the high-multiplicity regime largely disappears.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is not a complexity result. It proves that the cumulative offer process selects the projection of the agent-optimal slot-stable outcome. The paper gives no complexity classification or complexity bound for a problem. Its process makes at most \(|X|\) proposals, with finite branch choices at each step, so the paper’s main computational contribution is a structural correspondence and mechanism guarantee, not an unresolved algorithmic bottleneck of the kind ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed Mass Slot-Stable Selection also asks for more than Theorem 1 establishes. The theorem’s optimality is among slot-stable outcomes; the paper explicitly warns that not every stable outcome comes from a slot-stable one. Example 3 has no agent-optimal stable outcome at all, and Example 4 shows the cumulative-offer outcome can be Pareto dominated by another stable outcome. So dominance over *every* stable mass assignment is not an aggregate restatement of Theorem 1. If the mirror is repaired to ask only for a slot-stable outcome, it tracks the paper’s agent–slot reduction closely: the main new step is to aggregate its offer process and split mass at priority cutoffs.

That repair also requires a changed priority model. In the paper, each slot ranks contracts strictly. If priority rank is part of an agent’s complete type, a strict order gives agents distinct priority positions, undermining \(N\gg\tau\). Grouping them instead into priority classes and splitting mass at a cutoff is plausible, but it changes the priority structure and stability notion. A lottery-based version might be natural, though then the lottery rank itself must either be represented in the type space or abstracted into fractional rationing.

These points make the anchor weaker than the positive case suggests; they do not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large school cohort with repeated preference lists and shared priority categories is a credible high-multiplicity regime. If ChoCo treats the priority-class model as a recognized extension, an aggregate cumulative-offer question could be worth formalizing. The honest negative is therefore limited: this paper supplies no named complexity result for the proposed mirror to continue, and its closest faithful mass version looks like an aggregation of an already finite offer process. I cannot make a convincing universal case that no useful continuous question survives.
