What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns only Proposition 5’s Condorcet-rule application; it does not cover the general implementation characterization, other applications, or farsighted-robustness results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no numbered computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. It mentions that computing a vNM stable set is undecidable, but attributes that claim to Deng and Papadimitriou (1994); it is not a result proved or numbered here. The paper’s theorems characterize implementation, rather than classify an algorithmic problem’s complexity.

The strongest substantive foothold for a positive mirror is Proposition 5, proved in this paper: if the preference domain is a Condorcet domain, meaning a Condorcet winner exists at every profile, then the Condorcet rule is implementable in the vNM stable set by a rights structure. The paper describes the construction as requiring majority consent to replace the status quo. This is a useful anchor for a continuous analogue, but it does not qualify as a computational anchor under the stated rules.

That analogue could be Continuous Condorcet Rights Implementation. Let \(Z\) be a finite set of alternatives and \(T\) a finite set of strict rankings. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with support size \(\tau\). Given an explicitly listed finite domain \(D\) of such distributions, ask whether one rights structure implements the Condorcet rule for every \(\mu\in D\), and, if so, return that structure. Use states \(S=Z\) and let any coalition with mass greater than \(1/2\) have the right to replace one state with another. At \(\mu\), a transition is a dominance move exactly when more than half the mass strictly prefers the destination. Restrict \(D\) to societies with a unique strict-majority Condorcet winner. Then that winner dominates every other state, and the singleton containing it is the unique vNM stable set. The implementation is one fixed majority-rights structure across the domain.

A plausible regime is a large electorate with many residents but relatively few distinct ballots—for example, a city electorate with preferences restricted by a common single-peaked ordering of policy alternatives. Mass then describes the share of each voter type, and the shared ranking is a complete type description. The authors have an application directly in voting, so they could recognize this as a continuous-population version of their Condorcet implementation result. For the explicitly listed-domain problem above, I would expect tractability: check each society’s pairwise mass totals and construct the same rights structure. That is a modest algorithmic question, not a complexity result supplied by the paper.

The mirror covers the Condorcet-rule application, not the paper’s full characterization or its general implementation results. Its weakest point is also the decisive limitation: the paper studies a normative existence question, not the computation of rights structures or a complexity landscape. The proposed continuous version is plausible, but it creates a computational question the authors did not ask or answer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central problem for a ChoCo mirror is that this paper proves no computational result. Theorems 1–10 and the applications characterize which social choice functions admit rights structures under stated preference conditions. They do not give an algorithmic problem with an input, an output, and a complexity bound. The paper’s mention of undecidability is attributed to Deng and Papadimitriou (1994), not established here.

Proposition 5 is the strongest anchor, and its population version is sensible: a large electorate with a few ballot types, represented by masses, and majority rights between alternatives. But the proposed computational task adds little to that result. Given a list \(D\) of societies promised to have unique strict-majority Condorcet winners, the algorithm can return the same majority-rights structure every time. If it must check the promise, it computes pairwise mass totals for each listed society, in time polynomial in \(|D|,m,\tau,\) and \(L\). That is a straightforward tallying task, not computation of an implementing structure.

A more ambitious version could ask for rights-structure synthesis over every rational distribution in a succinctly described domain. The paper supplies no finite representation for such a domain, an SCF over it, or a continuum rights structure. Choosing one—say, circuits for rules and a restricted language of anonymous, measurable rights—would define a new computational problem whose complexity depends on those choices. The paper’s general classification also permits rights assigned to particular coalitions; a distribution over preference types alone does not encode those identity-based distinctions. Restricting rights to mass-based rules is natural for the Condorcet example, but it is an additional modeling choice.

So the strongest negative case is against this as a worthwhile computational mirror: the paper’s results are normative classifications, while its best population analogue yields a fixed construction and a simple weighted majority check. I would not claim that no continuous mirror is sensible in any scenario. The voting application makes a continuous Condorcet implementation a credible axiomatic extension. Its weakness as a ChoCo target is that the proposed extension does not expose the kind of computational question the programme is meant to chart.
