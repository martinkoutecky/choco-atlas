The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per capita capacities \(\kappa_s\) with \(\sum_s\kappa_s<1\), and list limits \(k,\ell\), decide whether \(M_{GS_k}>0\) and \(M_{BM_\ell}=0\). Each type records a student’s known priority tier, strict school ranking, and cardinal utilities; all schools share the tier order, with a common uniformly random tie break within each tier. In the population limit, evaluate each type’s truthful list and best permitted unilateral report by expected utility over its unknown tie break position, holding the aggregate society fixed; \(M_A\) is the total mass of types with a strictly profitable report under mechanism \(A\).

The model it lives in

A finite set \(T\) of applicant types, each with a preference ranking, cardinal utilities, and known priority tier, has rational masses \(\mu_t\). Schools have rational per capita capacities \(\kappa_s\); the constrained Boston and Gale–Shapley mechanisms use the shared tier order and uniformly random tie breaking within tiers. Each negligible applicant compares truthful and reported lists using expected utility over their unknown position in the tier.

What the mirror covers

The mirror targets Theorem 1’s separation between \(GS_k\) and \(BM_\ell\); it leaves the simulation based comparisons and Example 2’s comparison between \(GS_k\) and \(GS_\ell\) aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can positive mass separation, \(M_{GS_k}>0\) and \(M_{BM_\ell}=0\), occur in this tier based population limit?
  • For finite \(T\) and a fixed number of tiers, how hard is it to compute each mechanism’s manipulable mass and witness reports?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is one reasonable anchor, with an important qualification: the paper proves no named complexity classification such as NP-hardness or polynomial-time solvability. Its strongest named computational result is Theorem 1, proved here. It says that when all schools share a strict priority order and students know their own ranks but not others’ ranks, then for \(n\ge 4\), \(n>\sum_s q_s\), and \(m>k>1\), for any Boston list limit \(\ell\), there is an ordinal preference profile on which \(GS_k\) is manipulable but \(BM_\ell\) is not. The theorem’s claim holds for every cardinal utility profile consistent with that ordinal profile. This is a structural result about strategic behavior, not a complexity result; it is the best basis this paper offers for a continuous mirror.

My lead mirror would be Positive-Mass Manipulability Separation. Consider a large centralized school-admissions system with a small number of common exam-score priority tiers. Applicants know their tier but not their position within its random tie-break. A finite set \(T\) of types records each applicant’s strict school ranking, cardinal utilities over schools and the outside option, and priority tier. The input is rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita school capacities \(\kappa_s\) with \(\sum_s\kappa_s<1\), and list limits \(k,\ell\). Applicants know the aggregate type distribution. The mechanisms use the shared tier order and random order within each tier; the continuous outcome is the limit as type counts and capacities scale together. Individual assignments remain whole schools or the outside option, while assignments averaged over a type’s mass may be fractional.

For each type \(t\) and mechanism \(A\), compare the type’s expected utility from its truthful list with the best expected utility from any permitted reported list, averaging over its unknown tie-break position. Let \(M_A\) be the total mass of types for which some permitted report gives a strict gain. The problem is: given this instance, decide whether \(M_{GS_k}>0\) and \(M_{BM_\ell}=0\), returning the manipulated types and witnessing reports when the answer is yes. This asks whether the theorem’s separation survives at positive population scale, rather than merely for one student. The mirror changes population and capacity together; its object is not continuity in the outcome space.

The regime is plausible for a large urban admissions programme or a pool spanning multiple admissions years: many applicants, but comparatively few recurring combinations of preference pattern, valuation profile, and score tier. The paper itself notes that common exam priorities are used in practice. I would expect this problem to be Class A for fixed \(k\) and a fixed number of tiers: the common priority order suggests a finite sequence of allocation cutoffs, and with finitely many types those cutoffs should be piecewise linear in rank. A useful next question is whether computing best reports remains FPT in \(k\) when \(k\) is part of the input. The paper does not establish either algorithmic claim.

The soft spot is substantial. Theorem 1 assumes each student knows their exact rank; this mirror instead groups applicants by known priority tier and averages over an unknown tie-break position. The paper mentions lack of own-rank information as relevant, but leaves the generalization of the theorem’s GS part open. Also, its finite witness could amount to one manipulator whose population share vanishes as \(n\) grows. So the theorem does not imply positive-mass separation. The case for the mirror is that tier-level information and large repeated cohorts make the population object credible, while the question of whether manipulation persists at positive mass is a meaningful extension of the paper’s central comparison. That is a research question the authors might recognize, but it is not a result already contained in their theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the paper’s strongest anchor, but its witness is microscopic in two ways. It names one manipulable student, whose population share is at most \(1/n\). And in the unit-capacity construction, that student gets the undercontested school only if the relevant group of about \(a=\Theta(n)\) students all fall below them in the unknown priority order. With the paper’s uniform prior, that event has probability on the order of \(1/\binom{n-1}{a}\) when there are \(a\) lower-priority slots. The theorem counts any strict expected gain, however small; with bounded utilities, this gain vanishes as the population grows. So the theorem establishes a finite existence separation, not evidence of a positive-mass separation in the limit.

One could seek a positive-mass result by giving students priority tiers but hiding their positions within each tier, as the proposed mirror does. That is a plausible admissions model, and the paper itself flags the no-known-rank case as an open generalization. It also changes the theorem’s information structure: the theorem’s student knows their exact rank. Preserving that information requires rank or rank quantile to be part of a complete type. In the limit, each such type has zero mass, and the proposed finite-support model no longer applies. Pooling ranks restores positive-mass types, but asks a new Bayesian question rather than carrying the theorem’s witness into a continuum.

The proposed computation may also be modest. With finitely many types, tiers and permitted lists, a common priority order, and fixed \(k\), an atomless limit should make the composition ahead of each priority quantile deterministic. Expected utilities then look likely to reduce to evaluating finitely many reports against seat cutoffs and integrating over quantiles. That could be a useful result, but it does not look like the sort of optimization or complexity boundary that motivates ChoCo’s central examples. This is an informed expectation, not a complexity result. The paper’s simulation statistic raises a separate issue: it measures the share of sampled *preference profiles* on which a mechanism is manipulable. Matching that statistic continuously would require a distribution over whole societies, adding an outer probability model rather than just continuizing the population.

The negative case is strongest against the theorem-anchored mirror: the theorem’s positive gain can disappear at scale, while the better positive-mass formulation changes an explicit modeling assumption and may reduce to cutoff calculations. It is not airtight against every useful mirror. The tier-based case is recognizable as a question the authors could pursue, especially since they identify missing own-rank information as future work. The honest objection is that this paper gives little reason to expect that question to yield a substantial computational landscape.
