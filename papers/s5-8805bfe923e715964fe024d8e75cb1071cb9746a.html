The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s DA menu-in-outcome problem. It covers one result, and it is a qualified case: the paper’s lower bound concerns the memory needed to describe a mechanism while querying individual preferences, so the continuous version changes the computational input in a substantial way.

The anchor is the impossibility theorem in §5.2, referenced as thrm:MTR-DA-LB. The supplied text prints no numeric theorem number, so I would identify it by section and label rather than invent one. Proved in this paper, it states that DA has no applicant-proposing, linear-memory menu-in-outcome description; with \(n\) applicants and \(n\) institutions, *any* applicant-proposing menu-in-outcome description requires \(\Omega(n^2)\) memory. Here, linear memory means \(\widetilde O(n)\). The proof is a direct combinatorial argument, not a consequence of a complexity-theoretic assumption: a family of preference profiles forces the description first to learn and then to retain enough information to distinguish \(2^{\Omega(n^2)}\) cases.

A continuous counterpart could be called Mass-DA Menu-in-Outcome. Fix \(m\) institutions with per-capita capacities \(q_h\). The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support of \(\tau\) applicant types, with rational masses summing to one. A type gives an applicant’s ranking and the applicant’s priority class at every institution; thus, people grouped in one type agree on every feature relevant to the matching. Institutions rank priority classes, with a specified rule for splitting mass within a tied class. Capacities, masses, and type descriptions are part of the input, and their bit length is \(L\).

The instance also includes a focal applicant of negligible mass, with a fixed priority vector and a report that arrives in a second phase. In phase one, given \(\mu\) and the focal applicant’s priorities, compute her menu: the institutions to which she would be assigned if she reported that institution alone as acceptable. In phase two, given her reported ranking, return her highest-ranked institution in that menu and the complete type-to-institution allocation produced by applicant-proposing mass DA. The solution is the menu, the focal applicant’s assignment, and an exact rational allocation flow satisfying capacities and the defined mass-DA rule. There is no optimization objective here: this is a mechanism computation, so the required output is its prescribed outcome. The main question is whether it can be computed exactly in time and working space polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), or whether superpolynomial resource lower bounds remain.

A plausible regime is a large school district assigning a much larger applicant cohort than it has distinct combinations of rankings and priority categories: applicants may number in the tens of thousands, while the support of repeated preference and priority types is much smaller. Capacities scale with the cohort, and splitting mass within a tied class represents the aggregate assignment. This is a population-continuous model with an explicit high-multiplicity interpretation. It preserves the paper’s central object—the menu followed by a choice and an outcome—and the paper’s §3 characterization of DA’s menu through institution-proposing DA without the focal applicant gives a natural starting point for the mass version.

My expectation is Class A in regimes where \(\tau\) is moderate: the individual-profile family behind the \(\Omega(n^2)\) bound may compress to a short list of type masses, allowing type-level DA to compute the menu and allocation. The follow-up question is where that compression stops helping: how do resources grow when \(\tau\) grows, or when the priority descriptions themselves encode many distinct relationships?

The weakest point is that this is not the theorem’s original computation model. Its lower bound concerns a description that reads individual preference lists in a specified order; the proposed mirror gives an algorithm aggregate masses as input. The paper does not establish that the lower bound disappears under this change, and an opponent can reasonably say that the continuous problem is a new aggregate matching computation rather than a limit of the theorem’s description-complexity question. The case survives because the mechanism, focal menu, and two-stage description remain recognizable, while the high-multiplicity regime itself is sensible. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s whole agenda.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The §5.2 theorem is the one serious anchor: it gives an \(\Omega(n^2)\)-memory lower bound for applicant-proposing menu-in-outcome descriptions of DA. So I would not argue that the paper lacks a computational result. The problem is that the theorem measures a very particular object: a description that processes individual applicants’ preference lists in a prescribed order, calculates one named applicant’s menu before learning her report, then retains enough information to produce the full named matching afterward. It is not a lower bound for computing DA from an aggregate population input.

The proposed Mass-DA problem changes that object. It asks an algorithm to compute an allocation flow from type masses, with a marked applicant making a report in a second phase. That may be a sensible matching problem, but its complexity does not mirror the theorem’s description-memory question. The theorem’s applicant-proposing condition and memory bound concern how information is acquired and retained across the two phases; giving an algorithm the distribution as input removes that query discipline.

There is also a real obstacle to getting the theorem’s hard instances into a high-multiplicity regime without changing the matching model. Priorities in the paper are strict orders over named applicants. If priority positions count as part of an agent’s complete type, applicants cannot be interchangeable: each has a distinct priority position. And the lower-bound construction’s arbitrary sets \(B_j\) encode a large amount of preference information across applicants; that information does not disappear just because the input is written as a distribution. In the hard family, the support can grow with \(n\), and the type descriptions themselves carry the combinatorics.

One can replace strict priorities with priority classes and specify how mass is split within a class. That is a plausible co-modification, but it changes the role of the focal applicant. At zero mass, she cannot change the aggregate allocation; at positive mass, she is a distinguished atom whose effects depend on the chosen tie-breaking and allocation rules. A marked infinitesimal applicant against a mass background could still have a meaningful menu, but its computation would be a new individual-response problem in a continuum market, not a continuous version of the paper’s lower bound.

That is the strongest case against this anchor, but it is not airtight. A menu for a test applicant against a high-multiplicity background is recognizable and could be worth studying. The proponent’s specific expectation that the theorem’s hard family compresses to a small support is unsupported, though: the construction’s information lies in varied preferences and priorities, not merely in how many copies of each type there are.
