The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a high-multiplicity combinatorial-auction market with a finite catalogue of \(m\) item types and a continuum of bidder mass. I would anchor it on Theorem 1, rather than overclaiming that every detail of the paper’s final mechanism already survives unchanged.

A type \(t\in T\) is a complete bidder valuation \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb R_{\ge0}\), including all parameters relevant to demand and payment. The population is \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the mass of bidders with valuation \(v_t\), and \(|T|=\tau\ll N\) in the intended regime. To keep the market nondegenerate, let \(b_j\) be the supply of item \(j\) per unit population mass. In an \(N\)-agent refinement, there are \(N\mu_t\) bidders of type \(t\) and \(Nb_j\) copies of item \(j\). Items remain indivisible: each unit of mass receives an integral bundle \(S\subseteq M\).

An aggregate allocation is \(x_{t,S}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(S\), satisfying

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \]

and

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le b_j \]

for every \(j\). Its welfare is

\[ \operatorname{SW}(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq M}x_{t,S}v_t(S). \]

The fractional-looking variable \(x\) does not give any agent a fractional bundle; it records which measurable portions of a type mass receive different integral bundles.

This is plausible in a large market of standardized buyers—say, many firms demanding bundles of cloud-resource slots or contract components—with a small number of repeated submodular or XOS valuation profiles. The relevant comparison is not “many voters versus few candidates,” but \(N\) agents versus \(\tau\) distinct valuation types. A national-scale market with millions of bidders and perhaps hundreds of recurring valuation profiles is a genuine high-multiplicity regime. The supply scaling is a forced co-modification: without it, a continuum of bidders would face only a vanishing number of one-off items. The population, however, remains the continuous object.

The paper’s strongest anchor for this is Theorem 1, proved in the paper. It states:

For a combinatorial auction with \(n\) submodular, even XOS, bidders and \(m\) items, under Assumption 1, PriceLearningMechanism is universally truthful, uses \(O(n)\) demand queries and polynomial time, and achieves an approximation ratio \(O((\log\log m)^3)\) in expectation.

Assumption 1 requires that every supporting price of every item in every valuation clause is either \(0\) or lies in \([\psi_{\min},\psi_{\max}]\), that \(\Psi=\psi_{\max}/\psi_{\min}\) is polynomially bounded in \(m\), and that \(\psi_{\min},\psi_{\max}\) are supplied to the mechanism.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\[ \textsc{BoundedPrice-XOS-Truthful-Auction}_{\infty}. \]

Given \(m\), a finite type set \(T\), masses \(\mu\), item supplies \(b\), demand/value oracles for the valuations \(v_t\), and bounds \(\psi_{\min},\psi_{\max}\), design a universally truthful randomized mechanism using \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) type-level oracle queries such that

\[ \mathbb E[\operatorname{SW}(x)] \ge \frac{\operatorname{OPT}_{\infty}} {O((\log\log m)^3)}. \]

Here \(\operatorname{OPT}_{\infty}\) is the maximum welfare over the aggregate allocations \(x\) above. A solution must specify the mechanism, the posted prices and payments, and the resulting allocation rule. For every fixing of the random coins, each mass unit must face prices determined independently of its own report and receive a demanded bundle. To prevent truthfulness from becoming merely a consequence of zero individual influence, the intended semantics require finite integral refinements: whenever \(N\mu_t\) and \(Nb_j\) are integral, the mass mechanism should induce a universally truthful \(N\)-agent implementation whose normalized aggregate allocation converges to \(x\).

The paper’s price-learning construction has a natural mass version. Split each type mass into random submasses corresponding to the groups \(N_1,\ldots,N_{\beta+1}\), run the fixed-price auctions on those submasses, and update the price tree using aggregate allocations. Identical types need only one demand query per price vector, so the query burden becomes polynomial in \(\tau\), rather than in \(N\). The random ordering of individual bidders becomes a random measurable ordering of the mass. The paper’s key estimates are expectation inequalities over randomly selected bidders, so they are unusually well suited to this replacement.

I would expect this continuous problem to be Class A. There is a direct posted-price route: the Learnable-Or-Allocatable argument can be stated with mass-weighted supporting prices, and the paper’s \(O((\log\log m)^3)\) price-learning schedule remains meaningful. There is also an independent optimization reason. The continuous welfare problem has the configuration formulation

\[ \max \sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S), \]

whose dual has variables \(u_t\) and \(p_j\), with constraints

\[ u_t+\sum_{j\in S}p_j\ge v_t(S). \]

Separating these constraints for a fixed type \(t\) is exactly the demand problem

\[ \max_{S\subseteq M} \left(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\right). \]

Thus, under the paper’s demand-oracle model and standard rationality assumptions, aggregate welfare is amenable to the configuration-LP/separation machinery. That does not automatically prove universal truthfulness, but it shows that the continuous welfare object is computationally substantial rather than a weakened story.

This is not a collision with the paper. The paper never represents society by \(\mu\), never compresses repeated bidders into valuation types, and never proves a mass-allocation or high-multiplicity theorem. Its random partitions are over named bidders. The continuous question therefore asks what happens when the paper’s own mechanism is transported to the regime it naturally suggests.

I am deliberately not using a hardness anchor: the supplied paper has no numbered NP-hardness or completeness theorem of its own. Its statements about exact welfare hardness and hard demand queries are cited background or informal observations, not named results proved here.

The weakest point is truthfulness. In a genuinely atomless population, a single agent cannot affect aggregate prices, so dominant truthfulness can become too easy. Moreover, replacing the paper’s random finite ordering by a computable mass auction requires a formal fluid implementation, not just an appeal to analogy. The finite-refinement requirement above is meant to keep the question honest. If that requirement fails, the safe positive claim is still a strong one: the paper’s bounded-price welfare-and-posted-price problem has a natural Class A continuous-population formulation, while the exact universal-truthfulness correspondence remains an open part of the mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 1’s computational content is not population allocation; it is truthful mechanism design with individually strategic, finite bidders. The proposed continuum removes precisely that object.

In the atomless model, changing one bidder’s report does not change \(\mu\), prices, or the aggregate allocation. Universal truthfulness therefore becomes price-taking behavior: once prices are fixed, each infinitesimal agent simply chooses a demanded bundle. The configuration LP

\[ \max \sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S) \]

is a legitimate high-multiplicity welfare problem, but its dual separation oracle says nothing about universal truthfulness, payments, or the paper’s finite-agent incentive constraint. It is consequently not a continuous version of Theorem 1; it is a different competitive-equilibrium or welfare-optimization problem.

The proposed finite-refinement condition does not repair this cheaply. To preserve the paper’s semantics, a refinement with \(N\) bidders and per-capita supplies \(b_j\) requires \(Nb_j\) physical item copies. The theorem then sees

\[ m_N=N\sum_j b_j \]

items, not \(m\) item types. Its query bound is \(O(N)\), and its approximation parameter is \(O((\log\log m_N)^3)\). Compressing the copies and bidders back to \((m,\tau)\) requires a new batch version of the mechanism, not an interpretation of the existing theorem.

The same problem appears in the proof. PriceLearningMechanism depends on random subsets and sequential random orders of named bidders, with residual item sets changing after every demand query. A “random measurable ordering of mass” is not an implementation of that process. In a fluid market, items may be partially sold, whereas the price-update rule treats each item as sold or unsold. Replacing this by mass-weighted updates would require a new mechanism and a new Learnable-Or-Allocatable proof.

Nor does convergence of aggregate allocations establish universal truthfulness. Universal truthfulness must hold for every fixed random outcome and every individual deviation at every finite \(N\). A sequence of finite mechanisms can converge to a price-taking limit while retaining nonzero individual effects at every finite stage. Payments and an integral decomposition must be supplied and verified separately.

There is a genuine positive research direction here, but it is a new high-multiplicity, clone-symmetric auction model. The paper’s theorem does not establish that model’s compressed algorithm, truthful implementation, or finite-to-continuum dictionary. Thus the proponent has not shown a continuous mirror of Theorem 1—only a plausible adjacent project.

Still, this negative case is not airtight. Theorem 1 is a real computational anchor, and repeated bidder types with replicated supplies are economically sensible. A carefully proved type-level batching theorem could make the mirror worthwhile. I therefore cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists; the defensible conclusion is only that the proponent’s current formulation has not yet preserved the paper’s central computational object.
