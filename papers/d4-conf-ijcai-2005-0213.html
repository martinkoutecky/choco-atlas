The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s Theorem 3 (Maximality), proved here. It says that if \(\mathcal M\) is the class of modular utility functions, then every class \(\mathcal F\) strictly containing \(\mathcal M\) fails to permit 1-deal negotiation. More concretely, for any nonmodular utility \(u_1\), the proof constructs a modular utility for another agent and an initial allocation from which no sequence of rational single-resource deals can reach a welfare optimum. The paper’s construction gives a starting state with no beneficial first 1-deal.

This is a named outcome-guarantee result about computation by negotiation, though not a conventional complexity classification such as NP-hardness or membership in P. The paper’s discussion cites an NP-hard reachability result by Dunne et al.; that result is not a numbered theorem proved here, so I would not use it as an anchor.

A plausible regime is a large network of facilities—say, hospitals or depots—exchanging standardized, indivisible equipment. Many facilities share the same utility template over bundles, so the number \(N\) of agents can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct utility types. An agent’s type is its complete utility function over bundles, including any other preference detail the problem uses. Resource categories have fungible copies, with supplies scaling with population size. Each agent still receives an integral bundle; only the fraction of agents in each type-and-bundle state is continuous.

The corresponding problem is Mass One-Resource Optimal Reachability. An instance has a finite set \(G\) of resource categories, \(\tau\) utility types with explicitly represented rational functions \(u_t:2^G\to\mathbb Q\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), and an initial distribution \(x^0_{t,B}\) of mass across types \(t\) and integral bundles \(B\subseteq G\). A feasible state \(x\) satisfies
\[ \sum_{B\subseteq G}x_{t,B}=\mu_t \]
for each type and preserves each category’s supply
\[ q_g=\sum_{t,B:\,g\in B}x^0_{t,B}. \]
Its welfare is \(W(x)=\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}u_t(B)\).

A 1-deal of mass \(\delta>0\) pairs equal mass of type \(t\) holding \(B\) with type \(s\) holding \(D\), where \(g\in B\setminus D\), and transfers one copy of \(g\) per pair. Thus \(\delta\) moves from \((t,B)\) and \((s,D)\) to \((t,B\setminus\{g\})\) and \((s,D\cup\{g\})\). It is rational when it strictly increases \(W\), matching the paper’s side-payment criterion. The question is whether a finite sequence of such deals can reach a welfare-maximizing state; a yes solution is the sequence of deal types and mass amounts.

The connection to Theorem 3 is unusually direct. Duplicate its two roles into two equal-mass cohorts, each initially holding its role’s bundle from the paper’s construction, and scale resource supplies accordingly. Within either homogeneous cohort, no member can make a one-resource transfer to another member who holds the same bundle. Between cohorts, each possible first transfer has the same per-pair surplus as the corresponding 1-deal in the paper’s witness. Since those deals are not beneficial, transferring any positive mass is not beneficial either. Yet the better joint allocation remains feasible. Splitting the cohorts into smaller masses cannot open the route because every possible first move still has negative surplus. This makes the nonmodular obstruction a credible continuous-population mirror, rather than a claim that arbitrary discrete hardness automatically transfers.

For modular utilities, the natural mass analogue is also promising: each resource has a type-dependent marginal value independent of the rest of the bundle, so welfare separates by resource. Transfers can move mass from lower-value holders to higher-value nonholders until the relevant type capacities are filled. This suggests an efficient finite-path algorithm on explicit bundle-state input. For the general reachability problem, I would leave the complexity open; if pressed for a prediction, I would investigate Class C because the mass-state path question is new. The paper does not establish that hardness, and its cited discrete NP-hardness cannot simply be carried over when fractional mass transfers may create new paths.

The main weakness is a change in the convergence claim. The paper’s modular result says any sequence of rational 1-deals eventually reaches an optimum, relying on the finite number of allocations. With continuous masses, arbitrarily small improving transfers can produce infinite sequences. The reachability problem above asks whether some finite improving path exists, which is weaker. The authors should recognize the model as their same negotiation problem over repeated types and scaled supplies, but they might reject that altered quantifier as a mirror of their convergence guarantee. So I would pitch this as a strong, narrow extension of Theorem 3’s maximality boundary—not as a new continuous complexity classification, and not as a mirror of the paper’s full termination theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that Theorem 3 is a schedule guarantee, not a complexity result: every sequence of rational 1-deals is supposed to reach an optimum. Its proof relies on finitely many allocations. With continuous masses, even modular utilities allow an infinite sequence of improving deals that never reaches an optimum. For example, let a mass \(1/2\) of low-value agents hold one resource and a mass \(1/2\) of high-value agents hold none; the agents value possession at \(0\) and \(1\), respectively. Transfer \(\delta_n=2^{-(n+3)}\) mass at step \(n\). Every deal is rational, but the sequence leaves positive resource mass with the low-value agents, so its limit is still suboptimal. Requiring fairness or changing “eventually reaches” to a limit condition adds a dynamic assumption absent from the paper and moves toward an analytic convergence question.

The proposed finite-path problem avoids that failure, but changes the central quantifier from every rational-deal sequence to some sequence. That is a planner’s reachability problem, not the paper’s guarantee that a myopic negotiation process cannot get stuck. A more faithful continuous version therefore loses the paper’s central guarantee; the version that preserves a useful computational question is a different problem.

That objection does not defeat the proposed mirror outright. Repeated facilities with the same utility functions and scaled supplies are a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s stranded-state construction appears to survive duplication: no positive-mass first deal is beneficial, while a better allocation remains feasible. The class-boundary question could also be asked for mass reachability. So the negative case is weak precisely there: I can argue that the direct mirror breaks at the termination guarantee, but I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The finite-path formulation remains a plausible extension, even if it is not a direct mirror of Theorem 3.
