So there are no eligible anchors, no per-anchor continuous problems to state, and no complexity trichotomy to predict. The strongest honest case for a ChoCo mirror is that the paper suggests a population-level question—not that it establishes a computational one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to mirror. Results 1–4 are empirical findings about quiz scores, bidding, and efficiency; the paper contains no complexity theorem or algorithmic problem. That is the strongest reason against a ChoCo mirror.

The best candidate anchors are Results 2 and 3: the effect of announcing strategy-proofness on sincere bidding and efficiency. A natural large-market version could put mass over valuation and behavioral types, then measure sincerity or efficiency as averages over that society. Those quantities are mathematically well-defined. But the experiment supplies no rule that maps a person’s valuation, experience, and receipt of advice to their bid. Once that behavioral rule is supplied, the aggregate statistics can be integrated over the type distribution; the computational content comes from the added behavioral model, not from a problem this paper formulates or solves.

A stronger reconstruction would model bidders’ decisions and matching explicitly. But preserving the experiment’s three-person groups leaves a continuum of repeated small auctions: with a finite type support, aggregate outcomes can be computed by summing over type triples. Scaling the auction itself to a large market would require changing the supply and interaction structure. Either reconstruction could motivate further research, but neither yields a computational question anchored in these results.

The most promising addition would be an advice-design problem: choose what advice to give, or whom to target, to improve aggregate efficiency. That could be made computational. Yet it requires specifying the available advice, how people respond to it, and what constraints the designer faces—none of which this paper models as a computational problem. Calling that a mirror would overstate the connection.

So the proponent’s suggestion is plausible as a prompt for a new behavioral auction model, but it does not identify a continuous computational mirror of a result in this paper. The negative case is strongest on that boundary; it cannot establish that no useful large-population research inspired by the experiment exists.
