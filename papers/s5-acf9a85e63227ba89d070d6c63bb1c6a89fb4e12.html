The continuous mirror question

Given alternatives \(A\), a finite support \(T\) of complete rational value profiles \(v_t:A\to\mathbb{Q}\) on a common scale, and rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), let \(P_t\) be the weak order induced by \(v_t\). For each ranking \(o\in O(A)\), define \(W_\mu(o)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\sum_{(a,b)\in o\cap P_t}(v_t(a)-v_t(b))\). Find all rankings maximizing \(W_\mu\) and return their transitive-closure group order, as in Theorem 1(a).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution over complete preference-strength profiles; the decision variable is a group ranking \(o\in O(A)\), and the objective is its mass-weighted sum of member scores \(W_\mu(o)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the group-ranking selection in Theorem 1(a); it leaves the per-member value-function estimation procedure and Theorem 1(b)–(d) untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does maximizing \(W_\mu(o)\) admit an algorithm polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\), or is it hard as \(m\) varies?
  • Do restricted support size or profile structure yield efficient algorithms?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: Theorem 1(a) is a usable anchor under the prompt’s exception for a named optimization formulation that the paper computes with. It is proved in this paper. It says that the group weak order \(P_g\) is the transitive closure of the rankings \(o\in O(A)\) that maximize \(w_g(o)=\sum_i w_i(o)\), where each member’s score is \(w_i(o)=\sum_{(a,b)\in o\cap P_i}(v_i(a)-v_i(b))\). Equivalently, \(w_g(o)=\sum_{(a,b)\in o}\sum_{i\in I(a,b)}(v_i(a)-v_i(b))\). PRESEO computes this selection by evaluating rankings. This is an optimization characterization, not a complexity classification: the paper proves no P, hardness, or parameterized-complexity result.

My lead mirror is Continuous Second-Order Rank Aggregation. Let \(A\) be the alternatives and let \(T\) be the support of the society. A type \(t\) is a complete normalized value-difference function \(v_t:A\to\mathbb Q\), with \(\min_a v_t(a)=0\) and \(\max_a v_t(a)=1\); it induces the member’s first-order ranking \(P_t\) and, through the paper’s model, their second-order preference over rankings. The instance gives each type a rational mass \(\mu_t\geq 0\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Thus \(\tau=|T|\), and the rational values and masses have binary input length \(L\).

For every group ranking \(o\in O(A)\), define
\[ w_t(o)=\sum_{(a,b)\in o\cap P_t}\bigl(v_t(a)-v_t(b)\bigr), \qquad W_\mu(o)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t w_t(o). \]
The problem is: given \(A\), the supported types and their masses, find every ranking maximizing \(W_\mu(o)\), and return their transitive-closure group order, as in Theorem 1(a). The action is the choice of group ranking; the objective is the population-average vote magnitude. This preserves the paper’s ranking outcome and its second-order aggregation rule.

A plausible regime is a large public consultation or membership organization ranking a small slate of proposals. Members provide standardized preference-strength reports, and a type is their entire reported value profile, so people of one type are indistinguishable for this decision. With many respondents and repeated reports, \(N\) can be much larger than \(\tau\). That is a credible alternative to the paper’s six-person example, though it depends on a setting where strength reports are standardized enough for exact profile repetition. The authors should recognize this as their group-ranking step applied to a mass distribution: it keeps their member-level scores and replaces sums over individuals with mass-weighted sums.

I tentatively expect Class B behavior when \(m\) varies. The central choice among rankings remains, and its combinatorics depend on the alternatives. There is an exact discrete dictionary: a finite profile with \(n_t\) members of each type gives \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), with \(N=\sum_t n_t\), and the same maximizing rankings; conversely, rational masses can be realized by clearing denominators. Thus any hardness result for this precise discrete aggregation problem would transfer. The paper does not establish such hardness, so Class B is a research expectation, not a result of the anchor. The main follow-up questions are whether maximizing this particular score admits an efficient algorithm or pricing method, whether useful algorithms exist when \(\tau\) is small, and whether succinct rational masses change the complexity relative to explicitly repeated voters.

The scope is only the group-ranking selection characterized by Theorem 1(a), which the software computes. It does not mirror the per-member LP that estimates a value function from reported preferences, or the theorem’s Pareto and Arrow claims.

The weakest point is that the paper’s computational bottleneck is enumerating \(O(A)\), while the continuous population representation compresses repeated members but leaves that ranking search intact. Also, the paper gives no named complexity result; the positive case rests on Theorem 1(a) as a computed optimization formulation, not on a demonstrated complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that Theorem 1(a) is a correctness characterization, not a complexity result. The paper gives no complexity classification or scalable algorithm for selecting the group ranking; PRESEO enumerates rankings and is demonstrated only up to five alternatives. So the proponent has turned a theorem about what the constitution selects into a new complexity question, rather than mirrored a computational result whose complexity the paper studies.

That objection weakens under the programme’s allowance for a named optimization formulation that the paper computes with. Theorem 1(a) names the maximization, and PRESEO carries it out. Its continuous version is also easy to state faithfully: for each ordered pair \(a,b\), set
\[ q_{ab}=\sum_{t:\,v_t(a)>v_t(b)}\mu_t\bigl(v_t(a)-v_t(b)\bigr), \]
then find a ranking \(o\) maximizing \(\sum_{(a,b)\in o}q_{ab}\). A large consultation with repeated standardized preference-strength profiles gives a credible high-multiplicity regime. The type contains the complete reported value profile, and weighting it by its mass preserves the paper’s per-person contribution. Normalizing by population size does not change the maximizing ranking.

One can say the population representation merely compresses repeated inputs while leaving the ranking search intact. But that does not defeat the mirror under the stated rules: the continuous optimization question remains well-posed even if its answer turns out to be unchanged or uninteresting. Nor can the six-person committee example rule out a broader consultation scenario. I cannot honestly defend a universal negative here. The only anchor survives in the improved form above; the case against it rests on the fact that it is a characterization rather than a complexity theorem, and that is not enough to dismiss the computed optimization formulation.
