Theorem 2 is the numbered result that could supply a computational anchor, but the proposed LP does not mirror its strategic bidding result; a continuum of suppliers would change the equilibrium question. The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is the server’s score maximization problem P3, interpreted as a high-multiplicity procurement problem. There is an anchor problem, though: the paper calls P3 a 0–1 knapsack problem and says it is NP-hard, but that claim appears in prose, not in a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition. So P3 cannot be a compliant named hardness anchor under your rule.

The numbered results do not fix that gap. Theorem 1, whose proof the paper attributes to Aziz, Brandt, and Harrenstein (2013), gives a condition under which a perfect partition for a relaxed preference profile is Pareto-optimal for the original profile. Theorem 2, derived in this paper from scoring-auction theory, characterizes each supplier’s unique symmetric equilibrium bid: \(Q_k^*(\theta_k)\) maximizes \(Q_k^\top\alpha-C_k(Q_k,\theta_k)\), and \(P_k^*(\theta_k)\) is that cost plus the stated information-rent integral. Neither theorem classifies a problem’s complexity. The paper also claims POP runs in \(O(KN^3)\), but that claim is not a numbered theorem.

Still, P3 supports a concrete, credible mirror. Consider a large HFL operator procuring participation from many regional edge-server coalitions. Suppose coalitions bid in a small number of standardized packages, so many have the same buyer-relevant profile. A type \(t\) is a coalition’s complete P3 bid profile \((Q_t,P_t,E_t)\): its quality vector, price, and resource request. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the supplier population. The regime is plausible when there are many coalitions but relatively few such profiles—for example, across a large network using repeated service tiers. Each coalition remains an indivisible supplier with a binary win-or-lose contract.

Here is the candidate continuous problem, which I would call Mass Score Maximization. Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), an exact selected-mass target \(\rho\), a per-capita resource budget \(b\), quality weights \(\alpha\), and each type’s profile \((Q_t,P_t,E_t)\), define \(v_t=Q_t^\top\alpha-P_t\). Find the selected mass \(x_t\) of each type that solves

\[ \max \sum_t v_t x_t \]

subject to

\[ \sum_t x_t=\rho,\qquad \sum_t E_t x_t\le b,\qquad 0\le x_t\le \mu_t\quad\text{for every }t. \]

The solution is the vector \(x\). It says how much mass of each profile receives a winning contract; the fraction \(x_t/\mu_t\) is assigned that contract, while every individual coalition still receives an integral win-or-lose outcome. This preserves P3’s score objective, winner count, and resource constraint after normalizing by the total number of suppliers. It continuizes the population of suppliers, rather than the contracts or outcomes.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: it is a rational linear program with \(O(\tau)\) variables and constraints, solvable in time polynomial in the number of supported types \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\). The discrete-to-continuous dictionary is direct: if there are \(H\) coalitions, then \(\mu_t=n_t/H\), \(\rho=M/H\), and \(b=E_{\max}/H\). The continuous solution allows the selected count within a type to be fractional after normalization; the discrete problem requires that count to be integral. The P3 hardness can therefore dissolve when the object is bulk selection among interchangeable suppliers. Further questions include the integrality gap and rounding under the bandwidth budget, and whether the same benefit survives when coalition formation and equilibrium bidding are optimized jointly.

The mirror covers only the post-bid selection problem. It does not claim that Theorem 2’s finite-supplier bidding equilibrium, POP, or federated training itself has been continuized. That limited scope is a strength: Mass Score Maximization is recognizably the server’s P3 decision, with the same quantities and constraints, in a high-multiplicity supplier regime.

The weak point is twofold. First, the hardness sentence for P3 is not an admissible numbered anchor, so this is a strong candidate mirror but not a fully compliant anchor-based case. Second, standardized bid profiles may be a demanding assumption: if data quality, bandwidth, or price varies substantially across sites, \(\tau\) may be close to the number of coalitions. My case holds when repeated profiles are credible; it should not be generalized to every HFL deployment.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no admissible named computational result to anchor a negative verdict on. Theorem 1 is a Pareto-optimality characterization attributed to prior work; Theorem 2 characterizes equilibrium bids. The claims that POP runs in \(O(KN^3)\) and that P3 is NP-hard appear only in prose. That disqualifies P3 as a named-result anchor under the requested standard, though it does not make the underlying selection problem disappear.

The best objection to the proposed Mass Score Maximization mirror is that it isolates the easiest, most generic layer of DualGFL. Given repeated bid profiles, its LP directly allocates selected mass across types; it says nothing about forming coalitions, strategic bidding, or the training feedback that motivates the paper. In the experiments, there are only \(K=9\) or \(100\) edge servers and \(M=3\) or \(5\) winners. A continuum with a fixed selected fraction would require a larger regime in which both the number of suppliers and the winner quota scale, and repeated complete profiles would need to be plausible. Those assumptions may not describe the paper’s experimental setting.

But this objection does not defeat the stronger mirror. The task is regime-specific, and a large operator with many suppliers using repeated service tiers is plausible. For P3, a supplier’s complete type can be its bid profile \((Q_t,P_t,E_t)\); selection depends on those fields, not on the supplier’s identity. Scaling the winner quota and resource budget per capita is an allowed co-modification. The resulting LP is simple, perhaps a dull answer, but simplicity and limited scope do not make the question invalid under the stated rules.

So the strongest case against is that the paper lacks a qualifying named computational anchor, and its only plausible mirror is a generic selection layer detached from DualGFL’s main game structure. I cannot honestly turn that into the requested universal claim: the proposed mass-selection question survives as a credible population continuization, and the remaining objections are too weak to rule out every worthwhile scenario.
