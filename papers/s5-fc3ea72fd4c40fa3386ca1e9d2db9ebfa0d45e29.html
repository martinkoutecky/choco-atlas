The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\) partitioned into \(\rho\) parties, a designated party \(P^\dagger\), and \(\tau\) voter types, each a complete ranking \(\theta\) of \(C\) with positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), is there a nomination of one candidate from each party such that the nominee of \(P^\dagger\) strictly outscores every other nominee under Borda? For a nomination \(x\), the score of candidate \(c\) is \(B_\mu^x(c)=\sum_{\theta}\mu_\theta\bigl(\rho-\operatorname{pos}_{\theta,x}(c)\bigr)\), where rankings are restricted to the nominees.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic electorate represented by a distribution over complete rankings of the potential candidates; parties still choose one nominee each, and the target nominee must be the unique winner by mass-weighted Borda score.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Borda Possible President: Theorem 11’s NP-hardness with party size at most two and three voters, and Theorem 12’s W[1]-hardness parameterized by the number of parties with six voters. It leaves the paper’s results for other scoring-rule families and the rest of its complexity map untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which restrictions on rankings or candidate pools yield tractability for Borda Continuous Possible President?
  • How does the complexity landscape vary with support size \(\tau\), party size, and arbitrary binary-rational masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s Possible President problem under Borda. Its decision is unchanged: parties nominate candidates, and we ask whether the designated party can nominate someone who becomes the unique winner. The population is the part represented continuously.

Call the mirror Borda Continuous Possible President. An instance has a candidate set \(C\), partitioned into \(\rho\) parties, a designated party \(P^\dagger\), and a list of \(\tau\) voter types. Each type \(\theta\) is a complete ranking of \(C\) and has a positive binary rational mass \(\mu_\theta\), with \(\sum_\theta \mu_\theta=1\). A solution is a nomination \(x_i\in P_i\) from every party. Restrict each type’s ranking to the nominated candidates. If \(\operatorname{pos}_{\theta,x}(c)\) is candidate \(c\)’s position in that restricted ranking, its population Borda score is
\[ B_\mu^x(c)=\sum_\theta \mu_\theta\bigl(\rho-\operatorname{pos}_{\theta,x}(c)\bigr). \]
The question is whether some complete nomination makes the nominee of \(P^\dagger\) strictly outscore every other nominee. This is an existential decision problem, not a score-maximization objective. The party choices remain discrete; the society is the continuous object.

A plausible regime is a very large association or federation election in which constituent parties each put forward one nominee, and members fall into a small number of stable preference blocs. For the lead result, the blocs have exactly two complete rankings of the potential candidates, with masses \(2/3\) and \(1/3\). Thus the number of people \(N\) can be millions while \(\tau=2\); parties can have just two candidate options each. The paper itself defines voter types by shared preferences and notes weighted voters as a setting where types matter. Here the type is complete for this problem: preferences are the only voter attributes the winner calculation uses.

My lead anchor is Theorem 11, proved in this paper. It states that Possible President for Borda is NP-hard even when each party has at most two candidates and there are only three voters. In the theorem’s construction, voters \(a\) and \(b\) have the same ranking. Replace those three voters with two types: their shared ranking has mass \(2/3\), and voter \(c\)’s ranking has mass \(1/3\). For every nomination, each continuous score is exactly one third of the original three-voter score. Every comparison, including whether the target is the unique winner, is preserved. The reduction therefore proves NP-hardness of the continuous problem even with \(\tau=2\), fixed masses \(2/3\) and \(1/3\), and maximum party size two. Theorem 11’s reduction from a bounded-occurrence 3-SAT variant is proved here; the source NP-completeness result is cited as reference [22].

That is a particularly direct mirror: it retains Borda, the full rankings, the party partition, the target party, the existential nomination choice, and the unique-winner condition. It does not relax the question into one about fractional nominations. The hardness is carried by the candidate and party structure, not by having many individually distinct voters. I expect this mirror to be Class B: the discrete hardness transfers exactly, rather than dissolving with a continuous population.

A useful second anchor is Theorem 12, also proved here. It states that Borda Possible President is W[1]-hard when parameterized by the number \(t\) of parties, even with only six voters; its proof reduces from W[1]-hard Multicolored Clique, cited as [21]. For its continuous mirror, use the same Borda Continuous Possible President problem, restrict the support to at most six types, and parameterize by \(\rho\), the number of parties. Give each type the number of its occurrences among the six voters divided by six. Every continuous score is then exactly one sixth of its original score, so the reduction and its parameter are preserved. The mirror is W[1]-hard in \(\rho\), even with support at most six. I expect this parameterized version to be Class B as well.

The exact bridge is simple: an explicit finite election with \(N\) voters maps to masses \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/N\), preserving all Borda comparisons. Conversely, clearing the denominators of rational masses gives integer type multiplicities and preserves the same comparisons after scaling. Expanding those multiplicities into an explicit list of individuals can take exponentially many voters in the binary input length, so the concise reverse representation is a high-multiplicity electorate with binary counts. For both anchors, the forward hardness reductions use only three or six voters, so their continuous lifts have constant-size support and constant masses.

The scope I would claim is just these Borda Possible President results, not the paper’s full complexity map or Necessary President. The mirrors generate sharp follow-up questions: which restrictions on rankings or candidate pools yield tractability; what additional parameters beyond support and party size can help; and how does the boundary vary across the paper’s other scoring-rule families? The anchors already rule out algorithms based only on \(\tau\), or on \(\tau\) together with maximum party size, unless \(P=NP\). They also rule out fixed-parameter tractability in the number of parties unless \( \mathrm{FPT}=\mathrm{W[1]}\).

My weakest point is the payoff: this is a clean high-multiplicity question, but the continuous representation does not unlock a column-generation LP or other continuous-optimization method. The nomination choices remain combinatorial, and exact shared rankings are a demanding assumption for a real electorate. I still believe the mirror is one the authors would recognize: it preserves their problem verbatim apart from replacing voter counts with type masses, and the paper already treats the number of voter types as a natural complexity parameter. Its value is a precise Class B boundary, not a tractability result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about payoff. In Possible President, the parties still make discrete choices, and voter masses only weight the resulting Borda scores. For any nomination \(x\), if \(n_\theta\) of \(N\) voters have ranking \(\theta\), then setting \(\mu_\theta=n_\theta/N\) gives \(B_\mu^x(c)=\operatorname{scr}_{E_x}(c)/N\). The continuous formulation adds no new optimization machinery to these instances; the reductions’ combinatorics remain in the candidate choices. Both anchors therefore establish a narrow Class B transfer, rather than showing that continuous methods unlock this problem.

That objection does not defeat Theorem 11’s mirror. Its voters \(a\) and \(b\) share a ranking, so masses \(2/3\) and \(1/3\) reproduce every score up to the common factor \(1/3\), for every nomination. The unique-winner condition is preserved exactly. A large federation with two preference blocs is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and repeating the three-voter profile gives arbitrarily large populations with the same result. The fixed-size reduction makes the mirror a modest contribution, but its hardness is real.

Theorem 12 transfers just as directly: assign each of its six voters’ ranking a mass equal to its frequency divided by six. Every score is scaled by \(1/6\), the number of parties is unchanged, and the support has at most six types. Again, replication makes the population arbitrarily large without changing the outcome. This is a particularly clean hardness result for a population concentrated on a small number of complete preference types.

The more general mirror—Borda Possible President with arbitrary rational masses and support size \(\tau\)—is harder to dismiss. It is a succinct high-multiplicity version: clearing denominators recovers an integer electorate, though explicitly expanding binary-encoded multiplicities may take exponential space. The paper’s results with three or six voters do not classify that broader input regime. The authors already treat voter types as a parameter, which supports the claim that this is their problem. Nor does the population limit make the strict winner condition vacuous: rational masses can preserve a positive score gap.

So the best negative case is that these two anchors alone yield a thin, inherited hardness result with no apparent continuous-optimization payoff. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. Both anchors survive the direct check, and the arbitrary-mass version leaves a meaningful high-multiplicity question open.
