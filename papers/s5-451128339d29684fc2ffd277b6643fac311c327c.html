The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(S\), a finite support \(T\) of student types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a strict school ranking \(P_t\) and school-specific strict partial priority orders \(\succ_s\) over types, and rational per-capita capacities \(q_s\), compute a mass assignment \(x_{t,a}\) for \(a\in S\cup\{\emptyset\}\). Require \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\le q_s\), and individual rationality. Require stability: no positive mass assigned to a less-preferred option can claim a school that has spare capacity or has positive mass assigned to a lower-priority type. Require constrained efficiency: no other stable assignment can be coupled to the given assignment within each type so every student weakly improves and positive mass strictly improves. State the complexity in \(|S|\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length.

The model it lives in

A continuum of students represented by finitely many complete types, each carrying rational mass. Capacities are per-capita and scale with population size; \(x_{t,a}\) records the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(a\). Each student still receives one school—the mass assignment records how the type cohort is divided. Stability and constrained efficiency retain the paper’s priority-respecting and Pareto criteria.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s constrained-efficiency guarantee for EADA under strict partial-order priorities. It leaves aside the acyclic-priority failures in Theorems 2 and 3 and the outcome-range equivalence with SIC.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does an EADA procedure adapted to rational type masses always return a stable, constrained-efficient assignment under strict partial-order priorities?
  • Can such an assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(|S|\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length?
  • Does the outcome-range equivalence between EADA and SIC extend to mass assignments?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result: it states no hardness classification, running-time bound, or optimization formulation. Its main results concern the quality of mechanism outcomes. So there is no anchor that strictly meets your computational-result requirement, and I would not present one as if it did.

The strongest foothold for a follow-on mirror is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: when each school’s priority relation is a strict partial order, and EADA uses any linear-order extension of those priorities, its output is a constrained-efficient matching—stable and not Pareto dominated by another stable matching. This is a meaningful algorithmic guarantee, but it is not a complexity result. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 likewise give qualitative guarantees for acyclic priorities, not computational classifications.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large centralized school district whose students share a moderate number of profiles. A type records a student’s complete school ranking and all priority-relevant attributes, such as sibling or walk-zone status, score band, and any consent choices. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students of type \(t\). School capacities are expressed per unit of population, so they scale with district size. This preserves the paper’s matching question while replacing counts with shares; it does not assume students receive fractional schools.

The corresponding prospective problem, which Theorem 1 motivates but does not itself establish as a complexity result, is *Partial-Priority Constrained-Efficient Mass Assignment*. An instance gives schools \(S\), a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, a strict ranking \(P_t\) for each type, a strict partial priority order \(\succ_s\) over types at each school, and rational per-capita capacities \(q_s\). The output is a rational mass matching \(x_{t,a}\), for \(a\in S\cup\{\emptyset\}\), where \(x_{t,a}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(a\). It must assign all mass, respect capacities and individual rationality, and be stable: no positive mass can want a school that has spare capacity, or want a school while a lower-priority type occupies positive mass there. It must also be constrained efficient: there is no other stable mass matching to which the students can be reassigned so every unit weakly improves according to its type’s ranking and positive mass strictly improves. The computational question is to find such an assignment, or report that none exists, with complexity stated in the number of schools, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length.

I would tentatively expect the partial-order version to be tractable (Class A): the finite mechanism’s structure suggests adapting EADA to masses and processing only the supported types. That is a conjecture for the mirror, not a result of this paper. Further questions include whether a mass version of EADA achieves the guarantee in polynomial time in \(\tau\), whether the outcome-range equivalence with SIC survives, and whether the failures under acyclic non-transitive priorities persist in the mass model.

The weakest point is the computational gap itself. Theorem 1 neither bounds the mechanism’s runtime in the support size nor proves that its argument carries over to mass assignments. The scenario also relies on many students sharing complete preference-and-priority profiles. Those limits leave a credible follow-on problem, but they prevent this paper from supplying the required computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the gap in the anchor. Theorem 1 proves that EADA produces a constrained-efficient matching on a finite school-choice instance with partial-order priorities. Theorems 2 and 3 describe limits of EADA and SIC under acyclic priorities. None states a complexity bound, hardness result, or optimization formulation. The paper therefore gives no computational-complexity result to continuize.

The proponent’s district example is coherent. A type can include a student’s full school ranking, priority-relevant attributes, and consent choices. With rational type masses and per-capita capacities, a mass assignment \(x_{t,s}\) can record what share of each type attends each school. Stability and Pareto improvement can be defined over those type-level assignments. This does not obviously lose information the paper’s criteria require, and the finite examples could plausibly be represented by positive-mass types.

That makes the proposed question—whether one can find a constrained-efficient mass assignment—credible as a new computational problem. It does not, however, mirror a computational result of this paper. Theorem 1 gives an outcome guarantee for an existing mechanism; the paper does not make the runtime of that mechanism, the complexity of finding such an outcome, or the role of population multiplicity part of its contribution. Turning the guarantee into a support-size complexity question requires a new model and a new computational agenda. That may be worth studying, but this paper alone does not establish it as a worthwhile continuization.

I cannot make the stronger universal claim honestly. A high-multiplicity school district is a sensible regime, and the mass-assignment version does not collapse on its face. The negative case is strong only under the programme’s stated requirement for an anchor: this paper has no named computational-complexity result to mirror. If a prospective problem merely motivated by a qualitative theorem counts as an anchor, the case against is weak, and Theorem 1’s mass-assignment question survives.
