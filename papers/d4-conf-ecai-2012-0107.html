The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a possible continuous-population follow-up, but this paper has no numbered computational result to anchor the required mirror. It gives no theorem or proposition placing a problem in P, proving it hard, or establishing another complexity classification.

The closest formal results do not meet that bar. Theorem 1, attributed to Roberts [18], says that an implementable social choice function on the unrestricted domain must be an affine maximizer. Proposition 8, proved in this paper, says that every extended-VCG mechanism is almost-truthful. Both concern mechanism-design properties, not the complexity of computing a solution. The authors explicitly leave the complexity of computing payments for extended-VCG mechanisms as future work.

A plausible follow-up would represent a large population by a distribution over utility-vector types and ask how to compute payments for a Gini-based choice rule. But in an atomless population, one agent’s report changes zero mass, making individual incentive constraints vacuous; giving positive mass to strategic cohorts changes the paper’s individual-agent problem. That may motivate a new model, but it cannot supply the missing named computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that this paper contains no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is Roberts’ characterization of implementable choice rules; Proposition 8 establishes almost-truthfulness for extended-VCG mechanisms. Neither gives an algorithm or a complexity classification. The paper leaves payment computation as future work, but a complexity question prompted by that suggestion would be a new project, not a continuization of a computational result in this paper.

The best candidate is to compute extended-VCG payments from a distribution of utility-vector types. But in an atomless population, an individual’s report changes zero mass. The selected alternatives therefore do not depend on that report, and the paper’s transfers also exclude the agent’s own report. The individual has no way to affect either part of their outcome. In the paper’s unrestricted domain, the associated incentive bounds become vacuous: when two alternatives are reachable by changing one report, they remain winners regardless of that report, so the relevant supremum and infimum of utility gaps are unbounded. A continuous Gini aggregator over the population might still be well defined, but it would not preserve the manipulation problem that motivates these payments.

Giving a type positive mass can restore influence, but then the strategic actor is a cohort that reports collectively. The model needs a cohort objective, a coordination assumption, and a rule for assigning payments within the cohort. Those choices change the paper’s individual-agent incentive question. Keeping a finite number of agents and compressing identical utility vectors into types is a sensible high-multiplicity representation, but it retains finite-agent effects; as the population becomes atomless, an individual’s influence disappears. Neither move supplies a faithful continuous mirror of this paper’s mechanism.

So the proponent’s payment-computation idea does not provide a surviving anchor under the programme’s scope: it either loses the strategic content or introduces a different strategic unit. The negative case is strongest against calling this paper a continuous mirror. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile adjacent project exists—the authors themselves point to payment complexity, and a cohort-based or finite high-multiplicity extension might be useful—but those would be new models, not continuous versions of a computational result established here.
