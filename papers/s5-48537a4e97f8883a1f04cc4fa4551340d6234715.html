What the mirror covers

The nearest proposed task is computing the median rule from a sparse profile; it concerns evaluating the rule but does not mirror either theorem's axiomatic characterization. The paper's other stated results likewise give structural or axiomatic claims, not computational guarantees.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is unusually plausible at the level of the model. Take a large electorate or stakeholder group making judgments on a fixed agenda. A type is a complete judgment vector \(y\in Y\subseteq\{\pm1\}^K\); its mass \(\mu(y)\) is the fraction of the group with that vector. In a high-multiplicity regime, the number of people \(N\) is large while the number of supported judgment types \(\tau\) is comparatively small. The collective decision is a feasible view \(x\in X\), chosen to maximize weighted agreement with the issue-wise majorities:
\[ \sum_{k\in K}\lambda_k x_k\sum_{y\in Y}\mu(y)y_k. \]
That is precisely the paper’s median rule. The authors explicitly describe \(\mu\) as a distribution of voter weights and say that it abstracts from the exact number of voters. They would plainly recognize this population interpretation.

That fit also exposes the central weakness: the paper already has the continuous-population model. A proposed “mirror” with judgment vectors as types and population fractions as masses would restate its profile \(\mu\), rather than continuize a discrete society. And the paper’s named results do not provide the required computational anchors. The nearest are Theorem 1, proved in this paper: when the input space \(Y\) is thick, a rule satisfies ESME, Continuity, and Reinforcement if and only if it is the median rule; and Theorem 2, also proved here: in a weighted context, if \(X\) is rugged and \(Y\) McGarvey, or if \(X\) is distal and \(Y\) thick, those axioms with WESME characterize the weighted median rule. The paper draws on companion results for parts of their proofs. Both theorems are axiomatic characterizations, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. The displayed argmax defines the rule; it is not an algorithmic result.

A sensible computational follow-on would be: given issue weights \(\lambda_k\), a rational sparse distribution \((y^i,\mu_i)_{i=1}^{\tau}\), and an output space \(X\) specified by a Boolean constraint formula, find a feasible view maximizing the displayed objective. A decision version asks whether some \(x\in X\) attains score at least \(B\). This asks for the computation underlying the median rule, and it covers the median aspect of Theorems 1 and 2—not their characterization of the axioms.

For a succinct encoding of \(X\), this follow-on is NP-complete even with \(\tau=1\): put all mass on the all-\(+1\) input view, use equal issue weights, and let \(X\) encode independent sets by forbidding both endpoints of each graph edge from being \(+1\). Maximizing the median score then finds a maximum independent set. The hardness comes from the agenda and feasible-output constraints, not population multiplicity. With \(X\) listed explicitly, the optimization is easy to perform by scanning the list; its complexity depends on how the judgment space is represented.

So the positive case is that the paper’s model and the ChoCo population regime fit exactly, and its median rule yields a precise computational problem that the paper does not analyze. The weakest point is also decisive under the requested standard: that problem is a follow-on, not an anchor asserted by a named computational result in the paper, and the population continuization is already present in the source.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is the collision: the paper already makes the population continuous. It defines a profile \(\mu:Y\to[0,1]\) with total mass one, interprets \(\mu(y)\) as the weight of voters holding view \(y\), and explicitly abstracts from the electorate’s exact size. Theorem 1’s unweighted characterization and Theorem 2’s weighted characterization both range over these profiles. A large group with repeated judgment types is therefore not a new regime to introduce as a mirror; it is already inside the paper’s model. Restricting to finite electorates gives rational-mass instances of that model.

The two anchors are also axiomatic characterizations, not computational results. Theorem 1 characterizes the median rule using ESME, Continuity, and Reinforcement; Theorem 2 does so under weighted conditions. Neither gives a complexity bound or algorithm for a computational problem. The proposed Boolean-formula version adds an encoding for \(X\) that the paper does not specify. Its NP-hardness can be put into that encoding even with \(\tau=1\), while explicitly listing \(X\) makes scanning it easy. That may motivate a separate complexity project, but it does not supply a computational result in this paper to continuize.

The strongest repair is to choose a natural application, such as computing a weighted Kemeny ranking from a sparse distribution over rankings. That is a credible high-multiplicity computational question. But the paper already identifies ranking aggregation with Kemeny and defines its median rule on distributions over rankings. Studying the algorithmic complexity would be new work on a model the paper already formulated continuously; it would not be a continuous mirror of either theorem. The case against a worthwhile *computational project* is weak, but the case against a *continuous mirror of this paper* is strong: the population axis is already continuized, and the named results offer no computational claim to mirror.
