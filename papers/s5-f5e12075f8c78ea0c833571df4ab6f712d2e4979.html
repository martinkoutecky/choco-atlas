The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I can defend is a continuum of standardized one-unit bilateral trades, handled independently by a platform. Think of a large exchange processing many similar buyer–seller contracts. Each side has unit mass, and sellers are divided into signal types \(i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\), with mass \(\mu_i=1/N\); all buyers have the same type and no informative signal. Each seller type owns one item per matched pair. The type records the agent’s role, signal, and valuation rule, so agents within a type are indistinguishable for the mechanism. The number of agents is a continuum, while the number of types is \(N+1\).

My anchor is Theorem 12, proved in this paper in Section 8.2. It says that for every \(k\in\mathbb{N}\), there are valuation polynomials of degree \(k\) for which no Bayesian incentive-compatible, interim individually rational mechanism achieves a welfare approximation ratio better than \(k\), under the paper’s bilateral-trade constraints. The paper’s construction uses a uniform seller signal \(x_s\in[0,1]\), no informative buyer signal, and values
\[ v_s(x_s)=x_s^k,\qquad v_b(x_s)=k x_s^k. \]
Its proof shows that any admissible mechanism must refrain from trade almost everywhere. This is an approximation impossibility for mechanisms, not an NP-hardness result.

Here is the finite-support population question I would hand to a prover. For \(k\ge2\) and \(N\ge1\), take a continuum of independently matched buyer–seller pairs. A seller of type \(i\) has value \(v_s(i)=(i/N)^k\); the buyer’s value is \(v_b(i)=k(i/N)^k\). Seller types have mass \(\mu_i=1/N\), and the buyer type is constant. The mechanism acts separately on each pair and cannot pool payments across pairs. A direct mechanism chooses trade probability \(q_i\in[0,1]\) and a common payment \(p_i\) for each reported seller type \(i\). It must satisfy seller BIC and interim IR,
\[ p_i-q_i v_s(i)\ge p_j-q_j v_s(i)\quad\forall i,j, \qquad p_i-q_i v_s(i)\ge0\quad\forall i, \]
and buyer interim IR,
\[ \sum_i\mu_i\bigl(q_i v_b(i)-p_i\bigr)\ge0. \]
The objective is to maximize aggregate welfare
\[ W(q)=\sum_i\mu_i\bigl((1-q_i)v_s(i)+q_i v_b(i)\bigr). \]
The first-best welfare is \(W^{\mathrm{FB}}=\sum_i\mu_i v_b(i)\). The question is to determine the best attainable ratio
\[ \rho_{k,N}=\frac{W^{\mathrm{FB}}}{\max_{(q,p)\text{ feasible}}W(q)} \]
and its behavior as \(N\) grows. These are finite linear constraints and a linear objective, so the exact finite-support optimization is an LP. No trade is feasible and has ratio \(k\). In the atomless limit \(\mu(dx)=dx\), the construction is precisely the one in Theorem 12, which establishes ratio \(k\). Whether the finite-grid values \(\rho_{k,N}\) converge to \(k\), and at what rate, is a further question; the theorem does not itself prove that convergence.

The authors should recognize the mirror’s per-pair question: it preserves their welfare objective, BIC and interim IR requirements, and balanced bilateral payment. The population setting comes from repeating those trades with type masses, not from treating the paper’s signal randomness as population continuity. Of the programme’s complexity labels, the finite-support optimization is on the tractable side because of its LP formulation. The ratio barrier is a transfer of the per-trade mechanism impossibility under replication; it is not a transfer of computational hardness.

This covers only Theorem 12’s constructed family. It does not mirror the paper’s full classification of information structures or allow cross-market subsidies. My weakest point is that independent replication adds little strategic interaction: the cohort aggregate may look like bookkeeping around the original bilateral problem. The atomless limit also closely matches the paper’s existing signal integral, while the finite-type mass version is the more recognizably high-multiplicity ChoCo question—and its asymptotic lower bound still needs proof. The case survives if repeated, type-homogeneous bilateral trades are accepted as a sensible population regime, but it is a narrow mirror rather than a new computational landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 12 is the wrong kind of anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme. It proves that incentive compatibility and interim individual rationality force a welfare loss in a particular mechanism-design environment. It gives no complexity bound for computing a mechanism, and its allocation-zero conclusion comes from the payment identities and participation constraints. That is a meaningful impossibility result, but it does not itself pose the kind of computational problem ChoCo is charting.

The proposed \(\rho_{k,N}\) makes a reasonable finite model. Its population interpretation adds little, though: with pairwise budget balance and mechanisms run separately, replicating each trade leaves the incentive constraints unchanged and just scales aggregate welfare. The parameter \(N\) controls the discretization of the seller’s signal distribution; it is not the number of agents whose multiplicity the optimization must handle. As \(N\) grows, the question approaches the paper’s existing continuous signal model. The LP is a valid new calculation about that discretization, but it does not turn Theorem 12 into a computational-complexity result about a continuous society.

A stronger population model could coordinate decisions across many matched pairs. Keeping budget balance within each pair returns to the separate-trade model. Allowing transfers across pairs introduces a market-wide budget rule and new incentive constraints; that may be worth studying, but it changes the problem that Theorem 12 establishes. Also, each agent’s signal remains private: knowing the population mass of each signal type does not let the mechanism trade or allocate mass as though those signals were reported truthfully.

So the best negative case is that the anchor does not identify a ChoCo computational question without substantial added structure, and the natural finite version is primarily about signal discretization. I cannot make the universal claim airtight: a continuum of repeated, type-homogeneous bilateral trades is plausible, and an author might recognize the resulting population model as useful. That makes this a narrow and potentially worthwhile extension, but a weak fit to the programme’s stated complexity landscape.
