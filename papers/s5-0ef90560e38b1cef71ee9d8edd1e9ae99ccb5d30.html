The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest anchor-backed case for a continuous mirror here. The paper’s named results are about equilibrium and information aggregation, not computational complexity.

The closest results are Theorem 1, proved in the paper, which says that in any ambiguous voting game where voters lack confidence, no symmetric equilibrium has correct expected winners; and Theorem 2, also proved here, which says that with disjoint posteriors, a sequence of games has a sequence of symmetric equilibria satisfying full information equivalence. Theorem 3 extends the nonaggregation result to a specified model with abstention. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classifies its complexity, so none qualifies as an anchor under the requested criteria.

One could pose a new decision problem: given rational signal distributions and an ambiguity interval, determine whether a sequence of symmetric equilibria has full information equivalence. But that would be a computational wrapper around the paper’s equilibrium question, not a mirror of a named computational result. The main obstacle is also substantive: in an atomless electorate, one voter’s ballot has no effect on the outcome, so the pivotal-vote incentives driving Ellis’s results disappear unless the model is defined through a limit of finite electorates. That makes the positive case especially weak.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 rules out certain symmetric equilibria, Theorem 2 constructs equilibria under another condition, and Theorem 3 extends a nonaggregation result to abstention. They are results about strategic behavior and information aggregation; none states the complexity of a computational problem. One could add a task such as deciding whether rational game parameters admit an equilibrium with full information equivalence, but that would create a new problem rather than mirror a computational result in the paper.

There is also a substantive obstacle to making that new task a continuous-population version of these theorems. The strongest candidate mirror of Theorem 1 would ask whether ambiguity-induced hedging still prevents information aggregation when voters are represented by masses of signal types. But an individual in an atomless electorate has no effect on the winner, so cannot be pivotal. The incentive that drives the theorem disappears: any strategy is a best response, including one that makes expected winners correct. Theorem 2’s equilibrium construction has the same problem; with no pivotal incentives, disjoint posteriors are no longer what sustains aggregation. Theorem 3’s abstention result also loses its strategic bite, since an individual’s choice to abstain or vote cannot affect the outcome.

A more elaborate model could preserve pivotal incentives by keeping finite electorates, using finite-population or Poisson approximations, or treating whole type groups as coordinated blocs. But the first two retain the finite strategic units that the continuous society was meant to replace; the last changes who chooses and gives them collective influence absent from the paper’s game. These could motivate other research, but they do not provide a convincing population-continuous computational mirror of Ellis’s results.

The negative case is strongest against this paper as an anchor for ChoCo: it has no computational result, and its central strategic mechanism relies on individual pivotality that an atomless population erases. I cannot rule out a worthwhile computational project built around a newly defined equilibrium problem. That possibility is the weak point in claiming that no scenario could ever produce a worthwhile mirror.
