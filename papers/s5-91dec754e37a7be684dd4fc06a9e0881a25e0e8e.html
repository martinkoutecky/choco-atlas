The experiments estimate welfare outcomes. None asks for an algorithm to optimize an intervention or classifies a computational problem.

The Mexico City setting does support a sensible high-multiplicity model: rankings can be types, capacities can scale per capita, and priority scores or lottery positions can be included in the type description. But comparing per-capita top-\(k\) rates under BM, ABM, and DA would be a continuous-market welfare analysis. Writing \(c_k/n\) in place of \(c_k\) changes the scale of the reported quantity; it does not create a computational question that the paper’s named results pose.

The strongest computational rescue would be to ask for exact or approximate expected rank distributions from a mass distribution over rankings and priorities. It is a new evaluation problem, and the paper provides no complexity claim or algorithmic objective for it. Likewise, Theorem 2’s profile-by-profile dominance event may not be captured by the limiting average welfare distribution; retaining that event would require preserving more of the finite-profile randomness.
