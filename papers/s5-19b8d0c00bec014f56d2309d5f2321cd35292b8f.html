The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\ge3\) and binary-rational scoring vectors \(s\ne s_B\) and \(s'\), find alternatives \(x,y\) and a finitely supported rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings such that \(x\) loses pairwise to every other alternative, \(x\) uniquely maximizes \(s\)'s score, and \(y\)'s \(s'\)-score strictly exceeds \(x\)'s.

The model it lives in

Use ranking types \(\pi\in S_m\) with masses \(\mu_\pi\ge0\) summing to \(1\). For each choice of \(x,y\), maximize a common margin \(\delta\) subject to \(\mu[z\succ x]-\mu[x\succ z]\ge\delta\) and \(\mathrm{sc}^s_\mu(x)-\mathrm{sc}^s_\mu(z)\ge\delta\) for every \(z\ne x\), and \(\mathrm{sc}^{s'}_\mu(y)-\mathrm{sc}^{s'}_\mu(x)\ge\delta\). A positive optimum gives the requested society; the LP has one variable per ranking, with pricing over rankings as the algorithmic question.

The objection that survived

The paper gives no complexity bound for its constructions, so a polynomial witness finder would be a new algorithmic result rather than a result it establishes.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 1's separating-profile construction and its implication for comparisons among non-Borda rules; it leaves the paper's probability calculations and other characterizations alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the margin-maximization LP be solved exactly in time polynomial in \(m\) and the score-vector bit length using assignment pricing?

The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has zero qualifying computational anchors. Theorem 1 is a characterization attributed to Fishburn and Gehrlein (1976) and Okamoto and Sakai (2019); Proposition 1 and Theorem 2 are proved here. They establish structural facts about scoring rules and profiles. None gives a complexity classification or an algorithmic running time.

The strongest positive lead is Proposition 1: for any non-Borda scoring rule \(f\) and any scoring rule \(f'\), the authors construct a finite profile where \(x_1\) is a Condorcet loser, \(f\) uniquely selects \(x_1\), and \(f'\) does not select \(x_1\). Theorem 2, also proved here, says that for any non-Borda \(f'\), \(f\) CL-dominates \(f'\) if and only if \(f\) is the Borda rule. These are not computational anchors, but Proposition 1 gives a concrete witness-finding problem to continuize.

Call the prospective problem Continuous CL-Separating Society. Its input is \(m\ge3\) and two binary-rational scoring vectors \(s\ne s_B\) and \(s'\). A type is a complete ranking \(\pi\) of the alternatives, and \(\mu_\pi\) is its fraction of the population. The output is a finitely supported rational distribution \(\mu\) and an alternative \(x\) such that \(x\) loses to every alternative by pairwise majority, \(s\) uniquely selects \(x\), and \(s'\) excludes \(x\). The society itself is the witness; there is no intervention decision in the paper’s problem.

One exact optimization formulation maximizes a common margin \(\delta\) over \(x\), a competing alternative \(y\), and masses \(\mu_\pi\). Require \(\mu_\pi\ge0\), \(\sum_\pi\mu_\pi=1\), and, for every \(z\ne x\), require the pairwise margin \(\mu[z\succ x]-\mu[x\succ z]\ge\delta\) and the \(s\)-score margin \(\mathrm{sc}^s_\mu(x)-\mathrm{sc}^s_\mu(z)\ge\delta\). Also require \(\mathrm{sc}^{s'}_\mu(y)-\mathrm{sc}^{s'}_\mu(x)\ge\delta\). A solution with \(\delta>0\) is exactly the requested society. Proposition 1 guarantees such a solution for every input.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large electorate has many repeated complete ballots, with mass recording each ballot type’s population share. The LP has exponentially many ranking variables, while its witness has only \(O(m)\) constraints and can have \(O(m)\) supported types at a basic solution. For fixed \(x,y\), pricing a ranking reduces to a maximum-weight assignment: once \(x\)’s rank is fixed, the pairwise terms involving \(x\) and the positional score terms are additive over candidate-rank assignments. That suggests a Class A result via polynomial-time LP optimization with a pricing oracle. This is an algorithmic extension of the paper’s witness construction; the paper itself does not establish that complexity result.

The continuous version preserves the paper’s central relationship among rules: the finite profiles constructed in Proposition 1 are already rational-mass societies, and the Borda rule’s no-Condorcet-loser property extends to distributions. The mirror therefore has a credible “same answer” outcome. It covers the paper’s CL-dominance results, not its probability calculations or other characterizations.

The weak point is also decisive: this proposed search problem is not a named complexity result of the paper. The authors study dominance, not the computational cost of finding a separating society. So this is a plausible population-continuous research extension, but it cannot serve as an anchor-backed computational mirror under your stated standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes when a scoring rule ever selects a Condorcet loser; Proposition 1 constructs a separating profile; Theorem 2 characterizes the CL-dominance relation. None studies an algorithmic problem with an input, output, or running time. Under ChoCo’s anchor standard, there is nothing here whose complexity landscape the paper establishes.

The population story itself is plausible: a large election can naturally be represented by fractions of voters holding each ranking. But with a society \(\mu\) supplied, checking scores and pairwise majorities is direct aggregation, taking \(O(\tau m^2)\) time. The paper instead quantifies over profiles to establish properties of rules. Normalizing a finite electorate gives a rational distribution, so the natural continuous translation mainly restates that universal property over a simplex; it does not give the paper a population-level algorithmic problem.

The best broader mirror would make CL-dominance itself a decision problem: given two encoded scoring vectors, determine whether one dominates the other over all continuous societies. That is a coherent new problem. For rational score vectors, it can be posed as linear feasibility over ranking types, checking candidate pairs in turn; the ranking variables admit assignment-style pricing. But the society is not an input to this problem. It asks for a global property of two rules, so its computational interest would need to stand on its own rather than follow from a computational result in this paper. Encoding also restricts the paper’s score vectors, which may be arbitrary reals, to finitely described inputs.

The proponent’s witness-finding proposal is the strongest counterexample to a categorical rejection. Given rational scoring vectors, it asks for a society witnessing separation. Its margin LP is credible, and its assignment pricing may yield an efficient algorithm. Still, it makes the society the output of a certificate-synthesis task. That is a computational extension of the proof of Proposition 1, not a complexity result about what can be computed when a society is given. It could be worthwhile work, but calling it a mirror would broaden the programme from computational problems *on* continuous societies to algorithms for constructing witnesses to rule properties.

So the negative case is strong under the stated anchor criterion: no result in the paper supplies a computational classification to mirror. The universal claim that no scenario could produce a worthwhile continuous project is weaker. High-multiplicity elections are a sensible regime here, and constructive witness generation is a real possible extension. I can argue that neither is an anchor-backed computational mirror of this paper; I cannot honestly rule out their value as new research questions.
