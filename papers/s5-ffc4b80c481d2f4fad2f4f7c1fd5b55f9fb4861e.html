What the mirror covers

No mirror covers the paper's numbered results; the proposed generator optimization is a new project rather than a continuization of a result in the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper gives no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Proposition 1 is a welfarism representation theorem, cited from earlier work. Lemma 1 proves that the comparability conditions imply pairwise continuity. Theorem 1 classifies which social cost functions fit each comparability level, drawing on earlier results; it does not give an algorithm or complexity claim. Lemma 2 and Proposition 2 characterize invariant functions. None asserts that a computational problem is tractable, hard, or has a particular formulation.

The most plausible population regime is a large group of renewable generators sharing a small number of profiles. A type could specify a generator’s capacity, network connection, and curtailment cost; mass would be the fraction of generators of that type. The decision \(u(t)\) would be the curtailment assigned to type \(t\), with aggregate grid constraints and an objective such as minimizing \(\operatorname{ess\,sup}_t J_t(u(t))\) or \(\int J_t(u(t))\,d\mu(t)\). That is a concrete continuous-population control problem, but the paper’s curtailment discussion and its illustrative policies do not state a named computational result for it. Presenting it as an anchored mirror would overclaim. The paper’s central contribution is axiomatic guidance on choosing an objective, rather than a computational problem whose complexity can be continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 concern representation and axiomatic classification; Lemma 1 and Proposition 2 concern continuity and invariance. None gives a complexity claim or an algorithm for a specified computational problem. The examples recommend or derive social cost functions, but do not analyze the complexity of optimizing them. So there is no result here whose computational landscape continuization could chart.

The renewable-generator scenario is the strongest candidate for a new problem: define types by capacity, grid connection, and cost, then choose a curtailment level for each type subject to aggregate grid constraints. That can be a sensible high-multiplicity model. A stronger version could fix a comparability level and SCF, then ask how to optimize that objective over a large population. But those choices supply the computational problem the paper never states. The paper does not specify an input model or analyze the complexity of that optimization; making it a ChoCo mirror would turn its application into a new algorithmic project, not continue one of its results. Extending Theorem 1 to a continuum of agents would instead be an axiomatic question, outside the programme’s computational scope.

So the negative case is strong for a *mirror of this paper*: its contribution is guidance about which objective to use, not a computational problem with a result to continuize. It is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile continuous-population research could grow from the paper. The generator setting could motivate such research, but that would be a new contribution rather than a mirror.
