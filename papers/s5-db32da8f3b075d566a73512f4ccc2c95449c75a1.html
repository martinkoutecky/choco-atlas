The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that this paper has an unusually natural population to continuize: people providing preference feedback, grouped by their complete preference behavior. A plausible regime is a large annotation or product feedback program with millions of comparisons but a modest number \(\tau\) of recurring preference types. A type would specify the provider’s conditional preference distribution over response pairs and prompts; its mass \(\mu_t\) would be that type’s fraction of the feedback population. The paper’s examples of groups preferring concise, detailed, friendly, or simpler responses make such a regime plausible, though their experiments themselves use small groups.

The nearest named result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that, under the paper’s assumptions, the alignment gap for a single-reward RLHF policy and subgroup \(u\) is at least \(\frac{\lambda_\psi}{64\beta^2L_\pi}\frac{\epsilon(1-\eta(u))}{D^2}\). Here \(\epsilon=\operatorname{TV}(p_u^*,p_j^*)-\max_{k\ne u}\operatorname{TV}(p_k^*,p_j^*)>0\); \(\eta(u)\) is the subgroup’s population share; \(D\) bounds the reward features; \(\lambda_\psi\) is the minimum eigenvalue of the feature matrix; and \(\beta,L_\pi\) are the regularization and policy constants in the theorem. In short, the paper proves a lower bound on how poorly a single-reward policy can serve a sufficiently distinct subgroup.

That is a meaningful result about alignment, but it is not a computational-complexity result: it does not classify a problem as tractable, hard, or otherwise. The paper has no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition making such a classification, and its algorithms do not come with a named complexity guarantee. So there is no qualifying anchor under the requested standard. Theorem 1 can motivate a continuous question, but it cannot honestly anchor a complexity mirror.

If a mirror is still useful as a research question, the most faithful candidate is Continuous MaxMin Policy Alignment. Its instance would give a finite prompt set \(\mathcal X\), finite response set \(\mathcal Y\), prompt distribution \(P\), reference policy \(\pi_{\rm ref}\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over a finite set \(T\) of preference types, type rewards \(r_t(x,y)\), and regularization parameter \(\beta\). The task is to return a policy \(\pi\) whose worst supported type’s regularized reward is within additive \(\varepsilon\) of optimum:
\[ \max_\pi\ \min_{t:\mu_t>0} \sum_{x\in\mathcal X}P(x) \left[ \sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}\pi(y\mid x)r_t(x,y) -\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\rm ref}(\cdot\mid x)\right) \right]. \]
The output is the policy \(\pi\). This mirrors the paper’s max-min alignment objective at the level of the underlying policy problem, rather than treating its PPO or EM procedures as the problem itself. For explicitly listed finite inputs, I would expect additive approximation to be tractable by convex optimization; that is a conjectured classification for this mirror, not a result of the paper. Natural follow-ups are how the algorithm scales with \(\tau\), whether implicit or very large type sets admit efficient pricing, and what happens when types must be learned jointly with the policy.

The mirror fits the paper’s concern with serving diverse preference groups, but its soft spot is substantial: in the max-min objective, masses matter only through which types have positive mass. A tiny group and a large group impose the same minimum, by design. Mass does affect the paper’s pooled reward learning and Theorem 1’s bound, but the proposed policy objective is primarily about the set of groups, not their proportions. Also, the paper already writes aggregate preferences as an \(\eta\)-weighted mixture over subgroups, so the population-distribution step is close to its existing model. The positive case is therefore for a plausible *computational continuation* of the paper’s max-min alignment problem, not for a complexity result that this paper has already established.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 1 gives an analytic lower bound on subgroup alignment loss; Lemma 1 gives a reward-mismatch bound. Algorithms 1 and 2 describe procedures, but provide no running-time, approximation, or complexity guarantee. Theorem 1 is the strongest anchor the proponent named, and it does not classify a computational problem.

A continuous version of Theorem 1 could replace the finite mixture \(\sum_u \eta(u)p_u^*\) with an integral over preference types. That is a plausible analytic or statistical extension. The theorem already makes subgroup shares \(\eta(u)\) explicit, though, and the paper supplies no computational task for calculating the bound, learning the type distribution, or optimizing over it. Any complexity question would need additional assumptions about how preference types are represented and accessed; its algorithmic content would come from those assumptions.

The proposed MaxMin Policy Alignment task is a better candidate, but its population masses do no work in the stated objective. For any two distributions \(\mu,\nu\) with the same support, the objective
\[ \max_\pi\min_{t:\mu_t>0} F_t(\pi) \]
is identical. In a finite recurring-type regime, changing the sizes of the groups changes no policy choice. The task is max-min optimization over a set of type constraints; its continuous object is the policy over responses. Meanwhile, the paper already writes the finite-group max-min objective in equation (14). Studying its computation could be useful, but that alone does not make it a computational question created by continuizing the population.

The strongest repair is to let preference types range over a continuum and optimize worst-case utility over that population. But this requires choosing between an infimum over the support and an essential infimum under the population measure. Those choices treat rare and measure-zero preference types differently. With finitely many recurring types, masses again drop out. With atomless types, a computational formulation needs a representation of the type space and a way to optimize over it; the paper provides neither. That could motivate a new robust-optimization project, but its complexity would depend on the newly chosen type geometry and oracle model, not on a computational result in this paper.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large feedback program with millions of labels and a few recurring rubrics, like the paper’s concise-versus-detailed groups. That supports the population interpretation. It does not rescue the proposed complexity mirror: the paper’s egalitarian objective gives each supported group equal standing, regardless of its share. Weighting utility by population mass would make shares consequential, but it would change the objective toward aggregate reward—the paper already models that through its pooled preference distribution—without supplying a computational anchor.

So the negative case is strongest as a judgment about this paper’s fit for ChoCo: it has no qualifying named computational result, and the best proposed mirror either discards population masses or requires a new model whose complexity is supplied by extra assumptions. The universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists is harder to defend. A continuum-of-preference-types optimization problem may be worthwhile on its own; the material here does not establish it as a mirror of this paper’s computational contribution.
