The continuous mirror question

Given types \(t\) with masses \(\mu_t\), strict post rankings, weights \(\omega_t\), a feasible current allocation \(x_{t,p}\), and efficiently represented per-capita matroid rank constraints \(\rho_p\), determine whether there is a reassignment flow \(z_{t,p,q}\) that preserves each unit of mass’s current-to-new comparison, yields a feasible allocation, and has positive weighted vote margin \(\sum_t \omega_t \sum_{p,q} z_{t,p,q}(\mathbf{1}[q \succ_t p]-\mathbf{1}[p \succ_t q])\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete applicant types, with each type specifying its strict ranking, weight, and role in the post constraints. The current allocation \(x_{t,p}\) and challenger flow \(z_{t,p,q}\) assign mass while preserving \(\sum_q z_{t,p,q}=x_{t,p}\); resulting post masses satisfy the per-capita rank inequalities \(\sum_{t:(t,p)\in S} y_{t,p}\le \rho_p(S)\). Maximize the weighted support margin over feasible challenger flows and decide whether its maximum is positive.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is Continuous Weighted-Popularity Certification: given a proposed allocation of a population continuum, decide whether any feasible alternative wins more weighted support than it.

The paper’s strongest named anchor is Theorem 5.1, proved here. It says that a matching \(M\) is popular if and only if it is well formed and every promotion path \(L\) with respect to \(M\) has \(c(L)\ge 0\). There is no separately numbered theorem stating “the decision problem is in P”; immediately after Theorem 5.1, the paper uses this characterization to decide whether a given matching is popular by finding a minimum-cost path in an auxiliary acyclic graph. That polynomial-time decision procedure is the computational result this mirror follows.

Take many applicants in a centralized allocation system, such as a large internship or residency match. Each type \(t\) has the same strict ranking of eligible posts, the same weight \(\omega_t\), and the same role in every post-side constraint. A type’s mass \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The intended regime has many applicants but relatively few such types: for example, large recurring cohorts with shared eligibility and ranking patterns. Post constraints are represented by per-capita matroid rank functions \(\rho_p\), so their capacities scale with the population. This is the co-modification the mirror needs: an allocation sends mass to posts, while every individual still receives exactly one post.

For a precise instance, let \(x_{t,p}\) be the mass of type \(t\) currently assigned to post \(p\). It must satisfy \(\sum_p x_{t,p}=\mu_t\). For each post \(p\), its assigned mass must satisfy the rank inequalities \(\sum_{t:(t,p)\in S}x_{t,p}\le \rho_p(S)\) for every set \(S\) of eligible type-post pairs. Assume fallback options as needed to make a complete assignment feasible. The rank functions encode the continuous, per-capita counterpart of the paper’s post-side matroid constraints.

A challenger must be compared against the same infinitesimal applicants currently assigned to each post. So let \(z_{t,p,q}\) be the mass of type \(t\) moved from current post \(p\) to alternative post \(q\). Require \(\sum_q z_{t,p,q}=x_{t,p}\), and require the resulting masses \(y_{t,q}=\sum_p z_{t,p,q}\) to form a feasible allocation. The objective is to maximize the challenger’s weighted vote margin:

\[ \Delta(z)=\sum_t\omega_t\sum_{p,q}z_{t,p,q} \bigl(\mathbf{1}[q\succ_t p]-\mathbf{1}[p\succ_t q]\bigr). \]

The question is whether \(\max_z\Delta(z)>0\). If so, the maximizing reassignment is a witness that the proposed allocation is not popular; otherwise it is popular. This coupling matters: it compares each unit of mass’s new post to that same unit’s current post, rather than comparing assignment marginals as if the identities of the voters did not matter.

This is recognizably the paper’s problem: it preserves one-sided strict preferences, per-applicant weights, post-side matroid feasibility, and the defining “no alternative wins a weighted majority” test. Fractional type-level assignments just let portions of an interchangeable cohort receive different posts. The model does not claim to mirror every result in the paper; its scope is the given-matching certification problem behind Theorem 5.1.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, subject to having an efficiently separable representation of the per-capita rank constraints. The challenger problem is a linear optimization problem over reassignment flows and post-side polymatroid constraints; rank-inequality separation gives a continuous-optimization route to testing the maximum margin. Further questions include whether the paper’s promotion-path characterization has a useful mass-flow counterpart, whether an exact high-multiplicity dictionary holds for rational masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and how much integrality gap appears when moving between discrete and continuous certification.

The weak point is the matroid co-modification. A matroid that distinguishes applicants through identity-specific elements cannot be collapsed into a small set of types; those distinctions must be included in the type or the mirror loses the paper’s constraint structure. The case is strongest in regimes where repeated applicants genuinely have the same role in the post constraints, and where capacities scale with the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed LP does not continue the paper’s central structural result. Theorem 5.1 characterizes a *finite* matching through well-formedness and promotion paths built from individual applicants’ exchanges in matroids. The polynomial certification algorithm appears afterward as Problem 1 and an auxiliary-graph argument; it is not itself a numbered complexity theorem. The proposed mass-flow model asks a new question—whether any feasible reassignment can gain weighted support—and bypasses the promotion-path characterization. It may preserve the notion of popularity, but it does not show that the paper’s main insight has a continuous counterpart.

The harder problem is making its matroid constraints into a population model. The paper allows arbitrary matroids on individual applicant-post edges. Preferences and weights do not determine how those edges interact: duplicating an applicant as parallel elements, as independent clones, or under a scaled quota gives different feasible mass allocations and different exchange structure. The proposed per-capita rank functions choose one such scaling; they are not determined by the paper’s instance. If types must retain all the distinctions needed to preserve an arbitrary matroid’s exchange behavior, the number of types can grow with the population, defeating the intended compression. So the proposed mirror is strongest for a separately specified family of symmetric, population-scaled matroids, rather than for the paper’s general oracle-matroid setting.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. A large matching market with repeated preference profiles and uniform or otherwise symmetric scaled constraints is plausible; a sequence of clone-symmetric matroids gives a coherent limit, and the coupling in the proponent’s formulation handles comparisons between current and alternative assignments. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous-population question exists in that regime. The negative case is that it is a narrower, newly specified polymatroidal model, and the supplied argument does not establish that it carries Theorem 5.1’s promotion-path contribution into the continuum. That makes the mirror less compelling as a continuation of this paper, but it is not a decisive case against the question itself.
