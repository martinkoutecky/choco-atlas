The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of agent types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), role-based MC-net schemas with rational coefficients, a supplied maximum surplus \(p_{\max}\), and tolerance \(b\ge 0\), compute per-unit payoffs \(a_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t=p_{\max}\) such that \(\sup_{0\le q\le\mu,\,q\ne 0}\bigl(v_\mu(q)-\sum_{t\in T}q_ta_t\bigr)\le\epsilon^*+b\). Here \(q_t\) is coalition mass of type \(t\), \(v_\mu(q)=\sum_r\lambda_r\prod_{t\in P_r}q_t\prod_{t\in N_r}(\mu_t-q_t)\), and \(\epsilon^*\) is the minimum possible supremum over such payoff vectors. Determine the complexity as a function of the number of supported types, rule encoding size, and input bit length.

The model it lives in

A typed, high-multiplicity MC-nets game: the input gives rational type masses \(\mu_t\) and repeated role-based rule schemas; a coalition is represented by masses \(q_t\); each type receives per-unit payoff \(a_t\); and the objective is to minimize worst coalition excess subject to total payoff \(p_{\max}\), returning an additive-\(b\) approximation.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 2’s additive-\(b\) approximate least-core payoff guarantee; Theorem 3’s finite-termination result supports the algorithm. It leaves the paper’s empirical runtime trade-offs and coalition-structure generation problem outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of finding a most dissatisfied coalition as a function of \(|T|\) and the rule encoding size?
  • Does the additive-\(b\) payoff guarantee admit an efficient algorithm for restricted schema families?
  • Which core-hardness results transfer to this typed rule family and supplied-surplus convention?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. Given MC-nets rules \(R\), a maximum surplus \(p_{\max}\), and a bound \(b\ge 0\), CG\(\varepsilon\)B returns a payoff vector in an \(\varepsilon\)-core whose \(\varepsilon\) is at most \(b\) above the least-core value \(\varepsilon^*\). The paper’s Theorem 3 separately proves finite termination of that algorithm; neither theorem gives a polynomial-time bound. The paper also cites co-NP-completeness of core non-emptiness from [6], but that is prior work, not a numbered result proved here, so I treat it as context rather than my anchor.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Typed Continuous Bounded Approximate Least-Core Division. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of agent types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a maximum surplus \(p_{\max}\), and a rational tolerance \(b\ge 0\). A type records everything relevant to this game: an agent’s rule role and payoff treatment. The population may be very large, with many exchangeable agents in each of a small number of types.

To give MC-nets rules a mass-valued meaning, take each rule \(r\) to be a bounded-size schema over distinct types, with required types \(P_r\), excluded types \(N_r\), and rational coefficient \(\lambda_r\). A coalition is described by its included mass \(q\), where \(0\le q_t\le\mu_t\). Define its value by

\[ v_\mu(q)=\sum_r \lambda_r \prod_{t\in P_r}q_t \prod_{t\in N_r}(\mu_t-q_t). \]

This treats a rule as a pattern of interactions among selected and unselected population mass. At the all-or-none choices \(q_t\in\{0,\mu_t\}\), it recovers a rule-driven game over types; for partial cohorts, it counts the mass of matching interactions.

A solution is a per-unit payoff \(a_t\) for each type, together with its worst coalition excess, such that

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t=p_{\max}, \qquad \epsilon(a)=\sup_{\substack{0\le q\le\mu\\q\ne0}} \left(v_\mu(q)-\sum_t q_ta_t\right) \le \epsilon^*+b, \]

where \(\epsilon^*=\inf_{a:\,\sum_t\mu_ta_t=p_{\max}}\epsilon(a)\). The question is to return such an allocation \(a\) (and its attained bound). As in the paper, \(p_{\max}\) is supplied; computing it for the continuous coalition-structure problem can be studied separately.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity setting for large service or production cooperatives: many participants share a small set of roles, and the coalition value comes from recurring synergies among roles. The mirror keeps the authors’ underlying question—how to divide efficient surplus so coalitions have little incentive to object—and keeps its central computational shape: find the most dissatisfied coalition for a candidate allocation. They could reasonably recognize this as approximate least-core payoff division for a typed, replicated MC-nets game.

My expectation is Class B, hardness transferred, for unrestricted type counts and rule sets. For this rule family, coalition excess is multi-affine in \(q\), so its maximum over the mass box is attained at a corner. Thus a most-blocking coalition can be chosen all-or-none by type: fractional participation does not erase the subset-selection combinatorics. The paper’s cited core hardness is evidence that this is a real boundary, though proving that it transfers to this exact rule family and surplus convention is a further task. Restricted rule families may instead admit tractable pricing and a Class A result. The mirror also raises questions about the complexity of the pricing problem as a function of \(\tau=|T|\), whether positive \(b\) permits efficient approximation, and how the supplied continuous \(p_{\max}\) compares with the finite high-multiplicity surplus.

The weakest point is the rule extension. The paper’s MC-nets rules refer to named agents and trigger on their presence or absence; my mirror uses repeated type-level schemas and scales their contributions with matching mass. That is a deliberate model co-modification, not a limit theorem established by the paper. The case survives if the authors accept repeated role-based rules as a natural MC-nets regime. If they require arbitrary identity-specific rules to remain unchanged, this mirror would need a different construction.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is semantic, and it targets Theorem 2’s actual contribution. MC-nets rules test whether particular named agents are present or absent. If the population becomes atomless while the paper’s finite rule list stays fixed, those identities carry zero mass; under per-capita normalization, their contributions disappear. Theorem 3’s termination proof also depends on there being only finitely many distinct coalitions to add. Neither result, by itself, supplies a continuous-population algorithm.

The proponent’s role-schema construction is the best repair: replicate each rule over combinations of interchangeable agents and scale the values to keep total surplus finite. But that adds modeling choices the paper does not settle. A fixed rule, a rule copied across every matching tuple, and a rule attached to a particular interaction network can have different limits, and aggregate type masses alone may not determine the latter’s coalition value. So this construction is a plausible new typed MC-nets model, not a limit forced by the paper.

That objection does not defeat the strongest version of the mirror. In the proposed model, coalition excess is multi-affine in the type masses, so its maximum over the mass box occurs at a corner. The continuous pricing problem therefore reduces to checking the \(2^\tau\) all-or-none type coalitions. That may make the continuum less distinctive, but it still leaves a well-posed computational question about approximate least-core division as a function of the number of types. A hard or finite reduction is not, on this programme’s terms, a reason to reject the question.

So I would argue against prioritizing this paper on the ground that its finite-agent semantics do not select a unique continuous mirror. But I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile scenario survives: a large, role-based cooperative game with repeated interaction templates is plausible, and its bounded approximate payoff-division problem is recognizably the authors’ problem. The negative case is weak precisely at that opening.
