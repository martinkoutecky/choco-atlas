The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible route is a large climate-policy constituency: many people can be grouped into a small support of complete types, where a type captures its intergenerational evaluation—such as its discounting rule and concern for sustainability—and \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of the constituency. A decision might be to select a common consumption or investment stream, with the aim of finding the stream supported by the largest mass of types. That would make the population continuous in a setting close to the paper’s concerns.

But this is an extension of the paper’s subject, not a mirror of one of its computational results. The nearest numbered result is Theorem 1, attributed to Chichilnisky [11]: it states that an SP functional exists, with the paper’s displayed form \(W_{\mathrm{SP}}(u)=\sum_g \lambda_g u_g+\Phi(u)\), where the positive weights are summable and \(\Phi\) extends the limit functional. SP means the functional avoids dictatorship of both the present and the future and is increasing in the Pareto order. The paper reports this result from elsewhere; it does not establish a complexity classification or give an algorithm for computing such a functional.

So there is no qualifying anchor here, and no honest continuous problem per anchor to hand to a prover. The main mathematical objects the paper continuizes are utility streams over time and discount functionals—not a population of agents. My proposed constituency supplies a continuous population, but it adds voters and an aggregation rule the paper does not study. That is the weak point, and it is decisive for this task: the paper offers an interesting neighboring research direction, but no strong positive case for a continuous-population mirror of its named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only named result offered as an anchor is Theorem 1, the Chichilnisky existence result. It gives a functional \(W_{\mathrm{SP}}\) with the stated ethical and sustainability properties; it does not state the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s Poisson-horizon calculation likewise derives discount weights, rather than an algorithmic result. So there is no computational result here for a continuous-population mirror to classify.

The strongest version of the proposed climate constituency would give each type a complete preference over feasible consumption or investment streams, then ask which stream is acceptable to the largest mass—or how much mass must change type before a policy wins. That is a coherent computational social-choice question, and a constituency of many people sharing a small number of preference types is plausible. But it adds the population, the collective decision rule, and the computational objective. In the paper, a stream’s coordinates are generations and a discount functional evaluates that stream; the generations are not a population of voters choosing among streams. The proposed question would be worthwhile adjacent work, but it would not mirror Theorem 1.

The same problem defeats a closer-sounding reformulation: distribute evaluators across types and ask for a common functional satisfying their ethical demands. That turns the paper’s axiomatic question about preferences over time into an axiomatic aggregation question over people. The programme’s scope requires the population to be the continuous object and computational questions to be at stake; simply putting a distribution over evaluators does neither for the paper’s actual result.

I would not claim that no scenario could make such an adjacent project valuable. The negative case is weak as a universal claim about all possible research built from the paper’s themes. It is strong on the narrower point the proposed mirror needs: none of the paper’s named results supplies a computational problem to mirror, and adding a population and an algorithmic objective creates a new problem rather than a continuous version of one in the paper.
