The strongest unanchored candidate is a continuous participatory-budgeting consistency-recovery problem. A type would include the voter information supplied to the AI, that type’s human ballot and abstention category, and a distribution \(q_t\) over the AI ballots it may produce. The mass \(\mu_t\) is that type’s share of the population. Given abstaining mass \(a_t\), proxy mass \(x_t\le a_t\), project costs, a budget, and a specified continuous version of an aggregation rule \(R\), compute the paper’s normalized recovery score:

\[ \Delta_R(x)= \frac{C(W_R(\nu^x),W_R(\nu^H))-C(W_R(\nu^0),W_R(\nu^H))} {1-C(W_R(\nu^0),W_R(\nu^H))}. \]

Here \(\nu^H\) is the full human electorate, \(\nu^0\) is the electorate after abstention without AI proxies, \(\nu^x\) includes the specified proxy mass, \(W_R\) is the winning project set, and \(C\) is the paper’s pairwise consistency measure. A plausible regime is a city-wide participatory-budgeting vote with many residents but recurring complete voter profiles. This keeps population mass central and makes abstention and proxy representation natural quantities. I would initially expect the fixed-rule evaluation problem to be tractable once the continuous versions and tie-breaking are specified.

That is a plausible research question, not a mirror anchored by a result in this paper. Its weak point is substantial: the paper studies person-indexed empirical samples, and this proposal turns its measured recovery quantity into an exact computational task. The actual-voting trait sample is also limited, so the claim that real instances have moderate type support needs evidence.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its three numbered “key results” are empirical findings about human–AI consistency, abstention, and feature importance. The paper states no complexity bound or algorithm for a computational social choice problem. Its 363 elections and more than 50,000 AI personas are experimental scale, not a complexity analysis. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, there are therefore no anchors to assign to Classes A, B, or C.

The proposed consistency-recovery quantity is the closest candidate. Its type space can be made sound: a type could include the voter’s profile, human ballot, abstention category, and distribution over AI ballots. That preserves the relevant voter–proxy relationship, and a city-wide participatory-budgeting scenario with recurring profiles is plausible. So I would not reject it on grounds of identity, multiplicity, or the fact that the study’s matched sample is limited.

The problem is that, with proxy masses and aggregation rules fixed, \(\Delta_R(x)\) is an evaluation task built from applying a chosen rule and computing a finite project-set similarity. The paper does not pose an optimization or decision problem over \(x\). One could make a computational problem by choosing proxy masses to maximize recovery, imposing representation costs, or seeking a worst-case recovery guarantee. Those are coherent possibilities, but they add control objectives and constraints absent from the paper. They would be new algorithmic work motivated by its findings, rather than continuous versions of a computational result it established.

The other findings have the same gap. A population-level comparison of aggregation rules would extend the empirical resilience study; averaging feature effects over types would extend its explanatory analysis. Either could be useful, but neither gives this paper a computational problem whose complexity landscape the continuum could reveal.

It is weaker as a universal claim that *no* worthwhile ChoCo project could arise from the paper. Optimizing proxy allocation in a plausible high-multiplicity budgeting setting could be worthwhile.
