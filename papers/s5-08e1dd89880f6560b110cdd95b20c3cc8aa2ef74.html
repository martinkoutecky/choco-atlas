The anchor — algorithmic

Corollary 3.1

PDF text-layer excerpt — equations and symbol order may be approximateLet G be a directed graph with n vertices, and let opt(G) be the set of vertices matched under a speciﬁc maximum cardinality matching on G. Suppose that matchings are restricted to cycles of constant length, and pi = pj for any two players i, j. Then, with probability 1 −δ, there exists a matching that is individually rational for each player i, and has maximum cardinality up to O(k p |opt(G)| · ln(k/δ)).

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite directed compatibility graph \(A\) on types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), a fixed cycle limit \(\ell\), and \(k\) hospitals each holding \(\mu_t/k\) of type \(t\), choose nonnegative mass \(x_C\) on each compatible cycle \(C\) of hospital-labelled types \((i,t)\). Respect every supply constraint \(\sum_C a_{(i,t),C}x_C\le \mu_t/k\), and maximize \(\sum_C |C|x_C\) subject to each hospital’s matched mass being at least its internal optimum \(V(\mu)/k\), where \(V(b)\) is the maximum cycle-packing value at type supplies \(b\).

The model it lives in

A finite type compatibility graph with rational population masses and bounded cycle length; each hospital has the same fraction of every type. The decision variables are fractional amounts of compatible cycles over hospital-labelled types, constrained by each hospital’s type supplies and internal matching value. The objective is to maximize total matched mass while satisfying individual rationality.

What the mirror covers

Covers Corollary 3.1’s bounded-cycle equal-share guarantee and Theorem 2.2’s internal-versus-global comparison in the mass limit; leaves finite-sample concentration and the long-cycle counterexample outside this formulation.

Open questions for a prover

  • For hospital-specific supplies \(\mu_{i,t}\), which conditions preserve a full-efficiency individually rational packing?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is for a typed, fluid version of kidney exchange. Take a large pool of patient–donor pairs, grouped into \(\tau\) compatibility types. Each type must capture everything the model uses: in particular, whether a pair can participate in a cycle with another type. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), and hospital \(i\) owns mass \(p_i\mu_t\) of each type. This represents a regime with many pairs per type and a fixed or moderate number of types. It also captures the paper’s random ownership model in the limit: each type’s random hospital shares approach the proportions \(p_i\).

I would use two anchors. The lead is Corollary 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that when exchanges use cycles of constant length and each of \(k\) players owns each vertex independently with probability \(1/k\), there is a mechanism that is individually rational for every player and, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), returns a matching of size at least
\[ |\operatorname{opt}(G)|-O\!\left(k\sqrt{|\operatorname{opt}(G)|\ln(k/\delta)}\right). \]
The mechanism proposes a fixed optimal matching; if a player would do better alone, the players fall back to their internal optimal matchings. The paper proves the guarantee here. Its claim is about the mechanism’s performance, not its running time.

The supporting anchor is Theorem 2.2, also proved here. For a fixed maximum-cardinality matching, it bounds how much better a player’s optimal internal matching can be than the number of that player’s vertices matched by the fixed global solution. With probability at least \(1-\delta\), that difference is at most \(O(\sqrt{|\operatorname{opt}(G)|\ln(k/\delta)})\) for every player, provided either (i) only 2-cycles are allowed and each \(p_i\le 1/2\), or (ii) cycles have constant length and each \(1/p_i\) is an integer. This is a one-sided bound on the internal gain, not a bound on the absolute difference.

Here are the continuous problems I would put to a prover. Let \(T\) be a finite set of compatibility types, \(A\subseteq T\times T\) the directed compatibility relation, and \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) the mass of each type. Fix a cycle-length bound \(\ell\). A cycle pattern \(C\) is a compatible directed cycle of types of length at most \(\ell\); \(a_{tC}\) counts how many times it uses type \(t\). A matching is a nonnegative mass \(x_C\) on each pattern, subject to
\[ \sum_C a_{tC}x_C\le \mu_t. \]
Its value is \(\sum_C |C|x_C\). Write \(V(b)\) for the maximum value when the available type masses are \(b\).

The continuous counterpart of Theorem 2.2 is Fixed-Optimum Mass IR. Its instance is \((T,A,\mu,(p_i),x^\star)\), where \(x^\star\) is a specified optimal cycle flow and the proportions \(p_i\) satisfy either of the theorem’s two conditions. Ask for each player’s internal optimum and the gap between that value and their share of \(x^\star\). The solution consists of optimal internal flows and the exact gaps. In this fluid model, the answer is exact: player \(i\)’s outside option is \(V(p_i\mu)=p_iV(\mu)\), by scaling the cycle-flow LP, and their proportional share of the fixed global flow is also \(p_iV(\mu)\). The expected direction is therefore tractable, with zero gap. This is the population limit of the theorem’s sublinear finite-sample discrepancy; it is not a claim that the paper’s random finite instances have zero discrepancy.

The lead counterpart of Corollary 3.1 is Individually Rational Mass Cycle Packing. Use the same type graph, with \(p_i=1/k\) and player-labelled supplies \(\mu_t/k\). A feasible solution assigns mass to cycles over player-labelled types \((i,t)\), respecting each such type’s supply. Player \(i\)’s utility is the mass of their own vertices matched, and their outside option is the best cycle packing using only their own types. Ask for a feasible packing maximizing total matched mass subject to every player’s utility being at least their outside option. A solution is the cycle-mass vector together with its utilities.

This problem is an LP. More strongly, an optimal type-level packing can be split proportionally across the hospitals within each type: each hospital gets \(1/k\) of the matched mass of every type. Its utility is then \(V(\mu)/k\), exactly its internal optimum. Thus an individually rational global optimum exists in the continuous model. For fixed \(\ell\), the number of player-labelled cycle patterns is polynomial in \(k\tau\), so this formulation is polynomial-size. The direction is tractable. This gives the authors the same choice between global exchange and internal matching, while making the population itself a mass distribution.

That is a plausible mirror of the paper’s problem because it keeps the central objects and incentives: patient–donor pairs, bounded cycles, hospitals as players, matched vertices as utility, and the choice to participate globally or match internally. The co-modification is that indistinguishable pairs can be divided by mass and type-level cycle averages can be fractional. Every unit of mass is still assigned to an integral cycle; it is the amount assigned to a pattern that can be fractional.

The paper has no numbered theorem classifying one of its problems as in P or NP-hard. The closest named computational result is Corollary 3.1’s mechanism guarantee. The paper does note that finding a maximum-cardinality matching with 3-cycles is NP-hard, citing reference [2]; that is useful context, but it is not a numbered result proved here, so I would not use it as an anchor. It does sharpen the continuous question: does that discrete hardness persist for the finite-type mass LP, or does it dissolve under high multiplicity?

The weakest point is the type regime. In kidney exchange, compatibility can depend on patient-specific medical information, so a type that fully determines all relevant compatibilities may require many categories. If \(\tau\) grows close to the number of pairs, the compact high-multiplicity formulation loses its force. The mirror also assumes each hospital has the same proportion of every compatibility type—the fluid version of independent random ownership—and limits cycles to bounded length. These are meaningful restrictions, not properties of kidney exchange in general. Within that regime, though, the mirror is the same individual-rationality problem in a natural continuum limit, and it presents a concrete computational question with an LP answer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

My best case against starts with what these anchors actually prove. Theorem 2.2 is a concentration statement about random ownership of vertices in a fixed graph. Corollary 3.1 is a welfare guarantee for a veto mechanism. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem; the paper even says its mechanism is not a polynomial-time algorithm when 3-cycles are allowed. So a cycle-packing LP may be a computational question inspired by the paper, but it is not a continuous version of a complexity result the paper establishes.

For Theorem 2.2, the obstacle is that a vertex’s type must capture its compatibility with the rest of the graph. In an arbitrary graph those compatibility profiles can all be different, leaving \(\tau\) essentially as large as the population. In the special block-structured cases where a small type space is credible, independent ownership converges to exact ownership shares. If type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), let \(V(b)\) be the maximum cycle-packing value at supplies \(b\). Homogeneity gives \(V(p_i\mu)=p_iV(\mu)\); the share of a fixed optimal flow is also \(p_iV(\mu)\). The random internal-gain discrepancy the theorem bounds has vanished. Making ownership vary by type could retain a nonzero comparison, but it would replace the theorem’s uniform independent labeling with a different deterministic instance.

The same point weakens Corollary 3.1’s proposed mirror. With exact equal shares of every compatibility type, an optimal fluid cycle packing can be divided among hospitals so each receives \(1/k\) of the matched mass of every type. Each hospital then gets \(V(\mu)/k\), its internal optimum, while the global value remains \(V(\mu)\). The veto and fallback choices are unnecessary, and the finite-population loss disappears. With bounded cycle length and an explicit finite type graph, the resulting cycle-packing LP has polynomially many patterns. That is a clean fluid model, but it removes the ownership fluctuations that motivate the corollary.

A stronger proposal would give each hospital and compatibility type its own supply \(\mu_{i,t}\), then optimize cycle flows subject to each hospital meeting its internal outside option. That is a sensible high-multiplicity allocation problem, and its LP could be worth studying. But it no longer mirrors the paper’s i.i.d. ownership guarantee; it is a deterministic fractional mechanism-design extension. Calling it out does not establish that the authors’ original results have continuous computational counterparts.

This is the strongest negative case I can make: the paper offers probabilistic welfare analysis, not a named complexity result, and the most direct continuum limit erases the random effect those results quantify. But I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. Repeated compatibility types and hospital-specific masses are plausible, and the resulting individually rational mass-packing problem is close enough to the paper’s central design question that the authors might recognize it as theirs. The case against is therefore strongest as a boundary on what these two results mirror, and weak as a claim against every useful continuous formulation.
