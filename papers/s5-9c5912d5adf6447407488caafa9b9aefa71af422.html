The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has not raised an eligible anchor: the paper states no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its claims concern DBV’s axiomatic properties, finite examples, and a descriptive shock calculation. The 17-voter example is not a computational result, so there is no prover question to attach to it.

A continuous population is quite plausible here. Investors could be grouped by their rankings of assets, and the three ranking types in §4.1 already illustrate how counts can become masses. But plausibility of the population model does not supply the missing computational question. Computing DBV from masses means aggregating Borda scores and sorting candidates. Asking whether the DBV order persists after a profile change is an axiomatic check. Asking for the least mass that must change its ranking to alter the winner would be a new robustness problem, not a result the paper establishes.

There is a stronger possible extension: assign costs to ranking changes and find the least-cost mass transfer that changes the winner after clones are added. That could connect to continuous bribery and might be worth studying. But the paper supplies neither that cost model nor that optimization objective; adding them imports a new problem rather than mirroring one of its results.

So the strongest case against is that this paper offers no eligible computational anchor, and its natural continuous restatements are either direct calculations or new questions. The universal claim is less secure: a carefully chosen mass-manipulation problem could be a worthwhile extension. The evidence supports “no worthwhile mirror is established by this paper,” more firmly than “no worthwhile mirror can be devised in any scenario.”
