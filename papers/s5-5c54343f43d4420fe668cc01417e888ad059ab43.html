The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a possible continuous follow-up, not a mirror anchored in a named computational result. The paper has no numbered result asserting a complexity classification or giving an algorithm for an underlying computational problem. Theorem 14, proved here, says that the Minimal Overlap rule is the only bankruptcy rule satisfying Anonymity, Continuity, and \(\mu\)-Additivity. Proposition 13, also proved here, says that \(\mu\)-Additivity together with Continuity implies Claims Truncation Invariance. Both are axiomatic results, not computational ones.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime would be a failed institution with many retail creditors in a small number of standardized claim cohorts. Let \(T\) be the finite set of claim types, \(\mu_t\) the fraction of creditors of type \(t\), \(q_t\) their claim, and \(e\) the estate per creditor, with \(0<e<\sum_t\mu_tq_t\). For each replication \(r\) making every \(r\mu_t\) integral, form \(r\mu_t\) creditors of type \(t\), each with claim \(q_t\), and estate \(re\). Let \(x_{t,r}\) be the Minimal Overlap award per creditor of type \(t\).

A concrete proposed question is High-Multiplicity Minimal-Overlap Limit: given rational \((T,\mu,q,e)\), determine whether each limit \(a_t=\lim_{r\to\infty}x_{t,r}\) exists and, if it does, compute the award vector. A solution is the per-creditor award \(a_t\), satisfying \(0\le a_t\le q_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t=e\). This is an award-computation problem, not an optimization problem: the paper’s object is a rule, not an objective to minimize. The natural conjecture is a constrained-equal-losses limit, \(a_t=\max\{q_t-\lambda,0\}\), where \(\lambda\) satisfies \(\sum_t\mu_t\max\{q_t-\lambda,0\}=e\). With finite support, computing that candidate is straightforward by sorting claim levels; the substantive task is proving that the Minimal Overlap awards converge to it. I would expect this proposed problem to be tractable.

The regime is credible: the number of creditors \(r\) grows while the number of standardized claim types \(\tau=|T|\) stays small. The scope is narrow: it concerns the high-multiplicity behavior of the Minimal Overlap allocation characterized in Theorem 14, not a continuous version of the paper’s axiomatic characterization itself.

The weak point is decisive for the requested anchor standard: this is a new replication-limit question, not a computational question the paper names or proves hard or tractable. The authors might recognize it as a sensible extension of their rule, but it cannot honestly be presented as a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to mirror. Theorem 14 characterizes the Minimal Overlap rule through Anonymity, Continuity, and \(\mu\)-Additivity; Proposition 13 derives Claims Truncation Invariance. Neither states a complexity result or studies an algorithmic task. A continuous axiomatic characterization might be interesting, but it would continuize the paper’s axioms, which ChoCo places outside scope.

The proposed high-multiplicity limit has a credible population model: standardized creditor cohorts with claims \(q_t\) and masses \(\mu_t\). Identity is no obstacle, since creditors with the same claim are interchangeable under the rule. The trouble is what replication does to the rule’s distinctive component. With \(r\) replicas, estate \(re\), and fixed per-creditor claims, the estate eventually exceeds the largest claim \(q_{\max}\). In the paper’s decomposition, the Ibn Ezra part then allocates a total of only \(q_{\max}\), while the estate grows with \(r\). Anonymity spreads that bounded allocation across each positive-mass cohort, so its per-creditor contribution vanishes. The proposed limit therefore washes out the part that distinguishes Minimal Overlap; this is a genuine degeneration of the candidate mirror.

A stronger computational framing would take a finite-support distribution of claim types and ask for the award to each type. One could also represent the paper’s merger comparison directly: give each type a pair of claims to two firms and compare its awards before and after merger. Both are coherent follow-ups. Neither is a computational result of this paper, though: its central theorem characterizes a rule and its additivity property. To turn merger comparison into an optimization problem would require adding feasible merger choices, costs, and an objective that the paper does not specify.

So the negative case is strongest at the paper-to-programme boundary: this is an axiomatic bankruptcy paper, and its natural high-multiplicity extensions are allocation or limit questions rather than mirrors of computational results. The universal claim is less secure. A continuous study of bankruptcy allocations could be worthwhile on its own; the case against is that it would be a new line of work, not a continuous computational mirror of this paper.
