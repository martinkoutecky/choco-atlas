The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of rational processing times \(P\) and rational masses \(\mu_p\ge 0\), with \(\sum_{p\in P}\mu_p=1\) in each of two departments, decide whether a proportionally fair Pareto schedule exists and construct one if it does. A schedule is a measurable assignment \(q_{i,p}(s)\) on \(s\in[0,2]\), with \(q_{i,p}(s)\ge 0\), \(\sum_{i\in\{A,B\},p\in P}q_{i,p}(s)=1\), and \(\int_0^2 q_{i,p}(s)\,ds=\mu_p\); its clock is \(C(s)=\int_0^s\sum_{i,p}p\,q_{i,p}(r)\,dr\), and department \(i\)'s cost is \(F_i(q)=\int_0^2\sum_p q_{i,p}(s)C(s)\,ds\). Define Pareto schedules, utilities, and proportional fairness from \((F_A,F_B)\) as in the paper.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic limit of symmetric two-department single-machine scheduling, interpreting the repeated jobs as high-volume service requests. Each department has unit mass over processing-time types; a mass schedule assigns requests to positions on the machine, and fairness is evaluated using the departments’ aggregate completion-time costs.

The objection that survived

The continuous mass is of jobs or requests, while fairness remains between two fixed departments; the paper does not itself treat those requests as individual agents with their own utilities. The proposed service-request setting makes that interpretation plausible, but it remains the main boundary of the mirror.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 6 and 7 for symmetric instances of \(1\mid\mid(\sum C_j^A,\sum C_j^B)\), using the same aggregate costs and fairness notions. It leaves the price-of-fairness results and the mixed completion-time/tardiness problem unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the continuous schedule be represented with finite rational data and computed in time polynomial in the number of processing-time types and input bit length?
  • What approximation or rounding guarantee relates the continuous fair schedule to finite instances with many requests?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s symmetric two-agent problem: many repeated jobs from each of two departments share a machine, and fairness is judged by each department’s aggregate completion time. The continuum is over the job population. It leaves the paper’s two stakeholders and their fairness criteria intact.

Take a finite set of processing times \(P\). Each department has a unit of job mass, with mass \(\mu_p\) of processing time \(p\), where \(\sum_{p\in P}\mu_p=1\); the two departments have the same profile. Thus the types are \((A,p)\) and \((B,p)\), and the support has \(2\tau\) types if there are \(\tau\) distinct processing times. A plausible regime is two high-volume departments sending standardized orders to a shared production or service facility: the number \(N\) of orders per department is large, while the number of distinct processing-time types is small.

Here is a precise continuous scheduling problem. A schedule is a measurable assignment \(q_{i,p}(s)\) on normalized job rank \(s\in[0,2]\), where \(q_{i,p}(s)\) is the fraction of mass at rank \(s\) assigned to type \((i,p)\). Require \(q_{i,p}\ge 0\), \(\sum_{i,p}q_{i,p}(s)=1\) almost everywhere, and \(\int_0^2 q_{i,p}(s)\,ds=\mu_p\) for each owner \(i\) and processing time \(p\). Set \(C(s)=\int_0^s\sum_{i,p}p\,q_{i,p}(r)\,dr\), the completion time on the clock scaled by \(N\), and \(F_i(q)=\int_0^2\sum_p q_{i,p}(s)C(s)\,ds\). For discrete instances with \(N\mu_p\) jobs of each type, \(F_i\) is the limiting total completion time for department \(i\), scaled by \(N^2\).

Define Pareto schedules from the cost pairs \((F_A,F_B)\), and define utilities, KS fairness, and proportional fairness exactly as in the paper, replacing the discrete costs with \(F_i\). The two questions are:

  • Symmetric Continuous Proportional-Fair Scheduling (lead). Given rational \(P\) and rational masses \((\mu_p)_{p\in P}\) common to both departments, decide whether a proportionally fair Pareto schedule exists; if one exists, output it.
  • Symmetric Continuous KS-Fair Scheduling. On the same input, output a KS-fair Pareto schedule.

The anchors are the paper’s Theorem 6 and Theorem 7, both proved there. Theorem 6 says that, given a symmetric instance of \(1\mid\mid(\sum C_j^A,\sum C_j^B)\), it is NP-hard to decide whether a proportionally fair schedule exists among Pareto optimal schedules, and, if one exists, it is NP-hard to find it. Theorem 7 says that for a symmetric instance of the same problem, it is NP-hard to find a KS-fair schedule among Pareto optimal schedules. The first is my lead: it makes the sharper hardness claim, covering both existence and search.

In the continuous problems, both hardness results have an explicit tractable counterpart. Sort the processing times increasingly. For each \(p\), give the interval of total mass \(2\mu_p\) half to department A and half to department B, then proceed to the next processing time. This is a balanced SPT schedule: it minimizes total completion cost and has \(F_A=F_B=f\). By symmetry, the two departments have equal best and worst Pareto costs. Any schedule has total cost at least \(2f\), so its larger department cost is at least \(f\). It follows that this schedule maximizes the worse normalized utility, making it KS-fair. Its utilities are equal, and its total cost is minimal, so no Pareto schedule can improve the sum of proportional utility changes; it is proportionally fair as well. Sorting the \(\tau\) processing times and outputting the intervals takes \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) rational comparisons and \(O(\tau)\) blocks. Thus the predicted outcome for both mirrors is Class A, with existence always affirmative on these symmetric inputs.

This is not just a renamed scheduling problem: it keeps the single machine, owners, processing times, sum-of-completion-times objectives, Pareto comparison, utilities, and fairness tests. It replaces job counts and permutations by a mass ordering, exactly the high-multiplicity move. It also targets precisely the symmetric regime used by both hardness theorems, rather than claiming to mirror every result in the paper. I am not claiming a mirror of the paper’s price-of-fairness bounds here.

The weakest point is the identity of the population being continuized. The paper’s two agents are departments; the continuous population is their jobs or orders. A strict reading that requires the stakeholders themselves to form a large population could reject that as outside scope. The case is strongest when the resource-allocation population is understood as the many requests competing for service, with fairness still assessed between the two departments. A second open detail is the implementation meaning of the fractional schedule: it is an exact nonatomic limit, while a finite schedule would need an approximation or rounding guarantee. Those limits do not erase the mirror, but they mark what should be tested next: whether the authors accept requests as the population units, and how the continuous witness translates back to large finite instances.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6 and Theorem 7 are genuine computational anchors, but both concern the same two-agent problem. The difficulty is deciding how to divide indivisible jobs between two stakeholders so their aggregate completion-time costs satisfy a fairness criterion. The reduction uses many job lengths to encode Partition; it does not create a large population of agents. In the paper’s model, the agents are A and B. Their jobs are objects they own.

The proposed mirror turns those objects into divisible workload mass. That gives a coherent fluid-scheduling problem, but the society becomes continuous only if jobs count as people. A stronger version of the proposal could treat each job as a service request from an individual, with type \((\text{owner},p)\). Yet the paper’s fairness tests compare the two owners’ aggregate costs. If fairness instead applies to the requesters, the objective and fairness problem change; if it remains at the owner level, there are still only two agents. A model with many departments could make the agents themselves a population, but it would be a multi-agent extension rather than a mirror of either theorem.

This is the negative case’s main point, and it is a boundary of interpretation rather than a mathematical obstruction. If the programme accepts requesters or orders as the relevant population while retaining owner-level fairness, then the balanced fluid schedule is a sensible mirror of both anchors. Its easy solution cannot count against it: a continuous relaxation that removes the Partition obstruction may be exactly what the programme wants to discover. I therefore cannot honestly make a strong universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The case depends on whether workload mass, with fairness still measured between two fixed owners, qualifies as continuizing the population.
