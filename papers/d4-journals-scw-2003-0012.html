The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only proposed anchor—the distribution of respondents’ answer patterns—does not yield a computational result from this paper. The paper reports survey findings: for example, Table 7 reports acceptance of the Pareto principle, while Tables 3–6 report choices about compensation and redistribution. A continuous population of respondents is perfectly sensible: take each complete response pattern as a type and its share as mass. But computing the reported shares from a given distribution is just weighted counting. If the question is instead what those shares are in a wider population, the hard part is sampling, selection, translation, and measurement—not a computational social choice problem over a continuous society.

The strongest proposed upgrade would treat respondents as voters over redistribution policies and ask which budget-balanced policy best reflects their preferences. That would require a common policy set, a way to turn answers to the paper’s separate hypothetical cases into preferences over that set, and an aggregation rule or objective. The paper supplies none of these. Adding them might produce a worthwhile project, but it would be a new social choice problem whose computational questions come from the added model. On the paper’s actual finite menus, even aggregating a large mass of response types would remain straightforward.

The formal result mentioned in Section 2 does not fill the gap: Fleurbaey’s incompatibility result for full and strict compensation is attributed to earlier work, and it is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result of this paper. A continuous-population question about whether analogous axioms can be jointly satisfied would likewise be axiomatic. Turning the redistribution examples into an algorithmic task would again require adding a policy objective and mechanism the authors do not study.

So the negative case is strongest at the paper level: it offers no computational problem whose continuous mirror could chart a complexity landscape. Its survey data could motivate or inform a separate aggregation project, but that possibility does not give this paper a continuous computational mirror. The universal claim that no scenario could make such a project worthwhile is harder to defend; what the evidence supports is that any such project would have to supply its central computational question from outside this paper.
