The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a high-multiplicity continuum of patient–donor pairs, with exchange cycles replaced by cycle-mass variables. There is, however, a formal problem with the paper as an anchor: the supplied text contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Proposition, or Corollary. Thus no anchor satisfies the rubric literally. The closest substantive computational result is an unnumbered claim in Section 2.1.2, attributed to Abraham, Blum, and Sandholm (2007), and not proved in this survey.

That claim is that finding a maximum-cardinality kidney-exchange matching when exchange cycles of size greater than two are allowed is NP-complete; the cited work also proposes a scalable algorithm for the optimization problem. This is the best hardness anchor available in the paper, but it has no theorem number here.

The corresponding continuous problem would be Fractional Three-Way Kidney-Exchange Packing\(_\infty\).

An instance contains a finite set \(T\) of complete patient–donor types. A type records every feature relevant to feasibility and policy: patient and donor blood types, tissue or crossmatch category, size category, transplant centre or eligibility restrictions, and any other attribute used by the exchange rule. A society is a distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}^{T}\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of all patient–donor pairs of type \(t\).

Let \(\mathcal{E}_3\) be the set of feasible exchange cycles of length two or three over these types. For \(e\in\mathcal{E}_3\), let \(a_{e,t}\) be the number of times type \(t\) occurs in the cycle. The decision variable \(x_e\ge 0\) is the mass of cycles of pattern \(e\). It must satisfy

\[ \sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_3}a_{e,t}x_e\le \mu_t \qquad\text{for every }t\in T. \]

The objective is to maximize the fraction of patients transplanted:

\[ \max \sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_3}|e|x_e. \]

A solution is an exact feasible vector \(x\), together with the resulting transplant rate. No organ or transplant is fractional: \(x_e\) means that a corresponding fraction of otherwise ordinary patient–donor pairs is assigned to complete, integral exchange cycles.

This is recognisably the authors’ problem. It preserves the medical compatibility relation, the central exchange pool, the restriction to two- and three-way cycles, and the objective of maximizing the number of transplants. The only change is that a population containing \(n_t\) pairs of each type is represented by proportions \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and cycle assignments become mass assignments. It is therefore a population continuization, not continuity in the organ or outcome space.

The natural regime is a large national or multi-hospital exchange pool in which many pairs share recurring medical profiles. A particularly persuasive version is a high-volume liver or dual-graft programme, where blood type and finitely many size categories dominate compatibility. A kidney version is also defensible when tissue compatibility is represented by a fixed collection of recurring crossmatch classes. The intended regime has \(n\gg |T|=\tau\): thousands or more participating pairs, but a stable finite catalogue of pair types. This is consistent with the survey’s own repeated use of large pools generated by an underlying population distribution and its claim that two- and three-way exchanges capture most gains in sufficiently large markets.

I would expect this continuous problem to fall in Class A. For a fixed maximum cycle length of three, \(\mathcal{E}_3\) has at most \(O(\tau^3)\) type-level cycle patterns, so the problem is an ordinary linear program. The discrete NP-completeness can plausibly disappear because it is driven by assigning indivisible named pairs to hyperedges, whereas the continuum assigns mass to those same patterns. The continuous optimum would provide an upper bound and a natural starting point for rounding algorithms for finite exchange pools.

The main further questions are whether one can prove a useful additive rounding guarantee for the discrete problem, whether unbounded cycle lengths produce a genuinely hard pricing problem, and how the formulation changes when donor drop-out, centre quotas, dynamic arrivals, or dual-donor exchanges are added.

The weakest point is decisive under the stated rubric: this survey does not itself contain a numbered computational theorem, and its NP-completeness claim is merely attributed to Abraham, Blum, and Sandholm (2007). Even substantively, kidney types can be highly individualized because of HLA compatibility, making \(\tau\) grow with the population. Still, the mirror itself is strong: it asks exactly for the continuous-population version of the paper’s central exchange-packing problem, and the paper’s large-market and population-distribution discussion gives the authors a natural reason to recognise it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor fails the rubric before the modelling question arises. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting a computational result. Its only serious hardness claim—that maximum-cardinality exchange with cycles longer than two is NP-complete—is explicitly attributed to Abraham, Blum, and Sandholm (2007), together with their algorithm. Edmonds’s polynomial matching result and the priority-mechanism claims are likewise reported from other work. Thus the proposed anchor is not a result of this paper at all. On the stated rules, there are no admissible anchors to preserve.

Granting a more generous reading, however, does not produce a strong substantive objection. The proposed mass-packing model is coherent: a type can include the complete medical and institutional profile needed to determine compatibility, and \(x_e\) can represent mass assigned to whole, integral exchange cycles. The objective—maximizing the population fraction transplanted—does not depend on personal identity. Large pools with recurring blood-type, size, centre, and crossmatch categories are also a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The paper itself repeatedly invokes large markets generated by underlying population distributions.

The best technical objection is narrower. The NP-completeness result concerns an arbitrary compatibility graph on named pairs. In such an instance, preserving every compatibility relation may require treating each pair’s entire compatibility signature as its type; then the number of types can grow with the population, and the finite-type high-multiplicity interpretation disappears. Replacing that graph by a small catalogue of recurring medical types yields a sensible new model, but it is a structured special case rather than the general computational problem reported in the cited result. This weakens the claim that the mirror is genuinely forced by the paper, though it does not invalidate the special-case programme.

Nor can one fairly object that the resulting LP is “too easy” or that cycles become fractional: those are precisely the kinds of tractable continuous relaxations the programme seeks, and each unit of mass can still receive an integral cycle. If the external Abraham–Blum–Sandholm result is allowed as an anchor, the positive case is therefore quite strong.

So the honest negative conclusion is conditional but decisive under the assignment’s rules: this paper supplies no named computational result of its own, and the sole proposed anchor belongs to cited prior work. Beyond that formal point, the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists is weak; a high-multiplicity exchange-packing mirror is genuinely plausible.
