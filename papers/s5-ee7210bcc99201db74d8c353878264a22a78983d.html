The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) items and a supported ranking distribution \(\mu\), with \(\mu_t=n_t/r\) for integer counts \(n_t\) summing to \(r\), promised to be the empirical histogram of \(r\) independent Mallows draws with parameter \(\beta\), compute a permutation \(\sigma\) minimizing \(F_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_t\mu_t d_K(t,\sigma)\). Can this be done directly from the support and counts, with the high-probability guarantee and running-time bound of Theorem 7, while accounting for support size and bit length?

The model it lives in

The types are complete rankings \(t\in S_m\), and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of survey respondents reporting ranking \(t\). The decision variable is a collective ranking \(\sigma\); the objective is its average Kendall disagreement, \(F_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_t\mu_t d_K(t,\sigma)\). The input also records \(r\) and the Mallows sampling promise.

The objection that survived

Theorem 7’s guarantee applies to histograms generated by independent Mallows samples, not to arbitrary rational masses; the mirror must retain that sampling promise and encode the sample size.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the Theorem 7 algorithm and its failure bound be implemented from the sparse counts \(n_t\), with bit complexity polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length, without expanding the \(r\) reports?
  • How far does tractability extend beyond empirical histograms from the Mallows sampling regime, given that unrestricted Kemeny aggregation is NP-hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Mallows reconstruction result. Consider a large survey asking respondents to rank a fixed set of candidates, treatments, or policy options. A type is one complete ranking \(t\in S_m\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of respondents who gave that ranking. Respondents with the same ranking are interchangeable for this task. With many respondents and a short shortlist, the population can be much larger than the number \(\tau\) of ranking types observed.

The underlying decision is to choose a collective order \(\sigma\). A precise continuous problem is Mallows Histogram Kemeny Aggregation:

  • The input is a support of \(\tau\) rankings \(t\in S_m\) and binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), representing the histogram of \(r\) reports.
  • The reports are generated independently from a Mallows model with parameter \(\beta>0\) around an unknown order.
  • Find any permutation \(\sigma\) minimizing

\[ F_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_t \mu_t\,d_K(t,\sigma), \]
where \(d_K\) counts the pairs ordered differently. This is the average disagreement with the population; multiplying by \(r\) recovers the paper’s objective.

My anchor is Theorem 7, proved in this paper. It says that for \(r\) independent rankings generated from Mallows’ model with parameter \(\beta>0\), and any \(\alpha>0\), a randomized algorithm computes a maximum-probability order in time
\[ O\!\left( m^{1+O(\alpha/(\beta r))} 2^{O(\alpha/\beta+1/\beta^2)} \log^2 m \right) \]
with error probability below \(m^{-\alpha}\). The theorem’s maximum-probability order is exactly a minimizer of the sum of the \(r\) Kendall distances, so the histogram objective is the same optimization problem after normalization.

I expect this mirror to be tractable in the paper’s Mallows sampling regime. The histogram retains the multiplicities needed for the objective: the paper’s pairwise counts and average positions can be computed by summing over the \(\tau\) supported types and their rational masses. A compressed-input version should account explicitly for \(\tau\) and the mass bit length \(L\), rather than expanding the \(r\) reports. The question for a prover is whether the theorem’s algorithm and success guarantee carry through with that representation.

This covers Theorem 7’s Mallows reconstruction result, not the paper’s separate noisy-comparison results. The paper also notes that unrestricted Mallow reconstruction is NP-hard, citing prior work. That is a real boundary: for arbitrary \(\mu\), the weighted problem contains discrete Kemeny instances and hardness transfers. The tractable expectation here depends on the Mallows-generated population regime; it does not extend to every rational ranking distribution.

The weak point is that the paper models a finite collection of noisy reports, not a population distribution as its starting point. The mirror treats that collection as a mass profile, and the high-multiplicity scenario is strongest when the shortlist is limited enough that many respondents share observed rankings. That is a recognizable continuous-population version of the paper’s aggregation problem, though the histogram representation itself is not a novel idea.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the mirror has to choose between an exact population law and an empirical histogram, and those preserve different parts of Theorem 7.

If the society is the exact Mallows distribution \(\nu\) around \(\pi^\star\), the objective is expected disagreement,
\[ F_\nu(\sigma)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\nu}[d_K(t,\sigma)]. \]
For each pair, the Mallows law favors the order in \(\pi^\star\); those pairwise preferences are jointly consistent. So the population objective is minimized by \(\pi^\star\), which can be recovered from the pairwise marginals. The sample fluctuations that make reconstruction interesting in Theorem 7 have disappeared in this limit.

To retain those fluctuations, use the empirical histogram \(\mu\) of \(r\) sampled rankings. Then
\[ r F_\mu(\sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^r d_K(\pi_i,\sigma). \]
This is exactly the paper’s MRP objective in compressed form. It is a credible high-multiplicity scenario—many respondents ranking a fixed shortlist—and the type masses retain the information the objective needs. The theorem’s guarantee applies to histograms drawn from the stated sampling process; it does not automatically apply to arbitrary rational masses.

That leaves a real question about algorithms for a compressed, high-multiplicity input, but the negative case cannot make it disappear: this empirical-histogram mirror fits the programme’s scope. Saying that it is just a different encoding of the sample is not enough to disqualify it, and I do not see a deeper obstruction that defeats the best version. So my case against is weak: the exact-law version loses the theorem’s central difficulty, but the empirical version survives as a worthwhile mirror.
