The continuous mirror question

Given a catalogue \(G\), at most two complete valuation types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational additive values \(v_t(g)\), find a partition \(G=B_0\,\dot\cup\,B_1\) and assignment masses \(z_{tb}\ge 0\) such that \(\sum_b z_{tb}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t z_{tb}=1/2\), and \(v_t(B_b)\ge v_t(B_{b'})-\bar v_t(B_{b'})\) for every supported type \(t\) and \(b,b'\in\{0,1\}\). Each unit of mass receives one integral bundle; equivalently, an even finite population has \(N/2\) copies of each catalogue good and receives \(N/2\) copies of each bundle template.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic society with at most two valuation types, per-capita supply \(1/2\) of each indivisible catalogue good, and two integral bundle templates, each assigned to mass \(1/2\); find templates satisfying symEF1 for every supported type.

The objection that survived

The mass distribution μ does not affect the core bundle search: the coloring solves the two-agent problem, while μ only determines how types fill the two half-mass template slots.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror uses Corollary 13 for two valuation types and two bundle templates. It leaves the paper’s general sufficient condition, allocation-counting results, simulations, and other existence results outside this model.

Open questions for a prover

  • For more than two complete valuation types, under what conditions do two symEF1 bundle templates exist, and can they be found efficiently?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror I see is a narrow, constructive tractability result for a high-multiplicity society with at most two valuation types and two repeated bundle templates.

Anchor: Corollary 13, proved in this paper. It states that for two agents, a symEF1 allocation exists. In full: for any two agents with additive nonnegative valuations over a finite set of indivisible goods, there is a partition \((A_1,A_2)\) such that, for each agent \(i\) and each \(k,\ell\in\{1,2\}\), \(v_i(A_k)\ge v_i(A_\ell)-\bar v_i(A_\ell)\). The proof constructs the item graph from the agents’ ranked pairs of goods, shows it is bipartite, and uses a two-coloring to obtain the partition. The paper does not state a complexity-class theorem such as “in P”; the coloring construction yields that algorithmic consequence directly, in time polynomial in the number of goods and the valuation bit length.

A plausible regime is a large recurring aid or provisioning programme. The population has mass \(1\) and at most two complete recipient types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\). Each type has the same additive valuation vector across all recipients of that type. The programme has a fixed catalogue of \(M\) indivisible goods, replicated as the population grows. A finite approximant with \(N\) recipients has \(N/2\) copies of the catalogue, so each catalogue good has per-capita supply \(1/2\). Here \(N\) grows while the number of recipient types and catalogue goods stay fixed. Recipients still receive integral bundles; the fractional quantity appears only in aggregate supply.

Call the mirror Two-Template Continuum symEF1 Feasibility. Its instance is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), where \(|T|\le 2\), and rational additive values \(v_t(g)\) for each type \(t\) and catalogue good \(g\). Find a partition of the catalogue into integral bundles \(B_0,B_1\), and assignment masses \(z_{tb}\ge0\), such that \(\sum_b z_{tb}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_t z_{tb}=1/2\), and, for every supported type \(t\) and both \(b,b'\in\{0,1\}\),
\[ v_t(B_b)\ge v_t(B_{b'})-\bar v_t(B_{b'}), \]
where \(v_t(B)=\sum_{g\in B}v_t(g)\). The assignment masses describe which portions of each type receive each bundle; because the population is nonatomic, the required half-and-half split is feasible for any \(\mu\). The objective is feasibility: output the two-bundle partition and the assignment masses. The two-template condition means the finite approximant has \(N/2\) copies of each bundle, so every agent can be assigned either template while the item supplies balance.

This mirrors Corollary 13’s underlying allocation question, not a divisible-goods or lottery problem. The goods remain indivisible, each recipient gets one bundle, and every type must satisfy EF1 against either template. Applying the corollary to the at-most-two valuation vectors gives the two bundles; its graph-coloring proof gives a polynomial-time construction in \(M\) and the input bit length. The distribution \(\mu\) determines the assignment masses but does not make new fairness constraints necessary, because the guarantee covers both templates for every type.

The scope is Corollary 13’s two-agent result, lifted to a repeated high-multiplicity regime. It does not cover the paper’s general many-agent existence results, its allocation-counting results, or its simulations. Natural next questions are whether similar guarantees or efficient constructions hold with three or more valuation types, and what changes when the number of distinct bundle templates grows with the population.

The weak point is the restricted regime: two templates and at most two valuation types make the continuous problem a repeated form of the paper’s two-agent case. That makes the mirror plausible and its algorithm concrete, but modest. The manuscript contains no named NP-hardness result, so this anchor does not set up the programme’s usual question of whether discrete hardness dissolves in the continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Corollary 13: for two agents, a symEF1 allocation exists. It is an existence result rather than a stated complexity theorem, but its bipartite-graph proof gives a polynomial-time construction, so I would not dismiss it as noncomputational.

The best objection is to what the proposed mirror gets from that result. With two bundle templates, the distribution \(\mu\) does no work in the allocation problem. Once the two bundles are found, nonatomicity lets each type be split between them to meet the template masses. The algorithm therefore solves the paper’s finite, two-agent problem; \(N\), \(\mu\), and their bit lengths do not affect its computational core. This makes the mirror a thin repeated two-recipient setting, rather than a complexity question driven by a growing population.

A stronger version would let the number of agents and bundles grow together, keep a fixed set of valuation types, and scale goods supplies per capita while each recipient still gets an integral bundle. That is a coherent high-multiplicity model, and the paper’s definition applies naturally. But Corollary 13 no longer anchors it: it covers only two agents. Theorem 12 offers a sufficient coloring condition for general \(n\), not a complexity result for that growing-population regime. The stronger version could be worthwhile, but it would be a new problem built from the paper’s framework, not a mirror supported by this anchor.

I would not object that type-level prices are invalid, that the EF1 allowance disappears, or that the answer is already known; none of those defeats the mirror. My negative case is narrower: the version directly supported by Corollary 13 has little population computation in it, while the version with a genuine growing-population question outruns the cited result. That is a weak case against the anchor, though, because the two-template provisioning scenario is sensible and the programme explicitly permits per-capita supply to scale. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror on this basis.
