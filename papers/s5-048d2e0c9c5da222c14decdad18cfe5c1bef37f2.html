The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many types \(t=(x,j)\), with \(x\in[0,1]\), masses summing to one, and \(N\mu_t\) integral, let \(p_j\) be group \(j\)'s mass and \(N_j=Np_j\). For \(N_j\ge2\), set the public congestion parameter \(\alpha_j=\beta_j/(N_j-1)\) for \(\beta_j\in[0,1]\); singleton groups have \(\beta_j=0\). Find an exact deterministic rule \(f(\mu)\in[0,1]\) minimizing \(F_\mu(y)=\sum_j p_j\bigl[\beta_j+(1-\beta_j)\int |y-x|\,d\nu_j(x)\bigr]\), where \(\nu_j\) is group \(j\)'s conditional location distribution, and truthful for an individual location deviation that transfers mass \(1/N\) within their group under the paper's individual cost. Res-M gives a lower weighted median of weights \(\mu_t(1-\beta_j)\), with the smallest supported location as tie-break; if all weights vanish, choose the smallest supported location.

The model it lives in

Types are location–group pairs \((x,j)\) with mass \(\mu_{x,j}\); group mass is \(p_j\), and congestion scales as \(\alpha_j=\beta_j/(N_j-1)\), where \(N_j=Np_j\). The decision is a facility location \(y\in[0,1]\), the objective is \(F_\mu(y)\), and an individual report moves \(1/N\) mass within their public group. The exact rule is a weighted median computable from the support.

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 3.5’s location-only misreporting regime, its strategyproofness guarantee, and its exact social-cost optimum; leaves the private-group and maximum-cost results outside the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The lead is Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper’s Appendix A. It says that Res-M is strategyproof when locations are private and group memberships are public, and that it exactly minimizes social cost in that setting. Res-M chooses the smallest reported location \(x_i\) for which the total weight at or to its left is at least the total weight to its right, where \(w_i=1-\alpha_{g_i}(|G_{g_i}|-1)\). The paper does not give a named NP-hardness or complexity-class result; this is my one constructive algorithmic anchor. Sorting makes its implementation polynomial, though the theorem is not stated as a formal “in P” result.

A natural regime is a large city planning a shared parking facility along a corridor. There may be hundreds of thousands of residents, but many share a location zone and a publicly known vehicle class, so the number \(\tau\) of distinct location–group types is much smaller than the population \(N\). A type is \((x,j)\): location \(x\) and group \(j\), with that group’s congestion parameter as shared public data. Its mass \(\mu_{x,j}\) is the fraction of residents of that type. The facility still lies on the paper’s original interval \([0,1]\); the population is what becomes a distribution.

The necessary co-modification is to keep congestion intensity stable as groups grow. Set \(\beta_j=\alpha_j(N_j-1)\), where \(N_j\) is group \(j\)’s size, and take \(\beta_j\in[0,1]\). Thus \(\alpha_j=\beta_j/(N_j-1)\). This respects the paper’s Consistency of Aversion bound while preserving a fixed per-person congestion effect in a large population. For group mass \(p_j=\sum_{x}\mu_{x,j}\), let \(\nu_j\) be its conditional location distribution. A member at \(x\) has limiting cost \( |y-x|+\beta_j\int(1-|y-z|)\,d\nu_j(z)\). The resulting per-capita social cost is
\[ F_\mu(y)=\sum_j p_j\left(\beta_j+(1-\beta_j)\int |y-x|\,d\nu_j(x)\right). \]
For finite populations with \(\alpha_j=\beta_j/(N_j-1)\), this is exactly the original total social cost divided by \(N\), not just an analogy.

Lead mirror question — Public-Group Continuous Social-Cost Location (PG-CSL). The instance is a finite list of positive-mass atoms \((x_t,j_t,\mu_t)\), with rational locations and masses summing to one, public rational \(\beta_j\in[0,1]\), and a population size \(N\) such that each mass is an integer multiple of \(1/N\). Group memberships are public; an individual’s location report is private, and an individual deviation moves mass \(1/N\) from their true location to another reported location in the same group. The task is to give a deterministic rule that, on every such reported distribution, outputs \(y\in[0,1]\) minimizing \(F_\mu(y)\), and is truthful for those single-person location deviations under the paper’s original individual costs. A solution is such a rule and its exact output on the given instance. Res-M supplies it: sort the support locations and take a lower weighted median with atom weights \(\mu_t(1-\beta_{j_t})\); if all weights vanish, choose \(0\). This takes polynomial time in \(\tau\), the rational bit length \(L\), and \(\log N\), rather than requiring the planner to process all \(N\) residents individually.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: its social-cost objective is a weighted one-dimensional distance sum, so the exact optimum is a weighted median. The authors should recognize the question: it preserves their facility, congestion-adjusted social objective, public-group/private-location regime, and individual truthfulness, while expressing the electorate as type masses. The mirror covers Theorem 3.5’s regime and objective; it does not try to continuize the paper’s private-group or maximum-cost results.

The weakest point is that a strictly atomless population gives any one person zero influence on the facility, making individual strategyproofness vacuous. PG-CSL retains a \(1/N\) deviation quantum to preserve the theorem’s incentive meaning while compressing the society to a distribution. That makes it a high-multiplicity population mirror, not a purely atomless mechanism-design model. The continuous optimization itself is straightforward; the case is strongest as evidence that this paper has a sensible, faithful population mirror, not as a source of a difficult new complexity boundary.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 3.5 is about strategyproofness and optimality; it gives no complexity classification in terms of input size, hardness, or parameters. PG-CSL therefore adds a computational question to the paper rather than mirroring one the paper itself asks. That is a real scope concern for ChoCo, whose focus is the complexity landscape.

It does not defeat the mirror. The proposed population model preserves the theorem’s objective exactly under \(\alpha_j=\beta_j/(N_j-1)\), and its weighted-median rule preserves individual truthfulness when a report moves mass \(1/N\). That makes the mirror a legitimate high-multiplicity version of the problem. The location–group types can plausibly represent residents in the same zone with the same public group membership. The fact that an atomless limit would make unilateral influence vanish is also repaired by keeping the finite report quantum. Under the programme’s own criteria, those are strengths, not objections.

I also cannot rule out a better mirror by pointing to the paper’s maximum-cost results. A distributional version can define maximum cost as the essential supremum of cost over supported types, retain finite-\(N\) report deviations for incentives, and ask for strategyproof approximation guarantees. That is a recognizable extension of results such as Theorem 4.6. Any claim that such mirrors fail because they are easy or yield familiar answers would rely on grounds the prompt rules out.

So the negative case is weak. There is no named computational-complexity result in this paper, which limits the claim that it supplies a complexity question for continuization to mirror. But Theorem 3.5 still supports a faithful, well-posed population mirror, and I cannot honestly argue that no scenario makes it worthwhile.
