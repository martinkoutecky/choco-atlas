What the mirror covers

The proposed population allocation extends the \(CIS^\alpha\) formula to type masses; it does not mirror a computational result. The paper's axiomatic characterizations and derived properties have no qualifying computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. The numbered results are axiomatic characterizations: Theorem 3.1 characterizes \(CIS^\alpha\) by \(\alpha\)-individual rationality and equal surplus increment; Theorem 3.2 characterizes it by efficiency, additivity, \(\alpha\)-symmetry, and the \(\alpha\)-dummifying player property; Proposition 3.3 derives \(\alpha\)-symmetry from efficiency and \(\alpha\)-SSOP; Theorem 3.4 characterizes it by efficiency and \(\alpha\)-SSOP; and Proposition 3.5 derives the \(\alpha\)-dummifying property for games with at least three players. None asserts an algorithmic complexity result. The explicit formula makes the value easy to evaluate, but the paper does not present that as a named computational result.

The closest continuous object I can defend is a population version of the allocation rule. Let \(T\) be a finite set of player types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) has weight \(\alpha_t\) and standalone worth \(r_t\); let \(g\) be the grand coalition’s worth per capita. Ask for a per-capita payoff \(y_t\) for each type such that

\[ y_t=\alpha_t r_t+g-\sum_{s\in T}\mu_s\alpha_s r_s. \]

The solution is a type-level payoff vector, and \(\sum_t\mu_t y_t=g\). This matches the finite formula when there are many players with shared weights and standalone worths, and few types. A plausible regime is a large consortium of interchangeable units with a small number of institutional and productivity profiles. Computing the vector takes \(O(|T|)\) rational arithmetic operations, so this proposed question is tractable.

But that is a proposed computation of the paper’s value, not a continuous version of any named computational result. The authors might recognize the population allocation as a natural extension of \(CIS^\alpha\); the paper’s main contribution, however, is characterizing the value through axioms over finite-player games. Extending those axioms to a continuous population would not supply the missing computational anchor. That is the strongest honest positive foothold I see, and its weakness is decisive for this programme’s stated test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central obstacle is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorems 3.1, 3.2, and 3.4 characterize \(CIS^\alpha\) by axioms; Propositions 3.3 and 3.5 derive further axiomatic properties. None poses an algorithmic problem or establishes a complexity bound. The Harsanyi expansion in Theorem 3.2’s proof may have exponentially many terms, but the value itself has a direct formula: it needs only the grand-coalition worth and the singleton worths. Evaluating it takes \(O(|N|)\) arithmetic operations, or \(O(\tau)\) for the proposed type model.

The proponent’s large-consortium example is a sensible population interpretation. With type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita worths \(r_t\), and grand-coalition worth per capita \(g\), the allocation
\[ y_t=\alpha_t r_t+g-\sum_s\mu_s\alpha_s r_s \]
is a natural mass version of the finite formula. But computing it is just a weighted sum over types. It does not mirror a named result of the paper, and it supplies no complexity question for the programme to classify.

A more ambitious mirror could extend the characterizations themselves to continuum games. That would still be axiomatic work, which this programme excludes. It would also require choices the paper does not make: a representation of coalition worths over subsets of a continuum, and a type-level meaning for individual worth when nonatomic agents have measure-zero singleton coalitions. Those choices might support worthwhile research, but any resulting computational difficulty would depend on the new representation or on a new optimization task, not on the paper’s results.

So the negative case is strong for the programme’s stated test: the population reinterpretation is coherent but computationally immediate, while the paper’s actual contribution is axiomatic. The universal claim that no adjacent continuous computational question could ever be worthwhile is harder to establish; one could invent such a question. But it would be a new problem attached to this value, not a computational mirror of this paper.
