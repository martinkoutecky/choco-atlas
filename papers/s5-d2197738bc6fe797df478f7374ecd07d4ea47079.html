The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) over \(\tau\) jurisdiction types, per-unit entitlements \(p_t\), bounds, and per-capita supply \(\bar h\), let \(\mathcal A\) be the explicitly listed feasible vectors \(q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{\tau}\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t q_t=\bar h\) and, whenever \(p_t\le p_u\), \(q_t\le q_u\) and \(q_t/p_t\ge q_u/p_u\). Compute the unions of lexicographic maxima and minima of \(\mathcal A\) over all permutations of the type coordinates, together with the remaining neutral vectors and witnesses for the extrema.

The model it lives in

A finite-support society \(\mu\) over complete jurisdiction types; each type receives an integral per-unit seat count \(q_t\), while the total per-capita supply is mass-weighted. The feasible list \(\mathcal A\) is explicit, and the task is to classify its vectors by lexicographic extrema over type-coordinate permutations.

The objection that survived

The opponent's explicit-list concern remains: Proposition 2 does not bound constructing \(\mathcal A\) from \(\mu\); additionally, Appendix A's upper-bound calculation omits the potentially quadratic \(\mathrm{ISIN}\) scan in Algorithm 7, so the stated operation-count bound for the full procedure needs correction.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers computing the favoring, disfavoring, and neutral sets from an explicit feasible list \(\mathcal A\); it leaves generating that list and the paper's other findings outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can feasible vectors be generated or represented implicitly from \(\mu\) and the allocation constraints without enumerating \(\mathcal A\)?
  • Can the dependence on the number of types \(\tau\) be improved?
  • How broadly do realistic apportionment settings support many units sharing complete entitlement and bound profiles?

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I believe is neutral allocation over a mass society. The paper’s EU example has only 27 heterogeneous states, so I would not present that instance itself as high multiplicity. A better regime is a large federation assigning seats to many standardized local units. Each unit has an entitlement weight and statutory bounds; many units share the same complete profile, while the number of distinct profiles \(\tau\) stays small as the number of units \(N\) grows.

The named anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper’s Appendix A. It bounds the worst-case number \(E_{\mathrm{NEUTRAL}}\) of elementary operations used by the authors’ algorithm to compute neutral allocations from an explicitly supplied set \(X\) of \(m\) vectors in \(n\) coordinates. With \(P_p(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{p-1}(n-i)\) and \(Q_p(n)=\prod_{j=1}^{p}(n-j)\), its stated bounds are

\(\begin{aligned} 3+2\sum_{p=1}^{n-2}\bigl(m+n+P_p(n)\bigr)+4n(n-1)! \;\le\;&E_{\mathrm{NEUTRAL}}\\ \le\;&3+4\sum_{p=1}^{n-1}Q_p(n)\\ &+2\sum_{p=1}^{n-2}\left[2mn+ \left(\frac{np^2}{2}+\frac{np}{2}-\frac{p^3}{6}+\frac{p}{6}\right)P_p(n)\right]\\ &+2(n-1)!\left(\frac{n^3}{3}+\frac{5n}{3}\right). \end{aligned}\)

This is an operation-count result, not a theorem classifying the problem as polynomial-time or hard. In particular, its upper bound is polynomial in \(m\) for fixed \(n\), but has factorial dependence on \(n\). The paper also says its method applies to arbitrary subsets of \(\mathbb R^n\), which makes the proposed type compression a natural extension of the authors’ stated scope.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of \(\tau\) jurisdiction types, each with rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A type records everything relevant to feasibility, including entitlement \(p_t>0\) and any bounds. The per-unit supply is \(\bar h\). An allocation assigns each unit of type \(t\) an integer number \(q_t\) of seats, with \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t=\bar h\). For \(p_t\le p_u\), require \(q_t\le q_u\) and \(q_t/p_t\ge q_u/p_u\). The instance supplies the full finite list \(\mathcal A\) of feasible vectors \(q\).

The task is to partition \(\mathcal A\) into its favoring, disfavoring, and neutral allocations. For each permutation of the type coordinates, take the lexicographic maximum and minimum of \(\mathcal A\); favoring and disfavoring are the unions of those extrema, and neutral is what remains. A solution returns those three sets and the permutation or truncated-coalition witnesses for each extreme. There is no optimization objective in this paper’s task: the decision variable is the per-type seat count, and the output identifies which feasible allocations survive the authors’ coalition-based filtering.

The compression has a direct justification. If two units have identical entitlement \(p\), degressive proportionality forces them to receive the same number of seats: the monotonicity and ratio conditions together imply equality. Repeated coordinates therefore do not change any lexicographic comparison, and permutations of \(N\) units reduce to permutations of the \(\tau\) types. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of units of that type; total seats scale with \(N\), while \(\bar h\) can be fractional even though each unit receives an integer bundle. This is a continuous population mirror with an integral per-unit decision.

For this explicitly listed-set problem, I expect the tractable, Class A outcome in the bounded-support regime. Substituting \(n=\tau\) in Proposition 2 gives an \(f(\tau)\operatorname{poly}(m)\) operation bound, hence fixed-parameter tractability in support for this input format. It does not show polynomial time when \(\tau\) varies. Nor does it settle the stronger question where the feasible allocations are given only by their constraints rather than explicitly listed. That stronger problem, and whether the factorial dependence on \(\tau\) can be improved, are natural follow-ups.

The mirror covers the paper’s neutral-set computation and its operation-count analysis. It does not claim a continuous version of every result in the article or of the separate task of generating all feasible allocations. The weakest point is that this proposition starts with \(X\) already listed, and a convincing high-multiplicity scenario needs many genuinely identical entitlement profiles. The EU case has neither feature. The proposed federation regime is a defensible instance regime, but its relevance depends on authors accepting standardized local units as the agents in their allocation problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the only computational anchor, and it is a real one: dismissing it as axiomatic would be wrong. But it bounds the work of partitioning an explicitly supplied set \(X\) of \(m\) vectors in \(n\) coordinates. In the proposed mirror, if the full feasible list \(\mathcal A\) is also supplied, the algorithm sees only that list. The masses \(\mu_t\) matter only upstream, in deciding which vectors belong to \(\mathcal A\). Since the paper says its procedure applies to any such set, this version is essentially the same set-processing problem with \(n=\tau\); it does not give a computational result about a society presented as a distribution.

The stronger repair is to give the prover \(\mu\) and the feasibility constraints, then ask for the neutral allocations without first listing them. That is a genuine new problem, but Proposition 2 does not answer it: the prover must also deal with generating or representing a potentially enormous feasible set. If the task is to list every neutral allocation, the output itself may be enormous. That variant could be worthwhile, but it is no longer a mirror of the paper’s stated operation-count result.

The federation scenario is plausible, and high multiplicity supports it. There is, however, a structural limitation. For equal entitlements \(p\), degressive proportionality requires \(s_i\le s_j\) and \(s_i/p\ge s_j/p\), hence \(s_i=s_j\). Repeated units therefore collapse to one allocation coordinate per distinct entitlement profile. Rational masses can be represented as replication counts over a common denominator; the per-type seat decisions remain integral, with the masses weighting the total-seat constraint. This does not make the model invalid, but it means the direct mirror’s population compression is just a change from unit coordinates to type coordinates. A richer implicit-input question might escape that limitation, but would need its own computational result.

There is also a reason to audit the anchor’s quantitative claim: Proposition 2’s lower-bound proof charges the full recursion tree, while the stated algorithms exit immediately when \(X\) is a singleton. That makes the advertised two-sided operation count unreliable as written, though it does not by itself invalidate the upper bound.

So the best negative case is against the direct mirror: with \(\mathcal A\) explicit, it repackages the same generic algorithm; with \(\mathcal A\) implicit, it asks a different question. But I cannot honestly sustain the universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists. The federation model is defensible, and its implicit-input version remains open to a useful computational study. The negative case is weak against that improved mirror.
