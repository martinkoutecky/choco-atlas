The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) ordered outfall types with rational masses \(\mu_i>0\), \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), rational per-capita bounds and pollution tolerances, attenuation rates, initial pollution, and rational concave quadratic per-firm profits \(\phi_i\), compute an exact optimal per-firm discharge vector \(q\) maximizing normalized total profit subject to the cumulative pollution constraints.

The model it lives in

Types are ordered outfall classes with mass \(\mu_i\); each class has per-firm discharge \(q_i\), bounds \(a_i\le q_i\le u_i\), and profit \(\phi_i(q_i)=A_iq_i-D_iq_i^2\), with \(D_i>0\). Maximize \(\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\phi_i(q_i)\) subject to \(\sum_{j=1}^{i}K_j^i\mu_jq_j+K_1^i b_0\le b_i\) for each \(i\), where \(K_j^i=\prod_{\ell=j}^{i-1}k_\ell\) and \(K_i^i=1\); \(b_0\) and \(b_i\) are per-capita pollution quantities.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I see is for the paper’s grand-coalition discharge optimization, not its core or directional-convexity results. The population becomes continuous: it is a mass distribution over firm types. Discharge is still a continuous control, so its continuity is part of the model rather than the continuization itself.

My anchor is Lemma 3, proved in the paper. It says that for the nonlinear concave-profit case where the derivatives needed by the LLI algorithm are analytically computable, LLI takes \(O(n)\) iterations and at most \(O(n^3)\) time. Its companion, Proposition 2, also proved here, establishes that LLI’s output is an optimal solution to the grand-coalition SDP. Proposition 2 identifies what Lemma 3’s running time is for; Lemma 3 is the complexity anchor.

I would mirror them with Mass-Limit Grand-Coalition Sewage Optimization. Consider \(\tau\) ordered outfall classes. A type includes a firm’s outfall position, profit function, discharge bounds, and other characteristics used by the model. Class \(i\) has population mass \(\mu_i>0\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). A realistic regime is a large river corridor with many standardized plants at a modest number of shared outfall sites: the firm count \(n\) is large, while \(\tau\) is fixed or moderate. Expressing discharge, profit, and pollution limits per unit of population is the needed scaling as the population grows.

For a concrete, finite input, let each type’s per-firm profit be the rational concave quadratic \(\phi_i(q)=A_iq-D_iq^2\), with \(D_i>0\) and parameters chosen so profit is nondecreasing on \([a_i,u_i]\). Let \(q_i\) be the per-firm discharge assigned to the mass of type \(i\), and let \(K_j^i=\prod_{\ell=j}^{i-1}k_\ell\), with \(K_i^i=1\). Given rational masses, bounds, attenuation rates, initial pollution \(b_0\), and per-population pollution tolerances \(b_i\), the question is to return an exact optimal discharge vector for

\( \max_{q}\ \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\phi_i(q_i) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{j=1}^{i}K_j^i\mu_jq_j+K_1^i b_0\le b_i\ \ (i=1,\ldots,\tau),\quad a_i\le q_i\le u_i. \)

Equivalently, given a rational threshold \(W\), the decision version asks whether the maximum normalized profit is at least \(W\); a solution is an optimal \(q\), or a feasible \(q\) meeting the threshold. The type masses are the population object, and \(\mu_iq_i\) is the aggregate discharge contributed by each type.

The aggregation preserves the authors’ question. For firms of a type, pollution depends on their total discharge, while identical concave profit functions make equal per-firm discharge at least as good as any unequal assignment with the same total: by Jensen’s inequality, averaging preserves the pollution contribution and does not reduce profit. Thus type-level choices do not discard a relevant distinction. The objective and cumulative pollution constraints are the paper’s own, with totals normalized by population. The mirror covers the global optimization result only; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s coalition-value or core results.

I expect this problem to be tractable (Class A). In this explicit encoding it is a rational concave quadratic optimization problem with \(\tau\) variables and ordered cumulative constraints. The paper’s LLI structure is a natural specialized route, and Lemma 3 suggests an \(O(\tau^3)\) arithmetic-operation target. A prover should still establish the precise bit-complexity bound for the chosen encoding; the paper’s stated runtime is not itself a bit-complexity analysis. A natural follow-on is to extend the result from these quadratic functions to a precisely encoded broader family of concave profit curves.

The weak point is that the paper already claims polynomial time in the number of finite firms. This mirror does not show discrete hardness dissolving; its value is an aggregate formulation whose natural size is the number of types, \(\tau\), rather than the population \(n\). The other limitation is regime-specific: if firms’ exact locations, permits, or profit curves differ so much that nearly every firm is a distinct type, then \(\tau\) grows with \(n\) and the high-multiplicity advantage disappears. Shared outfalls and standardized plants make the proposed regime plausible, and the paper’s objective and constraints remain recognizable there.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 and Lemma 3 are real computational anchors: the paper claims an optimal algorithm for grand-coalition discharge and gives its running time. So I cannot make the decisive case that there is no result to mirror.

The proponent’s best version is coherent. Suppose many identical plants discharge at each of a modest number of outfall sites, with a type specifying site, profit curve, bounds, and other relevant data. Concavity lets the planner assign equal discharge within a type. Writing \(y_i=\mu_iq_i\), the aggregate discharge of type \(i\), gives the same ordered optimization over \(\tau\) sites:
\[ \max_y \sum_i \mu_i f_i(y_i/\mu_i) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{j\le i}K_j^i y_j\le b_i,\qquad \mu_i a_i\le y_i\le \mu_i u_i. \]
The objective remains concave. This does preserve the paper’s grand-coalition question; the fact that the paper’s decision variables are already continuous does not by itself defeat the mirror.

The negative case is that this formulation gains a small input representation only by restricting attention to repeated firms at shared sites. If firms retain distinct positions, costs, bounds, or pollution limits, those details belong in their types and the support can grow with the population. If firms share sites, aggregation contracts them into site-level nodes. That can be a sensible application, but it makes the result a weighted version of the paper’s existing optimization on fewer nodes. In the aggregate variables above, the paper’s LLI method can be applied to the \(\tau\) site functions directly. The integer number of firms was not creating a combinatorial choice: each firm already chose a continuous discharge, and the paper already claims a polynomial-time algorithm. The gain is compression of repeated data, not a new complexity landscape.

There is also a modeling price. The paper’s \(b_i\) are pollution tolerances and its \(a_i,u_i\) are discharge quantities. Treating them as per-capita quantities as the population grows assumes a corresponding scaling of river capacity, or of plant discharges and production. That may be appropriate for a growing watershed or a standardized-permit regime, but it is an additional substantive assumption. A spatial continuum of distinct positions could be a more ambitious mirror, but it would require specifying how the location-indexed pollution constraints and input data are represented; it is no longer the finite-support site model.

These points make the mirror less compelling as a priority: in its strongest tractable version, it appears to contract an already tractable optimization problem. But they do not establish that no worthwhile scenario exists. Shared outfall sites and normalized watershed capacity could be recognizable to the authors, and the resulting \(\tau\)-based bound is a valid high-multiplicity question. The honest negative case is therefore weak at its key burden: this anchor survives.
