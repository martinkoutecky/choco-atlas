The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that Theorems 1–3 are axiomatic, and that the paper does not prove a numbered complexity result. But the conclusion explicitly identifies STV’s NP-hardness of manipulation as a computational fact and a reason the rule matters. Theorem 1 then singles out STV as the unique clone-independent runoff scoring rule. Together, those passages point to a computational question about a rule this paper puts at its center.

A natural continuous question is: given a rational distribution over ranking types, what is the minimum mass and cost of preference changes needed to make a target candidate a possible STV winner under the paper’s parallel-universes tie-breaking? This keeps the population as the continuous object and turns the single-manipulator question into a mass-coalition question. That is sensible for large, anonymous electorates: a type can include the ranking and the cost of changing it, and voters sharing a type are interchangeable.

The question has real computational structure. For a fixed elimination order, STV’s round-by-round score conditions are linear in the resulting type masses; optimizing over mass transfers across possible orders creates an algorithmic problem involving many candidate sequences and potentially many output types. Its answer could be easy, hard, or parameterized by the number of alternatives and supported types. Any of those answers would be useful; none needs to preserve the discrete hardness result.

The weakness is that this is an indirect anchor. The continuous problem is a computational follow-on to Theorem 1’s identification of STV and to the conclusion’s manipulation claim, rather than a mirror of a complexity theorem proved in the paper. If the grader requires a numbered computational theorem as the only eligible anchor, the proponent’s case wins on that formal criterion. But the paper’s lack of such a theorem does not make every worthwhile continuous computational question empty.
