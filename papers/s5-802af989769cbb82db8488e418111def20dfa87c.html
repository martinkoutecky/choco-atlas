The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no qualifying computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its numbered items are models 1–16, and its tables and series report experimental outcomes. It does not state a named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifying a computational problem as tractable or hard. The closest formal result is McKelvey’s theorem, discussed in Section 5: when no equilibrium exists, majority-rule sequences can connect any two policies. That is an imported result about reachability, not a numbered computational-complexity result of this paper.

There is a plausible *unanchored* mirror in the paper’s central object: given rational masses over a finite set of complete preference types, find a policy \(x\) such that no policy \(y\) is strictly preferred to \(x\) by more than half the population, or report that none exists. A possible regime is a large policy-making body whose members share a small number of preference profiles. But the paper studies five-person committees, and its authors caution against quick extrapolation to larger political settings. So this candidate does not supply the required named-result anchor, and I can’t honestly assign it a complexity class as a mirror of a result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror starts with the paper’s actual contribution: it reports which models best predict outcomes in five-person laboratory committees. Its numbered items are candidate models, not computational results. The finding that outcomes often lie near a majority-rule equilibrium is an experimental comparison; McKelvey’s reachability theorem is imported background. Neither gives this paper a computational problem whose complexity a continuous mirror could chart.

The best candidate is the mass version of the equilibrium question: given a distribution over complete utility types, find a policy that no strict majority prefers to another policy, or determine that none exists. That is a coherent computational question. But the paper studies committees that propose and amend policies through a deliberative process. A mass distribution over utility types does not represent who gets to propose, how the agenda develops, or how particular members steer discussion. In an atomless population, an individual’s vote and procedural actions have zero mass; giving a few participants positive mass instead models coordinated blocs. A static electorate version could be useful, but it would be a new spatial-voting problem rather than a computational mirror of the paper’s reported result.

So the case against is strongest as a judgment about fit: this paper offers no named computational result to mirror, and its empirical finding depends on small-group deliberation that the population limit would shed. The case is weaker as a claim that no related continuous problem is worth studying; the mass equilibrium question could be worthwhile on its own.
