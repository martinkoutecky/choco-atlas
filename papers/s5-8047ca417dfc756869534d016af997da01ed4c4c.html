The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest answer is qualified: this paper has a natural continuous-population mirror, but it has no qualifying computational anchor. The numbered results—Theorems 1–10 and Corollaries 1–2—concern equilibrium existence, purity, continuity, delay, and core selection. None proves membership in \(\mathrm{P}\), NP-hardness, fixed-parameter tractability, an approximation guarantee, or an algorithm with a running-time bound. Claims about computational facts in cited literature are not results of this paper. Thus, strictly under ChoCo’s anchor rule, this is not a compliant positive case.

That does not support the opposing claim that no continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The best lead is Theorem 7, proved in this paper, although it is a structural rather than computational anchor. It states that, if every legislator has positive recognition probability and either the policy space is one-dimensional with a proper voting rule or the voting rule is oligarchic, then every no-delay stationary equilibrium satisfies
\[ A=\{q\}\Longleftrightarrow q\in K\Longrightarrow q\in A, \]
where \(A\) is the social acceptance set and \(K\) is the core. If \(\delta>0\), the final implication reverses as well: \(q\in A\Rightarrow q\in K\). Hence, with positive discounting, these conditions make \(A=\{q\}\), \(q\in K\), and \(q\in A\) equivalent.

A concrete mirror is a large legislative assembly whose seats represent repeated district types. A type \(t\) consists of a complete utility description, for example a quadratic ideal point \(a_t\), together with its recognition weight. The society is a rational mass vector \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}^{\tau}\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of seats of type \(t\), with \(\tau\ll n\). The policy space remains the paper’s \(X=[0,1]\); only the population is continuized. A proposal is accepted by strict majority mass. A proposer is drawn from type \(t\) with probability \(\lambda_t\), equal to \(\mu_t\) under equal recognition.

The resulting problem, which I would call Continuous Majority Bargaining Equilibrium, is:

Given \(T,\mu,q,\delta\), the quadratic utilities \(u_t(x)=-(x-a_t)^2\), and recognition probabilities \(\lambda_t\), output a pure no-delay stationary equilibrium \(x_t\) for every type \(t\), together with its social acceptance set. It must satisfy
\[ v_t=\sum_{s\in T}\lambda_s u_t(x_s), \]
\[ A_t=\{x\in X:u_t(x)\ge (1-\delta)u_t(q)+\delta v_t\}, \]
\[ A=\left\{x\in X:\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\mathbf{1}[x\in A_t]>\frac12\right\}, \]
and, for every type \(t\),
\[ x_t\in\arg\max_{x\in A}u_t(x), \qquad u_t(x_t)\ge (1-\delta)u_t(q)+\delta v_t. \]
The computational output should also decide whether \(q\in A\). Under the hypotheses corresponding to Theorem 7 and \(\delta>0\), this is equivalent to deciding whether \(q\in K\), and \(q\in K\) implies that \(q\) is the sole socially acceptable policy.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large chamber, council, or federation may contain many seats with identical district preferences and institutional roles. For rational \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), expanding each type into \(n_t\) identical legislators recovers the finite model exactly at the type-symmetric level. The authors would recognize the same status quo, utilities, discounting, proposals, voting, and stationary equilibrium; majority by population mass is the forced analogue of majority by seats.

I would expect the one-dimensional quadratic version to be Class A. The core is determined by weighted median information, Theorem 2 gives pure no-delay equilibria in one dimension, and each type’s acceptance set is an interval. This suggests a polynomial algorithm in \(\tau\) and the input bit length, though that algorithm is not supplied by the paper and must not be attributed to it. Theorem 3 and Theorem 10 would generate further questions about robustness as \(\mu\), \(q\), or \(\delta\) vary, but they are not computational results either.

The weakest point is fundamental: the paper studies a finite strategic game, while the continuum makes individual legislators non-pivotal and requires a mass-based voting rule. Moreover, the paper gives no complexity result for the proposed task, and the one-dimensional case may simply be a weighted-median reformulation rather than a striking dissolution of discrete hardness. So this is a genuine and defensible mirror against the claim of impossibility, but not a strong ChoCo paper under the programme’s formal anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is formal and strong: this paper contains no qualifying computational result to mirror. Theorem 7 is a structural equilibrium theorem, not a theorem about \(\mathrm{P}\), NP-hardness, approximation, parameterized complexity, or an algorithm. Theorems 3 and 10 are likewise topological and comparative-static results. The proponent concedes this, and under ChoCo’s stated anchor rule that concession is dispositive.

The proposed “Continuous Majority Bargaining Equilibrium” is therefore a newly invented computational problem, not a computational question raised by the paper. Its equations merely specialize the paper’s stationary-equilibrium definition to one dimension, quadratic utilities, majority rule, deterministic type-symmetric proposals, and no delay. Nothing in the paper asks one to compute such an equilibrium or gives an input encoding, output convention, or complexity claim for doing so. A later paper could certainly formulate that problem, but it would be a new contribution inspired by Banks and Duggan, not a continuous mirror of one of their computational results.

The high-multiplicity story is also less clean than the proponent suggests. In the original game, every individual legislator is simultaneously a possible proposer, a voter, and a member of identity-specific decisive coalitions. If one literally takes a population limit, each individual’s recognition probability tends to zero, while the paper’s hypotheses require positive recognition probability for every legislator. If instead one assigns recognition probability \(\lambda_t\) to a type, then the model has introduced a representative type-level proposer and type-level voting. That is a coherent new mean-field bargaining game, but it is not obtained automatically from the paper: one must define how type members coordinate proposals, how mass coalitions replace \(\mathcal V\), how ties are handled, and what counts as an equilibrium strategy. Those choices are precisely the substantive modelling work that the proposed mirror treats as already settled.

Theorem 7 does not rescue the case. Its conclusion concerns the relation among the finite game’s social acceptance set \(A\), status quo \(q\), and core \(K\). Replacing individual counts by masses can produce a sensible weighted-median analogue, especially for a large chamber with repeated district types. I would not deny that such a model is admissible. But admissibility is not a computational anchor. The weighted-mass formulation is an extrapolation of the theorem, and the requested computational task—outputting an equilibrium or deciding whether \(q\in A\)—is supplied by the proponent, not by the paper. The paper proves neither that task’s complexity nor even that its continuum counterpart has the same equilibrium correspondence.

Theorem 3 is no better. Upper hemicontinuity of the finite-population no-delay equilibrium correspondence is an analytic stability statement. A population version could ask how equilibria change with \(\mu\), but that would require a new theorem about a measure-valued parameter and a newly specified computational query. It is not a complexity result hidden inside the paper. Theorem 10 similarly studies the limit \(\delta\to1\), not a population limit. Replacing the finite profile by a mass distribution may yield a weighted core and a weighted median, but the resulting question remains an invented computational problem about a new model.

Nor can the missing anchor be repaired by pointing to Theorems 1–6, 8, or 9. They establish existence, purity under restrictions, delay properties, and core-selection facts. None formulates an optimization, decision, search, approximation, or parameterized-complexity problem. The presence of a continuous policy space and probability measures is irrelevant: the programme continuizes the population, whereas the paper’s continuity concerns policies, mixed proposals, and parameters.

The honest negative case therefore has an important limitation. I cannot plausibly claim that every continuous-population version of legislative bargaining is meaningless. A repeated-district legislature with mass voting is a defensible high-multiplicity regime, and a future paper might study the complexity of computing its equilibria. But that would be a new computational research programme built around this paper’s economic model. It would not be a qualifying mirror of any named computational result here. On ChoCo’s formal standard, the paper should therefore receive no continuous mirror.
