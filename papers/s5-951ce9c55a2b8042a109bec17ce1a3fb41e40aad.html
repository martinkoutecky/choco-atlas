The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a rubric-compliant case anchored in this paper’s computational results: it has no numbered result classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Theorem 1 and Propositions 2 and 4 are analytic claims about convergence, polarization, and shock elections. Proposition 3 is the closest optimization result: proved here under link independence, it characterizes optimal finite networks as symmetric, without self-links, and vertex-transitive. But it gives no computational classification. Theorem 3 and Lemma 1 in the appendix are cited spectral results, not complexity results.

The strongest positive foothold is Proposition 4, proved here in Appendix B.5: under the mean-field and wisdom assumptions, confirmation bias can produce a shock election in a large society, while a shock election never occurs without it. A continuous-mass extension is especially plausible here because the voting section already represents voters by five belief groups with shares \(f_i\), and studies majority outcomes in a mean-field society.

One precise follow-on question would take types \(R=\{EL,CL,S,CR,ER\}\), rational masses \(\mu_r\) summing to \(1\), initial beliefs \(b_r\), and a rational row-stochastic matrix \(K\), where \(K_{rs}\) is the attention a member of type \(r\) gives to type \(s\). Given confirmation-bias strength \(q\), form \(K^q\) by cutting each cross-type link with \(|b_r-b_s|>1-q\) and moving its weight to the self-link. Evolve type beliefs by \(x(t+1)=K^q x(t)\). Require that the Left has a majority initially, that \(K^q\) is primitive, and that its consensus belief is below \(1/2\). The decision problem is: does the Right win at any finite \(t\ge1\)? A yes-solution gives such a \(t\), the resulting \(x(t)\), and the vote shares \(\sum_{r:x_r(t)>1/2}\mu_r\), with the paper’s tie rule applied at \(1/2\).

A plausible regime is a very large campaign electorate concentrated in five stable ideology groups, with members of each group sharing an initial belief and type-level attention pattern. Then the number of voters can be large while the number of types stays at five, and time remains discrete as in the paper. The authors could plausibly recognize this as a population version of their shock-election question. The weakness is decisive for the requested grading, though: Proposition 4 proves a possibility result, not an algorithmic one. This proposed decision problem is a research lead inspired by the paper, not a mirror of a named computational result in it; the paper gives no basis for predicting its complexity class.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against starts with the paper’s result types: there is no named computational result here to continuize. Theorem 1 and Propositions 2 and 4 concern learning speed, polarization, and shock elections. Proposition 3 characterizes optimal networks, but gives no complexity classification or algorithmic problem. Propositions 5 and 6 concern media equilibria. None classifies a computational problem by its running time or solvability.

The shock-election proposal is the strongest possible lead, but its decision problem is new. Proposition 4 says that a shock election *can* occur under confirmation bias, and cannot occur without it under the stated assumptions. The proposed input-specific question—whether a given rational matrix \(K\) ever takes its belief vector across the voting threshold—asks for unbounded-time reachability. That could be an interesting computational problem, but the paper neither poses nor classifies it. The proponent’s own description is accurate: it is a research lead inspired by Proposition 4, not a mirror of a computational result.

A more faithful continuous-population formulation could use five complete voter types, type-level attention, and rational masses. But to keep those five types valid in a network model, members of each type must be interchangeable in the network features that affect learning. For general networks, type must also capture network position and attention patterns, so the support may grow with the number of agents. Restricting to five interchangeable groups instead imposes the block or mean-field structure that Proposition 4’s proof already uses through its group shares \(f_i\). That makes the population description cleaner, but it does not uncover a computational result the paper left unaddressed.

So the negative case is strong as a ChoCo screening judgment: this paper offers no worthwhile mirror of a computational result. Its weak point is the universal claim. The proposed reachability question may be worth studying in its own right; the paper’s lack of a computational result is decisive against calling it a mirror, but cannot prove that the new project has no research value.
