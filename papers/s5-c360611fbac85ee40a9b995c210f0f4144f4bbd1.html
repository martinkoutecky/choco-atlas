Open questions for a prover

  • For variable \(m\), what is the complexity of minimizing total variation subject to changing the unique winner?
  • Is the problem fixed-parameter tractable in support size \(\tau\), and can the possible final ballot types be handled by an efficient separation method?

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case starts with a natural population model. Let a voter’s type be their complete three-grade ballot over the \(m\) candidates, \(t\in\{0,1,2\}^m\), where \(2\) means strong approval, \(1\) weak approval, and \(0\) no explicit approval. In a large electorate with many repeated ballot patterns, \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters of type \(t\). This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for elections: the number of voters can greatly exceed the number \(\tau\) of distinct grade vectors. The paper’s rule already aggregates voters by approval counts, so replacing counts with masses preserves the procedure directly.

But the required computational anchor is absent. The only numbered result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: if the electorate is split into two parts that each elect \(A\), and \(A\)’s scores in the two parts have the same nonzero sign, \(S_A^{(1)}S_A^{(2)}>0\), or are both zero, then \(A\) wins the combined vote. That is a weak consistency theorem, not a complexity or algorithmic result; its continuous extension would be axiomatic, outside ChoCo’s scope. The paper has no numbered result establishing a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or parameterized. Its discussion of the absence of the No Show Paradox and strategy-proofness does not supply such an anchor either.

The most promising computational question I see is therefore an unanchored follow-up, not a mirror of a named result: Mass-Reallocation Winner Robustness for the simultaneous-approval rule. Given \(m\), a rational initial distribution \(\mu\) over ballot types, and its unique winner \(c\), find a distribution \(\nu\) that makes \(c\) cease to be the unique winner while minimizing the fraction of the electorate whose ballot type changes.

Precisely, set \(g_c(\mu)=\sum_{t:t_c=2}\mu_t\), \(a_c(\mu)=\sum_{t:t_c\ge1}\mu_t\), and \(n_c(\mu)=1-a_c(\mu)\). Apply the paper’s rule to these masses: if no \(a_c(\nu)>1/2\), there is no winner; otherwise candidates with \(g_c(\nu)>n_c(\nu)\) precede the rest, with the paper’s stated score and tie-break ordering. The objective is
\[ \min_{\nu:\,W(\nu)\ne\{c\}} \frac12\sum_{t\in\{0,1,2\}^m}|\mu_t-\nu_t|, \]
where \(W(\nu)\) is the set of top-ranked candidates, including ties. The solution is the minimizing profile \(\nu\) and the minimum mass moved. The total variation objective is exactly the least mass that must change ballot type to turn \(\mu\) into \(\nu\).

This question fits the paper’s discussion of how shifts in public approval can affect reelection, and an author could plausibly recognize it as a way to quantify that sensitivity. For fixed \(m\), I expect tractability: there are finitely many score and threshold regions, each expressible with linear inequalities. With \(m\) variable, my tentative bet is hardness driven by the candidate comparisons, so a Class B route is plausible; it would require proving that an appropriate discrete robustness problem embeds despite fractional mass changes. The further questions are the exact variable-\(m\) complexity, whether the problem is fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), and whether the exponentially many possible final ballot types can be handled by an efficient pricing or separation method.

The weak point is decisive for grading: the paper proposes an election procedure and argues for its properties; it does not study computational complexity. Winner determination from the masses is immediate, and the robustness problem adds an optimization question the authors did not pose. So there is a natural continuous population model and a plausible research lead, but no valid named computational anchor in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper to mirror. Its only numbered result, Theorem 1, is a conditional consistency claim about partitions of the electorate. Replacing voter counts with masses gives the same claim for a measurable partition; that is an axiomatic continuization, outside ChoCo’s scope. The paper states no complexity result, algorithmic question, or computational problem whose continuous version it analyzes.

The proposed Mass-Reallocation Winner Robustness problem is a plausible new question, but it is not a mirror of Theorem 1 or another result in the paper. The paper studies how voters grade a fixed slate and how the rule might shape candidates’ behavior between elections. It does not ask how much of the electorate must change ballots to displace a winner. The total-variation objective supplies a new intervention model; a cost based on grade changes, voter groups, or candidate-specific shifts would supply different ones. Even the strongest version—using complete grade vectors as types and a cost that reflects a voter’s change across all candidates—would still add the cost model and optimization target that the paper never specifies.

The paper’s closer link to winner robustness is its discussion of reelection incentives. But it does not formalize how a candidate’s policy choice changes the population’s grades: its symmetry argument is a heuristic, and it calls for empirical study. A computational model of those incentives would need to add the policy choices, voter response process, and payoff structure. That could be worthwhile research, but it would be a new dynamic model, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.

A distribution over ballot types is still a sensible high-multiplicity model for large elections. That establishes a plausible population regime, not a computational contribution by this paper. The negative case is strongest on that distinction: a natural type space and an interesting follow-up do not supply the missing computational anchor. It cannot rule out that a separately developed robustness problem would merit study; it can say that this paper offers no named computational result for ChoCo to continuize.
