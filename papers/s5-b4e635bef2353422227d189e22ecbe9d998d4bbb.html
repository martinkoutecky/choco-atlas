The continuous mirror question

Given rational finite support \(T\), masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational values \(v_t\), public budgets \(B_t\), and rational supply \(q\) per advertiser, let \(D\) be a common denominator of the masses. For each \(k\ge 1\), run the paper's ACA with \(N=kD\) advertisers, \(N\mu_t\) advertisers of type \(t\), each having value \(v_t\) and budget \(B_t\), and total supply \(Nq\). Determine whether \(X_t^{(N)}=N^{-1}\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t}x_i^{(N)}\) and \(P_t^{(N)}=N^{-1}\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t}\pi_i^{(N)}\) converge for every type as \(k\to\infty\); if they do, compute all limits within \(\varepsilon\) in time polynomial in \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\).

The model it lives in

A continuous advertiser population with finite type set \(T\), mass \(\mu_t\) of each type, per-advertiser value \(v_t\) and budget \(B_t\), and pageview supply \(q\) per advertiser. The ACA outcome variables are limiting per-type allocation and payment totals per advertiser; the computational objective is to determine their existence and approximate them.

The objection that survived

The normalized type totals may fail to converge, or their limits may not admit the claimed polynomial approximation; the paper's ~O(n) result establishes neither, and the proponent leaves both as open technical steps.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends Theorem D.2's terminal outcome computation to limiting per-type totals. It leaves the paper's incentive-compatibility, Pareto-optimality, supply-monotonicity, and general polymatroid results outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do the normalized type allocations and payments converge for every rational finite-type instance?
  • Can their limits be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), and does supply monotonicity hold for those limits?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a narrow, credible positive case for the computation of the Adaptive Clinching Auction’s outcome. I would not claim this paper supplies a hardness result: its named computational anchor is an algorithmic one.

A plausible regime is an ad exchange with many small advertisers using a finite menu of bid and budget settings. Let a type \(t\) be an advertiser’s value \(v_t\) per impression and public budget \(B_t\); let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s fraction of advertisers. Take the supported types to have distinct values, so each group of identical advertisers shares both value and budget. This gives \(n\) advertisers with \(n\) much larger than the number \(\tau\) of settings. The auction’s existing online supply remains pageviews arriving over time. If the advertiser population grows, scale total inventory with it, so \(q\) is the supply per advertiser. This describes a particular high-multiplicity market regime, not all ad auctions.

The lead anchor is Theorem D.2 (“Algorithmic Form”), proved in this paper. It says that, given \(n\) agents’ values \(v\), public budgets \(B\), and initial supply \(s\), the Adaptive Clinching Auction’s allocation and payment outcome can be computed in \(\tilde O(n)\) time. The auction itself builds on earlier work, but this paper proves the event-driven algorithmic result. Its divisible good is already continuous in the *outcome*; the mirror below continuizes the *advertiser population*.

Call the mirror Mass-ACA Outcome Approximation. An instance consists of a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, values \(v_t\), public budgets \(B_t\), a rational per-advertiser supply \(q\), and an accuracy \(\varepsilon>0\). Let \(D\) be a common denominator of the masses. For each \(N=kD\), make \(N\mu_t\) individual advertisers of type \(t\), each retaining their own budget \(B_t\), and run the paper’s ACA with total supply \(Nq\). Define
\[ X_t^{(N)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t}x_i^{(N)},\qquad P_t^{(N)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{type}(i)=t}\pi_i^{(N)}. \]
The problem is to establish that these per-population type totals have well-defined limits as \(k\to\infty\), and, on input \(\varepsilon\), return \(\widehat X_t,\widehat P_t\) within \(\varepsilon\) of those limits for every type. The target running time is polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\).

This keeps the ACA outcome problem intact in the finite approximants: an advertiser’s budget is still individual, even when many advertisers share a type. The continuous answer reports per-type mass totals, as the limit model calls for. It does not pool a type’s budgets into one coalition budget. The paper’s treatment of repeated values gives a way to define the finite ACA runs for identical advertisers; restricting distinct types to distinct values avoids introducing arbitrary tie priorities between different budget groups.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable, though that is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem D.2. Its event-driven description and the repeated, identical states within each cohort suggest that the \(N\)-agent evolution may admit a compact type-level description. The hard step is deriving and justifying that description, then proving approximation bounds in the binary-input model. The paper’s \(\tilde O(n)\) bound alone does not give a polynomial-in-\(\tau\) algorithm, especially when its real-valued formulas require finite-precision computation.

The scope is deliberately limited to ACA outcome computation for this advertiser regime. It does not mirror the paper’s full incentive-compatibility or Pareto-optimality claims, nor its comparison with capacity constraints or its general polymatroid model. Natural next questions are whether the same limit can be computed along the entire supply path, whether the paper’s supply-monotonicity property survives that limit, and whether allowing equal-valued types with different budgets introduces tie-breaking dependence.

The weakest point is that the paper never studies this population limit. In particular, convergence of the cohort totals—and useful numerical bounds for approximating them—remain to be proved. The setting is still plausible because the finite approximants preserve individual advertisers and their budgets, while the proposed continuous object only groups advertisers who share the model’s attributes. The fact that pageviews are already divisible does not make the population continuous, so it is not a prior-art collision for this mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious anchor is Theorem D.2. Its \(\tilde O(n)\) algorithm computes the ACA outcome from a list of individual values and budgets. A population mirror would instead return per-type totals in a limit. That may be a useful new problem, but it is not simply the theorem’s input and output with \(n\) replaced by masses; it needs a new limit theorem and numerical guarantees.

The strongest concern is that the proposed regime relies on many advertisers sharing exactly the same value and public budget. That is coherent under the paper’s assumptions, and a market with standardized settings is plausible. But the ACA’s event sequence depends on exact values and budget states. If advertisers’ settings vary, they create more types and the compression can disappear; coarsening them changes the auction being evaluated. This narrows the case for a useful high-multiplicity algorithm, though it does not rule out the finite-menu regime.

There is also a deeper tradeoff. In an atomless model, one advertiser’s report does not change the population distribution. If the auction is defined only on that distribution, individual incentive constraints become vacuous. Retaining the paper’s central truthful-mechanism claim would require tracking a distinguished advertiser against the aggregate population, beyond the proposed computation of type totals. The proponent avoids this by limiting the mirror to outcome computation, but that leaves the paper’s main strategic contribution outside the mirror.

These points weaken the case for this particular anchor; they do not defeat its best version. A finite menu of advertiser types is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and computing the limiting per-type ACA outcomes is a well-posed computational question the authors could recognize. Theorem 4.1’s supply monotonicity could also be studied for those aggregates, provided the limits exist. I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no scenario offers a worthwhile mirror. The negative case is real but weak: it shows that the proposed mirror sheds the incentive-compatibility content, not that its outcome-computation question lacks value.
