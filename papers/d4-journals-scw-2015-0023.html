What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a computational mirror. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 are axiomatic results; the optimization defining \(O\) is not computationally analyzed.

The case FOR (proponent)

I find no eligible computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, says that on linked geographies the optimal solution \(O\) is the only solution satisfying two-district determinacy, two-district uniformity, indifference, and consistency. Corollary 1 says no solution on linked geographies satisfies those four axioms and anonymity as well. Both are axiomatic results; neither asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithm.

The closest computational question suggested by the paper is to take voter measures \(\mu_A,\mu_B\), a number of districts \(t\), and an admissible geography \(\mathcal G\), then find a partition maximizing
\[ \left|\{g\in D:\mu_A(g)>\mu_B(g)\}\right|. \]
That is the optimization underlying their definition of the optimal solution for party \(A\), so the authors would recognize it. But the paper does not establish a named computational result about it. Its regular model also already represents voters using continuous measures. The missing input representation for \(\mathcal G\) and the absence of any complexity analysis make this a possible follow-up question, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper.

So the strongest honest positive case is limited: the paper defines an optimization problem over voter mass that could motivate computational study. Under the requested anchor rule, however, there are no anchors and no anchor-based prover questions to state.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes a solution by axioms, and Corollary 1 gives an incompatibility result among axioms. Neither classifies the complexity of computing a districting or supplies an algorithm. The definition of the optimal solution \(O\) gives a natural optimization objective, but a definition is not a computational result.

The strongest candidate mirror would ask for the complexity of finding an equal-mass partition into \(t\) admissible districts that maximizes the number won by party \(A\):
\[ \max_{D}\left|\{g\in D:\mu_A(g)>\mu_B(g)\}\right|. \]
That is a coherent problem, but it is a new computational question rather than a continuous mirror of a result in this paper. In fact, for its regular districting problems, the paper already allows voters to be represented by continuous measures over space. Turning that setup into an algorithmic study could be worthwhile, but it would add computational content the paper does not provide.

A high-multiplicity reformulation also faces a real modelling cost: a voter’s location matters because it determines which admissible districts can contain them. Grouping voters only by party preference discards that information; preserving it makes location part of the type, rather than giving the finite-type population model a natural compression. That is a reason the standard ChoCo mirror is less direct, not a proof that no suitable spatial formulation exists.

So the negative case is strong on the paper’s eligibility as a mirror: it has no computational anchor, and its regular model already has a continuous population. It is weak as a universal claim that no useful computational study could arise in any scenario. The optimization above is a plausible research direction; the honest objection is that it is not an anchored continuous mirror of this paper.
