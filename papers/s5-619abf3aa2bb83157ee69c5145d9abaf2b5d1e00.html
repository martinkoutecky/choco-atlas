What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorem 1's core-up-to-one guarantee and its entropy-based rule. It leaves the paper without any algorithmic, runtime, or complexity result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive seed is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: every outcome selected by the Max-Payment-Entropy Rule is budget-feasible and satisfies core-up-to-one. In the paper’s finite model, that means that for every nonempty voter group \(S\) and every project set \(T\) with \(\operatorname{cost}(T)\le b|S|/n\), some voter \(i\in S\) has \(u_i(T)<u_i(W)+1\).

That result suggests a credible population mirror. Consider a large city running participatory budgeting, with many residents but relatively few distinct additive utility vectors over the projects. A type is a complete utility vector \(u_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents with that vector. The outcome remains a set \(W\) of projects. Each type has a per-resident reserve \(r_t\) and payments \(p_t(c)\), with \(r_t+\sum_{c\in W}p_t(c)=1\), payments only for valued projects, and \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t(c)\le \operatorname{cost}(c)/b\). The latter is the paper’s payment cap \(q_c=n\operatorname{cost}(c)/b\), normalized by population size.

The resulting candidate problem, Max-Payment-Entropy PB\(_\infty\), is: given project costs, budget \(b\), and a finite rational distribution \(\mu\) over utility types, return a project set \(W\) and maximizing payments that maximize
\[ \sum_t\mu_t F_{a_t}(x_t)-\frac{\operatorname{cost}(W)}{b}, \]
breaking ties by maximum cost, with \(x_t\) and \(a_t\) defined as in the paper for each type’s payments and utilities. Its proposed guarantee is the continuum version of Theorem 1: for every measurable coalition \(S\) of positive mass and project set \(T\) with \(\operatorname{cost}(T)\le b\mu(S)\), a positive mass of residents in \(S\) has \(u_t(T)<u_t(W)+1\). This gives a concrete computational question about choosing projects for a continuous population, rather than merely making the paper’s finite society continuous.

The weakness is decisive under your anchor rule: Theorem 1 proves a proportionality guarantee, not that any problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise complexity-classified. The paper gives no named computational-complexity result, so Theorem 1 is not a qualifying anchor. Nor does it establish the complexity of the proposed optimization problem; maximizing over project sets still involves a combinatorial choice. I can’t honestly assign that problem to the programme’s tractable, hardness-transfer, or continuum-specific-hard class from this paper. Thus the mirror is plausible and concrete, but the paper supplies no eligible named computational result to anchor the requested positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only plausible anchor is Theorem 1. It proves a proportionality guarantee for the Max-Payment-Entropy Rule; it says nothing about the complexity of computing that rule, its optimal payments, or any other computational problem. The lemmas support that guarantee. None classifies a problem by tractability or hardness. On ChoCo’s stated standard, there is no named computational result here to continuize.

The proposed population version of Theorem 1 is coherent: type masses and per-capita payments give a natural way to state the guarantee for a continuum of residents. But that would extend an axiomatic result, not mirror a computational one. A stronger computational proposal—determine the Max-Payment-Entropy outcome from rational type masses and utilities—would be a new question about the optimization-defined rule. The paper neither poses nor analyzes that question, so it cannot supply an anchor for it.

That is a strong reason to reject this paper as a source of ChoCo’s computational mirrors. It does not establish the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous-population question could be built around this rule; the negative case is weaker on that universal claim than on the narrower conclusion that no qualifying anchor survives.
