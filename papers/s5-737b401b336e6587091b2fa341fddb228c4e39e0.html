The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is conditional. The paper contains no named NP-hardness, membership-in-\(\mathrm P\), FPT, approximation, or other formal computational result. Its claims that positive problems are “easy to compute” are informal or attributed to earlier literature. Thus, under the strict anchor rule, there is no fully compliant computational anchor.

The best arguable anchor is the unnumbered “Theorem” in Section 5, proved in Section 7.2. It states, in full, that competitive divisions exist for every problem. For positive problems, the common budget is \(+1\), and an allocation is competitive exactly when its utility profile maximizes \(\prod_{i\in N_+}U_i\) over the feasible profiles in \(\Gamma^*=\mathbb R_{++}^{N_+}\times\{0\}^{N_-}\); the competitive utility profile is unique, positive for attracted agents and zero for repulsed agents. For negative problems, the budget is \(-1\), and competitive profiles are precisely efficient, strictly negative critical points of \(\prod_i |U_i|\). For null problems, the budget is \(0\), and the competitive utility profile is exactly the zero profile. This is a structural optimization formulation proved in the paper, not a complexity theorem.

The corresponding continuous problem would be Positive Continuous Competitive Division with Equal Incomes. An instance consists of a finite item set \(A\), a finite set of complete preference types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational per-capita manna \(\omega\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^{A}\), and a normalized additive utility vector \(u^t\in\mathbb Q^A\) for every type. A type is complete: agents of type \(t\) have the same preference over every bundle, including the same goods, bads, and satiation points.

The decision variable is a bundle \(z^t\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{A}\) for each type, interpreted as the common bundle—or type-average bundle—assigned to each unit of mass of that type. Feasibility is

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t z^t=\omega. \]

Write \(U_t=u^t\cdot z^t\). On positive instances, the question is to output a feasible allocation and prices \(p\in\mathbb R^A\) such that, for every positive-mass type, \(z^t\) maximizes \(u^t\cdot y\) subject to \(p\cdot y\le 1\), and is parsimonious among its maximizing bundles. Equivalently, the allocation must solve

\[ \max \sum_{t\in T_+}\mu_t\log U_t \]

subject to feasibility, \(U_t>0\) for attracted types, and \(U_t=0\) for repulsed types. A tuple \((z,p)\) satisfying these conditions is a solution; the utility profile is unique, although allocations of utility-neutral items need not be.

This is a genuine population continuization. The regime could be a large organization assigning amenities, shifts, hazards, and other divisible resources to tens of thousands of employees, with perhaps \(20\)–\(100\) standardized role or preference types. The manna scales with the workforce, so \(\omega\) is naturally a per-capita supply. The type masses are the fractions of employees in each role, not fractional outcomes imposed on a finite committee.

The high-multiplicity dictionary is exact. If \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), the instance corresponds to \(n_t\) finite agents of type \(t\) and total manna \(n\omega\). The finite Nash product is

\[ \prod_{t\in T_+} U_t^{n_t}, \]

whose normalized logarithm is exactly the continuous objective above. Conversely, every finite homogeneous instance maps to a rational mass distribution. This is why the mirror should be recognizable to the authors as their competitive-division problem, rather than as an unrelated fractional allocation model.

I would expect this positive additive mirror to be Class A: the logarithmic formulation is a weighted Eisenberg–Gale-style convex program, with prices as dual certificates, and should be solvable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau=|T|\), and the input bit length under standard approximation conventions. The negative branch is substantially less promising: its weighted analogue maximizes or finds critical points of \(\prod_t |U_t|^{\mu_t}\), and the paper’s multiplicity results suggest that multiple competitive profiles may remain. That gives natural follow-up questions about selecting, counting, or continuously tracking negative competitive profiles.

The weakness is decisive: this is a plausible high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s main theorem, but not a mirror of a named complexity result. It may therefore be judged a modest Class A reformulation rather than a strong ChoCo case. Existing cake-division work is not a direct collision because it continuizes the commodity space, not the population, but the computational novelty would still have to come from proving the mass-compressed algorithmic landscape rather than merely observing that the theorem survives replication.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The formal negative case is decisive: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Its Section 5 “Theorem” is a structural characterization of competitive divisions, not a theorem about complexity, algorithms, approximation, parameterized tractability, or computation. “Easy to compute” in the introduction merely reports earlier literature. Proposition 1 counts competitive profiles, while Propositions 2–4 are axiomatic or continuity results. None supplies the required computational result.

The proposed positive mirror is nevertheless mathematically legitimate. For finite types it asks for

\[ \max \sum_{t:\mu_t>0}\mu_t\log U_t \]

subject to \(\sum_t\mu_tz^t=\omega\). But this is exactly the finite problem with identical agents grouped together. If \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), then

\[ n\sum_t\mu_t\log U_t = \sum_t n_t\log U_t = \log\!\left(\prod_t U_t^{n_t}\right). \]

Thus the proposed “continuous” objective is not a limiting identity or a new computational formulation: it is exact row aggregation of the paper’s already divisible-goods model. The masses merely become coefficients in a standard Eisenberg–Gale-type program. If one allows an arbitrary continuum of utility types instead, the model ceases to have a finite computational input unless an integration or demand oracle is supplied; that creates an encoding issue rather than a mirror of a result in this paper.

The strongest rescue would be to study the negative branch: weighted critical points of

\[ \prod_t |U_t|^{\mu_t}, \]

or the selection, counting, and tracking of competitive profiles under population perturbations. That could become a worthwhile high-multiplicity research problem. But it is a new computational programme, not a computational consequence of the paper’s theorem. Proposition 1 may motivate it, but does not establish a counting or complexity result to mirror.

I would not use weaker objections: standardized preference types are plausible, type-level prices are legitimate, and the fact that the answer may be tractable is not a defect. So the universal substantive claim—“no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario”—is too strong. The defensible verdict is narrower but strong under the stated gate: this paper offers a natural high-multiplicity market model, yet no compliant computational anchor, and the proponent’s mirror is an independent aggregation study rather than a continuization of a computational result proved here.
