The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), categories \(C\) with rational supply masses \(\rho_c\), and category-specific priority tiers, compute a rational assignment \(x_{tc}\). Each type records its eligibility and beneficiary status for each category, with \(B_c\subseteq E_c\). Require \(x_{tc}\ge 0\), \(\sum_c x_{tc}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_{tc}\le\rho_c\), and \(x_{tc}=0\) when \(t\notin E_c\). Let \(u_t=\mu_t-\sum_c x_{tc}\). For every category \(c\), require \(u_s=0\) whenever \(s\) has higher priority than \(t\) in \(c\) and \(x_{tc}>0\). Compute an assignment maximizing lexicographically \((\sum_{t,c}x_{tc},\sum_c\sum_{t\in B_c}x_{tc})\), with complexity measured in \(|T|\), \(|C|\), and the binary input length \(L\).

The model it lives in

A threshold-reserve market with a unit mass of agents divided among finitely many types. Types share eligibility, beneficiary status, and priority tiers; category supplies scale with population size. The variables \(x_{tc}\) are assigned mass, so each individual still receives either one unit or none. The objective is to maximize total assigned mass, then beneficiary-assigned mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6.1’s eligibility, priority, universal-domain, and max-in-max outcome properties. It does not establish the paper’s path independence, comparative statics, stability implication, or runtime bounds for the mass setting.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the priority-respecting lexicographic mass assignment be computed exactly in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|C|\), and \(L\)?
  • Can solutions for rational mass instances yield additive-error guarantees for discrete high-multiplicity instances?
  • Does IMMAM’s path independence survive when members of a priority tier can be split across assigned and unassigned mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 6.1, proved in this paper. It says that IMMAM, for the paper’s threshold reserve markets, returns a matching that complies with eligibility requirements, respects priorities, is max-in-max, and is defined on the universal domain. Here max-in-max means first maximizing the number of patients assigned an eligible resource, then maximizing beneficiary assignments among matchings that attain that maximum. The paper gives running-time bounds for IMMAM’s optimized versions separately in Section 6.1; those bounds are in the number of individual patients and resources. There is no numbered theorem asserting a complexity-class result, and no hardness result to mirror.

My lead is Threshold Reserve Max-in-Max Mass Matching. Consider a population of normalized mass \(1\), represented by a finite support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). There are categories \(C\), each with a rational supply mass \(\rho_c\). A type \(t\) records, for every category, whether its members are eligible and beneficiaries, and their priority tier. Thus \(B_c\subseteq E_c\), and each category has its own priority order over types; people within one type share a tier. The instance also records the binary rational input length \(L\).

The question is to output a rational mass assignment \(x_{tc}\). Its entries are the mass of type \(t\) receiving one unit from category \(c\): they do not mean that an individual receives a fractional vaccine. The assignment must satisfy
\[ x_{tc}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_c x_{tc}\le \mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{tc}\le \rho_c, \]
and \(x_{tc}=0\) whenever \(t\notin E_c\). Write \(u_t=\mu_t-\sum_c x_{tc}\) for unmatched mass. To mirror the paper’s priority condition, require that for every category \(c\), if type \(s\) has higher priority than type \(t\), then \(x_{tc}>0\) implies \(u_s=0\). Tied people within a type may be split across assigned and unassigned mass.

Among assignments satisfying those conditions, maximize lexicographically
\[ \left(\sum_{t,c}x_{tc},\quad \sum_{c}\sum_{t\in B_c}x_{tc}\right). \]
The first coordinate is total resource allocation; the second is beneficiary assignment. Any assignment attaining that lexicographic maximum is a solution.

A plausible regime is a countrywide vaccine rollout with millions of patients, a handful of reserve categories, and perhaps hundreds or a few thousand distinct types formed from age, occupation, safety eligibility, beneficiary status, and category-specific priority tiers. Category supplies scale with population size, so \(\rho_c\) is the reserve share, not a fixed dose count as the population grows. The paper’s categories may have independent priorities, which this mirror preserves. The co-modification is that people in a type tie at each category’s priority tier; that is a natural model for policy bands, but it is a real extension of the paper’s strict individual rankings.

I expect this problem to be Class A, though that is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 6.1. Without the priority condition, the mass assignment is a capacitated matching problem: rational capacities and the lexicographic objectives fit max-flow and min-cost-flow methods. A promising route is to adapt IMMAM’s repeated “can this assignment preserve the max-in-max pair?” check to type-category pairs and rational masses. The key question is whether those checks can preserve the exact priority condition in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|C|\), and \(L\). The priority rule is conditional, so the whole problem cannot simply be called an LP without proving that it has a suitable flow or optimization formulation.

The mirror covers Theorem 6.1’s threshold-reserve allocation and max-in-max objective. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s path-independence, stability, or comparative-statics results. Useful next questions are whether the priority-respecting mass problem has a polynomial algorithm in the support encoding; whether discrete high-multiplicity instances admit additive-error rounding from its solution; and whether path independence survives when tied cohorts can be split.

The weak point is that the paper already supplies algorithms polynomial in the number of individual patients, and it gives no hardness result whose difficulty might disappear in a continuum. The potential gain here is narrower: a method polynomial in the number of distinct types and the bit length, instead of having to process a huge repeated population. Also, the tie-within-type priority rule is less faithful to the paper’s strict orderings than its eligibility, beneficiary, and objective structure. The case survives if priority bands are accepted as the relevant large-market regime; if exact individual tie-breaks are essential, the support may grow with the population and the compression advantage disappears.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against Theorem 6.1 is that its priority condition makes the patients hard to compress into high-multiplicity types. A type must include every feature the problem uses, and each category has a strict ordering of the individual patients. So two patients with different positions in any category’s order are different types. If those rankings matter, the support can grow with the population; the proposed compression works only by replacing individual rankings with priority tiers.

That replacement is substantive. The paper’s condition says that nobody assigned from category \(c\) may outrank an unmatched patient in \(c\)’s ordering. In the proposed tier model, \(x_{tc}>0\) requires every higher-priority type to be fully matched, while people within a type may be split between matched and unmatched mass. This is a sensible rule for tied policy bands, but it does not preserve the paper’s strict individual priorities: under a strict order, which members of a band are selected can matter. Theorem 6.1 itself is an existence-and-properties result, not a complexity bound; the running-time claims appear separately in Section 6.1.

A more faithful mirror would include each patient’s priority scores—or rank positions—in their type. But with exact strict rankings, that usually makes the support grow with the population; with atomless scores, the input becomes a distribution over priority vectors rather than the paper’s finite set of patients and rankings. One can impose a structured density and study that model, but the structure would be an additional modeling choice, not a high-multiplicity relaxation supplied by the paper.

This is the strongest negative argument, and it does not defeat the best tier-based version. Priority bands are plausible in vaccine, admissions, and hiring systems, and a mass-allocation question for those systems is well-posed. The paper’s algorithmic setting also gives the mirror a concrete computational target. So the honest case against is narrow: exact priorities erase the compression advantage, while the useful compressed mirror changes strict rankings to tiers. That makes the mirror less faithful, but I cannot honestly claim it makes the tier version unworthy of study.
