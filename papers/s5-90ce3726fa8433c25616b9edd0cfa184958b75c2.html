The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is a nearby mechanism-design mirror: production economies naturally have many agents who share a preference type, and their population can be represented by masses. But it does not meet ChoCo’s anchor requirement.

The paper’s only named main result is printed as “Theorem.”—it is unnumbered and proved here. It says that for a strictly increasing, concave, nonlinear production function \(F\) with \(F(0)=0\), if the preference domain \(\mathcal R\) contains the class \(\mathcal L\) of linear preferences and is contained in the strictly monotonic domain \(\mathcal R_0\), then no direct mechanism \(\ell:\mathcal R^N\to Z\) satisfies both strategy-proofness and Pareto efficiency. The numbered Lemmas 1 and 2 are proof tools, not computational results. None of these results asserts a complexity or algorithmic classification, so there is no eligible anchor under the stated rules.

Here is the strongest plausible adjacent mirror. Let a type be a slope \(\lambda\in\Lambda\subset\mathbb R_{++}\), which completely determines the paper’s linear preference \(u_\lambda(x,y)=y-\lambda x\). Let \(\mu\) be the population distribution over slopes. In a large replicated economy, \(\mu_\lambda\) is the fraction of people with slope \(\lambda\), and \(z_\lambda=(x_\lambda,y_\lambda)\) is their per-capita bundle. A natural per-capita feasibility condition is
\[ \sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mu_\lambda y_\lambda \le F\!\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mu_\lambda x_\lambda\right). \]
The production technology is expressed per capita; the central continuous object is still the population distribution.

One could ask whether there exists an atomless SP–PE Production Mechanism: a direct rule that maps each reported distribution \(\nu\) over \(\Lambda\) to a measurable bundle schedule \(z_\nu(\lambda)\), satisfies the aggregate feasibility condition, is Pareto efficient, and gives each agent no incentive to misreport their slope. A solution would be the rule together with proofs of feasibility, efficiency, and strategy-proofness. For an individual agent of zero mass, a report does not change \(\nu\), but it can change the bundle selected from the rule’s schedule, so the incentive condition remains meaningful.

The regime is credible in a large production cooperative or workforce with many repeated preference types and relatively small support. The authors would recognize the question as closely related to theirs: it keeps the same preferences, production technology, and efficiency-versus-incentives tension. But the move to atomless agents changes the incentive problem: an individual can no longer affect aggregate reports as a finite agent can. That is the weakest point, and a serious one. This would be a new continuum-agent mechanism-design question, not simply their theorem with masses substituted for counts.

I would not give that adjacent question a Class A, B, or C prediction: those classes concern computational complexity, and neither this question nor the paper’s theorem is computational. So the strongest honest case is that the paper offers a natural high-multiplicity setting and a plausible neighboring continuous question—but no valid ChoCo anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The article gives no ChoCo result to continuize. Its theorem is an axiomatic impossibility statement: for the stated technology and preference domain, no mechanism satisfies both strategy-proofness and Pareto efficiency. The lemmas support that proof. There is no computational problem, input encoding, or complexity claim here. Asking whether an algorithm can find an SP–PE mechanism would add a computational question the paper never formulates.

The proponent’s atomless SP–PE model is a coherent nearby question, and a large workforce with repeated preferences is a plausible setting. The difficulty is that it changes the strategic object. In a population distribution, one person’s report leaves the distribution unchanged. To retain individual incentives, the model adds a type-indexed bundle schedule: an agent can choose the bundle assigned to another reported type. Those incentive constraints may be interesting, but they describe a continuum-agent screening problem. They do not supply the missing computational task in Leroux’s theorem. A finite-agent approximation preserves report effects of size \(1/n\); an atomless model removes them. Allowing finite-mass deviations would change the deviation concept.

There is a further modeling choice in the per-capita production constraint. Under literal replication of the paper’s fixed aggregate technology \(F\), per-capita output at average input \(x\) is \(F(nx)/n\), which tends to the linear recession technology as the population grows. Retaining the nonlinear \(F(x)\) per capita instead amounts to scaling productive capacity with population. The programme permits that kind of co-modification, so this is not a disqualifying objection; it does show that the paper does not dictate a unique continuous limit.

One could independently study computational versions of continuum mechanism design, but making one would require specifying a finite representation for mechanisms and a decision or optimization task. That is substantial new work, rather than a mirror of any named result here. The negative case is strongest as a ChoCo judgment: the proposed setting is plausible, but its natural question is axiomatic and its algorithmic counterpart is unanchored. I cannot establish that no worthwhile continuum mechanism-design research exists; I can say this paper provides no computational anchor for it.
