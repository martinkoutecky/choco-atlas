The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no anchors, and this paper has no numbered result establishing a computational complexity or algorithmic theorem. Its computational contribution is an MCMC framework assessed through experiments. That is the strongest case against a ChoCo mirror: there is no theorem-grounded result here whose complexity landscape the programme could chart.

I would not argue that school populations lack meaningful multiplicity. Students grouped by planning area, school level, demographics, and existing assignment make a plausible high-multiplicity society. The problem is where the paper’s decisions sit. Its plans assign whole graph nodes to schools, require each zone to be connected, and preserve one school anchor per zone. Student counts and characteristics are weights in the plan’s balance, diversity, and retention metrics. Treating those counts as masses can change the weights while leaving the feasible plans and the MCMC search over graph partitions intact.

A stronger mirror could let a planning area’s student mass split between schools. That creates a real continuous decision, but it changes the paper’s central object: a zone would no longer be a partition of planning areas. Preserving connected school zones would require a new geometric partition model; dropping that requirement would no longer capture the redistricting plans this paper evaluates. Either model might be worth studying on its own, but neither mirrors a named result in this paper. The negative case is therefore strong for *this paper as an anchor*, while the broader claim that no useful continuous school-redistricting problem exists would be too strong.
