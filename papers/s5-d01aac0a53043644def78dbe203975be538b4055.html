The continuous mirror question

Given a finite policy set \(R\), comparison set \(C\), finite type set \(T\), calibrated type utilities \(u_t\), known response map \(r_t(q)\in\{-1,0,1\}^K\), and rational polytope \(\mathcal M^0\subseteq\Delta(T)\), choose \(q\in C^K\) and a recommendation for each observed response-mass profile \(p\) to maximize \(\min_{p\in P(q)}\max_{x\in R}\min_{\mu\in\mathcal M(q,p)}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t u_t^\top x\), where \(P(q)\) is the set of profiles compatible with \(q\) and \(\mathcal M(q,p)=\{\mu\in\mathcal M^0:\sum_{t:r_t(q)=r}\mu_t=p_r\ \forall r\}\).

The model it lives in

A policy network has a continuum of stakeholders represented by finitely many complete preference types with masses \(\mu_t\). Each type has a known, commonly calibrated utility \(u_t\) and response pattern to the selected policy comparisons; the network observes the mass of each response pattern and chooses one policy to maximize guaranteed average stakeholder utility.

The objection that survived

Aggregate welfare requires a common cardinal calibration across stakeholder types, which pairwise answers alone do not provide. This restricts the mirror to institutions that supply that calibration and adopt aggregate utility as their decision mandate.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the offline max-min utility result, Theorem 1(b), and the offline min-max regret result, Theorem 4. It leaves the online elicitation methods, synthetic and real-data comparisons, and policy-tree learning procedures outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can either source hardness reduction be encoded in the type masses and response-pattern constraints, or does the population problem become tractable for fixed \(\tau\) or \(K\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is to active elicitation for a population of policy stakeholders. Imagine a large network of local housing agencies collecting input from thousands of administrators and caseworkers before choosing a common allocation policy. Members fall into a relatively small number \(\tau\) of recurring, complete preference types: everyone in type \(t\) has the same utility vector \(u_t\) over policy attributes and gives the same answers to pairwise policy comparisons. The network might have \(n\) respondents in the thousands but only tens of such types. Those figures describe a plausible regime, not a claim about the paper’s actual study.

This retains the paper’s central computational question: choose comparisons to ask, then use the answers to make a robust recommendation. The population becomes continuous through its type masses. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of respondents of type \(t\), with \(\mu\) drawn from a known rational polytope \(\mathcal M^0\). For a policy \(x\), define aggregate utility as
\[ \bar U_\mu(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t u_t^\top x. \]
Each of the \(K\) pairwise policy queries is asked across the population. For a query list \(q\), let \(r_t(q)\in\{-1,0,1\}^K\) be type \(t\)’s response pattern. The survey records the fraction \(p_r\) giving each pattern \(r\), and the compatible populations are
\[ \mathcal M(q,p)=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal M^0: \sum_{t:r_t(q)=r}\mu_t=p_r\ \text{for every }r\right\}. \]
The instance supplies the candidate policies and their attributes, the finite type set and its utilities and response patterns, \(\mathcal M^0\), and \(K\). A solution is a list of comparisons and a recommendation rule assigning a policy to each feasible response-mass profile.

My lead mirror is the population version of offline max-min utility elicitation:
\[ \max_{q\in C^K}\ \min_{p\in P(q)}\ \max_{x\in R}\ \min_{\mu\in\mathcal M(q,p)} \bar U_\mu(x), \]
where \(P(q)\) is the set of response-mass profiles compatible with \(q\) and \(\mathcal M^0\). The question is to find the comparisons and contingent recommendations that maximize guaranteed average stakeholder utility.

Its anchor is Theorem 1(b), proved in this paper: evaluating the objective of the paper’s simplified max-min utility query-selection problem is NP-hard, even when the recommendation set has only two items. That makes the source problem hard already with a very small downstream choice set. I would provisionally expect hardness transfers (Class B) when the number of stakeholder types can grow with the input: the source hardness lies in query selection and response contingencies, rather than in searching a large recommendation set. But the theorem does not prove hardness of this population formulation. Its uncertainty is one user’s utility vector; here it is an unknown distribution over known types. A reduction between those models remains to be shown. A natural next question is whether the population problem becomes tractable for fixed \(\tau\), fixed \(K\), or both.

A second, separate mirror is the population version of the paper’s offline min-max regret problem:
\[ \min_{q\in C^K}\ \max_{p\in P(q)}\ \min_{x\in R}\ \max_{\mu\in\mathcal M(q,p)} \left[\max_{x'\in R}\bar U_\mu(x')-\bar U_\mu(x)\right]. \]
Here the solution again consists of comparisons and a recommendation rule, now chosen to minimize worst-case regret in aggregate utility.

Its anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: evaluating the objective of the paper’s offline min-max regret elicitation problem is NP-hard even when the recommendation set consists of only two items. I likewise expect hardness transfers (Class B) when support size is part of the input, for the same reason: the original result already isolates difficulty in the elicitation and response structure. Whether its reduction survives the change from one uncertain utility vector to uncertain type masses is open. This version also raises the question of whether its regret objective remains hard under bounded support, and how its complexity compares with the max-min utility version.

I think the authors could recognize these as continuous analogues of their problem in the right scenario. Their Section 6 already connects pairwise elicitation to choosing among housing policies described by attributes such as fairness, effectiveness, and interpretability. A large service network could treat its constituent professionals as the “user” whose values the system elicits. The question still couples query choice to a robust downstream policy recommendation; it does not merely make the policy space or uncertainty continuous. The population replaces the single decision-maker, and the objective becomes aggregate utility or aggregate regret.

The scope here is deliberately narrow: the two offline query-design results above. I am not claiming mirrors for the online one-step-look-ahead methods, the observational-data estimators, or the policy-tree learning procedures. The paper’s continuous utility vector \(u\) is not itself a continuous society; the relevant move is the distribution \(\mu\) over stakeholder types.

The weakest point is the change from one decision-maker to a collective one. The paper elicits an individual policy-maker’s values, whereas these mirrors assume that a large group’s values should be aggregated by average utility and that its preference types are few and stable. Exact agreement within types is demanding, especially for cardinal utility vectors. The case works best for a standardized network with recurring roles or shared value profiles; it is much less convincing for a small leadership committee or a broad public with highly individualized trade-offs. That limits the scenario, but it does not erase the computational question in the network setting.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1(b) and Theorem 4 are genuine computational anchors: each proves NP-hardness for offline query selection with a recommendation set of just two items. But both concern one decision-maker’s latent utility vector. Every answer updates uncertainty about that same \(u\), and both utility and regret are evaluated against it. The stakeholder-population proposal changes what is uncertain and whose objective the recommendation serves.

That creates a dilemma for a faithful mirror. If the population consists of copies of one decision-maker type, its mass distribution is trivial and the elicitation problem reduces to the paper’s original one-user problem. If it contains several preference types, the decision-maker becomes a collective. The proposed objective \(\bar U_\mu(x)=\sum_t\mu_t u_t^\top x\) then assumes that one type’s utility can be compared cardinally with another’s. Pairwise answers do not establish that: rescaling one type’s utility by a positive constant preserves all its answers while changing aggregate utility and regret. Supplying commonly calibrated utilities, as the proposal does, makes the optimization well-defined, but adds an interpersonal welfare convention that the paper’s single-user model does not need. A majority rule, a least-advantaged criterion, or another aggregation rule would pose a different elicitation problem.

This applies to both anchors. For Theorem 1(b), a reduction would have to transfer hardness from uncertain individual utility to uncertainty about type masses while preserving the collective welfare objective. For Theorem 4, it would also have to preserve the meaning of regret when welfare is aggregated across people. The absence of those reductions does not itself defeat the mirrors; the deeper concern is that they ask a collective-governance question whose objective the paper does not specify. In its housing application, the paper elicits the values of agency decision-makers, while the people experiencing homelessness are the beneficiaries of the policy. Treating a large network of professionals as a population whose average utility should govern one shared recommendation changes that relationship.

The best version of the proposal is therefore a carefully calibrated, many-stakeholder institution that explicitly adopts aggregate utility as its mandate. That is coherent, and the authors might recognize it as an extension of active elicitation. My negative case is weak precisely there: the paper’s plural language about policy-makers leaves room for such a collective interpretation. I can argue that the two hardness results do not carry over as stated, and that a faithful one-user population collapses to the original problem. I cannot honestly rule out a well-motivated institution whose agreed aggregation rule makes the population version a worthwhile computational question.
