The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case offers no continuous mirror or named computational anchor; it says Theorems 1–3 are mechanism-design results, not complexity results. That is the strongest case against a mirror of this paper’s contribution. None states a running time or studies an algorithmic problem, and the paper gives no finite encoding for its arbitrary utility functions. Theorem 2’s explicit mechanism does not, by itself, supply a complexity result.

The best computational extension would give a society as a finite-support distribution over complete utility types and ask for the parallel-domain mechanism’s outcome. Given each type’s willingness-to-pay values \(p_{t,j}\), that reduces to computing \(\arg\max_j \sum_t \mu_t p_{t,j}\), a direct \(O(\tau m)\) aggregation. With arbitrary utility functions, the effort lies in encoding and evaluating those functions, not in population continuization. And in an atomless population, an individual’s report has zero effect on the outcome: the individual externality payment vanishes, so the paper’s dominant-strategy incentive condition loses its force. Treating a positive mass of people as one strategic bloc could restore an effect, but that changes the unit of agency and introduces a coalition or control problem the paper does not define.

A high-multiplicity setting for collective decisions is plausible, so this does not prove that no adjacent research question could be worthwhile. It does make the negative case strong for this paper: to get a nontrivial computational question, one must add a task such as mass manipulation, specify costs and utility encodings, and decide what counts as an agent. Those additions may be valuable, but they are new computational problems rather than continuous mirrors of the paper’s results.
