The continuous mirror question

Given finite pair and non-directed-donor type sets \(T_P,T_N\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), cycle and chain limits \(K,L\), a feasible fixed first-stage pattern plan \(x\), a recourse policy \(\pi\), and a vertex-failure mass budget \(r_v\), compute the exact worst-case recovered first-stage pair mass and return attaining failure and recovery allocations. Write \(s_t=\sum_q a_{tq}x_q\). Nature removes masses \(\gamma_t^S\) from selected participants and \(\gamma_t^U\) from unselected participants, with \(0\leq\gamma_t^S\leq s_t\), \(0\leq\gamma_t^U\leq\mu_t-s_t\), and \(\sum_t(\gamma_t^S+\gamma_t^U)\leq r_v\). Recovery allocates mass to policy-allowed typed cycles and chains, labeling each used participant as selected or unselected; each pattern must contain a selected pair. Respect the surviving selected and unselected type capacities, and maximize the mass of selected pairs recovered. For first-stage-only recourse, require every used participant to be selected and set \(\gamma_t^U=0\).

The model it lives in

A fixed first-stage mass plan over typed cycles and chains, with selected and unselected mass tracked separately. Nature removes up to \(r_v\) mass, and a recovery allocation over policy-allowed patterns maximizes recovered selected-pair mass; the question is to compute the minimum such recovery value and a worst-case failure allocation.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered result classifying a problem as polynomial, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its strongest named computational anchor is Proposition 5.1, proved here: for a fixed first-stage matching \(\tilde x\) and recourse policy \(\pi\), Algorithm 2 returns both the exact worst-case recovery value \(Z_Q^{\pi,*}(\tilde x)\) and a failure scenario \(\gamma^*\) attaining it. The algorithm adds failure scenarios to a feasibility-seeking master problem, solves the recourse problem for each, and stops when the master becomes infeasible. The proposition proves that this stopping condition certifies the worst-case value. It is a correctness result, not a polynomial-time guarantee.

My lead mirror is Mass-Robust Recovery Evaluation for Kidney Exchange. Consider a national or cross-country kidney exchange pool with many pairs and non-directed donors, where many participants share a finite set of clinically relevant profiles. A type records everything the model uses: pair or donor status, compatibility with the other profile classes, sensitization class, and recourse eligibility. The scenario is plausible if a registry has many participants but relatively few such profiles; compatibility profiles detailed enough to keep types interchangeable are the main constraint.

Let \(T_P\) and \(T_N\) be the pair and non-directed-donor types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. A type-level compatibility graph and cycle and chain limits \(K,L\) define a finite set of feasible patterns \(q\). A pattern is a cycle or chain of types; repeated type labels represent distinct participants of that type. Let \(a_{tq}\) count how many participants of type \(t\) pattern \(q\) uses. A first-stage plan is a nonnegative mass \(x_q\) for each pattern, subject to
\[ \sum_q a_{tq}x_q\leq \mu_t. \]
Thus \(x_q\) means a mass of parallel exchanges following pattern \(q\), not a fractional transplant for an individual. Fix this plan as the input, as Proposition 5.1 fixes \(\tilde x\).

For a clean first mirror, take the paper’s permitted vertex-failure-only case, \(r_a=0\). Let \(s_t=\sum_q a_{tq}x_q\) be the mass of type \(t\) selected in the first stage. Nature chooses failed mass \(\gamma_t^S\) among selected participants and, under full recourse, \(\gamma_t^U\) among other participants in the transitory graph. These are nonnegative rational masses, bounded by the corresponding available masses, with
\[ \sum_t(\gamma_t^S+\gamma_t^U)\leq r_v. \]
Under first-stage-only recourse, set \(\gamma_t^U=0\). The budget is therefore a mass-scaled version of the paper’s vertex-failure budget.

After the failures, choose nonnegative recovery masses \(y_q\) over patterns allowed by policy \(\pi\). Under full recourse, type capacities are \(\mu_t-\gamma_t^S-\gamma_t^U\); under first-stage-only recourse, they are \(s_t-\gamma_t^S\). For each pair type \(t\), let \(z_t\) be the recovered mass of first-stage-selected pairs. Require \(z_t\leq s_t-\gamma_t^S\) and \(z_t\leq\sum_q a_{tq}y_q\). The objective is to return
\[ Q_\infty^\pi(x)= \min_{\gamma}\ \max_{y,z}\ \sum_{t\in T_P}z_t, \]
together with a worst-case failure allocation and an optimal recovery plan. This asks how much of the first-stage patient mass can still be transplanted under the worst permitted failures, using the paper’s recovery policies and recovered-pair objective.

I expect this fixed-plan problem to be hard for continuum-specific reasons, though that is a conjecture, not a result of the paper. Fractional cycle and chain masses remove indivisibility from the matching decisions, but the adversary still minimizes over failures while the recovery stage optimizes in response. The resulting nested optimization may not reduce to an LP just because each individual stage has linear mass constraints. The useful research questions are whether bounded \(K,L\) and type-level structure enable pricing or duality, whether the worst failure allocation has a simpler extreme-point form, and whether complexity is fixed-parameter tractable in support size \(\tau=|T|\). Restoring arc failures raises a further question about the right mass semantics for crossmatch failures.

This mirror covers Proposition 5.1’s worst-case recovery evaluation for a fixed first-stage plan, restricted to vertex failures. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s arc-failure contribution or its outer choice of the first-stage matching. The restriction is deliberate: the paper explicitly allows \(r_a=0\), and the recovery question remains its own central computational problem.

The weakest point is whether kidney exchange admits enough repeated, fully interchangeable compatibility profiles for \(\tau\) to stay moderate. HLA compatibility can be highly individualized, and the paper’s arc failures are not captured by this vertex-only mirror. Those limits matter, but they do not erase the case for studying mass-based recovery in a registry regime with repeated clinical profiles; they define where this mirror should be tested first.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 5.1 is a thin anchor for a complexity mirror. It proves that Algorithm 2 returns the exact worst-case recovery value for a fixed matching in a finite graph. Its stopping certificate depends on a finite set of binary failure scenarios. It gives no running-time classification, and the paper’s central computational contribution is its finite-instance solution framework and experiments. A continuous version cannot inherit Proposition 5.1’s certificate: the failure allocations are now continuous, so the algorithm’s finite scenario-enumeration argument no longer applies.

The proponent’s better mirror—fix a mass allocation over exchange patterns, allow an adversary to remove a prescribed fraction of each type, then maximize recovered selected-pair mass—is still well-posed. But it is a new nested robust matching problem. With a fixed finite type set and bounded cycle and chain lengths, recovery at any particular failure allocation is a finite pattern-packing LP. The challenge is optimizing over the adversarial allocations; Proposition 5.1 provides no method or complexity insight for that step. This makes the proposed work harder to motivate *from this paper’s named result* than the case for suggests.

There is also a serious regime question. To preserve compatibility, a type must describe the pair’s clinically relevant profiles well enough that compatibility between types is reliable. Broad clinical categories risk inventing or deleting feasible exchanges; sufficiently detailed profiles may leave very few repeated types in actual registries. The paper’s experiments use graphs of 20 to 100 vertices, and do not establish a large population with moderate support. That weakens the practical case for this particular application.

Neither objection rules out the best version of the mirror. If many pairs really do share complete compatibility profiles, their mass can be allocated to cycles and chains while respecting per-type capacities; failures can remove mass, and recovery can reallocate it. With bounded cycle and chain lengths, the integrality lost by treating that mass as divisible may matter little at population scale. The proponent’s vertex-only restriction also sidesteps the harder question of how to scale arc failures.

So I can make a case that the paper supplies little complexity guidance and that a suitable low-support kidney-exchange regime is unestablished. I cannot honestly turn that into “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario.” A large pool with repeated, fully specified compatibility profiles would make the fixed-plan recovery question sensible, and its adversarial optimization remains a computational question. The anchor survives my objections, though its connection to Proposition 5.1 is less direct than the proponent claims.
