What the mirror covers

The proposed cohort version concerns the general reward-transfer LP; it does not carry over the paper’s individual-player results or its analyses of symmetrical and general self-interest levels.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Algorithm 1, “An algorithm to compute the minimal reward transfer matrix,” developed and evaluated in this paper. It takes an \(n\)-player, two-action social-dilemma payoff table and finds a reward-transfer matrix \(T\) maximizing the minimum diagonal entry. It does so by maximizing \(z\), subject to \(t_{ii}\ge z\), \(0\le t_{ij}\le1\), row sums of one, and an incentive constraint for every player and every profile of the other players: after transfers, choosing \(C\) must pay at least as much as choosing \(D\). The paper computes examples with this LP. It says the implementation enumerates \(2^n\) action profiles and has exponential memory and computation in \(n\), while the explicit payoff table is already of size \(O(n2^n)\).

There is an important qualification: the provided paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity classification. Algorithm 1 is its named computational contribution, but it is not a theorem proving tractability or hardness.

A continuous mirror I would put forward is Cohort Reward-Transfer Dominance. Its instance is a finite set \(H\) of strategically distinct cohorts, rational positive masses \(\mu_h\) summing to one, and rational per-member payoffs \(u_h(a)\) for every cohort action profile \(a\in\{C,D\}^{H}\). Assume that all-\(C\) is the social-welfare optimum and the instance satisfies the paper’s social-dilemma conditions at the cohort level. A contract chooses each cohort’s retained share \(\alpha_s\) and transfer shares \(q_{st}\ge0\) to other cohorts, with \(\alpha_s+\sum_{t\ne s}q_{st}=1\). Transfers from cohort \(s\) are pooled and divided among cohort \(t\)’s members, so a member of \(t\) receives

\[ v_t(a)=\alpha_tu_t(a)+\sum_{s\ne t}\frac{\mu_s}{\mu_t}q_{st}u_s(a). \]

The question is to find a feasible contract maximizing \(z=\min_t\alpha_t\), subject to \(v_t(C,a_{-t})\ge v_t(D,a_{-t})\) for every cohort \(t\) and every action profile of the other cohorts. A contract attaining the maximum is a solution. This preserves the paper’s transfer objective and dominant-action requirement at the cohort level, and the mass-weighted formula conserves total rewards.

The regime would be a large membership society—say, many thousands of members in a modest number \(\tau\) of interchangeable organizations or blocs, each with the same payoff role and a binding internal decision process. Each bloc chooses one action; its mass is its share of the population. Under an explicit payoff table, the mirror is an LP with \(O(\tau^2)\) variables and \(O(\tau2^{\tau-1})\) incentive constraints, so I expect tractability polynomial in the table’s encoding length. Whether the LP has an efficient separation method for compactly represented games is a further question.

The scope is the paper’s transfer-optimization algorithm and its general self-interest objective. I would not use the experiment tables as additional anchors: they give useful instances and patterns, but not numbered complexity results.

The weakest point is the change in who acts. In the paper, each individual player chooses \(C\) or \(D\); here, a whole cohort acts as one strategic unit. That can be sensible for organizations with collective governance, but it is not the ordinary high-multiplicity version where many independent, interchangeable agents retain individual choices. If cohort members instead make unilateral choices in a nonatomic population, an individual’s deviation has zero effect on population totals, and the transfer incentives that matter in the paper can disappear. So this is a credible mirror only for a bloc-decision regime, and its acceptance depends on whether the authors regard cohort-level dominance as their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Algorithm 1 is a real computational anchor: it computes the general self-interest level and reports exponential cost from enumerating action profiles. The absence of a numbered complexity theorem weakens the case for mirroring, but does not dispose of it.

The stronger objection is that the algorithm’s constraints concern each player’s unilateral deviation against every profile of the other players. In a nonatomic population of independently acting agents, that strategic structure disappears. If a type-\(h\) member has mass \(1/N\), changing their action changes population aggregates by \(O(1/N)\). Under bounded per-capita payoffs and transfers pooled by type, the member’s incentive difference is therefore
\[ \Delta_i v_i=\alpha_h\Delta_i u_i+O(1/N), \]
where \(\alpha_h\) is the retained share of their own payoff. In a strict dilemma, if defection has a positive private advantage, any \(\alpha_h\) bounded away from zero preserves it as \(N\) grows. At \(\alpha_h\to0\), the contract loses the strict dominance condition that gives the paper’s metric its force. The continuous population has erased the individual externality that reward transfer was meant to address.

The strongest repair is to let each cohort choose one action collectively and pool its members’ rewards, as the proponent proposes. That avoids the vanishing-deviation problem, but changes the strategic players: a cohort’s binding internal decision process now supplies the collective action. The model is then a finite game of \(\tau\) blocs. Its incentives and LP constraints still concern those blocs’ action profiles; the large membership within each bloc is not the source of a new computational question. This might be a sensible application to organizations, but it is a mirror of the paper only if the authors want to study cohesive organizations as players, rather than many independently acting agents.

The paper’s graphical games show the difficulty of preserving individual incentives while grouping by type. In the Cyclical and Circular games, players may share the same role, yet a player’s useful transfers depend on *which* neighbors affect them. Pooling by role spreads transfers across non-neighbors; retaining the neighbor-specific structure requires carrying the interaction network into the model. A sparse network might support a worthwhile extension, but it needs relationship structure beyond a distribution over behavioral types, and it no longer follows from the proposed type-level mirror.

So the negative case is strongest for the paper’s central setting: a population of separate self-interested agents. There, a type-based continuum either washes out the unilateral effects, or restores them by treating groups as strategic blocs and thereby changes the problem. The caveat is real: a contract model for collective organizations could be valuable if the authors regard those organizations as the agents of interest. I cannot rule out that narrower mirror; I do not think it establishes a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper’s individual-agent result.
