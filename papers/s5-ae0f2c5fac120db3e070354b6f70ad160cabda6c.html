Why no mirror

Both anchors are computational, but the paper's PCP-net already defines a probability distribution over deterministic CP-net types and asks for the mass of types that entail dominance. Interpreting that mass as a cohort fraction restates the paper's distributional model, so it does not supply the discrete-to-continuous bridge this programme seeks.

What the mirror covers

The proposed query covers dominance prevalence for tree-structured types and the PCP-net distribution; it leaves the paper's optimality-probability and most-probable-optimum results without a distinct population bridge.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous-cohort dominance query. Let the agent types be complete deterministic CP-nets on the same Boolean variables and dependency graph \(G\). A society is a mass distribution \(\mu\) over those types. Given two outcomes \(o,o'\), the question is to compute the mass
\[ \mu\bigl(\{N : o \succ_N o'\}\bigr), \]
the fraction of agents whose own CP-net entails that \(o\) is preferred to \(o'\). The answer is an exact rational; there is no intervention to optimize. A plausible regime is a large recommender-system population with millions of customers but a much smaller set of recurring preference models over the same product features. This asks about the population’s preferences, rather than uncertainty about one named customer.

The strongest hardness anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: computing \(p_N(o\succ o')\) for a PCP-net \(N\) and outcomes \(o,o'\) is \(\#\mathrm P\)-hard, even when the graph is acyclic, has longest path three, each node has at most one outgoing edge, and each node has at most four parents. Its close continuous-population mirror is the same mass query when \(\mu\) is represented compactly by the PCP-net’s independent rational distributions over local preference rules. Each complete deterministic CP-net is a type, and its mass is the product of its local-rule probabilities. This can describe a very large cohort without listing every type. The expected classification is hardness transfer (Class B) if the finite-population counterpart permits that same succinct multiplicity representation: the rational masses can be realized by scaling to a finite cohort, and the combinatorics are in the rule choices. For an explicit support list, however, Theorem 4’s reduction does not establish hardness—the represented population may have exponentially many types, so the list itself could be exponentially long. Further questions include whether hardness survives for explicit support, or under restrictions such as bounded treewidth.

A more direct fit to the programme’s support-list model is Continuous Tree-Cohort Census: the input is a list of \(\tau\) tree-structured deterministic CP-nets \(N_i\), each with rational mass \(\mu_i\), where \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and two outcomes \(o,o'\). Compute \(\sum_{i:N_i\models o\succ o'}\mu_i\). Its anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: dominance testing for a tree-structured deterministic CP-net on \(n\) variables can be solved in \(O(n)\) time. Applying that routine to each type gives a polynomial-time census, \(O(\tau n)\) dominance work plus exact rational arithmetic. This is Class A. It is the cleanest match to an explicit high-multiplicity society: the population may have \(N\gg\tau\) agents, with repeated complete preference models. It covers only dominance prevalence for tree structures, not the paper’s optimization task or more general preference queries.

The weak point is substantial: PCP-nets already define a probability distribution over deterministic CP-nets, and the paper already computes the mass of models entailing dominance. Under the product-mass encoding, the lead mirror is therefore very close to a restatement of the paper’s own model, and may count as a novelty collision under the programme’s criterion. The explicit-support census is a genuinely population-oriented formulation, but Theorem 4 does not transfer its hardness to that encoding. So the positive case is that high-multiplicity preference cohorts are sensible, and the tree result gives a precise, useful mirror; the paper itself leaves little room to claim that the succinct probabilistic version is a new continuous question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is a novelty collision, and it hits the lead anchor directly. A PCP-net already defines a probability distribution over complete deterministic CP-net types, and Theorem 4 computes exactly the mass \(\mu(\{N:o\succ_N o'\})\) for that distribution. The paper even motivates its construction by aggregating past customers’ preferences. Calling that mass a continuous cohort query changes the interpretation’s emphasis; it does not create a new computational question. Theorem 4’s hardness result is already the answer for the proposed mirror.

The explicit-support version is a sound population question, but it adds little computational content to Theorem 2. For a list of \(\tau\) tree-structured types, run the \(O(n)\) dominance algorithm on each type and sum the corresponding rational masses, for \(O(\tau n)\) dominance work plus exact arithmetic. The population statistic is then a weighted census of individual answers. Proposition 1 already connects population proportions to dominance for swap pairs; the census extends the query to other outcome pairs without introducing a new interaction between agents. For a richer compact encoding, the computational issue becomes inference over the encoding’s preference dependencies, which is already the territory of the PCP-net analysis.

The same collision applies to a stronger outcome-oriented proposal: ask which outcome is optimal for the largest fraction of a cohort. Propositions 4 and 5 already compute the probability of optimality and the most probable optimal outcome for the paper’s PCP-net distributions. With explicit support, the typewise algorithms give the cohort totals directly. A genuinely new collective choice objective could be interesting, but defining one would add an aggregation problem that this paper does not study; it would no longer be a mirror of its dominance or optimisation results.

I would not ground the negative case in a lack of plausible repeated types. A large recommender-system cohort with recurring CP-net profiles is credible, and the paper itself supplies that setting. The weakness is that the paper’s most natural population query is already present in its probabilistic model, while the support-list variant is a direct census built from its individual algorithm. That makes “no worthwhile mirror” a defensible case, not a proof: a programme interested in cohort statistics or a new collective objective could still find value here.
