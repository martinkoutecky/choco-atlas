The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor. Its theorems concern existence and structural bounds for dynamic monopolies, not whether a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The closest candidate is Theorem 1, proved here: for every natural number \(n\), Berger constructs a graph with more than \(n\) vertices and a dynamic monopoly of size \(18\). That is a concrete combinatorial result, but it does not classify the complexity of finding a smallest dynamo. The other numbered theorems likewise give structural bounds or constructions.

The strongest positive direction I can see is a new, population-scale version of the minimum-dynamo problem. Consider a large society partitioned into \(\tau\) exchangeable network types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Let \(K_{tu}=K_{ut}\geq 0\) describe the interaction strength between types \(t\) and \(u\), with every type having positive total interaction. A seed choice is a vector \(x\) with \(0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t\), where \(x_t\) is the mass initially white in type \(t\). If \(w_t^r\) is the white mass of type \(t\) at round \(r\), its white-neighbor fraction is

\[ z_t^r=\frac{\sum_u K_{tu}w_u^r}{\sum_u K_{tu}\mu_u}. \]

When \(z_t^r>1/2\), all of type \(t\) becomes white next round; when \(z_t^r<1/2\), all becomes black; at a tie, each agent keeps their color. The decision problem is: given a rational budget \(B\), is there a seed vector with \(\sum_t x_t\leq B\) whose evolution reaches \(w_t^r=\mu_t\) for every type after finitely many rounds? A solution is such a seed vector together with its finite activation trajectory.

A plausible regime is a very large, block-structured community where members of each type have the same preferences about which types they interact with, and campaigners can seed a fraction of each type. The number of agents can be enormous while \(\tau\) stays moderate. This is recognizably about the same majority-cascade question, and the population mass is the resource being optimized. Still, it is an extension: the paper studies simple undirected graphs with individual vertices and neighborhoods, not this type-level interaction model. I cannot assign the proposed problem to one of the programme’s complexity classes from results in this paper.

The main weakness is especially visible in Theorem 1’s construction. Its \(18\) seeds have vanishing population share as the graph grows: the construction has \(19+27n\) vertices, so the seed fraction is \(18/(19+27n)\to 0\). In an ordinary finite-type continuum model, those exceptional seed vertices disappear as zero-mass types; preserving their influence may require a hybrid model or interaction strengths that change with population size. So Theorem 1 motivates a continuous-population research question, but it does not provide a valid named computational anchor for one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a mismatch between the paper’s results and the kind of question a continuous mirror would ask. The paper proves structural statements about particular graph families: most notably, graphs of unbounded size can have a dynamo of 18 vertices. It gives no algorithm or complexity classification for finding a minimum dynamo. The proposed minimum-mass problem is a plausible optimization problem, but it adds that objective and a new network model; it does not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The scale of the results makes the mismatch sharper. In the construction of Theorem 1, the seed fraction is \(18/(19+27n)\), which tends to zero. Theorem 5 likewise gives \(O(n)\) seeds for graphs with more than \(2^n\) vertices, and Theorem 3’s bound \(n<k^2\) still permits \(k/n\) to tend to zero. Normalized population mass erases precisely these sublinear seed sizes. In an atomless graphon model, a null seed set contributes zero to the neighborhood integrals, so it cannot start the cascade. Locally finite measured graph models have the same problem for finite seeds, unless singular hubs are retained. Keeping such hubs as discrete exceptions gives a hybrid model; keeping the finite population scale or rescaling seed mass to preserve \(O(1)\) seeds gives up the normalized continuum the programme is meant to study.

The proposed finite-type kernel model avoids that degeneracy by changing regimes. It is coherent for dense, block-structured populations where members of a type share the same aggregate interaction pattern and campaigners can seed a positive fraction of a type. But it replaces the paper’s locally defined majority process on simple graphs with a mean-field block process. That could be a worthwhile problem in its own right; it does not show that the paper’s sparse duplication construction has a useful continuous mirror. A graphon version faces the same distinction: positive-mass seeding can yield an interesting cascade question, but it no longer captures the constant-size-dynamo phenomenon.

I would not claim that no population-scale majority-cascade problem is worth studying. Dense network cascades offer a credible scenario, and the paper’s subject makes that scenario recognizable. The negative case is narrower: none of the paper’s named results supplies a computational anchor for it, and the results most suggestive of continuization concern seed counts that vanish as population mass. The honest weakness is that a continuous minimum-seed problem on dense, finite-type networks might still be valuable—just as a new adjacent problem, rather than a continuous mirror of Berger’s results.
