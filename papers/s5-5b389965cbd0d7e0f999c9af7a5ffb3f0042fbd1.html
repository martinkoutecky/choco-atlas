The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type catalogue \(T\), a symmetric rational valuation function \(w:T\times T\to\mathbb{Q}\), and an atomless arrival path \(\theta:[0,1]\to T\), determine whether every deterministic causal free-dissolution policy can be defeated: for each \(c>1/(6+4\sqrt{2})\), is there such an instance on which the policy's normalized final FHG welfare is less than \(c\) times the offline optimum? Each agent must receive an integral finite-coalition assignment, though the policy may divide the mass of a type cohort among different assignments.

The model it lives in

An atomless population arrives as a type flow \(\theta\) over a finite catalogue \(T\), with symmetric rational valuations \(w\). A deterministic causal policy assigns each arriving agent to a finite coalition and may dissolve existing coalitions; type mass can be split across integral coalition assignments, and welfare is aggregated over the population.

The objection that survived

The lower-bound construction relies on two live named agents with indivisible matching capacity, while positive-mass hub cohorts in the fluid model can be divided among different coalitions.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the deterministic free-dissolution guarantee and barrier in Theorems 6.1 and 6.2; it leaves the random-arrival results and the matching-domain results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does every deterministic causal policy in the type-flow model admit a finite rational instance witnessing a ratio below each \(c>1/(6+4\sqrt{2})\)?
  • Can a matching-derived policy guarantee a ratio of at least \(1/(6+4\sqrt{2})\) in this model, and can it be implemented using the type support and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: the paper’s free-dissolution results support a continuous online FHG problem. They do not give an NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification, so the mirror would ask about the best competitive guarantee, not claim a standard complexity result.

Take a finite catalogue \(T\) of agent types and a symmetric rational valuation function \(w:T\times T\to\mathbb{Q}\). A type is the agent’s complete profile of valuations toward every type; agents of the same type are interchangeable for everything this model uses. Represent the population by a measurable arrival path \(\theta:[0,1]\to T\), with mass \(\mu_t=|\{s:\theta(s)=t\}|\) of type \(t\). The path records order: by time \(s\), a policy sees only the agents and valuations revealed so far, not the future path. It must maintain a partition of the arrived agents into finite coalitions; before assigning an arrival, it may dissolve an existing coalition into singletons, as in the paper.

For a final partition \(\Pi\), use normalized welfare
\[ \operatorname{SW}(\Pi)=\int_0^1 \frac{1}{|C_\Pi(s)|} \sum_{r\in C_\Pi(s)\setminus\{s\}} w(\theta(s),\theta(r))\,ds, \]
where \(C_\Pi(s)\) is the coalition containing \(s\). Each agent still belongs to an integral coalition; only the population and aggregate welfare are measured as mass. The offline benchmark is the maximum welfare over partitions of the whole population, regardless of arrival order.

This is a plausible regime for a large workforce or platform forming project teams from recurring, standardized profiles, where arrivals occur in waves and assignments can be dissolved without cost. A large number of agents can share a relatively small catalogue of complete interaction profiles, so \(n\gg\tau\) in discrete approximations. Arrival time serves to retain the paper’s online constraint; the object being continuized is the population.

The lead question is Continuous Free-Dissolution FHG Barrier: for every deterministic causal policy \(A\) and every \(c>\frac{1}{6+4\sqrt{2}}\), can an adversary give a finite type catalogue and arrival path on which \(A\)’s welfare ratio is below \(c\)? The instance is the type catalogue, its rational valuation matrix, and the hidden arrival path; a witness consists of such data that defeats the proposed policy.

Its anchor is Theorem 6.2, proved in this paper: no deterministic online coalition-formation algorithm with free dissolution has competitive ratio greater than \(\frac{1}{6+4\sqrt{2}}\) for symmetric FHGs. The proof builds an adversarial sequence of phases and derives a recurrence that would contradict a sequence-exclusion lemma cited from Varadaraja (2011). In the fluid version, repeated leaves at a given valuation level can be represented as a type cohort arriving over an interval. That makes a population-based adversarial construction credible, though the transfer itself remains to be proved.

The companion question is Continuous Free-Dissolution FHG Guarantee: construct a deterministic causal policy for this fluid model and prove that, for every admissible arrival path, its welfare is at least \(\frac{1}{6+4\sqrt{2}}\) times the offline optimum. Its anchor is Theorem 6.1, derived in the paper: such an algorithm exists for the discrete setting. The paper obtains the guarantee by transferring a cited online matching guarantee of \(\frac{1}{3+2\sqrt{2}}\) through the matching-to-FHG welfare bound, which loses a factor of two.

Together these anchors suggest that the fluid problem may have the same sharp value, \(\rho_{\mathrm{FD}}^{\mathrm{det}}=\frac{1}{6+4\sqrt{2}}\). The algorithmic side looks tractable in the online-competitive sense; the barrier looks like a transfer of a structural online obstruction, rather than a new continuum-specific hardness phenomenon. Those are forecasts, not results. Questions they generate include whether the barrier survives with \(\tau\) fixed, whether allowing mass to split across decisions improves the fluid policy’s guarantee, and whether the matching-based policy has an implementation polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The paper’s random-arrival results are outside this mirror: uniform random order may collapse to a predictable type flow in a continuum limit.

The soft spot is the deterministic fluid policy’s extra freedom. It can divide a cohort’s mass among different integral coalition outcomes, potentially emulating randomization or escaping the paper’s lower-bound construction. The repeated-leaf structure makes the continuous mirror plausible, but it does not establish the barrier or the matching algorithm’s compressed implementation. The case therefore rests on a specific, testable online mirror—not on a claim that the paper already contains a continuous computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is that both proposed anchors depend on sequential decisions about indivisible agents, while a population continuum gives a policy divisible mass to manage. The paper’s obstruction and the paper’s algorithm therefore do not carry over as stated.

Theorem 6.2’s lower bound keeps a particular pair of agents as the live state across successive phases. New leaves arrive with valuations tied to those named agents, and the algorithm must commit before it knows which later arrivals will matter. In a society of unit mass, two agents have mass zero. Under the proponent’s welfare integral, they cannot contribute to welfare or exert the same matching capacity as the two live vertices in the proof. Give the centers positive mass instead, and they become cohorts: the policy can allocate different portions of that mass to different coalitions. For example, a coalition containing hub mass \(p\) and leaf mass \(q\), with cross valuation \(w\), has aggregate welfare \(2pqw/(p+q)\). As \(p\) tends to zero this vanishes; for positive \(p\), the cohort has divisible capacity. Neither case is the single pivotal pair in the theorem. Preserving that pair as an indivisible resource would require retaining named agents as atoms, so the population would no longer be purely continuous.

The best version of the barrier question could use rational, piecewise-constant type flows and ask for the optimal deterministic guarantee under causal arrivals. That is a coherent new online model, but the paper’s proof does not establish its barrier. In the fluid model, a type arriving over an interval can be split across coalitions, allowing the policy to hedge across choices that are mutually exclusive for a discrete agent. Preventing that split would require specifying an indivisibility rule for mass. The exact constant in Theorem 6.2 is therefore not a sound benchmark for the fluid problem without a new argument.

Theorem 6.1 has a related problem. Its guarantee comes from an online matching algorithm on individual vertices and a matching-to-welfare transfer. With type masses, the natural matching object is fractional, and a policy may divide a cohort’s mass among partners. The theorem’s algorithm may inspire a fluid policy or yield a conservative guarantee after a discretization proof, but it does not identify the fluid model’s optimal ratio. The proposed “matching guarantee” mirror thus needs its own policy model and analysis; it is not a direct continuous counterpart of the theorem.

I would reject the claim that these two results already support a sharp continuous mirror. The negative case is weaker against the broader claim that some worthwhile mirror exists: a rational type-flow model for large platforms or workforces is plausible, and online welfare guarantees are algorithmic questions. The evidence here is that the paper’s central lower-bound mechanism relies on a vanishing set of pivotal individuals, while the natural atomless model changes what policies can do. That defeats the proposed transfer, but does not prove that every sensible continuous online coalition-formation problem is unworthy of study.
