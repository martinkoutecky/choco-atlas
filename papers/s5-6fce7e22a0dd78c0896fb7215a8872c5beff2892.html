The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result: it proves existence and equilibrium claims, but does not give an algorithm or classify a problem as tractable or hard. The closest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in the Appendix. Under desirability of wealth, for every \(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_0>0\), there is a threshold \(n(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_0)\) such that every economy with more than that many agents, under any attribute assignment \(\alpha:N\to\Omega\), has an \((\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_0)\)-Edgeworth state. Such a state is in the \(\varepsilon_1\)-remainder \(c(\varepsilon_0)\)-core, and its replicas remain in that core after excluding a remainder of at most the same population proportion: no coalition among the retained agents can improve on the replicated state while paying the communication cost. This is an existence theorem, not a qualifying complexity anchor. I would use it as the strongest honest starting point for a mirror, not present it as an algorithmic result.

The mirror I would propose is the Continuous Communication-Core Problem. Take a finite set \(T\) of complete economic types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Each type packages the attributes that matter to the economy: preferences, endowments, and other relevant characteristics. Represent society by \(A=\bigsqcup_{t\in T} A_t\), where \(A_t=[0,\mu_t]\) has Lebesgue mass. Individuals retain integral club membership; mass only allows part of a type to receive one club portfolio and the rest another.

A state consists of measurable private-goods allocations \(x(a)\) and a family of measurable clubs \(C\subseteq A\), with overlapping membership allowed. A club’s type profile is \(q_t(C)=\lambda(C\cap A_t)\). Its resource requirement and each member’s utility are specified by the club profile and the member’s type. Feasibility requires
\[ \int_A (x(a)-e_{t(a)})\,d\lambda(a) \leq \sum_{C} z(q(C)). \]
For a coalition \(S\), the communication cost is the paper’s cost scaled by mass, \(c(\varepsilon_0,S)=\varepsilon_0\lambda(S)\bar z\). The instance supplies finite rational descriptions of the type utilities and club rules—for example, piecewise-linear functions of private consumption and club profiles.

The problem is: find a feasible state and a retained population \(A_0\subseteq A\), with \(\lambda(A\setminus A_0)<\varepsilon_1\), such that for every integer replica \(r\), no coalition in \(A_0\times\{1,\ldots,r\}\) can form its own clubs and consumption plan, pay the mass-scaled communication cost, and make every member strictly better off than in the replicated state. A solution is the allocation, club structure, retained population, and a demonstration of that stability property. There is no scalar objective in the paper’s problem: it asks for a stable state, not a maximum or minimum.

The regime is plausible on the paper’s own terms: millions of people participating in overlapping large organizations—environmental agreements, trade organizations, or other broad collective activities—with relatively few distinct preference, endowment, and crowding types. Then population mass is the honest unit for membership and communication costs, while \(\tau=|T|\) can be far smaller than population size. The paper itself studies arbitrary attribute assignments in large economies and emphasizes replicated economies, so high multiplicity supports this mirror rather than colliding with it. The co-modification is to describe clubs and costs by measurable membership profiles; the population remains the object being continuized.

I expect a Class A result for a finite-type, rational, piecewise-linear version: the proof’s use of preferred sets, convexification, and separation suggests a continuous feasibility formulation. But efficient computation would still hinge on pricing possible overlapping clubs and handling utilities over membership portfolios. Theorem 2 gives no algorithm for that work, and the unrestricted version could reveal continuum-specific hardness.

The weakest point is substantial: a nonatomic population problem is not a limit theorem established by Theorem 2. The paper proves a result for sufficiently large *finite* economies and their replicas; it does not prove that a continuum club structure exists or that one can compute it. The proposed question is therefore a credible computational extension of their Edgeworth-core problem, not a result already contained in the paper. It covers that existence result only; I would not stretch it to the paper’s price-support theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor, and it is an existence theorem rather than a computational result. It supplies a threshold \(n(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_0)\), but no bound for computing that threshold, no algorithm for finding an Edgeworth state, and no complexity classification. Theorem 4 likewise establishes existence and price support. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, the paper offers no named computational problem whose complexity a continuous mirror could classify.

The proposed mirror also makes material modeling choices. In the paper, a club is an exact subset of agents; utilities can depend on an agent’s club portfolio, and resource requirements are attached to clubs. Equilibrium participation prices are indexed by both the person and the exact club. The proposed profile \(q(C)\) representation makes clubs with the same type masses equivalent and restricts utilities and technologies to finite descriptions based on those profiles. That is a coherent model for some societies, but it imposes anonymity and compressibility that the paper does not assume. Refining types to retain identity-sensitive club effects could make the support large and erase the proposed compression.

The computational formulation remains unspecified even after the piecewise-linear restriction. With overlapping clubs of unbounded size, there are continuously many possible profiles \(q\in\prod_{t\in T}[0,\mu_t]\), and the proposal needs a finite representation of the active clubs, portfolios, and blocking coalitions. The paper’s separation proof works in a space indexed by agents and possible clubs; it gives no finite pricing oracle or other computational structure for that space. The proof therefore provides little basis for expecting a tractable continuous optimization problem.

There are credible high-multiplicity scenarios here: the paper itself motivates large organizations and economies with many similar agents. I would not argue that population mass is meaningless, or that continuous economics literature has already answered the computational question. Those points would be weak. The more defensible negative case is that the article contains no computational result to mirror, and its strongest proposed computational problem depends on additional restrictions that define a new model. A finite-type, anonymous version might still be worthwhile research; the case against a universal “no worthwhile mirror” is consequently weak outside ChoCo’s narrower test.
