The continuous mirror question

Given a population scale \(N\), role counts \(N_i\), a rational reversible interaction kernel \(K\) satisfying \(N_iK_{ij}=N_jK_{ji}\), a fixed quasi-majority function \(f\), and initial opinion counts by role, consider independent synchronous updates using the paper's local switching probabilities. What is a multiplicative approximation to \(t_{N^{-c}}\), the least round \(t\) by which exact consensus occurs with probability at least \(1-N^{-c}\), and can it be computed in time parameterized by the number of roles, input bit length, and \(\log N\)?

The model it lives in

A stochastic role-count Markov chain: the society is given by rational masses \(\mu_i=N_i/N\) over a small set of roles, with opinions splitting each role's mass. Each transition aggregates independent per-agent updates into binomial count changes; the objective is the high-confidence hitting time for exact consensus.

The objection that survived

Theorem 1.3 bounds the protocol's runtime but does not give an algorithm for computing the proposed high-confidence horizon; approximating that horizon is a further computational task, even though consensus time is the paper's central subject.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the consensus-time guarantees of Theorems 1.3, 1.4, and 1.7 on role-homogeneous reversible networks. It leaves results for arbitrary unweighted graph configurations, technical lemmas, and graph-family corollaries outside that role representation untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can \(t_{N^{-c}}\) be multiplicatively approximated in time \(g(\tau,1/\epsilon)\operatorname{poly}(L,\log N)\)?
  • Can an aggregate algorithm capture both symmetry breaking near a tie and extinction of the last minority agents?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is a high-multiplicity consensus process on a role-based network. The population is continuous in the ChoCo sense: it is represented by rational masses over a small support of types, rather than by named people. But I would keep the finite population scale in the model. If we replace the random updates with their deterministic expectations, an exact tie can remain a tie forever, and a positive minority mass may never reach zero. That would lose the paper’s symmetry breaking and exact-consensus questions.

Take \(\tau\) recurring roles, with \(N_i\) agents in role \(i\), total population \(N=\sum_i N_i\), and mass \(\mu_i=N_i/N\). Agents in a role share the same local sampling profile: \(K_{ij}\) is the probability of sampling from role \(j\), with \(\mu_iK_{ij}=\mu_jK_{ji}\). The type is the role, together with the common interaction and update parameters; opinion is a changing state within that type. If \(q_i\) is the fraction of role \(i\) holding opinion \(1\), its mass is \(\mu_iq_i\).

For the process, an agent in role \(i\) holding opinion \(0\) switches to \(1\) with probability \(f((Kq)_i)\); an agent holding \(1\) switches to \(0\) with probability \(f((K(1-q))_i)\). Agents update independently and synchronously. The state is just the vector of role counts, whose transitions are sums of binomial random variables; no individual identities are needed. Thus the instance has rational role masses and opinion masses, but retains the finite-population randomness needed for consensus.

This is plausible for a large, well-mixed online community or organization with a few recurring interaction roles and a common local voting rule. It is a restricted weighted-network setting: the paper explicitly notes in Remark 3.4 that its results extend to reversible transition kernels. For equal per-agent stationary mass, the lifted \(N\)-agent chain has \(\pi(v)=1/N\), so \(\|\pi\|_2=N^{-1/2}\) and \(\|\pi\|_3=N^{-2/3}\), even when \(\tau\ll N\). That preserves the theorem’s population-scale fluctuation terms.

The lead question is Role-Mass Quasi-Majority Consensus Horizon. Its instance is \(N\), the role counts \(N_i\), rational reversible kernel \(K\), a fixed quasi-majority function \(f\), and initial opinion counts by role. For fixed \(c>0\), define \(t_{N^{-c}}\) as the least \(t\) such that the probability of exact consensus by round \(t\) is at least \(1-N^{-c}\). A computational version asks for a multiplicative approximation to this horizon, with complexity measured in \(\tau\), the input bit length, and \(\log N\). This keeps the paper’s underlying question—how quickly the voting process reaches consensus—while asking whether its type-mass representation supports efficient high-multiplicity analysis. This is my lead mirror.

The anchor is Theorem 1.3 (Main theorem), proved in this paper. In full, it says:

  • For arbitrary \(C_1>0\) and any \(\varepsilon(N)\to0\), if \(\lambda\le C_1N^{-1/4}\), \(\|\pi\|_2\le C_1/\sqrt N\), and \(\|\pi\|_3\le\varepsilon(N)/\sqrt N\), then from every initial configuration the consensus time is \(O(\log N)\) with high probability.
  • For \(C_2>0\) depending only on \(f\), if \(\lambda\le C_2\) and \(\|\pi\|_2\le C_2/\sqrt{\log N}\), then every initial configuration with \(|\delta(A)|\ge C_2\max\{\lambda^2,\|\pi\|_2\sqrt{\log N}\}\) reaches consensus in \(O(\log N)\) steps with high probability.

For the mirror, the question is whether the approximate consensus horizon can be computed efficiently on this role-mass input, and whether these same hypotheses give an \(O(\log N)\) horizon. The theorem supplies the runtime bound for the process; it does not itself give an algorithm for computing the horizon. I would expect a tractable approximation in the stated regime, using the aggregate state and concentration bounds. Whether it is FPT in \(\tau\) and input bit length remains open.

A separate mirror of Theorem 1.4 (Lower bound), also stated in the paper, is the Worst-Case Role-Mass Consensus Horizon: over initial opinion profiles, determine the worst high-confidence horizon for the given role population. The theorem says that under the first set of assumptions in Theorem 1.3, some initial configuration has \(T_{\mathrm{cons}}(A)=\Omega(\log N)\) with high probability. Its mirror asks whether a matching lower bound holds for some initial role masses, making the \(O(\log N)\) horizon sharp. I expect the approximate version to remain tractable; this is a lower bound on the process time, not evidence of computational hardness. The paper proves lower-bound results here; its Theorem 5.1 gives a related formulation under stronger assumptions.

A third, narrower mirror is Growing-Sample Role-Mass Consensus Horizon, anchored by Theorem 1.7, proved in the paper. The theorem states that if \(k=\omega(1)\), \(k=o(N/\log N)\), and \(C>0\), then best-of-\((2k+1)\) on an \(N\)-vertex \(\lambda\)-expander satisfying \(\lambda\le Ck^{-1/2}N^{-1/4}\), \(\|\pi\|_2\le C N^{-1/2}\), and \(\|\pi\|_3\le Ck^{-1/6}N^{-1/2}\) reaches consensus in \(O(\log N/\log k)\) steps with high probability from every initial configuration. Its mirror asks for an approximate high-confidence horizon when \(k\) is part of the input. I would again expect a tractable approximation in these regimes; the theorem’s faster *process time* is not a complexity bound for computing that time.

The scope here is the paper’s consensus-time results in role-homogeneous weighted networks, not every result on arbitrary unweighted expanders. Further questions include whether the horizon can be approximated in time \(g(\tau,1/\epsilon)\operatorname{poly}(L,\log N)\), how to handle the symmetry-breaking phase near a tie, and how to capture extinction of the last minority agents without expanding the state space.

The soft spot is substantial: in a general unweighted graph, vertices often have distinct neighborhood profiles, so representing them faithfully may require \(\tau\) close to \(N\). The compressed mirror is strongest for role-homogeneous networks, not arbitrary expander graphs. Also, the paper proves fast and sometimes tight process-time bounds; it contains no NP-hardness or algorithmic-complexity result about computing consensus horizons. So this is a credible population-continuization case, but a marginal ChoCo case unless computational analysis of distributed voting processes is within the programme’s intended scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is that the paper studies the round complexity of a distributed stochastic process. Its theorems say how long the process takes; they do not give an algorithmic problem whose solution is the consensus time. The proposed horizon task turns that performance measure into a new problem: given a succinct description of a stochastic process, compute its high-confidence hitting time. That could be worthwhile, but a result about that task would not mirror the paper’s theorems in the sense ChoCo is trying to study.

The role model is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and I would not reject it on the ground that roles are not types. The difficulty is preserving the results. If \(q_i\) is the fraction of role \(i\) with opinion \(1\), the deterministic mass dynamics are
\[ q_i' = q_i\bigl(1-f((K(1-q))_i)\bigr)+(1-q_i)f((Kq)_i). \]
For the one-role complete mixer, this becomes \(q'=H_f(q)\). In particular, \(q=1/2\) is fixed. For best-of-three, \(H_f(q)=3q^2-2q^3\), so every positive \(q\) stays positive at every finite round. The continuous dynamics therefore do not reach exact consensus from a tie, and generally approach consensus only asymptotically. The \(O(\log N)\) result in Theorem 1.3 relies on finite-population fluctuations near the tie and on the last minority agents eventually disappearing. A deterministic mass model loses both effects.

One can instead ask for time to reach an \(\varepsilon\)-consensus, or keep the finite-\(N\) randomness and ask for the proposed horizon. The first changes exact consensus into a tolerance question; its answer is a property of the dynamics, not a computational solution to the kind of choice problem ChoCo targets. The second keeps the count state, whose role counts range over \(\prod_i(N_i+1)\) possibilities. Computing its hitting time could define a genuine succinct Markov-chain problem. Its answer depends on the finite population scale and individual update noise, though; it is no longer the mass-only continuous counterpart. Theorem 1.3’s spectral and degree conditions also concern the underlying graph, which a small role kernel represents faithfully only for a restricted class of networks.

Theorem 1.4 makes the limiting problem sharper. In the deterministic continuum, an exact tie stays tied forever. If ties are excluded, initial biases can approach zero arbitrarily closely, so the time to escape can grow without a finite worst case. The paper’s \(\Omega(\log N)\) lower bound uses finite-\(N\) fluctuations to create a seed of order \(N^{-1/2}\); that scale supplies the \(\log N\) time. A continuous model has no canonical seed of that size. Keeping the finite population makes a worst-starting-profile horizon possible, but again the proposed optimization is over a finite stochastic chain, not a continuous lower-bound mirror of the theorem.

Theorem 1.7 has the same issue in a more pronounced form. As \(k\) grows, the mass update approaches a majority threshold: away from a tie, a fully mixed population moves essentially to one side in a round; at the tie, it remains at the tie. The \(O(\log N/\log k)\) behavior comes from finite-population fluctuations that amplify near balance and from eventual extinction. Replacing the process with its continuous limit erases those features. Keeping \(N\), \(k\), and the random count transitions preserves them, but makes “approximate the horizon” a new process-analysis problem rather than a continuous mirror of Theorem 1.7.

There is also a broader fit problem: the paper has no preference profiles, candidates, or collective choice outcome. It studies binary opinions on a graph, with updates determined by local neighborhoods. A campaigning or seeding problem could be added, and ChoCo might find that extension interesting, but it would introduce an intervention objective absent from the results being mirrored.

This is a strong objection to the three horizon mirrors as stated, not an airtight case that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. Role-homogeneous networks are plausible, and the paper really does prove runtime bounds for a randomized algorithm. A continuous stochastic aggregate model, or a campaign to accelerate consensus, could be valuable if the authors recognize that question as theirs. The negative case is weakest precisely there: proving that no such author-recognized computational question exists is not supported by the paper.
