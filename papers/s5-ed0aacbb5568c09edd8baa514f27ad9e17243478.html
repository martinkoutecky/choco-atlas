The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), eligible schools \(E_t\), utilities \(u_{tj}\), group indicators \(a_{tk}\), per-capita school capacities \(c_j\), and a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether nonnegative assignment masses \(x_{tj}\) exist such that \(x_{tj}=0\) for \(j\notin E_t\), \(\sum_{j\in E_t}x_{tj}=\mu_t\) for every type, \(\sum_t x_{tj}\le c_j\) for every school, and \(\sum_t a_{tk}\sum_{j\in E_t}u_{tj}x_{tj}\ge q\) for every group. Equivalently, maximize the minimum group utility. For a cohort of \(N\) students, Theorem 2’s total-utility threshold \(1\) corresponds to normalized threshold \(q=1/N\).

The model it lives in

Types encode each student profile used by the assignment problem: eligible schools, utilities, and group memberships. The mass \(\mu_t\) is assigned through \(x_{tj}\), with per-capita school supplies \(c_j\); the objective is to maximize the minimum group utility, or decide whether it reaches \(q\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the max-min fairness hardness result in Theorem 2 and the exact-proportionality hardness result in Theorem 3. It does not mirror the rounding guarantees in Theorems 1, 4, and 5 or the rank-dominance results.

Open questions for a prover

  • As cohort sizes grow, what is the minimum capacity augmentation needed to realize an optimal continuous max-min allocation with indivisible students while preserving its group utilities?
  • When can a continuous proportional allocation be realized integrally with no capacity increase?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make the mirror around the paper’s group-fair assignment problem, with Theorem 2 as the lead. It has a natural high-multiplicity regime: a large district assigning many students who share the same school eligibility, cardinal utilities and demographic-group memberships. Those exact profiles are the types; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the incoming cohort with profile \(t\). A district may have thousands or tens of thousands of students but only tens or hundreds of such profiles. Overlapping group memberships remain part of each type. This is most plausible where assignment utilities come from policy-defined features such as program eligibility or travel bands; if utilities are genuinely individualized, the support may be too large for this regime to help.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. The input is a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types, masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, schools \(j\) with per-capita capacities \(c_j\), and, for each type, its eligible schools \(E_t\), utilities \(u_{tj}\), and group-membership indicators \(a_{tk}\). Given a rational threshold \(q\), decide whether there are nonnegative masses \(x_{tj}\), zero for \(j\notin E_t\), such that
\(\sum_{j\in E_t}x_{tj}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\),
\(\sum_t x_{tj}\le c_j\) for every \(j\), and
\(U_k(x)=\sum_t a_{tk}\sum_{j\in E_t}u_{tj}x_{tj}\ge q\) for every group \(k\).
Equivalently, maximize \(\min_k U_k(x)\). Each individual still receives one school; \(x_{tj}\) describes how much of a homogeneous type cohort goes to school \(j\). It is not a fractional bundle for any student. Capacities scale with population, so a discrete instance with \(N\) students and capacities \(C_j\) gives \(c_j=C_j/N\). For Theorem 2’s total-utility threshold of \(1\), the corresponding normalized threshold is \(q=1/N\).

Theorem 2 is the strongest anchor. As printed, it says that when the number \(g\) of groups is part of the input, deciding whether the minimum utility received by any group is at least \(1\) is NP-complete even with only two schools. The paper proves this result here, by reduction from Set Cover. In the mirror, a type records the set of groups to which its students belong, along with their school options and utilities. The mass-transfer feasibility question above is an LP with \(\tau m\) variables and polynomially many constraints; it is solvable in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,g\), and the input bit length.

The reduction illustrates why this is a meaningful continuization. In its discrete form, each set is a student, and selecting students for the first school must cover every group within a capacity budget. In the mass model, a set type can be split across the two schools, turning that choice into fractional Set Cover. The original NP-hardness is therefore expected to dissolve. A natural follow-up is to quantify how well a continuous fair allocation can be realized by integral students at finite population sizes, especially how many extra seats suffice while preserving the group utilities.

Theorem 3 is a useful second anchor, though Theorem 2 remains my lead. As printed, Theorem 3 says that deciding whether an exactly proportional allocation exists is weakly NP-complete even with \(g=2\). The paper proves it here by reduction from Partition. In the continuous problem, compute each group’s standalone utility \(B_k\)—the maximum utility its mass could obtain using the school capacities if it were the only group—and ask whether an assignment mass \(x\) exists with the same assignment and capacity constraints and \(U_k(x)\ge B_k/2\) for both groups. The standalone benchmarks are themselves LP optima, so this proportionality question is also an LP feasibility problem.

In the paper’s Partition construction, for each item of weight \(x_i\), the two group-specific students can each be split across their private item school and the dummy school. Sending half of each student’s mass to the item school fills that school’s unit capacity and gives each group half the item’s utility. Doing this for every item meets both proportional shares without finding a partition. The discrete proof’s indivisible subset choice has disappeared. A further question is how the finite-population proportionality gap behaves as type counts grow, and when a continuous proportional allocation can be rounded with no capacity increase.

These anchors cover the paper’s max-min fairness and proportionality feasibility results, not its full range of results. In particular, they do not mirror Theorems 1, 4, or 5’s rounding guarantees, nor the rank-dominance application. Theorem 1 is relevant evidence for plausibility: the authors already optimize group utilities through a convex relaxation and then work hard to recover integral assignments while allowing small capacity violations. That makes a cohort-mass version recognizable as their assignment problem, with the same group utilities and school capacities.

The main weakness is that the continuous formulation is algebraically very close to the paper’s LP relaxation: aggregate the fractional assignment variables of identical students and one gets the mass variables above. The mirror promotes that relaxation from a tool for rounding into the population model itself, and interprets its variables as assignments of cohort mass, but it does not introduce a new optimization technique. That is still a credible Class A mirror: it asks the paper’s fairness questions for a society of repeated types, with per-capita school supplies, and makes the fractional answer the actual allocation of a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that these two anchors are not unanswered continuous problems: under the natural cohort model, they are exactly the fractional assignment problem the paper already formulates and solves. I would not argue that school assignment lacks a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A district can have many students sharing the same eligibility, utility vector, and group memberships. Those are complete types, and the paper’s group-utility objective does not depend on individual identities.

To make the mirror as strong as possible, let a type \(t\) encode its eligible schools \(E_t\), utilities \(u_{tj}\), and memberships \(a_{tk}\). Give it mass \(\mu_t\), and let \(x_{tj}\) be the mass assigned to school \(j\). For a finite cohort of \(N\) students with identical profiles within each type, normalize \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and capacities to \(c_j=C_j/N\). If \(y_{ij}\) is the paper’s fractional assignment for student \(i\), set
\[ x_{tj}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in t}y_{ij}. \]
This gives precisely the continuous assignment constraints, capacities, and group utilities. Conversely, any feasible \(x\) can be represented in the paper’s LP by giving every student of type \(t\) the same fractional assignment \(y_{ij}=x_{tj}/\mu_t\). A measurable continuum cohort can realize those masses by assigning each portion of a type to one school. Thus the match is exact at the level of feasible aggregate utility vectors, including overlapping groups and individualized-looking profiles once those profiles are included in the type.

For Theorem 2, the continuous max-min question is LP feasibility: add \(U_k\ge q\) for each group to those same assignment and capacity constraints. The paper explicitly identifies max-min fairness as a linear-constraint objective and presents polynomial-time convex-programming methods for its fractional assignment relaxation. The Set Cover reduction’s indivisible choices disappear under mass splitting, but the resulting LP is already the paper’s own relaxation. Calling it an assignment of cohort mass gives that relaxation a population interpretation; it does not create a new computational problem for ChoCo.

For Theorem 3, use the same LP after computing each group’s standalone benchmark \(B_k\) by a fractional assignment LP, and require \(U_k\ge B_k/2\) for the two groups. In the Partition construction, splitting each item’s cohort across its item school and the dummy school meets both shares fractionally. Again, that is a meaningful continuous interpretation, but its feasibility question is answered by the paper’s existing relaxation. Theorem 1’s rounding results also already address the natural finite-cohort bridge by recovering integral assignments while preserving the fractional utilities with bounded capacity violations. Sharper exact-capacity rounding questions may remain, but they are follow-up rounding questions rather than fresh mirrors of these hardness results.

So I would defeat both proposed anchors on the same ground: their best type model is sensible, yet its central computation is already present in this paper. The negative case is weaker if ChoCo counts making that equivalence explicit as a worthwhile formal dictionary result. But as a new continuous complexity contribution, the mirror appears to repackage the paper’s solved fractional assignment model.
