The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive case that meets the anchor rule: the paper has no named computational result—no theorem or proposition about a problem being in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

The closest result is Theorem 1, proved here: the ordinal gradual-bargaining solution is the unique solution satisfying Axioms 1–5. It is characterized by the path equation \(\phi_i'(t)=[n\,\partial f/\partial x_i(\phi(t))]^{-1}\) and the initial condition \(\phi(f(a))=a\). Theorem 3, also proved here, says that this path is compatible with both the Nash and Kalai–Smorodinsky solutions: its instantaneous utility gains are the limits of the gains from applying either one-shot solution to the next, vanishingly small agenda increment. Both are mathematical results about a finite set of bargainers. The equation is a characterization, not a computational-complexity result; Theorem 3 continuizes time, not the population.

The strongest follow-on I can see is a type-continuum ordinal bargaining path problem, but it is not anchored in a computational result of this paper. Suppose a large population of bargaining participants has \(\tau\) complete types, with positive rational masses \(\mu_r\) summing to \(1\). A type includes everything relevant to its members’ utilities and bargaining position. An agreement assigns each type a common per-capita utility \(x_r\). Let the agenda be \(F:\mathbb R^\tau\to\mathbb R\), with feasible profiles at time \(t\) given by \(F(x)\le t\); assume \(F\) is continuously differentiable, has positive partial derivatives, and has locally Lipschitz gradient. Given a rational status quo \(a\) and a terminal time \(T>F(a)\), the task is to compute the ordinal path’s value at \(T\), to a specified accuracy \(\epsilon\), where
\[ x(F(a))=a,\qquad \dot{x}_r(t)=\frac{\mu_r}{\partial_r F(x(t))}. \]
The output is the type-level utility profile along the bargaining path, not an optimizer for a separate scalar objective.

There is a concrete bridge to the paper’s model: if \(n_r\) identical bargainers form each group, the agenda is symmetric within groups, and \(\mu_r=n_r/n\), aggregating the paper’s path equation over a group gives exactly \(\dot{x}_r=\mu_r/\partial_rF(x)\). So this is a recognizable replication-based extension of the ordinal solution. A plausible regime would be a large, platform-mediated negotiation over a multi-issue compact, with many participants but relatively few stable utility and bargaining types.

For explicitly represented, well-conditioned agendas, I would expect approximate path computation to be numerically tractable; the precise complexity would depend on how \(F\) is represented and how its regularity bounds are supplied. That expectation is a new computational question, not a result established by this paper. It raises further questions about dependence on \(\tau\), finite-population approximation, and whether succinct agenda representations introduce hardness.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s bargainers may be organizations or a small committee, where replicating participants is not a natural regime. Even if a continuum of participants is convincing in a particular application, the paper supplies no computational anchor for this proposed problem. So the mirror is a plausible research extension of its ordinal path, but I would not present it as an anchored continuous-complexity case for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s admission settles the anchor test: neither Theorem 1 nor Theorem 3 states a computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes a solution axiomatically and gives its differential equation. Theorem 3 relates that path to limits of one-shot bargaining steps. Neither identifies an encoded computational problem or classifies its complexity.

The strongest version of the proposed population mirror is coherent. Suppose a large negotiation has finitely many complete participant types, with identical members of each type receiving the same per-capita utility. Restricting the original agenda to profiles that are equal within each type gives the weighted equation \(\dot{x}_r(t)=\mu_r/\partial_rF(x(t))\). That is a sensible extension for some large, platform-mediated negotiations.

To turn it into a complexity question, though, one must choose how \(F\) is represented, what access an algorithm gets to its derivatives, and what regularity bounds are supplied. The paper specifies arbitrary smooth agendas but supplies no finite input model. A computational result would therefore depend on new choices about the agenda encoding and numerical task. The proposed type-level path may be worth studying on its own; the paper provides no computational result for it to mirror.

Theorem 3 admits a better follow-on: study how a finite population of each type approximates the type-level path. Its result concerns the limit of shrinking time steps, while the population remains finite. Population approximation would be a new convergence question. It would not turn the theorem into a complexity result about computation over a population distribution.

A large-participant scenario can make the type model plausible, but plausibility does not supply the missing computational anchor. The negative case is strong under ChoCo’s mirror criterion. It is weaker as a universal claim about research value: a carefully specified computational study of type-level bargaining could be worthwhile, but it would be a new computational agenda built from this paper’s mathematics, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its computational findings.
