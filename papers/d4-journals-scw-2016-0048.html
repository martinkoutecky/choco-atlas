What the mirror covers

The proposed calculation uses the paper’s principal-eigenvector scoring function, but does not mirror it as a computational result; it leaves the zero-sum scoring results and the group-and-individual characterization unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population mirror is an unanchored one: take voter types to be complete rankings of the alternatives, with a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a support of \(\tau\) rankings. Set \(A_\mu(i,j)=\sum_{t:\,i\succ_t j}\mu_t\) for \(i\ne j\). For societies where this relation graph is strongly connected, the question is to output a rational vector within \(\varepsilon\) of the normalized principal right eigenvector \(R(A_\mu)\). The vector is the solution; there is no separate optimization objective. A large electorate with relatively few distinct ballots is a plausible regime, and the paper explicitly discusses voter counts as edge weights and count-versus-percentage scale invariance. The natural expectation is tractability.

But this is not a qualifying case under your anchor rule. The paper has no numbered result asserting that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. Proposition 1, proved here, says that \(R(A)\) is the unique scoring function satisfying axioms (A1)–(A6) on primitive matrices. Proposition 6, also proved here, characterizes \(R(A^\top A)\) for the group-and-individual method. Those are axiomatic characterizations, not computational results. Theorem 1 is a Perron–Frobenius result cited from elsewhere, not a complexity result of this paper.

The candidate mirror is plausible as a new computational question about a population-derived input, but it does not have a named computational result in the paper to anchor it, and the authors already allow real-valued relation weights. So the honest conclusion is that I cannot make a compliant positive case from this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no named computational result to continuize. Propositions 1 and 6 characterize scoring functions axiomatically; Proposition 4 does the same for the zero-sum score. Theorem 1 is an imported Perron–Frobenius result. Turning those characterizations into axioms on a distribution over voter types would continuize an axiomatic question, which is outside ChoCo’s scope.

The proposed voter-distribution model is coherent, and the paper itself invites interpreting relation weights as voter counts or percentages. But once \(\mu\) is given, its pairwise matrix
\[ A_\mu(i,j)=\sum_{t:\,i\succ_t j}\mu_t \]
can be formed from the \(\tau\) supported rankings in \(O(\tau m^2)\) time. Computing \(R(A_\mu)\) then reduces to the ordinary principal-eigenvector problem on an \(m\times m\) rational matrix. The population is continuous in the input model, but this does not expose a new complexity landscape: there are no exponentially many transfer variables or pricing problem, and the paper states no computational question whose complexity this calculation would mirror. The same applies to computing the zero-sum score from \(R(A_\mu)\) and \(L(A_\mu)\).

Proposition 6 offers a stronger possible adaptation: represent agents by types, then compute group and individual centralities from a mass-weighted Gram matrix. That is well-defined when agents of a type are genuinely interchangeable, but the central computation still reduces to a finite eigenvector problem—on alternatives or on the type support. For HITS-style networks, where nodes are individual pages with their own link neighborhoods, a large population of interchangeable nodes would require a special block structure. Such a model may be worth studying, but it is not a computational result of this paper.

One could add a more ambitious question: how much voter mass must move between ranking types to change the eigenvector winner? That may produce a nontrivial continuous optimization problem. But it adds manipulation or control to a paper that studies neither, so it has no discrete computational result here to mirror. Its complexity would arise from the newly added strategic problem, not from continuizing one of the paper’s results.

I can make a strong case that this paper is a poor ChoCo anchor: its results are axiomatic, and its closest computational readings appear to be routine spectral calculations. I cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous project can be built from it. The distribution-over-rankings model is sensible and recognizable from the paper’s voting discussion; the negative case is strongest on programme fit, weaker on whether that adjacent problem could be worth pursuing.
