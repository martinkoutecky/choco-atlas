The continuous mirror question

Given a finite directed unsigned graph \(H=(T,A)\) with maximum indegree at most \(6\), an initial role state \(y:T\to\{-1,+1\}\), and target role \(u\in T\), form the measured society \(X=T\times[0,1]\) with \(\nu(\{t\}\times B)=|B|/|T|\), and arcs \((s,q)\to(t,q)\) whenever \((s,t)\in A\). Under synchronous \(d_{(3,4)}\), with every member of role \(t\) starting at \(y(t)\), decide whether there is an integer \(k\ge 0\) such that \(\omega_k(u,q)=+1\) for almost every \(q\in[0,1]\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of synchronized copies of \(H\): each \(t\in T\) is a role cohort of mass \(1/|T|\), and a measure-preserving \(q\)-matching carries each module arc. Opinions remain constant within each cohort, and the decision asks whether the target cohort ever becomes positive under the paper's fixed local rule.

The objection that survived

The role-mass vector alone does not encode the matched local neighborhoods, so this mirror depends on treating the repeated-module graphing as an admissible network co-structure.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's hardness result and Theorem 2's polynomial trajectory bound; it does not separately cover Theorem 1 or Corollaries 1–2.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a replicated network-role population. It keeps the paper’s local, synchronous opinion rule and discrete time intact, while making the population continuous. That fits the paper particularly well: its subject is how many individuals change state through local interactions, and its central computational question is whether a designated opinion configuration is ever reached.

Let \(T\) be a finite set of roles in a network module, with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). A role is a complete description of an agent’s position in the module: its signed incoming and outgoing links and its initial opinion. The society is \(X=\bigsqcup_{t\in T}\{t\}\times[0,1]\), with measure \(\nu(\{t\}\times B)=\mu_t|B|\). For each module edge \(s\to t\), connect \((s,q)\) to \((t,q)\) for every \(q\in[0,1]\). Thus the population is a continuum of repeated modules, with type \(t\) occupying mass \(\mu_t\). Each agent has finitely many neighbors, just as in the paper.

Give every member of role \(t\) the same initial opinion \(y_t\). At each integer time, each agent updates according to the paper’s underpopulation rule, using the opinions of their neighbors in that module. Opinions remain constant within each role, so the evolving society is described by a vector of \(|T|\) binary opinions. The decision witness is a time \(k\): does a designated set of roles \(U\) ever have opinion \(+1\) simultaneously? A “yes” solution can include such a \(k\); a “no” answer follows once the role-state vector repeats. There is no optimization objective in the paper’s reachability problem.

The many-agent regime is a large network repeated across branches, sites, or cohorts: each module has the same standardized roles and interactions, while the number of modules is much larger than the number of roles. Module identity does not affect the update rule, so agents in the same role are indistinguishable for this problem. This makes mass meaningful as a population share, even though the paper’s fixed number of local contacts means mass does not change the threshold calculation.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that U-RT is PSPACE-complete even for the fixed dynamics \(d(3,4)\), unsigned directed graphs of maximum indegree \(6\), and target sets of size \(1\). The paper proves this by simulating a polynomial-space Turing machine in the graph’s evolution.

The corresponding continuous problem is Cohort U-RT: given the role masses, a directed unsigned module \(H\), a role-constant initial opinion vector, and a target role \(u\), decide whether the entire positive-mass cohort of role \(u\) ever has opinion \(+1\) under \(d(3,4)\). This is the strongest mirror because it preserves the paper’s underlying reachability question: neither the rule nor the network interaction is replaced by an easier aggregate process. The theorem’s hard graph becomes the module \(H\); its continuum lift repeats that graph across the population. Using uniform masses \(\mu_t=1/|T|\), the answer is exactly the same on every copy. The problem remains PSPACE-complete in the role-graph input size, so this is a Class B mirror: the discrete hardness transfers. The authors could plausibly recognize it as their U-RT problem applied to a high-multiplicity, modular network regime.

A second, narrower anchor gives the other side of the paper’s boundary. Theorem 2, proved here, says that for every structurally balanced signed undirected graph \(G=(V,E,\lambda)\), initial opinion configuration, and underpopulation rule \(d(c_+,c_-)\), the number of distinct configurations in the evolution is \(O(|E|+|V|\Delta^4)\); more precisely, it is less than
\[ 4|E|+2|V|+2|V|(2\Delta+3)(2\Delta+4)^3+2. \]
The proof reduces to the symmetric-dynamics case and uses a bound cited from earlier work. Its continuous mirror is Cohort U-RT restricted to structurally balanced undirected modules: given the same role-constant initial state and target roles, decide whether they are simultaneously positive at some time. Theorem 2 bounds the trajectory length polynomially in the module size, so this restricted reachability problem is in P. This is a tractable mirror, distinct from the directed hardness case.

The further questions are whether the tractability boundary extends to broader classes of continuous network populations, whether restrictions on the role graph yield useful algorithms beyond structural balance, and what changes when agents within a role may start with different opinions.

The weakest point is that this construction is exact through replication: the continuous population adds many agents, but the computation still takes place in the finite role graph, and the masses do not affect the evolution. The hard module itself may be artificial as a social scenario. So this is a credible mirror for a high-multiplicity regime of the paper’s problem, not evidence that continuity dissolves its hardness or that the paper’s dynamics benefit from continuous-optimization tools.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the hard anchor, and the role-replication construction is the right version to test. Its weakness is that the population measure does no computational work. Every module has the same graph \(H\), the same initial role opinions, and the same evolution under \(d(3,4)\). So asking whether the positive-mass cohort for role \(u\) ever turns positive is exactly asking whether node \(u\) does so in one copy of \(H\).

That leaves a modeling problem beyond the redundant mass parameter. An agent’s update depends on its particular neighbors. A type distribution over roles does not specify which individual copies are linked; the construction supplies that information through the shared module coordinate \(q\). If copies are synchronized, \(q\) can be discarded and the question reduces to finite-graph U-RT. If copies can differ, role-level states no longer determine updates: one must retain the module structure or the full state of each module. The continuous input then describes a distribution of network instances, rather than the paper’s single network evolving under its rule. Theorem 3 does not supply a result about that distributional problem.

Theorem 2 has the same limitation. In identical copies of a structurally balanced module, the population’s trajectory is just the module’s finite trajectory repeated across \(q\). The polynomial bound transfers, and one can compute aggregate statistics such as the positive mass at each step by inspecting that trajectory. But the bound concerns the evolution of the module’s graph; changing the masses does not change it. The stronger version, with modules starting in different configurations, again needs a distribution over whole module states. That is a coherent question, but it changes the unit whose distribution is being studied.

A sparse continuum network could preserve the paper’s finite local neighborhoods, but it needs an explicit graphing or matching structure to say who influences whom. A type-mass vector alone cannot encode it. A dense population limit has a different problem: the fixed thresholds \(c_+\) and \(c_-\) become negligible when neighborhoods grow with population size. Scaling them with degree would define a different dynamics. These alternatives expose the gap between a continuous population description and the paper’s local graph computation; they do not rule out every networked population model.

The paper plainly has computational results to mirror: Theorem 3 and Theorem 2 are named complexity and trajectory results. Neither is already answered by the analytic mean-field literature described in the paper. The objection here is about whether the proposed lift earns its place in this programme, rather than about missing results or prior art.

I cannot honestly claim to have eliminated Theorem 3’s mirror. Repeated modular populations are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and under the programme’s definition of type, the construction appears admissible. The best case against is that it adds a continuum of copies without making population mass operative; that may make it a low-yield ChoCo contribution. But the stated standard says that a well-posed mirror can stand even when its answer is unchanged, so this objection does not defeat the anchor. The negative case is therefore weak, and the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists is not supported by this paper.
