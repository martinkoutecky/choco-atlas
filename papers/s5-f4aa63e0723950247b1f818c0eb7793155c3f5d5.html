What the mirror covers

The proposed case names Theorems 12 and 13, about stability and uniqueness of the complete criminal network; it supplies no mirror for these or for the paper's other results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive lead is the criminal-network application. Theorem 13, proved in this paper, states that for criminal networks with \(n\) players, \(\{g^N\}\) is the unique level-\((n-1)\) farsightedly stable set. Theorem 12, also proved here, says that \(\{g^N\}\) is a level-\(K\) farsightedly stable set for every \(K\ge n-1\). These give a sharp, closed-form prediction for that model.

They are not, however, named complexity results: neither theorem establishes that a specified computational problem is in P, hard, or belongs to a parameterized class. The paper’s claim of “tractability” concerns the stability concept and its application to this model; it does not give a running-time analysis. So these theorems are near-misses, not valid anchors under the stated requirement. I can’t honestly supply a prover-ready continuous problem *per anchor* when there is no qualifying anchor.

A plausible direction for a separate extension would be a large, anonymous population of criminals sharing the same loot and risk technology, represented by a continuum network. But the paper models deviations as single bilateral link changes between identified players, and its result depends on a horizon of \(n-1\), which grows with the population. A continuum version would need to define what counts as a positive-mass link deviation and how farsightedness scales; those choices could change the underlying problem. That is the weakest point, and it is decisive here: this paper suggests a possible population-level extension, but does not provide a named computational result that can support the requested mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The criminal-network results are the strongest candidates, but Theorems 12 and 13 establish stability predictions, not computational complexity. They do not give an algorithm or a complexity bound for computing a level-\(K\) stable set. Theorem 1 proves existence, and Theorem 3 characterizes the level-1 set; neither supplies such a bound either. One could pose “compute a stable set” as a new problem, but that would be a computational question built on this paper, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in it. The paper’s use of “tractability” concerns the economic concept and its application, not running time.

The proposed large population of identical criminals also runs into a more basic modeling problem. The payoff and reachability conditions depend on the network’s components and on which individuals have maximal degree. A distribution over degrees does not determine those features: for example, a cycle and two disjoint cycles can have the same degree for every player but different components. A full graphon can encode adjacency, but changing one link between two individuals changes it on a set of measure zero. That erases the bilateral deviations from which the paper builds its improving paths. Giving a positive-mass block the ability to form or sever links would preserve visible changes, but it replaces pairwise moves with coalitional ones. The authors mention that kind of modification, so it is a plausible research direction; it is still a new network-formation problem, not a population-distribution version of the stated theorems.

The criminal payoff rule has a further limit issue. When a positive fraction of a group ties for maximal degree, each receives only a vanishing share of the fixed loot as the group grows. If instead a few leaders retain positive individual shares, they have zero mass in an atomless population. Scaling the loot or preserving exceptional agents could address this, but each choice changes what the continuum represents. Likewise, the \(n-1\) farsightedness threshold counts individual link moves; it does not directly give a finite population-scale horizon for mass changes.

So I would reject Theorems 12 and 13 as anchors for ChoCo, and the proposed criminal extension does not rescue them: it needs a new state space, deviation rule, and computational problem. The honest limit of this negative case is that I cannot rule out a worthwhile graphon or block-deviation complexity project. I can say that this paper does not yet identify one, and its named results do not give the requested computational foothold.
