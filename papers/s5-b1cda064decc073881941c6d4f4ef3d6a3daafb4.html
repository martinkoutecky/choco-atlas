The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(A\), a finite support \(\mathcal{T}\) of voter types, and rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), where each type \(t\) specifies a subset \(S_t\subseteq A\) and a strict order \(\pi_t\) on \(S_t\), find a total ranking \(\sigma\) of \(A\) minimizing \(\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mu_t d_t(\sigma)\), where \(d_t(\sigma)\) counts the pairs in \(S_t\) whose order under \(\sigma\) disagrees with \(\pi_t\).

The model it lives in

A society is a finite distribution over complete voter types, each encoding the alternatives compared and their order; the decision variable is a total candidate ranking, and the objective is expected pairwise disagreement. For complete ballots this is Kemeny aggregation with normalized population masses.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the binary ordinal Kemeny aggregation formulations in Theorem 7.1, including partial comparisons. It leaves aside the cardinal Hodge projection, its robust and sparse variants, and the PageRank and currency applications.

Open questions for a prover

  • What approximation guarantees are achievable for expected pairwise disagreement with partial ballots, as functions of the number of candidates, support size, and input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s ordinal Kemeny problem. Its population setting is a large platform or repeated preference panel over a fixed catalog: many people submit pairwise ratings, and a much smaller number of preference and coverage patterns recur. A type is the complete information relevant to this problem: which alternatives that cohort compared, and the strict order it gave those alternatives. Its mass is the fraction of the population with that type. For example, millions of users might fall into a few recurring cohorts, each rating a standard subset of the catalog in the same order.

The named anchor is Theorem 7.1, proved in this paper. For binary pairwise data, it shows that four formulations are equivalent: weighted least squares over total rankings represented by sign differences; maximizing correlation with the aggregated pairwise data over the convex hull of permutation rankings; weighted \(l_1\)-minimization over the transitive pairwise region; and minimum feedback arc set on the directed graph of weighted pairwise margins. The theorem does not itself state a complexity class. Immediately afterward, the paper says these problems are NP-hard because minimum feedback arc set is NP-hard; it also cites Dwork et al. for Kemeny hardness with at least four voters. So the equivalence is proved here, while the hardness is a consequence of known hardness, not a new hardness proof in the theorem.

I would call the mirror Distributional Kemeny Aggregation. An instance consists of a candidate set \(A\), a finite support \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\) types, and binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), encoded in total bit length \(L\). Each type \(t\) specifies a subset \(S_t\subseteq A\) and a strict total order \(\pi_t\) on that subset. The question is to find a total ranking \(\sigma\) of \(A\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t\,d_t(\sigma), \]
where \(d_t(\sigma)\) is the number of pairs in \(S_t\) on which \(\sigma\) disagrees with \(\pi_t\). A solution is any minimizing permutation. Equivalently, the decision version asks whether the minimum is at most a given rational threshold \(B\).

This is the continuous-population form of the paper’s Kemeny question: choose a global ranking minimizing the population’s pairwise disagreement. A finite electorate with \(N\) voters maps directly to masses \(\mu_t=n_t/N\); the total discrete Kemeny cost is \(N\) times the displayed objective, so the optimizing rankings coincide. The mirror also allows partial ballots, matching the paper’s allowance for missing pairwise comparisons. The convex hull in Theorem 7.1 is over *output rankings*; the \(\mu_t\) here describe the *input population*. Thus this mirror continuizes the society, not the outcome space.

I expect hardness to transfer, placing this mirror in Class B. The complete-ballot subcase is ordinary Kemeny aggregation. The paper gives NP-hardness for at least four voters; duplicating each of four ballots any number of times preserves their population fractions and the optimal ranking. Thus a large population and small support do not remove the hardness: the difficult combinatorics live in the number of alternatives. Under the usual binary encoding, the threshold decision version is NP-complete, since a proposed permutation can be checked in polynomial time. In particular, the cited four-voter hardness means that treating \(\tau\) as small—even bounded by four—does not by itself yield a polynomial-time algorithm unless \(P=NP\).

This case covers the binary ordinal Kemeny result in Theorem 7.1, not the paper’s broader cardinal Hodge projection, its robust \(l_1\) variants, or its PageRank application. Further questions include whether restricted comparison graphs or restricted type patterns make exact aggregation tractable, and what approximation guarantees are possible for expected Kendall disagreement.

The weak point is the regime’s fit to the paper’s modern-data motivation. Exact repeated ranking and coverage patterns may be rare when users rate different subsets of a large catalog, and the paper’s central fast method uses cardinal data and real-valued gradient rankings rather than Kemeny’s permutation output. This mirror is therefore a strong match to an explicit, computationally hard subproblem in the paper, not to its full statistical-ranking agenda. The narrowness is also why I find it credible: the authors explicitly formulate Kemeny as their problem in Section 7.1, and repeated cohorts in standardized comparison tasks provide a sensible high-multiplicity setting for that specific problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is about the platform story. A voter type must include both the alternatives a person compared and their order on those alternatives. For movie ratings, exact repetitions of that combined pattern may be rare, so the support could grow almost as fast as the user population. That would make the proposed high multiplicity regime a poor fit for the paper’s motivating data.

But a stronger version of the mirror survives: repeated elections or preference panels over a fixed slate, where large cohorts submit the same complete ballot. Given type masses \(\mu_t\), ask for a total ranking minimizing expected pairwise disagreement,
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\,d_K(\sigma,\pi_t). \]
For a finite electorate with counts \(n_t\) among \(N\) voters, setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) preserves the minimizers. Even four recurring ballot types can encode the cited Kemeny hardness, and duplicating those ballots produces arbitrarily large populations. That is a plausible population regime, and the problem’s output depends on the alternatives’ ranking, not on voter identities. Hardness driven by the number of alternatives is a Class B result under the programme’s rules, not a reason to reject the question.

Theorem 7.1 gives an optimization equivalence; the paper then invokes known NP-hardness for the equivalent feedback arc set problem. Its convex hull \(\mathcal K_1\) makes the *output-ranking* formulation continuous, but it does not make the voter population continuous. Likewise, the paper’s aggregation into pairwise weights may make the mirror’s answer a normalized restatement, but that alone does not rule out the population-distribution question.

So the weak point is the fit to the paper’s movie-data example, not the best version of the mirror. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor under the stated gate: the repeated-ballot version is sensible, well-posed, and survives high multiplicity.
