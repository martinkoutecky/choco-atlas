What the mirror covers

The mass formulation carries over the \(q\)-fixed-majority threshold and can express the paper’s \(q\)-bottom-majority claims; these remain axiomatic results, with no computational complexity analysis.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is the paper’s \(q\)-fixed-majority result. The natural continuous society has complete rankings as types: for a fixed candidate set \(A\), let \(\mu\) be a rational distribution over rankings in \(P(A)\). The mass \(\mu_t\) represents the fraction of the electorate with ranking \(t\). For a committee \(W\) of size \(k\), define its score as \(\sum_t \mu_t f_k(r(t,W))\); the outcome is a maximum-score committee. This fits a large election with millions of voters but a relatively small number \(\tau\) of distinct ballots.

The closest named result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that a weakly separable committee scoring rule with scoring vector \(\alpha\) satisfies the \(q\)-fixed-majority property for committee size \(k\) exactly when
\[ q>\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)+\gamma_k(\alpha)}, \]
where \(\Gamma(\alpha)=\alpha_1-\alpha_m\) and \(\gamma_k(\alpha)=\alpha_k-\alpha_{k+1}\). In the continuous model, the same score comparisons replace voter counts with masses, so the threshold carries over directly. This is a plausible population mirror: the paper’s condition is explicitly about a proportion of voters, and its scores aggregate additively over voters.

A concrete computational question suggested by that mirror is: given rational \(m,k,\alpha,q\), does there exist a distribution \(\mu\) and a committee \(W\) such that at least \(q\) mass ranks \(W\) in the top \(k\) positions, but \(W\) is not the unique winning committee? One could ask for a yes/no answer or a rational distribution witnessing failure. For the weakly separable rules covered by Theorem 1, the theorem gives the answer by comparing \(q\) with its threshold; its proof also supplies a two-ranking witness when the threshold condition fails.

But Theorem 1 is not a named computational-complexity result: it characterizes an axiomatic property. The paper gives no numbered result asserting that a problem is in \(P\), hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. The witness-search question is a computational reformulation suggested here, not a problem whose complexity the authors study. Thus it is a near-miss rather than a qualifying anchor under the programme’s criteria. The continuous population formulation is coherent and likely recognizable to the authors, but its result remains an axiomatic characterization, precisely the kind of continuization the programme excludes. There are no eligible computational anchors in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive weakness is eligibility. Theorem 1 characterizes when a rule satisfies an axiom; the paper gives no theorem about an algorithm, computational complexity, or optimization problem. The \(q\)-fixed-majority property is a sensible population question, and the proposed ranking distribution is a natural model. Those facts establish that the axiom can be stated over a continuous society. They do not supply a computational result to mirror.

The proponent’s witness-search question is the strongest repair: given a rule and \(q\), find a society where a committee has \(q\) support but loses uniqueness. That is a computational problem one could study, but it is the negation of the axiom turned into a search task. Theorem 1’s two-ranking construction is a proof witness, not an algorithmic result. The paper never makes finding such witnesses, or deciding whether they exist, its subject.

A stronger extension would ask for the exact \(q\)-threshold for arbitrary committee scoring functions, since the paper leaves that threshold open outside the separable case. This would be a natural follow-up to its mathematics. Yet it remains a threshold question about an axiom. The paper already quantifies over electorates of every finite size and expresses support as a proportion; every rational mass distribution has a common denominator and corresponds to one of those profiles. This does not make the continuous model defective. It means the proposed extension needs a new computational input and task before it becomes a ChoCo complexity question.

One could add a task such as finding the minimum mass transfer needed to elect a target committee. That could be a worthwhile continuous-population problem, but the paper supplies no transfer operation, costs, or target-election objective. It would be a new project using the rules studied here, not a mirror of a result in this paper. So the negative case is strong on the paper’s lack of eligible anchors. It is weaker as a universal claim: a useful adjacent computational project may exist, but none of the proposed routes establishes a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
