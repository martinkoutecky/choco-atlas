The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are Propositions 1–5 and Lemma 1, and they concern equilibrium existence, turnout, welfare, and policy functions. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. So under the anchor rule, there are no anchors for which I can honestly provide a continuous problem.

The closest result—and the strongest basis for a research extension—is Proposition 2, proved here. It says that if the information-quality distribution has a log-concave density, then as the Poisson electorate grows, every sequence of equilibrium quality thresholds converges to a unique limit \(\tau_1^*\). That limit is positive, is below \(1\) except when \(p=0\) and the policy is purely marginal, decreases as the partisan share \(p\) rises, and increases when the information distribution improves in the monotone likelihood ratio order. This is a result about a large-electorate equilibrium, not about the complexity of computing one.

A plausible *new* computational question growing directly from Proposition 2 would be High-Multiplicity Turnout Equilibrium. The instance would specify a two-state common-interest election, a rational partisan mass \(p\) on each side, and rational masses \(\alpha_i\) of independent voters with signal qualities \(q_i\), for \(i=1,\ldots,\tau\). Each independent’s signal identifies the superior party with probability \((1+q_i)/2\). Given the paper’s policy rule with a positive marginal-vote weight \(\eta\), the decision variables are \(\sigma_i\in[0,1]\): the fraction of quality-\(q_i\) voters who vote for their signal rather than abstain. A solution is a symmetric limiting equilibrium strategy vector, with each \(\sigma_i\) optimal against the aggregate vote masses. One precise way to define the limit is to take each type’s expected payoff gain \(D_n(q_i;\sigma)\) from voting rather than abstaining in the corresponding Poisson election and use the sign of \(\lim_{n\to\infty} nD_n(q_i;\sigma)\) for its best-response condition. The output could include the resulting limiting turnout and expected policy.

The regime is credible: a large electorate can have many voters but few distinct partisan, information-quality, and signal classes. Members of a type share the information accuracy and relevant preferences; the signal realization distinguishes the two signal types. The paper’s authors would recognize the question as computing the large-electorate turnout equilibrium their model studies. I would expect tractability under restrictions preserving the threshold structure and suitable regularity of the quality distribution, but that is a conjecture for this computational extension, not a result of the paper. The paper’s \(\tau_1^*\) characterization provides a promising scalar route in its continuous-quality setting.

The weak point is also the reason this cannot serve as a compliant mirror: the paper already derives the large-electorate threshold analytically, while the proposed question adds a computational task the authors do not study. Moreover, an exact atomless population erases an individual’s pivotal influence; retaining the marginal incentive requires defining it as a normalized limit of finite-population payoff changes. This is a sensible extension of their equilibrium analysis, but it does not turn any of their named results into a computational complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchor rule: this paper has no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Propositions 1–5 and Lemma 1 establish equilibrium properties, asymptotic behavior, and welfare results. Proposition 2 is the closest candidate, but it characterizes a limiting equilibrium threshold and its comparative statics; it does not ask for an algorithm or classify computational complexity. That leaves no qualifying result to mirror.

The proposed finite-type turnout problem is a reasonable extension. Large electorates can contain many voters in each information class, and the paper itself studies a Poisson population and a large-election limit. I would not reject that regime on multiplicity grounds. But computing an equilibrium strategy vector adds a computational task to the paper. It does not turn Proposition 2 into a computational result. The paper’s threshold characterization already supplies the analytical object; the extension would ask for an algorithm to evaluate it.

There is also an encoding choice the proposal has to add. Proposition 2 assumes a continuous distribution \(F\) with a log-concave density, but the paper gives no finite representation of arbitrary such distributions. A finite rational list of quality masses is a natural input for computation, yet it changes those assumptions. Keeping continuous \(F\) instead requires choosing a parametric representation, oracle, or approximation rule. Likewise, the proposed \(\lim_{n\to\infty} nD_n\) retains individual incentives by rescaling payoffs that vanish in the continuum limit. That is a defensible bridge from the finite game, but it confirms that the computational problem needs extra modeling choices.

So the negative case is strong at the anchor gate and weaker as a claim that no worthwhile project could be built. An algorithmic study of limiting turnout might interest the authors as a new extension. The material here does not establish that it is a continuous computational mirror of a named result, and I would not claim that every possible equilibrium-computation project is unworthy.
