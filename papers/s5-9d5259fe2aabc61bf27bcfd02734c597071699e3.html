What the mirror covers

The proposed radius optimization concerns the voter-side objective behind Theorems 3.6 and 3.8; it does not cover Proposition 3.10 or the simulation comparisons of electoral rules. No computational result in the paper anchors it.

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest case for a continuous mirror is limited by the paper’s results: it contains no named computational complexity result. Proposition 3.5 and Theorems 3.6 and 3.8, with Corollaries 3.7 and 3.11 and Proposition 3.10, establish dynamical identities and bounds. None classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard, or gives a computational formulation that the paper then solves. So there is no qualifying anchor under your rules.

The closest analytic anchor is Theorem 3.6, proved here. Under its bounded-space, voter-attraction, and noise assumptions, it proves
\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[D^{(t+1)}\mid\mathcal{F}_t\right] \le q_t^2D^{(t)}+\sigma_\varepsilon^2, \qquad q_t=1-\eta_{\min}+L_\eta R_t. \]
Thus a smaller winner radius gives a tighter one-step contraction bound. This is a useful basis for a computational question, but it is not itself a complexity result.

If analytic anchors were acceptable, my strongest candidate would be Continuous Winner-Radius Optimization. A type is a voter’s policy position and, if voters use different response or noise mechanisms, the parameters specifying that behavior. In the simplest case, all voters share the mechanism, so only position distinguishes types. An instance gives a rational policy space \(\Omega\), a finite support of \(\tau\) rational positions \(x_r\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_r\) summing to \(1\), and the rule’s feasible set of winners \(\mathcal W\). The question is to return an \(\varepsilon\)-approximate minimizer \(\hat w\in\mathcal W\) of
\[ R_\mu(w)=\max_{r:\,\mu_r>0}\|x_r-w\|, \]
with \(R_\mu(\hat w)\le \min_{w\in\mathcal W}R_\mu(w)+\varepsilon\). The solution is the winner and its radius; Theorem 3.6 then gives the corresponding contraction certificate \(q=1-\eta_{\min}+L_\eta R_\mu(\hat w)\). The paper’s Section 4.4 explicitly studies a centrality oracle minimizing this radius, so the authors could plausibly recognize the question. For Euclidean distance and convex \(\mathcal W\), it is a convex minimax-location problem, expressible as a second-order cone program; I would expect it to be tractable.

The regime is a large electorate whose members fall into a modest number of cohorts with shared positions and response parameters—for example, a survey population grouped into policy-position and behavior types. Mass is cohort share, and the number of people can be much larger than \(\tau\). This mirrors the voter-side results, especially Theorem 3.6 and the radius-envelope comparison in Theorem 3.8. It does not cover the candidate-side Proposition 3.10.

The weakest point is also why this remains only a candidate mirror: the proposed optimization minimizes a geometric bound, while the paper’s named results analyze dynamics rather than computational complexity. Moreover, mass values affect disagreement \(D\) but not the maximum-radius objective itself. The paper’s own oracle comparison shows that minimizing radius and minimizing next-round disagreement are distinct. A useful follow-up would therefore be to formulate and classify the mass-sensitive problem of minimizing continuous-society next-round disagreement. That is a plausible research direction, but it is not an anchor-backed case from this paper under the stated standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point is also the main obstacle: this paper states no computational problem whose complexity or algorithms a continuous mirror could classify. Theorem 3.6 gives a one-step bound; Theorem 3.8 propagates that bound; Proposition 3.10 gives a candidate-side analogue. None asks for an algorithm or studies its running time. The paper’s oracle comparison is the closest computational object, but it is a simulation benchmark, not a result about computing an electoral rule. So the proposed mirror would add a new algorithmic question to the paper rather than continue one of its computational results.

A continuous population of voters grouped by policy position and behavior is sensible here. But the proposed winner-radius problem is a poor computational anchor. In the paper’s oracle, the winner can be any point in the policy space, independently of the candidate slate and electoral rule. The task is therefore geometric center-finding. Restore the rules the paper actually compares, and winner-take-all rules offer at most \(K\) outcomes to check; the Fractional rule specifies one outcome. Allow every point in \(\operatorname{conv}(C)\) or \(\Omega\), and the problem becomes a policy-design oracle rather than computing those rules. Also, \(R_\mu(w)=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\sim\mu}\|x-w\|\) depends on which positions occur, not their masses: a tiny positive cohort can determine the objective. That does not invalidate the metric, but it leaves the proposed computation largely untouched by continuization.

The stronger repair is to optimize the mass-weighted next-round disagreement. For a shared response function \(g\), define \(F_w(x)=(1-g(\|x-w\|))x+g(\|x-w\|)w\) and minimize \(\int\|F_w(x)-\int F_w\,d\mu\|^2\,d\mu\). This uses the population distribution in a substantive way, and Section 4.4 gives it a recognizable foothold in the depolarization oracle. But over the actual candidate slate it reduces to evaluating finitely many options. Over all of \(\Omega\), it again selects a hypothetical policy directly, not a winner under one of the studied rules; a complexity question would also need a computational representation for \(g\), the feasible region, and any noise. That could be a worthwhile follow-up in its own right, but it is not a computational result this paper leaves unresolved.

The negative case is not airtight. A cohort model is plausible, and the depolarization oracle is close enough to the paper’s own framing that its authors might recognize an algorithmic version as their problem. The strongest objection is narrower: the paper supplies analytic bounds and simulation objectives, not a computational question to mirror. If those are sufficient anchors for ChoCo, the proponent’s case remains plausible.
