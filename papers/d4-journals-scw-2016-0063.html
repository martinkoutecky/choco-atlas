What the mirror covers

Proposition 2 covers convex strategyproofness of PS; the paper also extends this result to generalized PS and establishes convex envy-freeness of random serial dictatorship in Proposition 3. The geometric characterization and these incentive and envy results are not computational claims.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result of the required kind: no theorem or proposition establishes a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. Theorem 1 is a polyhedral separation theorem cited from McLennan. Propositions 1–3 characterize or establish incentive and envy properties; they do not analyze the complexity of computing a solution to a problem.

The closest possible anchor is Proposition 2, proved here: *the probabilistic serial mechanism is convexly strategyproof*. Its positive analogue would be a continuum-agent assignment market in which preference rankings are types and their masses are population fractions. But a single-agent report deviation—the object Proposition 2 studies—has zero effect in a nonatomic population. Giving a group positive mass and letting it deviate instead changes the strategic question, so I cannot honestly present that as a continuous mirror of the proposition.

Under the requested anchor rule, there is therefore no qualifying continuous problem to state. The paper’s use of divisible probability shares and continuous time concerns outcomes and mechanism operation, not a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is decisive for ChoCo’s stated scope: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 is a cited separation theorem; Propositions 1–3 characterize or establish incentive and envy properties. None poses an algorithmic problem or classifies its complexity. Studying the computational cost of these mechanisms would introduce a new question rather than mirror a result here.

Proposition 2 is the closest anchor, but its direct population limit collapses. Give each ranking type mass \(\mu_t\) and scale object supplies per capita. In continuum probabilistic serial, a single agent’s report changes no mass, so it changes no allocation. Every report induces the same lottery for that agent; convex strategyproofness holds vacuously. The proposition’s substance—comparing outcomes under different reports while holding everyone else fixed—has disappeared.

The strongest repair is to let a positive-mass cohort split among reports, then ask whether its members’ assignments improve. That is a meaningful high-multiplicity question, and it may be worth studying. Yet it changes the strategic object: the cohort now changes the market that determines its own assignments. “The cohort’s average lottery improves” and “every member weakly improves” are distinct tests, and the paper supplies no reason to prefer either. Its convexity result concerns mixtures of one agent’s actions against a fixed profile; it establishes no property of collective deviations. This is the best route to a ChoCo project, but the connection to Proposition 2 is too loose to make it a mirror of the paper’s result.

Proposition 3(ii), on convex envy-freeness of random serial dictatorship, offers a cleaner population analogue: compare the allocation of a representative of each positive-mass type with allocations received by other types. That comparison does not vanish for an atomless society. It still yields an axiomatic question, though. Asking whether the property holds in a fluid limit, or computing a utility witness for it, does not supply the missing computational result. The paper’s use of lotteries and continuous time likewise concerns outcomes and mechanism operation, not a continuous society.

So the negative case is strong under the programme’s computational gate: none of the paper’s results anchors a computational mirror, and the most natural direct continuation of its central proposition is vacuous. Its weak point is the cohort-deviation repair. Authors interested in incentives might recognize that as an adjacent problem, and a positive-mass manipulation study could be valuable. The paper alone gives no basis for calling that problem its continuous mirror.
