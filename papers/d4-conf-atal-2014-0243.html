The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-compliant positive case from this paper: it contains no named computational result of the kind your brief requires. Theorems 1–16 analyze dependence and power functions against axioms, establish structural properties, and combine those properties. None gives a complexity classification or an algorithmic result; the paper also does not compute with a formal encoding that could qualify under your exception.

The closest near-miss is Theorem 4, proved here: for a two-player Boolean game, if the game has more than one strict Nash equilibrium, then the veto-based dependence relation is complete, \(Vbf(B)=A\times A\). That is a structural fact about dependence, not a complexity result about a computational problem. Its two-player premise also gives little support for a many-agent mirror: scaling the population changes the premise, while taking a distribution over two-player games would make instances—not the society—the continuous object.

The most plausible setting suggested by the paper is a large web-service or recommender-system deployment with many agents sharing the same Boolean goal and control role. One could imagine types as goal-and-control schemas and \(\mu\) as their population shares, then ask for type-level power rankings. But the paper defines neither Boolean games over type masses nor a mass-based ranking problem, and no named result supports its computational analysis. So I would treat that as a possible future direction, not a mirror of one of this paper’s results. The missing computational anchor is the decisive weakness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to mirror. Theorem 1 compares a dependence function with axioms; Theorem 2 shows another function satisfies them; Theorems 3–5 relate veto dependence to Nash equilibria; and Theorems 6–16 analyze power functions and their axiomatic combinations. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic result. That rules out a direct mirror under the programme’s stated criteria.

Theorem 4 is the closest candidate the positive case identifies, but it says that a two-player game with multiple strict Nash equilibria has a complete veto-dependence relation. Its subject is the structure of a relation between strategic agents, not the computation of that relation. Duplicating players to make a large society does not supply the missing computational result. The stronger possible rescue is a symmetric family of games with many replicated agent schemas, asking how veto dependence or power rankings behave in a population limit. But veto dependence asks whether a *particular* agent is indispensable across all choices of the others. In an atomless population, a particular agent has zero mass. Replacing individual vetoes with positive-mass or threshold coalitions might be sensible, but it changes the dependence notion and the question. Theorem 4 does not tell us which change to make or provide a result for that new model.

The web-service or recommender-system scenario has a related problem. A type would need to capture an agent’s goal, controlled propositions, and relevant position in the dependence structure. Repeated services might be grouped when their games are symmetric, but a type distribution alone does not preserve who depends on whom. A graphon model of dependence, followed by a continuum version of PageRank or another power function, is the strongest version of this idea. It could motivate a separate project; it would require new semantics and new results, rather than continue a computational question addressed in this paper.

The continuum issue is therefore more fundamental than the paper’s literal finite-agent definitions: its named results concern logical and structural properties of agent-level relations and rankings. Turning mass into the object of study would require inventing a population model and a computational objective first. That may be worthwhile research, but it is not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results. The negative case is strong for the paper as written; it cannot rule out every future population model one might build around Boolean games.
