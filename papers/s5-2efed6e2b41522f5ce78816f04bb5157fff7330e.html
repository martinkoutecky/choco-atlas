Why no mirror

Theorems 2 and 3 provide named algorithmic guarantees, so the paper does contain computational results. But in a continuum, feedback from all served mass would provide unbounded observations, while retaining finite feedback requires adding a sampling protocol the cases do not establish as part of the authors’ problem. The proposed continuous question therefore changes the bandit model, and no anchor has a surviving mirror as stated.

What the mirror covers

The candidate anchors cover the regret guarantees in Theorems 2 and 3 and the regret lower bound in Theorem 4; the one-shot allocation benchmark does not resolve the bandit feedback issue.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying anchor for a continuous-complexity mirror. The paper’s named theorems concern regret, not whether a problem is tractable, hard, or parameterized-tractable. The closest is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: when \(m\ge n\), every algorithm has surrogate regret at least \(\Omega(m\sqrt{T}/n)\); if also \(T\ge\max\{n,m^2\}\ge2\) and \(m/n\ge\lceil2338\ln T\rceil\), its actual regret has the same lower bound. That is an information-theoretic learning lower bound, not a computational-hardness result. Theorems 2 and 3 give regret upper bounds. The cited hardness result for the one-shot Santa Claus problem is prior work, not a numbered result proved here.

There is a plausible population scenario: a large subscription service with users grouped into a small number of complete valuation-distribution types, with item supply scaled to the population. Type mass would be each group’s population share; decisions would allocate item supply across types; the objective could maximize minimum cumulative expected utility per capita. But the paper’s bandit feedback is central, and many identical users would pool observations. That can change the regret lower bound Theorem 4 proves, which relies on a particular hidden assignment across agents and items. Preserving that lower bound may require a feedback model that no longer follows naturally from the continuous population.

So this is a possible new research question about how minimax regret changes as population size grows relative to type support. I would not present it as a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results: the required kind of named anchor is absent.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest candidate is Theorem 4, but it is a lower bound on statistical learning, not computational complexity. It holds for any algorithm, regardless of how much computation it uses: the proof bounds how well the learner can distinguish instances from their feedback. Theorems 2 and 3 likewise give regret guarantees. Algorithm 1’s \(O(mn)\) time per round is a useful implementation bound, but the allocation step simply assigns each item to the agent with the largest discounted UCB. The benchmark is a polynomial-size LP. None of these results charts the complexity of solving a computational social choice problem. The paper’s Santa Claus hardness statement is cited prior work, not a result proved here. On ChoCo’s stated remit, there is no qualifying computational result to mirror.

The subscription-service scenario is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: users can be grouped by their complete vectors of item-value distributions, with inventory scaled per capita. The obstacle is that the paper’s distinctive result depends on a finite-sample feedback process, and a population limit does not specify how that process scales. If every allocated user returns feedback, serving positive mass gives infinitely many samples in the continuum model. If the service instead collects a fixed number of samples per type, learning no longer scales with population mass; the resulting question is a typed bandit-regret problem. That could be worth studying, but its central question is statistical regret, not the computational complexity landscape ChoCo targets.

One could also mirror the expected-utility allocation benchmark in the continuous population model. With finite type support \(\tau\), this gives a max-min LP over type-item assignments, with \(O(m\tau)\) assignment variables. It is a coherent continuous allocation problem, but it sheds the paper’s bandit contribution; the paper does not establish a complexity result for that LP. Making it computationally harder would require adding constraints or structure absent from the paper.

So the negative case is strong for the paper as written: its proved contributions concern regret under partial information, and its allocation computation is simple. It is weaker as a universal claim: a fixed-feedback, type-stratified version of the subscription scenario could support a worthwhile continuous bandit study. That would broaden ChoCo toward statistical online allocation, rather than supply a continuous mirror of this paper’s computational results.
