The continuous mirror question

Given rational weight types \(t\) with weights \(w_t>0\), masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a quota density \(q\), a deletion budget \(\kappa\), and a tagged player of fixed weight \(b\), is there a deletion vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\) such that \(I_q(\mu-x)>I_q(\mu)\), where \(I_q(\nu)=\inf_{\alpha\in[0,1]^\tau,\,\sum_t\nu_t w_t\alpha_t=q}\sum_t\nu_t[\alpha_t\log(2\alpha_t)+(1-\alpha_t)\log(2(1-\alpha_t))]\), and the infimum is \(+\infty\) when the quota is unreachable? For a finite-precision decision version, require \(I_q(\mu-x)\ge I_q(\mu)+\varepsilon\) for a supplied rational gap \(\varepsilon>0\).

The model it lives in

A large association has a population of chapters grouped into a small number of statutory weight types. The tagged chapter remains a single player; the other chapters are represented by type masses. A feasible deletion removes mass \(x_t\) from each type while leaving the quota fixed. The objective is to increase the large-deviation rate of the tagged chapter’s pivotal-coalition probability.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: CONTROL-BY-DELETING-PLAYERS-TO-DECREASE-PBI is NPPP-complete. Its instance is a weighted voting game \(G\), a distinguished player \(i\), and a limit \(k<|N|\); it asks whether at most \(k\) other players can be deleted so that \(i\)’s probabilistic Penrose–Banzhaf index strictly decreases. The paper cites Rey and Rothe [27] for the NPPP upper bound and proves hardness here by reduction from E-MINORITY-SAT. This is the lead because it is a hardness result about changing a player’s power, the question a continuous mirror can address directly.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large membership association whose chapters are the players in a weighted voting game. Suppose many chapters share one of a small number of statutory vote weights, and chapters with the same weight have the same status and removal conditions. Then a type \(t\) is a vote-weight class \(w_t\), and \(\mu_t\) is its fraction of the chapters. The regime has \(n\) chapters and \(\tau\) weight classes, with \(n\gg\tau\). Removing mass \(x_t\) means removing a fraction \(x_t\) of the chapters in class \(t\); it does not mean splitting an individual chapter. A distinguished chapter \(p\), with fixed weight \(b\), is tracked separately and is not removable. A large cooperative or association with many similarly weighted members makes this a credible instance regime, even though the paper’s EU example itself has few players.

Here is the continuous question I would put to the authors. Let the input be rational type weights \(w_t>0\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a quota density \(q\), a deletion budget \(\kappa\), and the distinguished chapter’s weight \(b\). Keep the quota fixed when members leave, as in the paper. After deleting mass \(x\), the remaining masses are \(\nu_t=\mu_t-x_t\), where \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\).

For a random coalition, each member is independently included with probability \(1/2\), as in the probabilistic Banzhaf definition. In a population of size \(N\), a coalition is pivotal for \(p\) when its total weight lies in the interval just below the quota, of width \(b\). In the high-multiplicity limit, the exponential rate of that event is

\[ I_q(\nu)= \inf_{\substack{\alpha\in[0,1]^\tau\\ \sum_t \nu_t w_t\alpha_t=q}} \sum_t \nu_t\left[ \alpha_t\log(2\alpha_t) +(1-\alpha_t)\log(2(1-\alpha_t)) \right], \]

with \(I_q(\nu)=+\infty\) if the quota is unreachable. Here \(\alpha_t\) is the fraction of type \(t\) selected into the coalition. The expression is the per-capita relative-entropy cost of a coalition profile; the probability that such a profile occurs is, to exponential order, \(\exp(-N I_q(\nu))\). Thus a higher rate means exponentially fewer pivotal coalitions.

Call the resulting problem Asymptotic-PBI-Rate Deletion: given those inputs, is there a feasible deletion vector \(x\) such that

\[ I_q(\mu-x)>I_q(\mu)? \]

A solution is such a vector of deleted type masses. For a bit-model version with a robust comparison, one can include a rational gap \(\varepsilon>0\) and require \(I_q(\mu-x)\ge I_q(\mu)+\varepsilon\). This is an asymptotic mirror of Theorem 1: it asks whether deleting a positive fraction of a large society can reduce the distinguished member’s power exponentially, rather than merely changing its finite-population index by any strict amount.

I expect this rate problem to be Class C, provisionally: its outer choice maximizes a convex large-deviation rate over a fractional deletion region, which suggests a nonconvex global search even though the inner rate calculation is a convex optimization. That is a research hypothesis, not a hardness result. In particular, I would not claim that the paper’s NPPP-hardness transfers. Its reduction encodes exact coalition counts, and the rate keeps only their exponential order.

The weakest point is also the main modeling qualification. This is not the exact continuous-PBI value for a continuum of players: the exact index is defined by counting finite coalitions, while a continuum population has no uniform random subset of individuals. The large-deviation rate is a principled limit of those same pivotal-coalition probabilities, but it can collapse finite differences that the theorem distinguishes. In particular, equal rates do not imply equal finite PBI values, and a single distinguished player’s weight can affect a lower-order factor without changing the rate. I think the authors could recognize it as a worthwhile asymptotic version of their deletion-control problem, but they would be right to reject it as a full replacement for Theorem 1.

This mirror covers only the strict-decrease result in Theorem 1. It also raises two follow-up questions: whether nonincrease and maintenance have useful rate-level versions, and which subexponential terms must be retained to recover the paper’s exact comparisons when the leading rates tie. Theorem 2’s nonincrease and maintenance results are therefore not additional anchors here: their exact distinctions are especially vulnerable to what the rate limit discards.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational anchor: it proves NPPP-completeness for a named deletion-control problem. A large association with many chapters in a few statutory weight classes is also a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The case against cannot rest on the absence of a computational result or on treating repeated weights as an illegitimate type.

The difficulty is that the Penrose–Banzhaf index of a distinguished player is a local probability. In a replica with \(N\mu_t\) members of each type, it is the probability that a random coalition’s weight \(S_N\) falls in the pivotal interval, \(Q_N-b\le S_N<Q_N\), where \(b\) is the distinguished player’s weight. As \(N\) grows, coalition mass concentrates around half of each type’s mass. The tagged player has vanishing population mass, so the literal continuum has no ordinary pivotal event for that player. Recovering a nonzero index requires retaining a fluctuation scale around the concentrated mass.

The proposed rate keeps only the exponential scale of that local probability. That discards distinctions central to Theorem 1’s reduction, which encodes exact counts of pivotal coalitions. In a standard majority regime, for example, the quota is at or above the random coalition’s mean weight. At the mean, the initial rate is \(0\); above it, the event is already an upper-tail event. Deleting any positive mass of positive-weight members while holding the quota fixed moves the mean down, so the rate rises (or pivotality becomes impossible). The rate comparison then ignores which types are deleted—the very choice the finite control problem asks about. This is a mismatch in the observable being studied, not a complaint that the asymptotic answer might be easy.

A stronger version could retain Gaussian-scale terms, the tagged player’s finite weight, and lattice effects. That gives more detail when the leading rates tie, but it also needs an \(N\)-indexed sequence, a specified quota-centering regime, and a scale for the deletion budget. A fixed number of deletions has zero mass in the limit; deleting positive mass moves the coalition mean at population scale. Those choices define distinct asymptotic problems. Tracking total influence for a whole type avoids the tagged-player degeneracy, though it changes the target from one player’s power to bloc power.

This is a serious objection to the proposed rate formulation as a mirror of Theorem 1. It does not establish that every asymptotic, fluctuation-sensitive version would be unworthy: for off-center quotas, a rate-level deletion problem could still be meaningful and computationally nontrivial, and authors might recognize it as a useful asymptotic question about power. The honest negative case is therefore limited. It shows that the strongest proposed mirror loses the paper’s exact control signal in an important regime; it cannot rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror in every regime.
