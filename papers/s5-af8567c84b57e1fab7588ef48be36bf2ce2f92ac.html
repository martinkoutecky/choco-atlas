The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive scenario is large scale crowdsourced binary labeling. A type could be a worker cohort whose members share the same signal distributions under \(H=0\) and \(H=1\), error costs, and training context. If there are many workers but only a few cohorts, \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of workers in cohort \(t\). The paper itself discusses crowdsourcing and biased training data, so this setting is plausible to its authors.

A concrete extension would be Type-Mixture Asymptotic Belief Design. Its input is a finite set \(T\), rational type masses \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\), finite-alphabet signal laws \(P_{t,h}\), the true prior, and the error costs. For populations with \(N_t/N\to\mu_t\), every local worker and the fusion agent use a common advertised belief \(q\), and each worker reports according to the paper’s likelihood-ratio rule. The question is to find \(q\) maximizing the fusion agent’s true error exponent and return the maximizing belief and exponent. Writing \(p_{t,h}(b;q)\) for the probability that a type-\(t\) worker reports \(b\) under \(H=h\), the natural exponent to optimize is \(\beta(\mu,q)=\max_{s\in[0,1]}-\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log\big(\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}p_{t,0}(b;q)^{1-s}p_{t,1}(b;q)^s\big)\). For fixed finite signal alphabets, I would expect this version to be tractable: as \(q\) varies, report probabilities change only at finitely many likelihood-ratio thresholds, and on each resulting interval the exponent is a one-dimensional Chernoff optimization. That is a proposed extension, not a result proved in the paper.

The nearest anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper in Appendix F and building on Tsitsiklis [6]. It says that for sub-Gaussian private observations, some common belief \(q^*=q_0=\cdots=q_N\), used with the paper’s likelihood-ratio rules and belief update, asymptotically attains the optimal fusion-agent risk exponent as \(N\to\infty\). The exponent, optimized over all decision rules, is \(\beta^*=\max_{q_1} C\big(\mathrm{Bern}(p_{\hat H_1|H}(1|0)),\mathrm{Bern}(p_{\hat H_1|H}(1|1))\big)\); this also equals the optimal cooperative star-network exponent from [6]. It anchors the homogeneous, one-type case of the proposed question.

The weakness is substantial: Theorem 2 characterizes an asymptotic risk exponent; it does not give a runtime bound or classify a problem as tractable or hard. Theorem 1 gives a necessary condition for optimal beliefs, Lemma 1 gives coordinate-wise convexity, and Algorithm 1 is a numerical person-by-person procedure, but none is a named complexity result. The paper’s continuous signals and its \(N\to\infty\) analysis also do not themselves make the society a distribution over types. So this is a credible research extension, but under a strict reading of the requested anchor rule, the paper has no qualifying named computational-complexity anchor. Allowing Theorem 2 as an optimization anchor gives the positive case above; requiring an explicit complexity result leaves no defensible anchored mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper has no named computational-complexity result to continuize. Theorem 2 characterizes an asymptotic Bayes-risk exponent through a Chernoff-information optimization. It gives no algorithm, running-time bound, or hardness result. Theorem 1 is a necessary condition on optimal beliefs, and Lemma 1 establishes coordinate-wise convexity. Algorithm 1 is a numerical person-by-person search without a complexity or approximation guarantee. So the paper offers no result about computational feasibility for a continuous mirror to carry forward.

The proposed type-mixture problem is coherent, and a large crowdsourcing cohort is a plausible repeated population. But it is a new belief-design problem, not a continuous version of a computational problem established in the paper. With finite signal alphabets and a common belief \(q\), each worker’s likelihood-ratio rule changes only at finitely many thresholds as \(q\) varies. The type masses then weight the cohort-specific Chernoff terms. This puts the computational work in the signal channels and the chosen optimization objective; the population distribution supplies weights. Allowing a separate belief for every cohort would make a richer problem, but it would move further from Theorem 2’s common-belief result.

There is also a limit issue. In a literal continuum, each cohort’s report share becomes deterministic, so the fusion agent’s error risk vanishes. A nonzero exponent is recovered by keeping a sequence of finite populations with \(N_t/N\to\mu_t\) and measuring the decay rate as \(N\) grows. That is a legitimate large-deviations question, but it is not a computational result about decisions over a continuous society.

I would not claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The crowdsourcing interpretation makes the repeated-type regime sensible, and the type-mixture extension could be useful in distributed detection. The negative case is strongest under ChoCo’s stated brief: this paper supplies no computational-complexity anchor, and the proposed extension’s value would have to stand on its own rather than follow from a result in the paper.
