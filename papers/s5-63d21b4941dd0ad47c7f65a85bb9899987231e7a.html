What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror concerns the strongly group strategy-proofness characterizations in Theorem 3 and Corollary 1; it leaves the paper's other propositions, examples, and special-case comparisons aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying anchored case for a continuous computational mirror: the paper states no named complexity result—no algorithmic bound, hardness result, or parameterized classification.

The nearest result is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that when \(|V|=2\), a two-valued social choice function is strongly group strategy-proof exactly when it is Paretian and almost-independent of irrelevant alternatives; when \(|V|>2\), it is strongly group strategy-proof exactly when it is Paretian and conflict-independent. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result. Corollary 1’s characterization of the larger-society rules as exactly the veto rules is also not a complexity result.

A coherent population-continuum analogue is easy to state: take a finite set of complete weak-preference types over \(A\), including types that rank the distinguished alternatives \(a,b\) either way or are indifferent; represent a large society by a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types; and let a rule map \(\mu\) to \(a\) or \(b\). A coalition’s action is to shift some of its mass to reported types, and it manipulates if all shifted mass weakly prefers the new outcome and positive mass strictly prefers it. One could ask which such rules are strongly group strategy-proof. This fits a large electorate with repeated preference types, but its solution is a characterization of rules, not an algorithm or complexity classification. Also, the paper already allows societies of arbitrary cardinality, so moving to continuum cardinality alone would not supply the missing computational contribution.

Thus there is no honest named anchor here from which to ask whether discrete hardness dissolves, transfers, or becomes continuum-specific. The candidate mirror is an axiomatic extension, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the only plausible anchor, and it does not state a computational result. It characterizes which two-valued social choice functions are strongly group strategy-proof. Corollary 1’s veto-rule description is the same kind of result. Neither gives an algorithmic problem, an input representation, a running time, or a hardness classification.

The proponent’s distributional model is coherent: take a finite set of preference types, represent the electorate by a rational mass distribution, and define manipulation through positive-mass misreports. But asking which rules on those distributions are strongly group strategy-proof remains an axiomatic characterization. Writing the masses as binary rationals does not make the rule itself a finite input: the paper ranges over functions, without specifying how to encode them. Fix a rule and evaluating it may be trivial; ask whether a rule is strategy-proof and the complexity depends on a representation the paper never supplies.

One could add a computational task—for example, testing strategy-proofness for succinctly represented rules, or finding a minimum-mass manipulation. Either might be worthwhile research. But the rule language, testing problem, or manipulation objective would be new choices, not computational questions mirrored from this paper. The arbitrary-cardinality society in the paper is not itself a continuous population model, but replacing agents with masses does not supply the missing computational contribution.

So the negative case is strong on fit with ChoCo: there is no named computational result here to continuize. Its limit is that a new paper could build an interesting algorithmic agenda from this setting; I cannot rule that out. It would be a new agenda, though, rather than a continuous computational mirror of the results De Simone and Bhaskara Rao prove.
