The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 4

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 4 — survived: A mass version over recurring types preserves the stable-matching and deferred-acceptance question.
  • Theorem 2 — fell: The cohort-level random graph changes the paper’s person-level random model, and its threshold question is not computational.

The continuous mirror question

Given finite candidate and job type sets \(A\) and \(J\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_j\) with totals \(1+\alpha\) and \(1\), and a strict partial preference list for each type, compute the candidate-proposing stable mass allocation \(x_{aj}\) and the unmatched mass of each type.

The model it lives in

Each type represents agents sharing the same preferences and acceptability. The decision variables are masses \(x_{aj}\geq 0\), with \(\sum_j x_{aj}\leq\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{aj}\leq\nu_j\). For each mutually acceptable pair \((a,j)\), define \(r_a(j)=\mu_a-\sum_{j'\succeq_a j}x_{aj'}\) and \(r_j(a)=\nu_j-\sum_{a'\succeq_j a}x_{a'j}\); stability requires \(r_a(j)=0\) or \(r_j(a)=0\). The requested allocation is candidate-optimal among stable allocations, comparing assigned mass on every preference-list prefix.

The objection that survived

Theorem 4 gives no encoding-sensitive runtime for rational masses: the proponent predicts a polynomial-time type-level algorithm but does not construct or analyze it.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s candidate-optimal stable outcome and unmatched mass. It leaves the random-market thresholds of Theorems 1 and 2 and the individual rank bound of Theorem 3 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a type-level deferred-acceptance algorithm compute the candidate-optimal mass allocation exactly in time polynomial in the support size and binary input length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest computational anchor is Theorem 4, cited from Gale and Shapley (1962) and McVitie et al. (1970). The paper gives the candidate-proposing deferred-acceptance algorithm, and Theorem 4 says it returns a stable matching that is best possible for every candidate and worst possible for every job; its result is independent of proposal order, including which proposals are made; and the unmatched agents are the same in every stable matching (the Lone Wolf Theorem). The theorem does not explicitly state a complexity class, but the displayed algorithm has at most one proposal per listed pair, so it gives a polynomial-time algorithm on an explicit finite input. The paper contains no named hardness result or explicit P/NP classification; Theorem 4 is its clearest algorithmic anchor.

A continuous mirror I would defend is Candidate-Optimal Stable Mass Matching. Its input is finite sets of candidate types \(A\) and job types \(J\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_j\) with \(\sum_a\mu_a=1+\alpha\) and \(\sum_j\nu_j=1\), and a strict partial preference list for each type over the types on the other side. All members of a type share that type’s full preferences. The solution is a rational matrix \(x_{aj}\geq 0\), where \(x_{aj}\) is the mass of type \(a\) matched to type \(j\), with row and column sums bounded by their respective masses. A matched pair must be mutually acceptable.

For a mutually acceptable pair \((a,j)\), the mass of type \(a\) that would prefer \(j\) to its current assignment is
\[ \mu_a-\sum_{j'\succeq_a j}x_{aj'}, \]
and the mass of type \(j\) that would prefer \(a\) is
\[ \nu_j-\sum_{a'\succeq_j a}x_{a'j}. \]
The allocation is stable when these two quantities are never both positive. The problem is to compute the candidate-proposing canonical stable allocation, with the expected properties expressed at the type level: each candidate type’s assignment stochastically dominates its assignment in any other stable allocation, each job type’s assignment is stochastically dominated by its assignment in any other stable allocation, and the resulting mass allocation is independent of proposal order. These dominance claims can be stated precisely by comparing, for every preference-list prefix, the mass assigned within that prefix. There is no welfare objective; the output is the canonical stable allocation and its unmatched mass.

A plausible regime is a large hiring or placement market with many applicants and positions in recurring cohorts. Each cohort shares its ranking over counterpart cohorts, and the number of people is much larger than the number of cohorts. If there are \(q\) near-identical people per type, then the support size stays fixed while population size grows with \(q\). Mass is the fraction of the market in each cohort, and a cohort’s mass can split across counterpart types. Stable matching, partial lists, and deferred acceptance remain the central objects, so I expect the authors to recognize this as a high-multiplicity version of their problem. I expect this computation to be tractable, in Class A terms, by a mass version of deferred acceptance with work polynomial in the type support and input bit length.

The strongest paper-specific supporting result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper from Theorems 9 and 10 and the sandwiching lemma. For \(M_{n,d,\alpha}\), where \(0\leq\alpha=o(1)\) and \(1\leq d\leq n\), it gives the threshold
\[ d_0=\ln n\cdot \ln\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{\alpha+\frac{1}{n(1+\alpha)}}\right). \]
For every constant \(\epsilon>0\), when \(d>(1+\epsilon)d_0\), with high probability all stable matchings are perfect; when \(d<(1-\epsilon)d_0\), with high probability none is perfect.

The corresponding continuous question is Perfect Stable Mass Threshold. For each \(n\), take \(n(1+\alpha)\) candidate types and \(n\) job types, each of mass \(1/n\), so total candidate mass is \(1+\alpha\) and job mass is \(1\). Generate a random bipartite graph on the types with edge probability \(d/n\), and give each type an independent uniform ranking of its neighbors. Every member of a type shares that type’s graph neighborhood and ranking. Determine the sharp threshold in \(d\) for whether all stable mass allocations cover the job mass, versus none covering it, with high probability. With \(q\) identical people per type, the population grows as \(q\) while the support size \(\tau=n(2+\alpha)\) stays smaller by a factor of \(q\). This is a coherent cohort-level high-multiplicity model, and it asks the paper’s own perfect-stability question. For its instance-by-instance stable-allocation computation I expect Class A; the asymptotic threshold itself is a probabilistic result rather than a P/NP classification, and I expect its proof to require continuum-specific analysis.

My weakest point is that the random graph changes scale: in the paper, edges and rankings belong to individual agents; in the proposed mirror, they belong to cohorts and are shared by all their members. That change creates the high-multiplicity regime, but it also changes the scarcity and blocking behavior that drive Theorem 2. The \(\ln^2 n\) threshold may shift, and Theorem 2 does not prove the mass version. The continuous stable-matching computation still stands as a plausible mirror of Theorem 4 if the threshold result does not carry over. The mirror covers the deferred-acceptance and perfect-stability results; I would leave Theorem 3’s individual rank bound out, since rank for a type whose mass splits among partners needs a separate, less direct formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the threshold mirror is that it changes the kind of question the paper answers. Theorem 2, like Theorems 1 and 3, is a probability statement about independently sampled random markets. Its proposed mirror asks for a threshold in a random graph whose vertices are types and whose edges and rankings are shared by everyone in a type. That is a coherent stochastic model, but it does not ask for the complexity of computing an outcome from a given society distribution.

There is a real modeling tradeoff here. Keep the paper’s independent, person-level random preferences, and complete types encode those preferences; in the random regime, the support may be close to the population size. Instead, group people into cohorts that share neighborhoods and rankings, and the random environment is now drawn over cohorts. Replicating each cohort increases population mass without reproducing the paper’s independent-person randomness. Thus the proposed \(\ln^2 n\) threshold is a plausible question about a different random market, but it is not a continuous computational mirror of the paper’s main result.

Theorem 4 is the harder anchor to dismiss. It is a classical result cited in the paper, not the paper’s contribution, and its content is the structure of deferred acceptance rather than a stated complexity classification. The proposed rational-mass problem also needs its own encoding-sensitive algorithm: expanding masses into individual clones can take time exponential in the binary input length. Still, neither point defeats the mirror. Type-level deferred acceptance is a natural candidate for a compressed algorithm, and stable matching between recurring applicant and position cohorts is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of the same problem. The individual-identity objection is unavailable once a type captures every feature the model uses.

So the negative case removes the threshold anchor as a computational mirror, but it does not defeat the deferred-acceptance anchor. I cannot honestly support the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: under the stated standard, Theorem 4 leaves a credible, author-recognizable question. The anti-case is therefore weak at its decisive point.
