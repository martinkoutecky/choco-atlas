The continuous mirror question

For a continuum of recipient mass with finitely many complete types, unit budgets, common valuations and sizes, and per-capita sequential arrival streams of indivisible goods with positive-density spread at most \(\gamma\), determine the optimal prefix-wise \(\alpha\)-FEF\(1\) guarantee \(F_\infty(\sigma)\) when every good has size at most \(\sigma\), and determine \(\lim_{\sigma\to0}F_\infty(\sigma)\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete recipient types, while the evolving state is a mass distribution \(\nu_t(\theta,A)\) over integral bundles \(A\). At each arrival, decision variables transfer mass from eligible bundle states to states obtained by adding the indivisible good, with unassigned mass sent to charity; the objective is the supremal deterministic prefix-wise feasible-envy factor.

The objection that survived

The case FOR (proponent)

I would defend a continuous mirror, but narrowly. The paper contains no named NP-hardness, membership-in-\(\mathrm P\), FPT, or similar theorem; its strongest computational anchors are online impossibility and approximation-frontier results. I therefore use those honestly, rather than relabelling them as complexity-class results.

The natural mirror is a high-multiplicity online fair-division model. Let \(\Theta\) be a finite set of complete recipient types. A type \(\theta\) includes its budget \(B_\theta\), valuation \(v_\theta\), and size function \(s_\theta\). The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\Theta\). In the main examples, \(\mu\) has one type: a large population of identical households, machines, or students.

Goods still arrive online and remain indivisible. To obtain a nontrivial limit, an arrival \(g_t\) comes with a per-capita multiplicity \(q_t\): in an \(N\)-agent realization, there are \(Nq_t\) distinct copies of \(g_t\). A fluid allocation state is a distribution \(\nu_t(\theta,A)\) over agent types and integral bundles \(A\). The action at round \(t\) is a mass transfer \(x_{t,\theta,A}\) satisfying

\[ 0\le x_{t,\theta,A}\le \nu_{t-1}(\theta,A), \qquad \sum_{\theta,A}x_{t,\theta,A}\le q_t, \]

with assignment permitted only when \(s_\theta(A)+s_\theta(g_t)\le B_\theta\). The remaining copies go to charity. Thus \(x_{t,\theta,A}\) is a fraction of agents receiving one whole good; no agent receives a fractional bundle. This is exactly the high-multiplicity interpretation: fractional masses at the type/state level, integral bundles for every unit of population.

The objective is to maximize the worst-case \(\alpha\) such that, at every prefix, almost every agent satisfies

\[ v_\theta(A_i)\ge \alpha\,v_\theta(S\setminus X) \]

for every feasible finite comparison set \(S\) in another agent’s bundle or in charity, with some \(X\subseteq S\) and \(|X|\le k\). The policy is deterministic, irrevocable, and nonanticipating.

My lead anchor is Theorem E.6, “Limiting density frontier,” proved in this paper in Appendix E. The theorem fixes \(n\ge1\) and \(\gamma>1\), and considers common valuations, sizes common across agents, unit budgets, and either known or unknown horizons. It proves that for every \(\alpha<1/(1+\ln\gamma)\), Threshold\((\alpha)\) is prefix-wise \(\alpha\)-FEF\(1\) once all goods are sufficiently small. Conversely, for every \(\alpha>1/(1+\ln\gamma)\), no deterministic online algorithm achieves \(\alpha\)-FEF\(1\) for any positive item-size bound; the hard instances have realized density spread exactly \(\gamma\). Hence the limiting supremum factor is \(1/(1+\ln\gamma)\).

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Fluid Limiting-FEF\(1\) Frontier. Given \(\gamma>1\) and a maximum per-good size \(\sigma\), consider a continuum of identical recipient types with unit budgets, common valuations and sizes, and arbitrary finite online arrival streams whose positive-density spread is at most \(\gamma\). Determine

\[ F_\infty(\sigma) = \sup_{\pi} \{\alpha:\pi\text{ guarantees prefix-wise }\alpha\text{-FEF}1\}, \]

and compute \(\lim_{\sigma\to0}F_\infty(\sigma)\).

I expect exactly the paper’s answer:

\[ \lim_{\sigma\to0}F_\infty(\sigma) = \frac{1}{1+\ln\gamma}. \]

The upper bound is the same threshold policy applied to the distribution of current bundle states. The lower bound scales the paper’s phase construction into per-capita cohorts of goods. The result is therefore a genuine population-continuous question, while retaining the paper’s online uncertainty, charity, indivisibility, and feasible-envy notion. It is Class A below the frontier and a Class-B-style transferred lower bound above it, not continuum-specific hardness.

A second useful anchor is Proposition G.1, “Density-spread lower bound for arbitrary item sizes,” proved in this paper. It states that for every \(k\ge1\), \(\gamma>1\), and \(\alpha>\gamma/(2\gamma-1)\), no deterministic online algorithm guarantees \(\alpha\)-FEF\(k\), even with known horizon, two agents, equal unit budgets, common valuations, uniform item sizes, and realized density spread exactly \(\gamma\).

Its continuous mirror is:

Fluid Arbitrary-Size Density-Bounded FEF\(k\). Given a continuum of identical recipients with unit budgets and common values and sizes, an online stream with exact density spread \(\gamma\), and fixed \(k\ge1\), determine the largest deterministic guarantee \(\alpha\)-FEF\(k\).

I expect the same impossibility threshold:

\[ \alpha>\frac{\gamma}{2\gamma-1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{no deterministic fluid policy can guarantee }\alpha\text{-FEF}k. \]

The two-continuation proof scales directly: replace the two agents by a large homogeneous population and replace each finite arrival block by a per-capita block. The lower-bound reasoning uses only capacity, common values, and the existence of a charity pool, so it is not driven by named-agent multiplicity.

A third, distinct mirror is Theorem 7.4, “MarginalsDoNotSuffice,” proved in Appendix I.8. It states that for every \(k\ge1\), no deterministic online algorithm guarantees exact FEF\(k\) when given only the exact value multiset, size multiset, and horizon, even with two equal-budget agents, common valuations, and sizes common across agents. The impossibility remains even if total value and maximum item value are also supplied.

The continuous question is:

Fluid Marginal-Prediction FEF\(k\). A continuum of identical agents receives indivisible goods online. The predictor supplies exact per-capita value and size marginal measures, the horizon, total value, and maximum item value, but not the joint value-size coupling. On each arrival the actual pair \((v(g_t),s(g_t))\) is revealed. Must a deterministic policy guarantee exact FEF\(k\) for every joint coupling and order consistent with those predictions?

I expect the answer to remain no. The two indistinguishable continuations in the paper can be replicated with \(N\) identical agents and then normalized as \(N\to\infty\). The missing information is precisely the joint type distribution of goods; separate marginals cannot tell the policy whether high-value goods are feasible or oversized. This is not a collision with the paper’s own type-count model: those are types of arriving goods, whereas the mirror makes the recipient population continuous.

The regime is plausible rather than artificial. A large food bank may serve many households from a few storage-and-preference profiles; a cloud scheduler may allocate repeated job classes across many identical machines; a course-allocation system may serve many students with a small number of budget and valuation types. The paper’s common-valuation and equal-budget lower bounds are therefore unusually good high-multiplicity candidates.

The weakest point is that the paper’s central difficulty is online uncertainty, not the number of named agents. Continuization may preserve the impossibility rather than dissolve it. Moreover, scaling goods per capita is essential: with only \(O(1)\) goods and a growing population, each arrival becomes negligible. I regard that scaling as the natural co-modification forced by the population limit, not as changing the problem’s object. The mirror keeps time, indivisibility, charity, budgets, and FEF\(k\) intact; it only replaces repeated recipient identities by population mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper does not naturally produce a ChoCo-style continuous-population problem. Its difficulty lies in irrevocable arrival information and the evolving composition of individual bundles, not in the number of agents. A continuum of recipients therefore does not compress the relevant state into a finite distribution over fixed types.

The proposed mirror for Theorem E.6 exposes this. With finitely many arrivals and \(N\to\infty\), every good has vanishing population mass, so the model ceases to be a meaningful allocation problem. To avoid that, one must replace each arrival by \(Nq_t\) copies. That is a legitimate repair, but it changes the model into a flow or batch-arrival problem. The state is then not merely a distribution over recipient types: it is a measure \(\nu_t(\theta,A)\) over all integral bundles \(A\) currently held by agents. Even with one initial recipient type, the possible \(A\)'s are subsets of the arrival history, potentially \(2^T\) states. Feasible-envy constraints depend on the composition of those bundles and on knapsack-feasible subsets, so bundle averages do not suffice.

Thus the resulting object is an infinite-dimensional online control problem, not the population LP or finite-type pricing problem that motivates continuization elsewhere in ChoCo. The small-good limit makes this worse: as \(\sigma_{\max}\to0\), each agent may accumulate arbitrarily many goods, so the bundle-state space expands rather than collapses. The nontrivial limit in Theorem E.6 is primarily a limit in item granularity and arrival supply, not in population structure.

Proposition G.1 does not improve the case. Its two-continuation argument scales to \(N\) identical recipients, but that scaling merely multiplies every capacity and supply by \(N\). The proof still uses the same per-agent indistinguishability argument. The continuous population contributes no new computational variable; it is bookkeeping around a two-agent information-theoretic obstruction. A fluid version is definable, but its content is an online-advice lower bound, not a population-continuization result.

The same objection is strongest for Theorem 7.4. The missing information is the joint value-size coupling of arriving goods. Replacing two identical agents by a continuum does not alter that information problem. A continuous version would really be a model with a measure of arriving good types, while the recipient population could remain a Dirac mass at one type. That is a legitimate hybrid model, but it makes the continuous goods stream—not the society—the substantive continuous object.

The proponent’s repairs are coherent: scale supplies per capita, retain integral bundles for each unit of mass, and require fairness almost everywhere. But after those repairs the problem is best viewed as high-multiplicity online allocation with a measure-valued bundle-state process. It has no evident finite representation, no obvious column-generation formulation, and no reason to inherit the computational advantages that motivate ChoCo’s continuous mirrors.

This is not a decisive impossibility argument. Identical households, machines, or students are plausible high-multiplicity populations, and the three proposed fluid questions are mathematically meaningful. In particular, the lower bounds really can be scaled. The honest negative conclusion is therefore narrower: these anchors do not justify spending the programme’s effort on a continuous *population* mirror. They motivate a separate project on fluid online allocation and prediction under indivisible bundles.
