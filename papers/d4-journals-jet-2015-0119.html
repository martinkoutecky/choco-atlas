The continuous mirror question

Given a rational population distribution \(\mu\) over a finite value set \(V\) and per-capita supply \(\kappa\), find an algorithm that outputs, for every reported population measure \(\nu\), an anonymous treatment schedule \(x_\nu(v),p_\nu(v)\) that is feasible and truthful for every infinitesimal agent, and maximizes revenue \(\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v p_\mu(v)\) relative to \(EFO_\kappa(\mu)\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic multi-unit market has value types \(v\in V\) with masses \(\mu_v\), per-capita capacity \(\kappa\), service probabilities \(x_v\), and expected payments \(p_v\). Maximize \(\sum_v\mu_v p_v\) subject to \(0\le x_v\le1\), \(0\le p_v\le v x_v\), \(\sum_v\mu_vx_v\le\kappa\), and the pairwise truthfulness constraints \(v x_v-p_v\ge v x_s-p_s\) for all \(v,s\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is the multi-unit prior-free revenue problem. The paper has no named NP-hardness or parameterized-hardness result, so the right anchor is an algorithmic approximation result: Theorem 6.4, proved in the paper, states that in multi-unit environments the Random Sampling Empirical Myerson mechanism (RSEM) is a \(12.5\)-approximation to \(EFO^{(2)}(v)\). RSEM randomly splits the agents into a sample and a market, puts the highest-valued agent in the market, derives ironed virtual values from the sample, and serves market agents to maximize ironed virtual surplus. Here \(EFO^{(2)}(v)=EFO(v^{(2)})\), where \(v^{(2)}\) replaces the highest value by the second-highest value. The guarantee is over RSEM’s random partition.

A credible high-multiplicity regime is a large platform selling identical service units to many users whose values fall into a modest number of shared categories. A type is a value category; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users in category \(t\). There are many users but relatively few supported types, \(\tau\), and supply scales with population: the platform can serve a fraction \(\kappa\) of the society. Each user still receives either a unit or no unit; a fractional type allocation means serving that fraction of the type’s mass. This is a natural co-modification of the multi-unit model.

I would hand a prover the Nonatomic Prior-Free Multi-Unit Revenue Problem. Fix a finite value alphabet \(V\) and a per-capita supply \(\kappa\). The input population is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(V\). A solution is one anonymous direct mechanism that, for each submitted population measure \(\nu\), returns a service probability \(x_\nu(v)\) and expected payment \(p_\nu(v)\) for each reported value. It must be feasible, \(\sum_v \nu_v x_\nu(v)\leq\kappa\), and truthful for every infinitesimal agent: holding \(\nu\) fixed, a user with value \(v\) must prefer the treatment for report \(v\) to the treatment for any report \(z\). The task is to find the smallest \(\beta\) for which one such mechanism guarantees revenue at least \(EFO_\kappa(\mu)/\beta\) for every \(\mu\).

Define \(EFO_\kappa(\mu)\) as the maximum revenue over type treatments \((x_t,p_t)\) satisfying capacity, individual rationality, and envy-freeness:
\[ \max \sum_t \mu_t p_t \]
subject to
\[ 0\leq x_t\leq 1,\qquad 0\leq p_t\leq v_t x_t,\qquad \sum_t\mu_t x_t\leq\kappa, \]
and
\[ v_t x_t-p_t\geq v_t x_s-p_s\quad\text{for every }t,s. \]
In the continuum, removing one highest-valued individual changes no mass, so the natural counterpart of the paper’s \(EFO^{(2)}\) benchmark is \(EFO_\kappa(\mu)\).

I expect this problem to be exactly solvable in Class A, with \(\beta=1\). For each reported measure \(\nu\), choose an optimal solution to the envy-free linear program above and use it as the treatment schedule. A unilateral report does not change the aggregate measure in a nonatomic society, so the chosen schedule stays fixed; its envy-free constraints then give incentive compatibility. Conversely, any truthful schedule at a fixed \(\nu\) must satisfy those same pairwise constraints, so it cannot earn more than \(EFO_\kappa(\nu)\). The LP has \(O(\tau)\) variables and \(O(\tau^2)\) constraints, giving polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\).

This is a close mirror of Theorem 6.4’s objective and benchmark in a multi-unit setting. The mechanism takes the realized submitted population measure as its aggregate input and assumes no prior distribution over that input. The mirror covers the revenue-approximation result for multi-unit auctions; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s general downward-closed result, its position and matroid reductions, or its reserve-price comparison.

The weakest point is that the exact guarantee relies on each individual having zero mass. That removes the finite-agent incentive problem that motivates RSEM, so the authors could regard this as a useful nonatomic limit rather than a mirror of their finite mechanism-design challenge. The natural next questions are whether the same collapse to envy-free optimization holds for continuum position auctions, and how quickly the exact nonatomic guarantee degrades when agents have small but nonzero mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The multi-unit platform is the strongest possible setting for this paper: many users, a small set of shared value classes, and supply proportional to the population. I would not object to its high-multiplicity story. The difficulty is that the nonatomic limit changes the strategic problem behind Theorem 6.4.

At a fixed population measure \(\mu\), one user’s report has zero effect on the measure. The mechanism can choose a treatment schedule for that \(\mu\), and truthfulness then amounts to the pairwise envy-free constraints on the schedule. Thus the continuum counterpart of \(EFO^{(2)}\) is just \(EFO\): removing the highest-valued individual removes zero mass. RSEM’s sample and market also have the same type proportions in the limit, so its sampling no longer creates the finite-population uncertainty that its analysis handles. The theorem’s central challenge—one incentive-compatible mechanism working across profiles where an individual report can change the outcome—has disappeared.

A more faithful attempt would retain an individual mass \(\varepsilon=1/N\) and study how the finite-auction guarantee approaches its nonatomic limit. But the population measure alone then does not specify the strategic problem: identical proportions with different \(N\) give different report effects and sampling fluctuations. The question needs the finite granularity as an additional parameter, so it becomes a high-multiplicity family of finite auctions rather than a problem determined by a continuous society.

That makes my negative case narrow. The atomless revenue problem is coherent, and a convergence-rate study could interest the authors. The paper’s reference to envy-freeness and incentive compatibility becoming equivalent in large economies gives that direction a natural connection, though it is not itself a continuous computational treatment. I can argue that this is a weak mirror of Theorem 6.4’s computational question; I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous project around that limit.
