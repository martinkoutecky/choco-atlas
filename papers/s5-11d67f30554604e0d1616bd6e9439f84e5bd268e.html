What the mirror covers

No continuous mirror is anchored to a result; the paper’s empirical estimates and findings about observed judging behavior are not computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify an eligible anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its central findings are empirical estimates. For example, Table 3, line 1 reports that before trimming the estimated favoritism was \(0.365\) points and statistically significant; the estimated change after trimming was \(-0.240\) and not significant at conventional levels; the resulting estimate, \(0.125\), was also not significantly different from zero. That is evidence about observed behavior, not a named result about complexity or algorithms. I can’t honestly present it as an anchor or give a continuous problem “per anchor.”

The most plausible follow-on would concern large, repeated evaluation settings. A type could include an evaluator’s affiliation relation to the candidate, calibration under the rubric, and—if strategic behavior is modeled—the evaluator’s preferences and costs. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of scoring assignments made by type \(t\). Each type reports \(x_{t,a}\in[0,100]\) for performance \(a\); the rule removes a fraction \(\rho\) of the score mass at each end and averages the rest. The six-judge rule in the paper corresponds to \(\rho=1/6\). Given rational type masses, baseline reports, per-type costs for changing scores, and a target performance, Continuous Trimmed-Evaluation Manipulation asks for the least total mass-weighted cost of score changes that makes the target a winner, and returns an optimal set of reports. This is a well-defined computational question about the paper’s aggregation rule. The weighted trimming calculation itself can be done from the support, but the paper provides no basis for classifying the manipulation problem’s complexity.

A plausible regime would be a large evaluation platform with many repeated assignments and a small number of recurring evaluator profiles. The paper’s actual regime is a poor high-multiplicity fit: panels have only five to seven judges, and the analysis controls for judge-specific tendencies. If those tendencies, prices, and other relevant traits must all be included in a type, the number of types may approach the number of individual judges. The authors might recognize the proposed manipulation problem as a related theoretical extension; the paper does not study score-changing costs or optimize strategic reports. Its main question is whether trimming changes favoritism in observed behavior, which a population model could express as a mass-weighted empirical estimand, but that alone does not supply a computational-complexity question.

So the positive case is limited: trimming has a natural population-level formulation, and the paper motivates a possible manipulation problem for a follow-on study. It does not contain a named computational result that this programme can mirror. That absence is the weakest point, and it prevents this from being a strong anchored case for a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Its central findings are causal estimates of how trimming correlates with judges’ scores. Table 3’s estimated change in favoritism is an empirical estimand, not a theorem about an algorithmic problem. So the proponent has no eligible result as an anchor; the proposed manipulation problem is a new question.

The paper’s data also resist the proposed population model in a specific way. Trimming and score ranks are calculated within each run’s panel of five to seven judges, while the regressions compare judges within a run and control for judge-by-season tendencies. Pool those observations into types and the model loses the panel composition and judge-specific variation that make those comparisons possible. Preserve them in the types and the support tracks individual judge-run contexts, rather than a small set of interchangeable evaluator profiles. A larger platform with recurring evaluator profiles could make a sensible high-multiplicity regime, but pooling its reviews would not preserve the paper’s within-panel question.

The proposed Continuous Trimmed-Evaluation Manipulation adds score-change costs and a target winner. Neither is part of the paper. It changes a study of observed behavior under a policy change into an optimization problem about strategic score changes. A still closer strategic model would need to specify judges’ payoffs and reputational incentives, which the paper discusses but does not formalize. There is a further mismatch: the paper’s incentive story concerns a judge’s influence and reputation within a small panel. In a continuum panel, an individual has zero mass and cannot move the aggregate score; giving a type positive mass models a coordinated bloc of judges instead. Keeping the actual small panels preserves individual influence, but leaves no continuous population inside the decision being studied.

A large review platform could support a worthwhile continuous manipulation problem, and I would not rule out that adjacent project. But it would not be a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper: there is no such result, and the proposed optimization supplies a new decision problem the paper does not ask. That is the strongest honest case against a mirror here. The broader claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile continuous work is too strong to defend from this paper alone.
