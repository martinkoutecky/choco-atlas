The continuous mirror question

Given finite worlds Ω with rational prior \(P\), type masses \(\mu_a\), a partition \(\Pi_a\) for each type, a feasible risk-neutral grand contract \(q_a(\omega)\), and coalition worth functions \(V_\omega(x)\) specified by rational production LPs, decide whether the contract is in the ex-interim core. Equivalently, for every nonzero \(x\in[0,\mu]\) and every positive-prior cell \(I\) in the common refinement \(\Pi^\vee_{A(x)}\) of the participating types’ partitions, test whether \(\mathbb{E}[V_\omega(x)\mid I]\le\sum_{a\in A(x)}x_a\mathbb{E}[q_a(\omega)\mid I]\), where \(A(x)=\{a:x_a>0\}\).

The model it lives in

A society has finitely many complete agent types carrying rational masses μ; each type has a shared information partition and risk-neutral preferences. For each world ω, a rational production LP defines coalition worth \(V_ω(x)\) from coalition composition \(x\). Given a feasible per-capita grand contract \(q\), search over nonzero \(x\) and the information cells induced by its participating types for a positive conditional excess \(\mathbb{E}[V_ω(x)\mid I]-\sum_a x_a\mathbb{E}[q_a(ω)\mid I]\); any positive excess witnesses blocking.

The objection that survived

The paper allows arbitrary coalition worths, so the production-LP representation and any resulting tractability come from added structure rather than from its theorems.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the risk-neutral ex-interim membership characterization in Theorem 2, with parallel mass-composition versions of Theorems 1 and 3; it leaves general risk preferences, core nonemptiness, and the paper’s broader blocking analysis aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For growing τ, what is the complexity of ex-interim core membership when each \(V_\omega\) is given by a rational production LP and the relevant information refinement depends on the coalition’s support?
  • Away from zero-slack boundaries, when does the discrete type-count grid converge to the continuous core-membership answer?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a Bayesian cooperative economy with many agents in a few complete types. A type records everything relevant to an agent: their risk preferences, information partition, and production characteristics. A society is a mass vector \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{\mathcal A}\) with \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\). The possible worlds and their common prior remain finite, as in the paper; it is the population that becomes continuous.

A plausible setting is a large production cooperative whose members fall into a few dozen skill and information categories. Hundreds of thousands of people may share those categories, and a coalition’s worth may depend on how much of each category joins and on which market or technology state occurs. Let \(V_\omega(x)\) be the worth of a coalition with mass composition \(x\in[0,\mu]\) in world \(\omega\). For a concrete computational input, let each \(V_\omega\) be the value of a rational production LP with type-specific capacity limits \(x\). A grand contract assigns each member of type \(a\) a per-capita payoff \(q_a(\omega)\). It is feasible when \(\sum_a\mu_a q_a(\omega)\le V_\omega(\mu)\) in every world. A deviating coalition of composition \(x\) may choose per-capita payoffs \(r_a(\omega)\), feasible when \(\sum_a x_a r_a(\omega)\le V_\omega(x)\) in every world. The decision is whether a given feasible grand contract is in the relevant core; a witness of failure is a composition and a feasible contract that blocks it.

This is a population continuization, with the natural co-modification that coalition supplies and payoffs scale with mass. It is not a continuization of the paper’s uncertainty or outcome space. In the \(n\)-agent version, type counts \(n_a\) give masses \(\mu_a=n_a/n\), and allowed coalition compositions lie on the grid \(x_a=k_a/n\). With continuous \(V_\omega\), these grids become dense as \(n\) grows. This gives a concrete high-multiplicity bridge, while leaving open how exact core membership behaves at zero-slack boundaries.

The lead anchor is Theorem 2. The paper states it as its own result in Section 5; its proof is omitted, and it relies on Lemma 2. In full, for a risk-neutral BCG and a grand contract \(c^N\), that contract is in the ex-interim core if and only if, for every coalition \(S\subseteq N\) and every information set \(I\in\mathcal I^\vee_S\),
\[ \mathbb E[\omega(S)\mid I]\le \sum_{j\in S}\mathbb E[c^N_j\mid I]. \]
Here \(\mathcal I^\vee_S\) is the partition formed by the nonempty intersections of one information set from each member’s partition.

Its continuous counterpart, Continuous Ex-Interim Core Membership, takes the finite worlds and rational prior, type masses \(\mu\), each type’s partition \(\Pi_a\), the production-LP descriptions of \(V_\omega\), and a feasible grand contract \(q\). It asks whether \(q\) is unblocked by every coalition composition \(x\in[0,\mu]\). For \(A(x)=\{a:x_a>0\}\), let \(\Pi^\vee_{A(x)}\) be the nonempty intersections of information sets from the partitions of the participating types. The exact question is whether
\[ \mathbb E[V_\omega(x)\mid I]\le \sum_{a\in A(x)}x_a\,\mathbb E[q_a(\omega)\mid I] \]
for every \(x\) and every positive-prior cell \(I\in\Pi^\vee_{A(x)}\). A solution is “in the core” or “blocked,” with a violating \(x,I\) as a blocking witness.

This is the best mirror because information is central to the paper’s ex-interim notion, and a type can naturally include an information partition. The continuous question preserves that feature instead of reducing the problem to uncertain coalition worth alone. With production-LP worth functions, my first algorithmic expectation is tractability for fixed \(\tau=|\mathcal A|\), by enumerating type supports and solving an LP separation problem for each support and information cell. For unrestricted \(\tau\), I would expect the support and information-pattern combinatorics to retain hardness; whether that hardness embeds cleanly from a discrete version is a further question, not a result of this paper.

Two other anchors support the same mirror on their own terms. Theorem 1, also stated by the paper as its result (with proof omitted; the paper notes that a similar reduction appeared earlier in Suijs and Borm), says that for risk-neutral agents \(c^N\) is in the ex-ante core exactly when, for every \(S\subseteq N\),
\[ \mathbb E[\omega(S)]\le\sum_{j\in S}\mathbb E[c^N_j]. \]
The corresponding Continuous Ex-Ante Core Membership problem asks whether, for every \(x\in[0,\mu]\),
\[ \mathbb E[V_\omega(x)]\le\sum_a x_a\mathbb E[q_a(\omega)]. \]
Under the stated production-LP representation, the violation search is an LP over coalition masses, so I expect this version to be tractable.

Theorem 3, stated by the paper with a straightforward proof omitted, says that \(c^N\) is in the risk-neutral ex-post core exactly when, for every coalition \(S\) and every world \(\omega\),
\[ \omega(S)\le\sum_{j\in S}c^N_j(\omega). \]
The corresponding Continuous Ex-Post Core Membership problem asks whether
\[ V_\omega(x)\le\sum_a x_aq_a(\omega) \]
for every \(x\in[0,\mu]\) and every world. With the same production-LP representation, I also expect this membership test to be tractable. It is a useful boundary case: stability must hold in each world, so the prior does not enter the test.

These anchors are computational characterizations, not formal P or hardness theorems. The paper itself warns that its linear characterizations do not by themselves make the finite-agent problems easy, because the coalition constraints are exponential in the number of agents. The continuous questions ask whether replacing agent subsets with type-mass compositions makes those constraints manageable under a succinct worth representation. Natural follow-ups are whether the corresponding *core nonemptiness* problems have efficient separation algorithms; whether the ex-ante, interim, and ex-post inclusion relation in Corollary 1 carries over; and where discrete hardness survives when the number of types grows.

The weakest point is the added structure needed to make this mirror computationally definite. The paper permits arbitrary coalition-value functions on a finite agent set; it does not posit repeated interchangeable members, masses, or a production-LP encoding of \(V_\omega\). The authors would likely recognize the resulting question as a continuous-population version of Bayesian core stability, but whether they accept this particular large-cooperative scenario depends on whether its type categories and aggregate production values count as a faithful model of their coalitions. Their existing uncertainty over worlds is not itself a collision: the proposed mirror changes the population, not the source or distribution of uncertainty.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper gives no canonical finite input for a continuous core problem. Theorems 1–3 characterize risk-neutral core membership by inequalities over named-agent coalitions. The worth function \(v_\omega(S)\) is otherwise arbitrary, and the paper explicitly leaves efficient algorithms open. Extending those values to a function \(V_\omega(x)\) over fractional coalition masses requires a new representation. In the proponent’s version, the production-LP encoding supplies that representation—and much of the expected tractability—rather than anything established by the paper. Different extensions could agree on every finite coalition and still give different answers between the grid points.

That concern is sharpest for Theorems 1 and 3: once \(V_\omega(x)\) is supplied, their mirrors are direct aggregate inequalities, and any easy separation may come from the chosen value-function encoding. Theorem 2 adds information partitions, but repeated agents of each type share one partition, so the relevant cells depend on which types participate; the remaining search is over type masses. That may make the problem routine for fixed support, or hard when support grows. Neither outcome invalidates the question.

This does not defeat the proposed mirrors. A large cooperative economy with repeated skill and information categories is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and type masses preserve the relevant information if worth depends on coalition composition. The paper’s theorems are named computational characterizations of core membership, even though they do not classify its complexity. So the “no computational result to mirror” objection would overstate the case; the missing encoding is a real weakness in the proposed bridge, not a decisive disqualification.

I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The most I can argue is that the paper does not itself determine the continuous computational problem: a worthwhile mirror would need to justify its aggregate worth representation and explain which algorithmic question comes from the paper rather than from that added structure. The production-LP proposal is coherent enough that this leaves the negative case weak.
