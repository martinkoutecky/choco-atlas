Why no mirror

Both anchors are computational, and a typed matching market is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But the concrete mirror requires a finite DAG whose closed subsets biject to every stable mass allocation; the four-type example has infinitely many such allocations, so this representation cannot exist as stated. A polyhedral or parameterized representation would be a different target, not a surviving mirror of these anchors.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the DAG representation in Theorem 1.1 and its construction in Theorem 5.5; it leaves the paper's other lattice and rotation results without a continuous analogue.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a typed continuum stable matching market. Consider a large, recurring one-to-one allocation market in which preferences are genuinely expressed over a modest number of counterpart categories: for example, a centralized placement market with many applicants and positions, grouped into a fixed set of profile and position types. A type is a complete description for the model: its members have the same strict ranking of opposite-side types, and no other person-level feature affects matching. The regime of interest has many agents but relatively few positive-mass types. This is plausible for standardized markets, not for every matching market.

Let \(R\) and \(P\) be the two finite sets of types, with rational supplies \(a_r\) and \(b_p\). A solution is an aggregate matching \(x_{rp}\ge 0\), with row and column sums bounded by those supplies; the remaining mass is unmatched. These fractions describe how much of each type is paired with each other type. Each infinitesimal agent still receives one integral partner; only the aggregate assignment is fractional. Call \(x\) stable if, for no pair \((r,p)\), is there both positive mass of type \(r\) assigned to types it ranks below \(p\) (or left unmatched) and positive mass of type \(p\) assigned to types it ranks below \(r\) (or left unmatched). Such masses could pair and form a blocking pair.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1.1, proved in this paper as the combination of its rotation-characterization and runtime results. It says that for every finite stable matching instance, a directed acyclic graph \(G\) can be computed in \(O(n^2)\) time, with a bijection between all stable matchings and the closed subsets of \(G\)—subsets containing every predecessor of each included vertex. Thus the theorem gives a compact, computable representation even when the instance has exponentially many stable outcomes. Its core contribution is exactly the problem I would mirror: representing *all* stable outcomes through a polynomial-size precedence structure. The paper’s Theorem 5.5, also proved here, supplies the explicit \(O(n^2)\)-time construction of the rotation predecessor graph. The exponential number of matchings mentioned in the introduction motivates the representation; it is not a hardness result.

The continuous question is Typed Mass-Rotation Representation: given \(R,P\), their strict rankings over opposite-side types, and rational supplies \(a_r,b_p\), construct a polynomial-size labeled DAG \(D\), a base stable mass matching \(x^0\), and rational mass-change labels \(\Delta^\rho\) for its vertices. Require that for every closed subset \(S\subseteq V(D)\), the allocation
\[ x(S)=x^0+\sum_{\rho\in S}\Delta^\rho \]
is feasible and stable, and that this gives a bijection between the closed subsets of \(D\) and all stable mass matchings. The complexity target is polynomial in the number of types and the bit length of the supplies and rankings, not in the denominator-expanded population. A solution is this exact encoding, with a polynomial-time algorithm to produce it.

I expect this question to be Class A, though it is a research conjecture, not a consequence of the paper’s theorem. With finitely many ranked types and rational capacities, deferred acceptance plausibly becomes a mass-flow process: proposals consume capacity, rejection chains move mass, and a rotation records a minimal change in the assignment. The technical prize would be showing that these mass rotations and their precedence relations capture every stable aggregate outcome with a compact encoding. Further questions follow naturally: whether type-level stability always has finitely many outcomes; how the rotation structure changes with capacities; and what precise correspondence links the continuous representation to finite high-multiplicity instances.

The authors could recognize this as their problem because the object being represented remains the stable matching set and the computational task remains finding its structure. The type-level market makes the population itself the scalable object, while capacities and fractional aggregate assignments are the necessary co-modifications for a continuum of agents.

The weakest point is the type model. The paper ranks named individuals using strict preference lists; this mirror ranks counterpart *types*, making members of a type indistinguishable and allowing aggregate splitting. That is defensible only in markets where preferences and priorities really are specified by those categories. It also leaves open whether every such market has the finite, DAG-indexed outcome set the proposed problem asks for. This is a meaningful risk, but it is where the continuization should be tested: it does not erase matching or stability, and it asks whether the paper’s central representation theorem survives in a genuine high-multiplicity regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed mirror is structural. In its typed mass market, the stable outcomes need not form a finite set of matchings connected by cover steps. For example, let types \(A,B\) and \(X,Y\) each have mass \(1\), with rankings \(A:X\succ Y\), \(B:Y\succ X\), \(X:B\succ A\), and \(Y:A\succ B\). For every \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), assign masses
\[ x_{AX}=x_{BY}=\lambda,\qquad x_{AY}=x_{BX}=1-\lambda. \]
Every such allocation is stable: at each potentially blocking pair, one side has no mass assigned to a less preferred type. Thus even this four-type instance has an interval of stable mass matchings.

That defeats the proposed Theorem 1.1 mirror: a finite DAG has only finitely many closed subsets, so it cannot biject to this interval. More fundamentally, the interval has no covers between distinct outcomes. Rotations defined as minimal cover changes therefore cannot serve as the steps of a finite chain through all stable mass matchings. Adding rational labels to the DAG does not fix that mismatch; finitely many additive labels still produce only finitely many outcomes.

The stronger rescue would be to represent the stable mass outcomes by a polytope or a parameterized family, perhaps using rotations to describe its extreme points. That could be a worthwhile new result, but it does not rescue the proposed rotation-graph mirror. A direct description already follows from the blocking definition: for each type pair \((r,p)\), either no \(r\)-mass is assigned below \(p\), or no \(p\)-mass is assigned below \(r\). This gives a compact complementarity formula from the rankings and supplies. Asking for a canonical polyhedral description or an efficient algorithm to optimize over that set adds a new computational target; neither follows from the paper’s cover-and-rotation result.

Theorem 5.5’s \(O(n^2)\) construction has the same problem. It counts proposals and rotation events among named agents. In a type-compressed market, aggregate flows can change by continuously varying amounts, and expanding rational supplies into individual copies can make \(n\) far larger than the input bit length. The theorem gives no compressed runtime or procedure for finding the proposed mass representation.

So the specific DAG and runtime anchors do not survive their continuous reinterpretation. But the broader claim that *no* worthwhile mirror exists is weak: a compressed representation of stable mass outcomes could still be valuable. Continuum matching models already give a credible large-market setting, though the cited supply-and-demand framework studies existence, uniqueness, and limits rather than computational representation ([A Supply and Demand Framework for Two-Sided Matching Markets](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/a-supply-and-demand-framework-for-two-sided-matching-markets/)). I can make a strong case against these anchors; I cannot honestly rule out every stronger, differently formulated computational question.
