The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(\mathcal T\) with masses \(\mu_t\), resource types \(\mathcal R\), additive utilities \(u(t,r)\), and rational per-capita supplies \(s_r\) with \(\sum_r s_r=L\), compute the maximum \(q\) and feasible mass assignment \(x_{t,b}\) such that \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b} b_r x_{t,b}=s_r\), and every bundle \(b\) with \(x_{t,b}>0\) gives its recipient utility \(\sum_r b_r u(t,r)\ge q\). Each bundle contains exactly \(L\) indivisible resources; determine the complexity as a function of the type supports and \(L\).

The model it lives in

A population distribution over complete valuation types, with per-capita supplies of resource types and mass variables over integral bundles. The objective maximizes the least utility of any positive-mass type-bundle assignment.

The objection that survived

The finite-type model does not preserve Theorem 3's independent random-utility matrix or its matching-threshold analysis; it mirrors the underlying allocation problem rather than that stochastic asymptotic law.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers max-min bundle allocation over finite user and resource types. It leaves aside the paper's i.i.d. tail characterizations, its fixed-agent limit, and its price-of-fairness convergence results.

Open questions for a prover

  • When \(L\) is part of the input, what are the exact and approximation complexities of deciding whether all population mass can receive an integral bundle with utility at least \(q\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is for a *nearby version of the underlying allocation problem*, but it cannot meet your anchor requirement: this paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity classification.

A plausible continuous question is High-Multiplicity Max-Min Bundle Allocation. Let \(\mathcal T\) be finitely many complete user types, with population masses \(\mu_t\), and \(\mathcal R\) finitely many resource types, with per-capita supplies \(s_r\) totaling \(L\) resources per user. A type \(t\) user gets utility \(u(t,r)\) from a resource of type \(r\). For each type \(t\) and integral bundle \(b\) of \(L\) resources, let \(x_{t,b}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned that bundle. Require \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\) and that total use of each resource type respects its supply. The task is to maximize \(q\) such that every bundle receiving positive mass gives its assigned type utility at least \(q\). A plausible regime is a large service population divided into a fixed number of repeated user and resource-profile types, so the population is much larger than the type support. This preserves indivisible bundles for each user while making the population and resource supplies continuous.

That is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of max-min fair allocation, but it is not an anchor from this paper. Its i.i.d. continuously distributed utility matrix almost surely gives users distinct full utility profiles; the proposed finite-type regime therefore changes the paper’s instance regime and loses the random-matrix structure behind its results. With fixed \(L\), the finite-type formulation can be expressed through mass variables over bundles, but that does not establish a complexity result for the paper’s model.

The nearest numbered results do not qualify. Theorem 1, proved here, gives relative convergence in probability of \(M_{N,N}\) to \(F^{-1}(1-\frac{\ln N}{N})\) for regularly varying upper-tail quantiles or finite positive upper endpoint, and additive convergence under the paper’s stronger tail assumptions. Theorem 3, also proved here, gives relative convergence of \(M_{N,LN}\) to \(L F^{-1}(1-\frac{\ln N}{N})\) for Weibull-type tails or a finite positive endpoint. Theorem 4, proved here, gives \(M_{N,K}/((K/N)\mu_U^N)\to1\) in probability for fixed \(N\), \(K\to\infty\), and finite first and second utility moments. These are asymptotic probabilistic characterizations, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or in another complexity class. The random-graph threshold in Theorem 2 is cited from elsewhere and is likewise not a complexity classification.

The paper does say in prose that the \(K>N\) allocation problem is NP-hard, and describes the balanced case as polynomially solvable via bottleneck assignment. Neither statement appears as a numbered result of the required kind. So I would not present the proposed continuous allocation question as anchored by this paper. The positive case is limited to the plausibility of a high-multiplicity version of the *underlying* allocation problem; the paper’s named results do not substantiate the requested computational-complexity mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central objection is that none of this paper’s named results gives the computational-complexity anchor ChoCo is looking for. Theorems 1 and 3 characterize random max–min values asymptotically; Theorem 4 does the same in a fixed-agent limit. The paper’s NP-hardness statement for \(K>N\) is in prose, not a numbered complexity result. Theorem 2 is a cited random-graph threshold. So the positive case identifies a potentially sensible high-multiplicity allocation problem, but it does not anchor a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 1’s balanced case shows why a direct mirror is difficult. Its key structure is a random \(N\times N\) matrix of independently drawn utilities: the value is tied to whether the threshold graph has a perfect matching near edge probability \(\ln N/N\). If an agent’s type records their utilities for the \(N\) labeled resources, then independently drawn continuous rows are almost surely distinct. Grouping agents into finitely many types loses that realized utility information. And a continuum of average edge masses does not retain the sparse local structure that makes the matching threshold work. One can preserve that structure by keeping the individuals and random matrix, but then the society has not become a high-multiplicity distribution over a fixed type space.

Theorem 3 has the same obstacle in the \(K=LN\) regime: its lower bound depends on random \(L\)-matchings in a growing matrix, while both agents and goods scale. The proposed finite-type bundle model is a reasonable nearby allocation problem. With fixed user and resource types, fixed per-capita supplies, and integral bundles, its mass variables could support algorithmic questions of its own. But those assumptions replace the independent agent–resource utilities and the tail-driven matching argument that produce Theorem 3’s asymptotics. They may motivate a separate high-multiplicity study; they do not preserve this result’s model.

Theorem 4 is a still poorer anchor: \(N\) stays fixed while \(K\) grows, so its asymptotic axis is the resource pool, not the population. A continuous version obtained by making goods divisible would continuize the outcome or resource side. The LP relaxation in the paper’s numerical section does just that: it allows fractional assignments while retaining individually indexed agents. It is not a continuous-population mirror.

The universal negative case is weaker than the theorem-specific one. A large service population with finitely many complete valuation types and finitely many resource types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and max–min bundle allocation there may be worth studying. I cannot rule out that the authors would recognize it as their problem. The defensible conclusion is narrower: this paper’s named results do not supply the requested computational anchors, and preserving their random-matrix arguments appears to require keeping agents individuated. That is a strong case against claiming a mirror of these results, but not proof that no worthwhile continuous-population question lies near the paper.
