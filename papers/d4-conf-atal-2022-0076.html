The strongest plausible mirror is the paper’s underlying maximum-weight assignment problem. Consider a dense urban dispatch system with many requests and vehicles, where requests fall into a relatively small set of operational classes—say, origin zone, time window, and service requirements—and vehicles into classes by location and capability. A type must specify the exact utility vector, eligibility, and other attributes used by the model; the classes cannot merge agents whose relevant preferences differ. If there are \(N\) requests but only \(\tau\) request types and \(\sigma\) vehicle types, with \(\tau,\sigma \ll N\), write \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\) for their population shares and \(w_{ij}\) for the utility of matching request type \(i\) with vehicle type \(j\).

Call the problem Type-Mass Maximum-Weight Matching: given finite supports of request and vehicle types, binary-rational masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\), and binary-rational utilities \(w_{ij}\), find a matrix \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{i,j} w_{ij}x_{ij} \]
subject to
\[ \sum_j x_{ij}\leq \mu_i \quad\text{for every }i,\qquad \sum_i x_{ij}\leq \nu_j \quad\text{for every }j. \]
Here \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of requests of type \(i\) assigned to vehicles of type \(j\); the constraints enforce one-to-one capacity in aggregate. The solution is the allocation matrix. This is a precise population-continuous version of the assignment objective in §3.1, and the paper’s large urban settings make a high-multiplicity regime plausible, even though its evaluated batches are small.

I expect this problem to be tractable by linear programming. In fact, for masses arising from finite populations with unit capacities, scaling by the population size gives the usual bipartite matching polytope, so the aggregate optimum preserves the discrete optimum. That makes this a plausible continuous model, but not a case where discrete hardness dissolves: maximum-weight matching is already tractable. It mirrors the allocation objective and scalability motivation, not PALMA’s decentralized interaction, its privacy accounting, or the PLDP guarantee. Preserving those would require a separate model of individual action distributions and privacy constraints.

That gap is the weakest point, and it is substantial: the paper’s contributions are PALMA, PLDP, and empirical performance, not a named complexity theorem about a matching problem. So this mirror is a credible adjacent question suggested by the paper’s setting, but it cannot honestly be presented as an anchor-based positive case under the stated rules. Further questions include whether a type-level decentralized protocol can retain individual PLDP guarantees and PALMA’s communication properties, and how the optimization changes when eligibility is sparse or types must be refined to preserve exact utilities.
