The continuous mirror question

Given finite additive \(2\)-D valuation types \(T\), masses \(\mu_t\), item categories \(G\) with per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and integral bundles \(S\subseteq G\), decide whether there are variables \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S\ni g}x_{t,S}=q_g\), such that every supported bundle for type \(t\) satisfies \(u_t(S)\ge(3/4)\lambda_t\) for correlated goods, or \(u_t(S)\ge(11/9)\lambda_t\) for correlated chores, where \(\lambda_t=\max_y\min_{y_S>0}u_t(S)\) over distributions \(y\) with \(\sum_S y_S=1\) and \(\sum_{S\ni g}y_S=q_g\); alternatively, optimize the common guarantee.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity configuration model with type masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita item supplies \(q_g\), and variables \(x_{t,S}\) assigning mass to integral bundles. The feasibility objective is to meet item capacities while guaranteeing each type a specified fraction of its continuum MMS value; the optimization version maximizes that common fraction.

The objection that survived

The continuous \(3/4\) limit and the per-capita MMS benchmark are not consequences proved by Corollary 4, and scaling inventory changes the shared-feasibility problem rather than merely renaming finite allocations.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 4's correlated additive goods and chores guarantees in the high-multiplicity regime. It leaves the structural transformation results, inverse-externality non-existence theorem, full externalities, mixed goods and chores, and MNW/MEW questions untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does column generation or separation solve the correlated additive continuum MMS problem in polynomial time under compact value encoding?
  • When can a continuum solution be implemented or rounded for a finite high-multiplicity population without losing the MMS guarantee?
  • Can multiple valuation types create a genuine shared-feasibility obstruction even when each type's individual continuum MMS benchmark is attainable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity allocation mirror in which both the population and the inventory scale, while each individual still receives an integral bundle.

Take a large population of patients, households, or students. There are finitely many complete valuation types \(T\), with population distribution \(\mu\), and \(|T|=\tau\ll N\). An item \(g\) is a scarce category—say a vaccine dose, hospital slot, or course seat—with \(q_gN\) identical copies, where \(q_g\in[0,1]\). A type \(t\) has exactly the paper’s two-dimensional valuation: for an integral bundle \(S\subseteq G\),

\(u_t(S)=v_t(S)+v'_t(G\setminus S)\).

In the additive case this is \(u_t(S)=\sum_{g\in S}v_{tg}+\sum_{g\notin S}v'_{tg}\). The externality still depends only on whether the agent receives \(g\), not on which other agent receives it.

A continuous allocation is a mass assignment \(x_{t,S}\), meaning that mass \(x_{t,S}\) of type \(t\) receives the integral bundle \(S\). It must satisfy \(\sum_Sx_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}=q_g\). This is not a lottery over fractional bundles: whenever \(x_{t,S}>0\), the corresponding agents receive \(S\) integrally. A rational \(x\) can be implemented by taking sufficiently many discrete copies, so this is directly a high-multiplicity relaxation.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3 in the appendix, proved in this paper; the main text presents the corresponding statement as Theorem 1. It says that an allocation \(A\) is \(F\)-fair and \(E\)-efficient under the original 2-D valuations \(V\) if and only if it is \(F\)-fair and \(E\)-efficient under the transformed 1-D valuations \(W=T(V)\), where \(F\) ranges over EF, EF1, EFX, PROP-E and its stated relaxations, and MMS, while \(E\) ranges over PO and MUW. The appendix prints one relaxation as PROP1-X; the main text uses PROPX-E.

The corresponding problem is Continuum Special-Externality Fair Allocation. Given \((T,\mu,G,q,v,v')\), find a mass assignment \(x\) satisfying the supply constraints and a selected fairness condition \(F\), together with PO or maximum utilitarian welfare. For example, continuum EF requires that whenever \(x_{t,S}>0\) and \(x_{t',S'}>0\), \(u_t(S)\ge u_t(S')\); EF1 and EFX use the paper’s item-removal conditions applied to the bundles in the support of \(x\). For MUW, the objective is to maximize \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}u_t(S)\).

The paper’s transformation remains meaningful pointwise: \(w_t(S)=u_t(S)-v'_t(G)\). It subtracts a type-dependent constant from every bundle, so comparisons, Pareto improvements, utilitarian welfare differences, and exact MMS thresholds are preserved. The continuous problem is therefore an LP over bundle columns, with the same pricing question that motivates continuization generally. For additive valuations, I would expect the MUW and several fairness variants to be Class A problems in regimes where bundle pricing is separable; for compact general valuations, the pricing problem may itself be difficult. The further questions are exactly the programme’s questions: which fairness notions admit efficient separation, how rounding behaves for \(N\) agents, and whether the transformation continues to support algorithms rather than merely identities.

The second anchor is Theorem 2, proved here. It states that in the presence of externalities there may be no \(\alpha\)-MMS allocation for any \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) when the MMS value is positive, and no \(1/\alpha\)-MMS allocation for any \(\alpha\in(0,1]\) when the MMS value is negative. The paper proves this for inverse externalities using explicit twelve-item, three-agent constructions, adapting earlier nonexistence examples.

Its continuous mirror is Continuum Inverse-Externality MMS Feasibility. For a type \(t\), define the continuum MMS value by letting \(\mathcal Y(q)\) be the distributions over integral bundles satisfying the per-capita supply constraints, and setting \(\lambda_t=\max_{y\in\mathcal Y(q)}\min_{S:y_S>0}u_t(S)\). The question is whether there exists a feasible \(x\) such that every used bundle of type \(t\) satisfies \(u_t(S)\ge\alpha\lambda_t\) for goods with \(\lambda_t>0\), or \(u_t(S)\ge\lambda_t/\alpha\) for chores with \(\lambda_t<0\). One may ask either the decision version for a fixed \(\alpha\), or the best achievable guarantee.

This question tests whether Theorem 2’s obstruction is genuinely about externalities or only about a small integral population. In a one-type society, the finite nonexistence disappears: the distribution \(y\) witnessing \(\lambda_t\) itself gives a continuum MMS allocation. With several valuation types, however, their acceptable bundles can compete for the same item masses, so a genuine continuum-specific feasibility obstruction may remain. My expectation is Class A for fixed \(m\) or explicit bundle valuations, with possible Class C behaviour when \(m\) is compactly represented and pricing becomes hard. It is not an automatic hardness transfer from the paper’s three-agent examples.

A useful third anchor is Corollary 4, derived here from Corollary 3 and cited approximation algorithms. It says that for correlated 2-D externalities one can obtain \(3/4+1/(12n)\)-MMS for additive goods and \(11/9\)-MMS for additive chores. The continuous problem is Continuum Correlated-MMS Optimization: under positive externalities for goods, find an \(x\) giving every type at least \(3/4\) of its continuum MMS value—the limit of \(3/4+1/(12n)\)—and under negative externalities for chores, find an \(x\) giving the corresponding \(11/9\)-MMS guarantee. I would expect this to be Class A in the additive high-multiplicity regime, via the transformed 1-D configuration problem and mass-flow rounding.

The paper contains no named NP-hardness, parameterized-hardness, or “in P” theorem of its own, so I would not manufacture one. Its value for ChoCo is that it supplies three clean anchors: an exact valuation transformation, a sharp finite-population impossibility, and explicit approximation guarantees. The continuous mirror covers these results, not the paper’s full-externality discussion, MNW/MEW, or mixed goods-and-chores cases.

The weakest point is the inventory co-modification. If every original item remains a single indivisible object while the agent population becomes nonatomic, almost everyone receives nothing and the mirror degenerates. Replicating item categories at per-capita supply is therefore essential. It is defensible for vaccines, seats, beds, or repeated course places, and is explicitly the sort of forced co-modification allowed by the programme, but it is the point at which a referee could argue that the mirror has changed the resource-allocation regime too substantially.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s eligibility gate: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1/Theorem 3, Theorem 2, and Corollary 4 are structural or existence statements, not complexity, algorithmic, approximation-complexity, or parameterized results proved for a computational problem.

Theorem 3 is especially unsuitable as an anchor. Under complete allocation, in the additive case,

\[ u_i(S)=v'_i(M)+\sum_{g\in S}(v_{ig}-v'_{ig}), \]

so \(T\) merely subtracts the constant \(v'_i(M)\) from every bundle of agent \(i\). It preserves bundle comparisons, Pareto comparisons, utilitarian-welfare maximizers, and MMS thresholds for exactly the same finite allocations. In a mass model it remains a pointwise affine renormalization: for fixed type masses, MUW changes by the fixed constant \(\sum_t\mu_t v'_t(M)\). Nothing about population multiplicity, continuum computation, or pricing is doing work. A high-multiplicity formulation may be interesting, but it is ordinary high-multiplicity fair allocation after the paper’s own reduction, not a computational mirror of the paper’s contribution. Moving to full externalities could make the continuous problem genuinely different, but then the paper’s theorem no longer supplies the anchor.

Theorem 2 does not rescue the case. Its obstruction is a finite indivisible packing construction with three agents and twelve named items. The proposed \(\lambda_t\) is not the theorem’s MMS value carried into a limit; it is a new benchmark over distributions of integral bundles satisfying aggregate supply constraints. Scaling item copies per capita necessarily permits fractional assignment of copies across types, changing the object whose MMS feasibility is being studied. Keeping the twelve items as indivisible atoms leaves only finite inventory against an atomless population, so almost all mass receives nothing. Coupling replicated items into indivisible blocks preserves the original discrete packing structure by construction rather than continuizing the population. Each choice is defensible as a new model, but none is determined by Theorem 2.

Corollary 4 is only a transfer of finite-agent guarantees from cited MMS algorithms. It gives no running-time theorem for a high-multiplicity problem, no limiting argument as \(n\) disappears, and no mass-allocation or rounding result. Replacing \(3/4+1/(12n)\) by \(3/4\) is not a consequence of the corollary. Moreover, the proponent’s claim that the whole continuous problem is an LP is true plainly for MUW, but not for EF, EF1, EFX, or MMS: support conditions and maximin partitions introduce disjunctive or partition structure.

The proponent’s scaled-inventory model is therefore legitimate, and I would not object that high multiplicity is implausible or that co-scaling inventory is forbidden. The honest objection is narrower but decisive: it produces a new high-multiplicity fair-allocation research problem, while the paper contributes no named computational result whose population continuization is being studied. A related continuous allocation paper could certainly be worthwhile; this paper does not provide a qualifying mirror for ChoCo.
