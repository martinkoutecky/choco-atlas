The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive case that meets your anchor rule. The natural population mirror is easy to describe, but its non-atomic version erases the strategic feature that the paper’s results analyze.

The closest named result is Proposition 3, proved in the paper, with its proof in the Proofs Appendix. It says that voting according to the private signal—\(v_i(A,z,t_i)=A\) and \(v_i(B,z,t_i)=B\)—is a Bayesian Nash equilibrium if and only if

\[ \delta\le \frac{ r\left[p^{2K-N-1}-(1-p)^{2K-N-1}\gamma\right] +(1-r)\left[(1-p)^{2K-N-1}\gamma-p^{2K-N-1}\right] }{ r(1-p)^{2K-N-1}+(1-r)p^{2K-N-1} }, \qquad r=\frac{p(1-q)}{p(1-q)+(1-p)q}. \]

That is an equilibrium characterization, not a stated complexity result for a computational problem or its continuous-population version. Propositions 1–5 characterize equilibria or compare their efficiency; none classifies a problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. So I would not count Proposition 3 as a qualifying computational anchor.

The closest continuous question would be: given \(\kappa\in(1/2,1]\), signal accuracies \(p,q\), and payoff parameters \(\gamma,\delta\), does the private-signal strategy form a Bayesian Nash equilibrium in a continuum electorate? Let a type be \((t,s)\), where \(t\in\{A,B\}\) is the voter’s team and \(s\in\{A,B\}\) their private signal. Conditional on state \(S\), each team has mass \(1/2\), and its mass with signal \(S\) is \(p/2\), with the remaining \((1-p)/2\) receiving the other signal. The public signal \(z\) is observed by everyone and has accuracy \(q\). A type’s action is its vote, and the outcome is \(A\) or \(B\) if that vote receives mass at least \(\kappa\), and \(C\) otherwise. The proposed problem asks whether voting \(s\) is an equilibrium.

A large party primary or shareholder vote with many voters and only a few team–signal types makes this population description plausible. Under the usual non-atomic equilibrium, though, each voter has zero mass and cannot change the outcome alone. Every vote is therefore a best response, regardless of \(\delta\) or the paper’s Proposition 3 threshold. Under private-signal voting, the state-matching alternative gets mass \(p\): it wins when \(p\ge\kappa\), and otherwise the outcome is \(C\). Those aggregate outcomes do not recover the paper’s strategic condition.

So this would be a trivial, tractable equilibrium-existence question, but not a persuasive mirror of Proposition 3. Restoring the paper’s pivotal-voter incentives would require finite pivotal units or positive-mass coalition deviations, changing the model or solution concept. That loss of pivotality is the strongest objection to the continuous mirror; the low number of voter types is the strongest point in its favor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–5 characterize Bayesian Nash equilibria and compare their efficiency; Results 1–4 report experimental behavior. Proposition 3 gives an equilibrium condition, not the complexity of deciding or computing anything. Turning its inequality into a decision problem would impose a new computational framing on an already stated formula.

The proposed continuum version makes the mismatch especially clear. With types \((t,s)\) and a non-atomic electorate, an individual voter has zero mass and cannot change whether an alternative reaches threshold \(\kappa\). Every vote is therefore a best response, whatever \(\delta\) or Proposition 3’s threshold. In the finite game, by contrast, that proposition depends on the chance that one voter is pivotal among the other \(N-1\) voters. Keeping those pivotal incentives requires retaining finite electorates or changing the deviation unit to a positive-mass bloc. Either move changes the strategic question; neither supplies a computational result from this paper for ChoCo to classify.

A large primary or shareholder vote with a few team–signal types is a credible high-multiplicity setting, so I would not reject the mirror on grounds that the population story is implausible. But that supports only an analytic continuum game or a new computational extension—such as optimizing an information policy—not a computational mirror of a result the paper establishes. That is where the negative case is strongest. Its limit is the universal claim: a finite-size study of pivotality might be valuable, but it would be a new research question rather than the programme’s intended complexity mirror.
