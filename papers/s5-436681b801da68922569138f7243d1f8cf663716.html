The continuous mirror question

Given two alternatives \(a,b\), rational mass \(p\) of type \(a\succ b\), and mass \(1-p\) of type \(b\succ a\), consider a nonatomic population whose members’ utilities are drawn independently from a common distribution \(\mathcal D\) on \([0,1]\). For positive mean utility, let \(v_H=\mathbb E[\max(U_1,U_2)]\) and \(v_L=\mathbb E[\min(U_1,U_2)]\), for independent \(U_1,U_2\sim\mathcal D\), and define \(W_a=pv_H+(1-p)v_L\) and \(W_b=(1-p)v_H+pv_L\). Which alternative maximizes \(W_j/\max\{W_a,W_b\}\)?

The model it lives in

Two ranking types have masses \(p\) and \(1-p\); each type contributes its expected rank utilities \(v_H\) and \(v_L\) to aggregate welfare. Choose the alternative with larger aggregate welfare, equivalently larger normalized welfare; comparing \(p\) with \(1/2\) returns a mass-majority winner.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s two-alternative optimality result. It does not cover Theorem L.1’s finite-sample approximation guarantee or the paper’s results for general \(m\), scoring rules, and welfare approximations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest credible mirror is an electorate with many voters but few distinct rankings. A natural scenario is a large election where voters fall into a small number \(\tau\) of ballot cohorts. A type is a complete ranking, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. The decision remains the paper’s decision: choose one alternative. Utilities are drawn as in the paper, conditional on each voter’s ranking.

My lead is Binary Mass-Majority Distortion. Its instance is two alternatives \(a,b\), rational masses \(p\) and \(1-p\) on rankings \(a\succ b\) and \(b\succ a\), and a common utility distribution \(\mathcal D\) on \([0,1]\). In the continuum, aggregate welfare is deterministic: if \(X_{(2)}\) and \(X_{(1)}\) are the larger and smaller of two independent draws from \(\mathcal D\), then
\[ W_a=p\,\mathbb E[X_{(2)}]+(1-p)\,\mathbb E[X_{(1)}], \qquad W_b=(1-p)\,\mathbb E[X_{(2)}]+p\,\mathbb E[X_{(1)}]. \]
The question is to return an alternative maximizing \(W_j/\max\{W_a,W_b\}\). The solution is mass majority: return \(a\) when \(p\ge \tfrac12\), and \(b\) otherwise. Comparing the rational masses takes time polynomial in their bit length.

The anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in the paper: when \(m=2\), for every \(\mathcal D\), majority is both an expected-distortion-maximizing rule and an expected-welfare-maximizing rule. The continuous problem is its population limit, and majority remains the exact choice. This is a small but direct computational result: the authors should recognize both the input types and the winner-selection question.

A broader computational anchor is Theorem L.1, also proved in the paper. For \(\mathcal D\) supported on \((0,1]\) with mean \(\bar u>0\), the paper samples \(B=2\log(2m/\delta)/(\bar u^2\varepsilon^2)\) batches of \(nm\) utilities, estimates every alternative’s expected distortion, and selects the alternative with the highest estimate. With probability at least \(1-\delta\), that alternative achieves at least \(1-\varepsilon\) times the optimal expected distortion.

Its mass-based mirror, Mass-Profile Expected-Distortion Approximation, takes \(m\) alternatives, a support of \(\tau\) rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and a population scale \(N\) such that \(N\mu_t\) is integral for every type. It also takes a sampling oracle for \(\mathcal D\) and a positive lower bound on its mean. In each utility realization, the \(N\mu_t\) members of type \(t\) draw utilities independently as in the paper; for each alternative \(a\), let \(W_a\) be its resulting welfare. The task is to return an alternative \(a\) whose value
\[ \mathbb E\!\left[\frac{W_a}{\max_b W_b}\right] \]
is at least \(1-\varepsilon\) times the maximum such value, with success probability at least \(1-\delta\). Expanding the mass vector into its \(N\) voters gives the paper’s sampling algorithm directly. It runs in time polynomial in \(N,m,1/\varepsilon,1/\bar u,\log(1/\delta)\), given the sampling oracle.

These anchors are computationally meaningful, though neither supplies the preferred high-multiplicity bound polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the binary input length \(L\): Theorem L.1 is polynomial in \(N\). I would expect approximation to be tractable when the population is explicit, as the theorem shows; whether the general mass-profile problem admits a polynomial-in-\(L\) algorithm remains open. Theorem 3.1’s binary mirror is tractable directly from the masses. The paper provides no named NP-hardness or comparable complexity-hardness result to anchor a hardness-transfer question; its negative theorems concern approximation guarantees.

The weakest point is that a true continuum averages away the individual utility randomness. Its distortion is a ratio of aggregate expected welfares, whereas the paper studies the expectation of a random ratio. That is a real change in the objective. The binary anchor survives it: Theorem 3.1 already proves majority optimal for every finite electorate and every \(\mathcal D\), and the mass-majority rule is also the natural continuum limit. The broader sampling mirror preserves the paper’s random-ratio objective by retaining a finite population scale, but its known running time does not establish high-multiplicity efficiency.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither named result is a complexity result about choosing a winner from a population distribution. Theorem 3.1 characterizes an optimal rule for two alternatives; Theorem L.1 approximates a stochastic objective by sampling finite utility profiles. Their computational content comes from different places, and neither gives a substantial population-complexity question to continue.

For Theorem 3.1, the best mirror would allow arbitrary \(m\) and a support of \(\tau\) ranking types. Let \(r_t(a)\) be alternative \(a\)’s rank in type \(t\), and let \(v_r=\mathbb E[X_{(m+1-r)}]\) for the paper’s utility distribution. In an atomless population, aggregate welfare is \(W_a=\sum_t\mu_t v_{r_t(a)}\). The continuous distortion is \(W_a/\max_b W_b\), so selecting the best alternative is selecting the largest of these weighted scores. For two alternatives, \(W_a-W_b=(2p-1)(v_1-v_2)\), giving mass majority. This is a coherent population model, but it replaces the paper’s random ratio with a deterministic score calculation. Theorem 3.1 itself says nothing about computational complexity, and extending its majority conclusion to rational masses does not create a complexity problem for the programme to classify.

Theorem L.1 is the harder anchor. Its objective depends on random, finite voter-level utility draws: each sample batch produces a random ratio of aggregate welfares. A mass profile alone does not specify those finite-sample fluctuations. Keep a population scale \(N\), and the same mass profile can yield different distortion distributions at different \(N\); the proposed algorithm then runs in time polynomial in \(N\), which restores the finite electorate the mirror was meant to replace. Let \(N\) grow without bound, and the random aggregate welfares concentrate on their expectations. The objective then becomes the deterministic score problem above, rather than the sample-based expected-distortion problem of Theorem L.1.

One could still study sample access to \(\mathcal D\) in the continuous model. But that would be a question about estimating the rank-score values \(v_r\); the population masses enter only as weights in the resulting score sums. It does not recover a computational landscape driven by the continuous society. I think this is the best negative case, though it has a real weak point: the deterministic score problem is a legitimate continuous-population question, and the paper’s expected-welfare analysis gives it a natural connection. The case against rests on its lack of population-specific computational content, not on the claim that its answer is too easy or already known.
