The continuous mirror question

Given finite supports \(T_D,T_H\) of complete doctor and hospital types, rational masses \(\mu_t,\nu_s\) summing to \(1\) on each side, doctor rankings over hospital types, a proto-metric on doctor types, and strict hospital-type rankings over doctor clusters, can an algorithm running in time polynomial in \(K_D,K_H,L,1/\varepsilon\) output a finite lottery over feasible couplings \(X\) such that the expected doctor-type prospects satisfy PIIF and, with probability at least \(1-\varepsilon\), no realized coupling admits a positive-mass blocking swap?

The model it lives in

Doctor type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), hospital type \(s\) has mass \(\nu_s\), and each outcome is a coupling \(X\) with row sums \(\mu_t\) and column sums \(\nu_s\). A lottery over such couplings represents randomized assignments of individual units of mass; PIIF applies to the expected type-level prospects, and stability rules out positive-mass swaps between assignments when the doctor and hospital type both prefer the swap.

The objection that survived

The theorem’s runtime is measured in the number of named agents, so it does not establish a polynomial bound in support size and bit length; that bound remains the central new result to prove.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.2’s positive compatibility result for proto-metrics and strictly fair hospital preferences. It leaves the Gale–Shapley composition failures and the local-proposing impossibility results for unfair preferences or general metrics aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper’s proposal-and-rejection algorithms be implemented directly on rational type masses with runtime polynomial in support size and bit length?
  • Does the proposed positive-mass swap condition yield the intended continuous analogue of the paper’s approximate stability guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity version of fair and stable matching. The paper already randomizes over matchings, but its population is still a finite list of named doctors and hospitals. The proposed mirror makes the populations themselves distributions over complete preference types. The fractional quantities then describe the average share of each type assigned to each other type; every individual in the nonatomic population still receives one hospital or doctor.

A plausible regime is a large centralized placement market for a standardized cohort—for example, residents and residency positions in a particular specialty and year. Many residents may share the same complete ranking and qualification cluster, and many positions may share the same priority policy. The relevant comparison is a very large number of agents against a much smaller support of repeated types, say \(N\) agents and \(K_D,K_H\) doctor and hospital types, with \(N\gg K_D+K_H\). This is a regime-specific claim, not a claim about all residency matching. A type must include every feature the problem uses: a doctor’s full preference order and fairness-relevant attributes, or a hospital’s priority over doctor clusters. Positions in one hospital type are interchangeable to doctors; otherwise their identities would have to remain distinct types.

Lead mirror: \(\varepsilon\)-Fair-and-Stable High-Multiplicity Matching. An instance gives finite doctor and hospital type supports \(T_D,T_H\), rational masses \(\mu_t,\nu_s\) summing to \(1\) on each side, doctor rankings over hospital types, a proto-metric \(d\) on doctor types, and for each hospital type a strict ranking of doctor clusters, with indifference within a cluster. Masses and preferences are encoded in binary; let \(L\) be the input bit length. The similarity metric’s zero-distance classes are the clusters.

A matching outcome is a finite lottery over coupling matrices \(X\), each with \(X_{t,s}\geq 0\), row sums \(\mu_t\), and column sums \(\nu_s\). Each matrix is a feasible type-level mass matching and can be implemented as a measure-preserving matching of the nonatomic populations. Write \(\bar X=\mathbb{E}[X]\) and \(q_t(s)=\bar X_{t,s}/\mu_t\). For every pair of doctor types \(t,u\), require PIIF: there must be a prospect \(p_{t;u}\) over hospital types such that \(\mathrm{TV}(p_{t;u},q_u)\leq d(t,u)\) and \(q_t\succeq_t p_{t;u}\). Thus a type-level fractional assignment is the average outcome of integral individual assignments, not a change to the kind of object assigned.

For stability, call a mass-swap contract active in a realized \(X\) if some \(X_{u,s}>0\) and \(X_{t,s'}>0\), where \(t,u\) are in different clusters, type \(t\) prefers \(s\) to \(s'\), and hospital type \(s\) prefers \(t\)’s cluster to \(u\)’s. Those positive flows permit a positive mass to exchange partners profitably. A solution is \(\varepsilon\)-stable if the lottery produces no active mass-swap contract with probability at least \(1-\varepsilon\). The computational question is to output such a lottery in time polynomial in \(K_D,K_H,L,1/\varepsilon\). It is a feasibility problem: the paper gives no welfare objective, so I would not add one.

My anchor is Theorem 1.2, the paper’s informal main compatibility theorem, proved here in Section 5. It says that with a proto-metric, strictly individually fair hospital preferences, arbitrary doctor preferences, and an approximation parameter \(\tau\in(0,1)\), an efficient algorithm finds a fair and stable probabilistic allocation. The hospital-proposing variant is exactly PIIF and \(\tau\)-approximately stable; the doctor-proposing variant is \(\tau\)-approximately PIIF and \(\tau\)-approximately stable. Here approximate stability means that a blocking pair occurs with probability at most \(\tau\), and the paper states running time polynomial in \(1/\tau\). The formal analyses give round bounds of \(O(n|C|/\tau)\) and \(O(n^2/\tau)\) for the two variants. This is the paper’s strongest suitable anchor: it contains no comparable NP-hardness result.

I would expect this mirror to be Class A, under the theorem’s restrictions. The paper’s algorithms already pass probability mass through proposals and rejections, and the hospital-first result supplies the particularly clean target of exact PIIF with approximate stability. That makes a support-based mass algorithm a credible question for these authors: can their construction work directly on binary rational type masses, with complexity depending on the support rather than on the number of individuals represented? It is not automatic—the paper’s bounds depend on \(n\)—but it is a concrete computational question rather than a claim that continuity is novel. The mirror covers Theorem 1.2’s positive compatibility result, not the paper’s broader impossibility results.

The weakest point is also the reason to state the question this way. The paper’s theorem already uses randomized assignments, and its algorithms operate on probability mass; a reviewer could see a type-level continuum as merely repackaging that machinery. The case holds if the deliverable is genuinely support- and bit-length-based computation for rational masses, rather than just running the finite algorithm on an enormous population. Extending beyond proto-metrics or strictly fair hospital preferences would be further questions, not part of this lead mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1.2 is a genuine computational anchor: it gives algorithms for finding fair and stable allocations. I can’t rule out a continuous mirror on the ground that the paper asks only axiomatic questions or lacks a named algorithmic result.

The strongest objection is that the proposed compression to preference types depends on changing how the market describes preferences. In the paper, each doctor and hospital has a strict ranking over named agents on the other side. When the market grows, a doctor’s ranking among many cloned hospital positions can distinguish that doctor from others who otherwise look alike. Grouping those positions into interchangeable types requires rankings over types, with indifference among copies; scaling the market also requires type-level capacities. Those changes affect which blocking contracts can occur, so the paper’s theorem does not directly become an algorithm on a small type support.

The best repair is still plausible: model interchangeable positions as hospital types, use masses for their capacities, and retain complete rankings over types. That gives a coherent high-multiplicity matching problem, and a lottery over couplings can represent individual assignments. But then the central computational question is whether the paper’s algorithms can be compressed to run in \(\operatorname{poly}(K_D,K_H,L,1/\varepsilon)\). The stated bounds are in the number \(n\) of named agents—\(O(n|C|/\tau)\) and \(O(n^2/\tau)\)—so they do not establish that compression. The paper’s use of probability mass makes this an especially natural follow-up, while also making a substantial new result less certain.

There is also a stability choice to settle. A positive flow between two type pairs can create a blocking contract even when the amount of flow is arbitrarily small. Requiring no blocking mass at all is a sharp condition; a more quantitative bound on blocked mass may better express approximate stability, but it needs a definition the authors would accept.

So the negative case is weak. It can argue that the mirror requires a substantive type-level market model and that the paper’s runtime bounds do not yet yield a support-based algorithm. It cannot honestly defeat the repaired mirror: a large cohort matched to interchangeable position types is credible, and the resulting computational question is well-posed.
