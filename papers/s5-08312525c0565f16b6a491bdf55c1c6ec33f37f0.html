The continuous mirror question

Given buyer and seller types with rational masses summing to \(1\), rational strictly concave quadratic utilities increasing over feasible ranges, and seller generation caps, compute the unique maximizer of weighted social welfare subject to mass-weighted energy balance and the caps, together with a supporting clearing price.

The model it lives in

A price-taking microgrid with finitely many household types, each carrying rational mass \(\mu_t\); \(d_t\) and \(a_t\) are per-household allocations, and the objective weights each type's utility by \(\mu_t\), with energy balance \(\sum_{t\in B}\mu_t d_t=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t a_t\) and \(0\le a_t\le g_t\).

The objection that survived

The paper gives no runtime or convergence bound for its iterative auction, so this mirror does not inherit an algorithmic guarantee from the paper.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the price-taking efficient allocation in Proposition-4 and can extend to the fixed-surcharge allocation in Proposition-7. It leaves the price-anticipation results in Propositions 1–3 aside because individual market power vanishes in the atomless limit.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s price-taking allocation problem. The paper does not give a named P/NP-hardness result, so these anchors are equilibrium and optimization characterizations, proved in the paper and used in its simulations. That is a narrower computational foothold than a complexity theorem, but it is a real one.

Take a large islanded microgrid: many households have the same demand utility, or the same generation and retained-energy utility. Let a type encode the household’s role, utility, and, for sellers, per-household generation. If \(B\) and \(S\) are the buyer and seller types, give each type a rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_{t\in B\cup S}\mu_t=1\). In a plausible regime, thousands or millions of homes fall into a few dozen or hundred types: for example, standardized load profiles and PV-system sizes. The mass is the population share; \(d_t\) and \(a_t\) are per-household energy quantities. This uses the paper’s own setting and a regime it explicitly motivates: its introduction notes that many energy-auction agents may represent domestic units, and its analysis treats price taking as appropriate when individual agents have little market power.

My lead anchor is Proposition-4, proved here in the appendix. It says that under price taking, buyers and sellers follow the strategies in Eqs. (21)–(23); the equilibrium demands and availabilities are the unique solution to the energy-balance constraint, seller generation caps, and the social-welfare maximization in Eq. (20); and this equilibrium has no efficiency loss because it maximizes social welfare. In the continuous mirror, the underlying problem is Type-Continuous Efficient Allocation. Given the type masses, utilities, and seller generations, find the unique maximizer
\[ \max_{(d,a)\in X_\mu} \left( \sum_{t\in B}\mu_t u_t(d_t) +\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t v_t(g_t-a_t) \right), \qquad X_\mu= \left\{ (d,a)\ge 0: \sum_{t\in B}\mu_t d_t=\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t a_t,\ a_t\le g_t \right\}, \]
and return the per-type allocation and clearing price. For a finite computational input, one could encode the utilities as rational, strictly concave quadratics on the feasible ranges; the paper’s differentiable, strictly concave utility assumptions remain intact. The objective is then a concave quadratic program in a number of variables linear in the support size \(\tau\). I expect this mirror to be Class A, solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The finite-population dictionary is especially clean here: with divisible energy and identical agents within each type, the weighted allocation is the same optimization as assigning that per-capita amount to each of the \(n_t\) households, where \(\mu_t=n_t/N\).

A second, more ambitious anchor is Proposition-7, also proved here. It states that with price taking and surcharge \(p_s>0\), the buyer and seller strategies are those in Eqs. (21)–(23); the equilibrium quantities uniquely solve Eq. (36) subject to balance and seller caps; increasing aggregator revenue along the stated Pareto front entails lower social welfare; and some surcharge \(p_s^{\mathrm{OPT}}\) maximizes revenue. The corresponding problem, Type-Continuous Surcharge Design, uses the same instance and feasible set, and for each surcharge selects the unique maximizer of
\[ \Omega_{\mu,p_s}(d,a) = \sum_{t\in B}\mu_t u_t(d_t) +\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t v_t(g_t-a_t) -p_s\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t a_t. \]
It asks for a revenue-maximizing surcharge and its induced allocation, welfare, and revenue
\[ R(p_s)=p_s\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t a_t(p_s), \qquad U(p_s)= \sum_{t\in B}\mu_t u_t(d_t(p_s)) +\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t v_t(g_t-a_t(p_s)), \]
as well as the resulting revenue–welfare frontier. For the stated quadratic input family, I expect Class A: fixed-surcharge allocation is a concave quadratic program, and the remaining choice is one-dimensional. A concrete question is whether the monotone changes in which type constraints are active give a polynomial-size parametric description, so the best surcharge and frontier can be computed efficiently. Proposition-7 establishes existence, not that algorithm; for unrestricted utility-function inputs, tractability of this outer revenue search should remain an open question.

The main weakness is also the boundary of the case: Propositions 1–3, on price anticipation, are not faithful atomless mirrors of the paper’s strategic game. In a continuum, an individual household has zero market power, so price anticipation by named agents disappears into price taking. The authors might accept the price-taking mirror because they explicitly analyze that regime and connect it to large populations, but it does not continue their distinctive finite-player market-power analysis. The surcharge problem is also an extension of their allocation model, not a complexity question the paper itself asks. I would therefore lead with Proposition-4 and treat Proposition-7 as a credible secondary mirror, rather than claim that the whole paper continuizes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper never identifies a computational problem whose complexity it studies. Its propositions characterize equilibria and welfare; the auction algorithm is iterative, but the paper gives no complexity result for it. The utility functions are arbitrary differentiable concave functions, not finite computational inputs. The proponent supplies such inputs by choosing quadratics, but that choice creates a new optimization problem rather than uncovering a computational question the paper left open.

That weakens both proposed anchors. For Proposition 4, grouping identical households by type gives the stated weighted allocation problem, but its KKT conditions reduce it to finding a common clearing price: each type’s allocation follows from its marginal utility at that price, and the price makes aggregate demand equal aggregate supply. With standard finite encodings such as piecewise-linear concave utilities or quadratics, this is ordinary convex optimization. The household setting makes high multiplicity credible, but the resulting computation has no evident population-driven bottleneck for a complexity programme to investigate.

Proposition 7 adds a surcharge parameter, but at each fixed surcharge the same clearing-price calculation applies; the remaining task is a one-dimensional revenue search. For the proposed quadratic inputs, that is a parametric optimization problem whose difficulty comes from the chosen utility representation, not from making the population continuous. The proposition also establishes a revenue–welfare tradeoff and existence of a revenue-maximizing surcharge; it does not identify a computational obstacle that the type-mass formulation resolves.

The paper’s distinctive price-anticipation results, Propositions 1–3, fare worse as mirrors: in an atomless population, an individual household has no market power, so those results give way to price taking. Modeling a positive-mass type as a strategic coalition could preserve market power, but that changes the strategic unit and needs its own justification.

Still, this does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The paper’s domestic-unit setting supports a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the type-mass versions of Propositions 4 and 7 are well-posed. My negative case is strongest on programme fit: they appear to be routine convex allocation and scalar surcharge-design problems, not an unaddressed computational landscape in this paper. But “routine” is a judgment about value, not a fundamental obstruction, and I cannot honestly claim that these anchors fail under every plausible scenario.
