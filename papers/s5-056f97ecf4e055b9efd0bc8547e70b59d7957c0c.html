Theorem 1 is the closest computational result: it says ReACT-TTC terminates after finitely many rounds and returns a feasible assignment. But that is an algorithm-correctness claim, not a complexity classification. The other theorems concern mechanism properties, which the programme places outside its computational scope. So I can’t honestly provide an anchor-backed continuous problem under your stated rules.

There is a plausible *unanchored* mirror to the optimization problem in Section 3.4: define each type by a complete preference order, feasible-resource set, and initial endowment; let \(\mu_t\) be its population share; and let \(x_{t,j}\) be the mass of type \(t\) reassigned to resource \(r_j\). Maximize \(\sum_{t,j}\mathrm{Sat}_t(r_j)x_{t,j}\), subject to type-mass, resource-capacity, feasibility, and individual-rationality constraints. In a large EV fleet with relatively few such types, this is a sensible population model. With fixed satisfaction values, its stated optimization objective becomes a linear program.

But that would mirror the paper’s assignment formulation, not a numbered complexity result—and ReACT-TTC is not shown to optimize that objective. The missing anchor is therefore the decisive weakness, not the plausibility of a continuous assignment model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper supplies no admissible anchor for the requested complexity case. Its five numbered theorems establish termination and feasibility, individual rationality, Pareto optimality, core stability, and strategy-proofness. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The \(O(q_{\max}|\mathcal A^{\mathrm{nc}}|^3)\) runtime estimate appears in prose and gives no discrete-versus-continuous complexity result. Under the programme’s criteria, the proponent has not attached a continuous question to a named computational result.

Their most plausible replacement is the mass-assignment model: types include preferences, feasible resources, endowments, and satisfaction parameters; \(x_{t,j}\) records mass assigned from type \(t\) to resource \(r_j\); capacities scale with population. That is a coherent high-multiplicity scenario for repeated EV cohorts. But it changes the paper’s central contribution. The paper’s algorithm is ReACT-TTC, and its theorems concern properties of that mechanism. The proposed linear program instead maximizes aggregate satisfaction; the paper does not show ReACT-TTC optimizes that objective, and explicitly observes that Pareto-efficient choices need not maximize aggregate satisfaction. So the LP gives a plausible new problem, but it does not supply the missing anchor.

The closest attempt to repair that gap would ask for an optimal cycle-resolution order, a combinatorial challenge the paper itself identifies. Yet its cycles and overlapping resolutions are defined over individual endowed agents. A population model would need a new type-level notion of cycles and a new account of what it means to resolve them. Keeping the original cycle structure retains an atomic mechanism; moving to divisible mass yields a flow or assignment problem and no longer studies that mechanism’s cycle sequencing. A nonatomic limit would also make the paper’s individual strategy-proofness guarantee vacuous unless the model were supplemented with additional structure.

That is a case against an *anchored* mirror, not a convincing proof that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. Large cohorts of similar EV users are credible, and the mass-assignment question is sensible on its own terms. I cannot honestly defeat that stronger scenario with a fundamental objection; the negative case is strongest on the paper’s lack of a named computational result, and weak against the universal claim.
