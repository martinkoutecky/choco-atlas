The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive verdict is qualified. This paper does admit a credible continuous-population mirror, but it contains no named computational result: no theorem or proposition asserting NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), parameterized tractability, or similar. Its named results—Propositions 1 and 2, Lemma 1, and Corollary 1—are analytical. I therefore cannot honestly present this as a complexity mirror already anchored by the paper. I can, however, give a precise continuous version of its central report-choice problem.

The natural regime is a large school-choice or college-allocation market. There are \(N\) applicants, with school capacities scaling as \(q_sN\). Applicants fall into finitely many complete types \(T\), where a type includes the cardinal match values \(v\), the decreasing report-dependent utility function \(\rho\), the relevant priority or eligibility class, and the attainability distribution induced by the market environment. A society is a mass vector \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of applicants of type \(t\). This is plausible for large districts with repeated cohorts, standardized priority categories, and a finite menu of preference-intensity and rejection-aversion profiles: \(N\) may be hundreds of thousands while \(|T|=\tau\) is hundreds or thousands.

Each unit of mass still submits an ordinary integral rank-ordered list \(R\). Only the aggregate report profile is fractional. Let \(x_{t,R}\) denote the mass of type \(t\) submitting \(R\), with \(\sum_R x_{t,R}=\mu_t\). Given type \(t\)’s attainability law \(P_t\), define

\[ U_t(R)=\mathbb E_{P_t}\!\left[v_t(s_R(A))+\rho_t\!\left(R(s_R(A))\right)\right], \]

where \(s_R(A)\) is the highest-ranked attainable option in state \(A\). The aggregate objective is

\[ W(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_R x_{t,R}U_t(R). \]

This is genuinely a population continuization: agents receive integral assignments, while masses of agents and aggregate assignments are fractional. A finite election or matching market with counts \(N\mu_t\) is the corresponding high-multiplicity discretization.

My lead anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It states that, for a fixed true value vector \(v\) and a non-truthful ROL \(\widetilde R\), truthful reporting \(R\) yields at least as much utility as \(\widetilde R\) for every decreasing report-dependent function \(\rho\) if and only if, for every rank \(r\),

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{r} \left[ \Pr(A_i=1,A_j=0\ \forall j<i) - \Pr(A_{\widetilde i}=1,A_{\widetilde j}=0\ \forall j<i) \right]\ge 0. \]

Thus the truthful lottery must first-order stochastically dominate the lottery induced by the deviation in the cumulative probability of receiving one of the first \(r\) reported positions. The proposition further says that truthfulness is optimal for every decreasing \(\rho\) if and only if these inequalities hold against every non-truthful ROL.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuum Universal-Honesty. Given a finite type set \(T\), mass vector \(\mu\), values \(v_t\), and attainability laws \(P_t\), decide whether the truthful mass profile

\[ x^T_{t,R_t}=\mu_t,\qquad x^T_{t,R}=0\quad(R\ne R_t) \]

is a best-response profile for every choice of strictly decreasing functions \(\rho_t\). If the answer is yes, output the cumulative-dominance inequalities

\[ F_{t,r}(R_t)\ge F_{t,r}(R) \]

for every type \(t\), alternative report \(R\), and rank \(r\), where \(F_{t,r}(R)\) is the probability under \(P_t\) of being assigned within the first \(r\) positions of \(R\). If the answer is no, output \(t,R,r\) witnessing a violated inequality and a decreasing \(\rho_t\) for which the positive mass \(\mu_t\) strictly benefits from deviating.

This is recognisably the authors’ problem: it preserves their two utility components, their ROL action, their attainability beliefs, and their notion of strategy-proofness. It merely asks for the exact mass of agents choosing each report. In the exogenous-\(P_t\) version, I would expect a Class A result: the aggregate layer is a linear optimization problem, and the only serious issue is whether the best-report or separation problem over permutations can be solved efficiently. If \(P_t\) is supplied through a structured matching mechanism, that pricing question becomes the computational core.

A second useful anchor is Proposition 1, proved in the paper. It states that for every ROL \(\widetilde R\), there is an infinite set of attainability distributions \(P\) such that \(\widetilde R\) is strictly optimal for every report-independent utility vector \(v\) and every strictly decreasing \(\rho\). In the continuous mirror, take a positive-mass block of exchangeable applicants and ask:

Continuum Strict-Report Support. Given \(\mu\), a target report \(\widetilde R\), values \(v_t\), report-dependent utilities \(\rho_t\), and attainability laws \(P_t\), decide whether the all-\(\widetilde R\) profile is the unique mass-optimal profile:

\[ U_t(\widetilde R)>U_t(R) \quad\text{for every }t\text{ with }\mu_t>0 \text{ and every }R\ne\widetilde R. \]

A solution is the target mass profile together with the strict utility margins; a negative answer supplies a type and profitable deviation. Proposition 1 says that, for every target ROL, there are infinitely many belief environments in which the answer is yes—even when the target conflicts with every applicant’s report-independent ranking. This is a meaningful continuous question because it asks whether a positive mass of otherwise exchangeable applicants can rationally self-select into the same non-truthful report.

I would also expect this fixed-belief problem to be Class A when reports and attainability states are explicitly represented, although implicit rankings again create a permutation-pricing problem. Any hardness found in that pricing layer would concern the report space or the mechanism’s attainability structure, not the mere replacement of \(N\) agents by mass.

The natural extension, and the main further question, is to make \(P_t\) endogenous: given school capacities, priority distributions, and the aggregate report masses \(x\), derive the attainability laws generated by continuum deferred acceptance and require

\[ x_{t,R}>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R\in\arg\max_{R'}U_t(R';P_t(x)). \]

That would be a genuine mean-field equilibrium problem. The paper does not analyze it, but its propositions provide pointwise conditions that any such equilibrium must satisfy.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper studies one participant’s decision problem with an exogenously fixed attainability distribution. It does not compute a matching, characterize a continuum equilibrium, or prove a complexity bound. A referee could therefore say that the fixed-\(P\) mirror merely repeats the individual problem \(\mu_t\) times. I agree that this makes the positive case weaker than the programme’s best examples. Still, the mirror is not a change of outcome space or a superficial fractionalization: the agents themselves become a high-multiplicity population, and the central computational question is whether the aggregate report profile and its attainability laws can be computed or certified efficiently.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the programme’s eligibility gate: this paper contains no named computational result. Propositions 1 and 2, Lemma 1, and Corollary 1 are analytical characterizations of one participant’s optimal report. There is no theorem about complexity, algorithms, approximation, parametrization, or computation. Thus the proposed mirrors are new computational problems inspired by the paper, not continuizations of a computational contribution.

Even setting that aside, the fixed-\(P_t\) mirrors do not make the population computationally relevant. The proposed aggregate objective is

\[ W(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_R x_{t,R}U_t(R), \qquad \sum_R x_{t,R}=\mu_t. \]

Because \(U_t(R)\) is independent of \(x\), this decomposes completely:

\[ \max_x W(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\max_R U_t(R). \]

Universal honesty is therefore equivalent to checking, separately for every positive-mass type, whether its truthful report maximizes the original one-person utility. The magnitude of \(\mu_t\) is irrelevant; only whether it is zero matters. Replicating each type \(N\mu_t\) times produces no new population problem, just \(N\) independent copies of the same decision problem. There is no aggregate feasibility constraint, mass transfer, market-clearing condition, or objective in which the continuum changes the object being computed.

This defeats the proposed mirror of Proposition 2 even in its stronger form. One may ask for all cumulative-dominance inequalities, quantify over every decreasing \(\rho\), and search over all reports. But with \(P_t\) fixed, that remains a collection of individual stochastic-dominance tests. If the attainability law is explicitly represented, the question is simply the proposition restated for each type. If \(P_t\) is implicit, any difficulty comes from the chosen encoding of the attainability distribution or from enumerating reports; the paper provides no structured pricing problem or computational representation from which a complexity result could be inherited. Calling that an exponential-variable LP does not create one: no population-level LP is present.

Proposition 1 is weaker still. The proposed “unique mass-optimal profile” is unique precisely when

\[ U_t(\widetilde R)>U_t(R) \]

for every relevant type and every alternative report. That is just the individual strict-optimality condition repeated over positive-mass types. The mass profile adds no combinatorial content. A stronger version could ask for a belief law making \(\widetilde R\) optimal uniformly over all \(v\) and all decreasing \(\rho\), or could characterize the whole set of such laws. But this remains a pointwise question about \(P_t\). The paper’s infinite family of belief distributions does not become a population result merely because many exchangeable participants hold those beliefs.

The only serious rescue is the proposed endogenous construction \(P_t=P_t(x)\), using continuum deferred acceptance and a fixed-point condition. That could be a worthwhile mean-field matching project, but it is no longer a mirror of either proposition. The paper holds other participants’ reports and the attainability distribution fixed and studies one agent’s best response. The endogenous version introduces a new strategic game, market-clearing map, equilibrium concept, priority model, capacity scaling, and possibly common shocks. The paper supplies none of these.

There is also a genuine continuum hazard. In a standard atomless matching limit with deterministic type masses and per-capita capacities, aggregate cutoffs are often deterministic and an individual has zero influence on them. The paper’s central object—a nontrivial distribution over which options are attainable—can then collapse to deterministic attainability. To preserve the uncertainty that drives self-selection, one must add common market shocks, random aggregate states, or some other extra source of uncertainty. That may be sensible, but it is a newly designed stochastic mean-field model rather than a consequence of the paper’s continuization.

So the proponent’s fixed-belief anchors fail: the population is merely a weighting device, and the proposed computational content is either the original individual problem or an unspecified representation issue. The endogenous extension is the one place where a natural continuous model might be built, but it would be new matching-equilibrium research without a computational theorem in this paper to anchor it. I therefore find the negative verdict convincing for this paper under ChoCo’s computational-anchor standard, while admitting that the absolute claim that no interesting mean-field school-choice model could ever be developed is a modelling judgment, not something the paper itself can prove.
