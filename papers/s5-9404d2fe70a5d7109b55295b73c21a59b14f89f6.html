The strongest case against a worthwhile mirror is that the paper supplies no qualifying computational result to anchor one. Equations (18) and (22) formulate stochastic and cooperative network problems, and the authors describe converting them to mixed integer programs and solving an example. But they state no complexity result or general algorithmic theorem. Equation (23) defines the Shapley value; Table 5 reports a calculation for three distributors. Neither is a named computational result of the kind the analysts were asked to mirror. The proponent was right not to invent an anchor.

Their suggested mirror—many distributors grouped by operating profile, with type-level coalition values and a limiting profit allocation—does not fill that gap. A distributor’s complete type would have to include its capacities, facility costs, routes to particular retailers, and demand exposure. In this model, those network-specific details help determine which coalitions are feasible. Grouping distributors by a coarser profile could change the game; requiring exact profiles may leave little multiplicity. A large network of repeated regional operators is plausible, but its scaling assumptions would need to be specified.

There is a harder version: model a continuum of retailer types and preserve their demand and transport relationships. But a retailer’s costs depend on its links to other retailers, and demand uncertainty enters through scenarios. A useful continuum model would need to specify how those relationships and joint scenarios aggregate. The paper does not provide that model or a computational result about it.

The Shapley allocation adds another modeling choice. Its finite-player formula averages marginal contributions over player orders. With a continuum population, one must choose how finite games approach the limit and how individual payoffs become an allocation density. The paper does not choose such a construction; supplying one would be a substantial extension rather than a direct mirror of a result it proves.

So the negative case is strongest at the assignment’s anchor gate: there is no named computational result here for the proponent’s mirror to survive or for this analyst to defeat. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question could be built from the model. A continuum logistics model of repeated retailers or distributors is imaginable, but it would require additional structure, and this paper gives little reason to think it is already a natural, well-posed computational mirror.
