The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result that qualifies as an anchor under your criteria. Its numbered propositions and lemma establish choice consistency, representation, and axiomatic characterizations; none classifies a problem’s complexity or gives an algorithm or a formulation used to compute a solution.

The closest result is Proposition 4, proved here: if an aggregation rule on strict individual orderings satisfies unrestricted domain, neutrality, strong neutrality, anonymity, Pareto, and endogenous independence of irrelevant alternatives, then the rule is Borda. Each voter gives \(0\) points to their lowest-ranked state, \(1\) to the next, and so on; states are socially ranked by their aggregate points. That is a substantial characterization, but it is not a computational result.

A plausible adjacent population question would be: given a candidate set \(C\) and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over strict rankings, compute the Borda winner set
\[ \arg\max_{c\in C}\sum_t \mu_t\bigl(m-\operatorname{pos}_t(c)\bigr). \]
Here types are rankings, mass is the share of the population holding each ranking, and a large electorate with many voters but relatively few distinct ballots is a credible regime. The exact winners can be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length. This extends the Borda rule to population mass, but it turns Proposition 4’s axiomatic characterization into a routine evaluation problem. I would not present it as a mirror of a computational result from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest proposed anchor is Proposition 4, but it characterizes an aggregation rule; it does not state a computational problem or result. Its hypotheses concern which social rankings are possible under axioms, and its conclusion identifies Borda. Giving the population a continuous description does not turn that characterization into an algorithmic task.

The most natural population version is sound: let \(\mu\) distribute mass over strict rankings and rank states by their expected Borda scores, \(\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathrm{score}_t(x)\). This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime for elections, and the mass interpretation can be clearer than voter counts. It also yields an immediate evaluation task: with explicit alternatives and a support of \(\tau\) rankings, calculate the weighted scores and sort them. That task comes from Borda’s definition; Proposition 4 neither formulates it as an open computational question nor supplies a hidden optimization problem for continuization to expose.

One could ask how Proposition 4’s axioms behave over an atomless population. That would put the population on a continuum, while keeping the inquiry axiomatic—the paper’s subject, but outside ChoCo’s computational remit. Or one could add manipulation, robustness, or succinctly represented alternatives. Those may lead to worthwhile computational questions, but they introduce an intervention, encoding, or objective absent from the paper. The proponent’s anchor therefore does not survive as a computational mirror.

The negative case has a clear limit: this paper gives no basis for ruling out every useful future algorithmic project built around Borda. The claim that none could ever be worthwhile is broader than the evidence supports. What the paper itself offers is a strong case against a mirror of its results: the only named central result is axiomatic, and its natural continuous-population extension yields no computational problem that the paper leaves open.
