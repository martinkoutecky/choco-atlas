The strongest lead for a follow-on is Result 2: in the complete-information experiment, expected per-capita payoff is higher under TTC than under DA or BOS, while DA and BOS are not significantly different; comparing information conditions, payoff rises under TTC and BOS and is unchanged under DA. That is an empirical finding, not a complexity anchor, and the paper supplies no theorem suggesting its ordering should persist in a continuum.

A plausible large-cohort setting would be a school district assigning many students across a fixed set of schools. A type would specify everything relevant to the student’s preferences, payoff, district, and priority class; mass would be the fraction of applicants of that type; school capacities would scale with the cohort. Type-level reports could feed continuous versions of BOS, DA, or TTC, and the resulting school-assignment flows could be evaluated by average payoff and blocking mass. The paper’s unique student payoffs and individual lottery priorities make that compression a real modeling choice, though: the type count may grow with the cohort unless many students share those attributes.

That gives a reasonable motivation for studying continuum school-choice outcomes, but not yet a paper-anchored computational question. In particular, the experiment’s individual strategic decisions do not carry over automatically: in a nonatomic model a single student has no effect on aggregate allocations, so a truth-telling equilibrium question may lose the strategic content the authors measured. This is the weakest point, and it prevents me from making a stronger honest case from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Results 1–3 are experimental comparisons of reported preferences, payoffs, and blocking pairs. None states a complexity claim or an algorithmic problem. Result 2—the proponent’s best anchor—is a finding about average payoffs from human subjects’ reports under particular experimental conditions.

A sensible continuous population of students is easy to describe: give each type a preference order, payoff vector, district, and priority information; scale school capacities with population; then measure average payoff and blocking mass under DA, TTC, or BOS. That is a plausible high-multiplicity school-choice model. But computing those measures for a given distribution is not the question this paper asks. To make it a computational problem, one must add an objective or intervention—for example, minimizing the mass that must change its report to reach a payoff target—or specify a model of strategic behavior. Those additions may support worthwhile research, but they supply the computational question the paper itself lacks.

The stronger proposed mirror also runs into a mismatch with the experiment. Its students have distinct payoff rows and positions in a realized lottery, both relevant to outcomes. Grouping students only by ranking loses information needed to evaluate the reported dollar payoffs; including every relevant feature makes the experimental types essentially individual. A large district with repeated payoff and priority classes, or with a separately modelled lottery, is a reasonable new setting. Yet the paper’s information effects concern how individuals respond after learning the whole profile. Type masses alone do not specify those responses. An equilibrium or learning model would have to be added, and the paper supplies no such model. Results 1 and 3 face the same issue: continuous truth-telling and blocking-mass measures can be defined, but they remain outcome statistics unless paired with a new computational task.

So the negative case is strongest as a verdict on this paper: its empirical contribution gives ChoCo no computational anchor, and its best continuous restatement becomes a different study once made computational. The universal claim is weaker. Large school districts are a convincing high-multiplicity regime, and a continuous computational study of school assignment could be worthwhile on its own. I would not claim that no such study is possible; I would claim that this paper does not establish one as its continuous mirror.
