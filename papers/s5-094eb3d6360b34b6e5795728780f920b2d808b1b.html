The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I can defend is a large-market version of the paper’s almost-budget-balanced SSVCG rebate design. The paper’s divisible good is continuous only in the outcome space, which by itself is outside ChoCo’s scope. The mirror I propose makes the population continuous: many agents are represented by masses of complete types.

The natural regime is a large resource-sharing system, such as an ISP serving millions of subscribers drawn from a small catalogue of service and utility classes. A type \(t\) includes the agent’s entire valuation function and every other parameter the mechanism uses. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite type set \(T\), with \(\tau=|T|\) fixed or small while the number \(N\) of agents grows. To keep per-agent resource competition meaningful, the total capacity scales with the population; this is the per-capita-supply co-modification allowed by the programme. The mass \(\mu_t\) then describes the fraction of subscribers of type \(t\).

My anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper. For the surrogate valuation \(v(a,\theta)=\theta U(a)\), with \(U\) satisfying Assumption 2, the authors express their worst-case linear-rebate problem as an uncertain convex program. The variables \(x_i=\sum_{j=2}^i c_j\) encode the rebate coefficients, and \(x_n\) is the objective value. The normalized bid vectors \(\theta\) index constraints encoding budget feasibility and the worst-case surplus objective; voluntary participation supplies the remaining constraints. Theorem 6 proves that this specific program satisfies Assumption 3: its objective is Lipschitz, its constraint map is uniformly Lipschitz in the uncertainty parameter, and violations bound distance to the feasible set. Together with Theorem 5, also proved here, this means an \(\varepsilon\)-cover of the bid-constraint set yields a sampled convex program whose optimum is within \(K_1K_2K_3\varepsilon\) of the original optimum. This is an approximation result, not a polynomial-time classification. The paper has no named NP-hardness or standard complexity-class result to anchor on.

Here is the continuous-population problem I would ask a prover to study: Continuum SSVCG Rebate Design. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), true valuations \(v_t\), a common surrogate \(U\), and an accuracy target \(\varepsilon>0\). A report profile is a joint distribution \(\lambda\) over types and scalar bids, with type marginal \(\mu\); its bid marginal is \(\nu_\lambda\). Thus a positive mass of agents may share a type but submit different bids.

For a reported population \(\lambda\), the planner chooses allocations to maximize reported surrogate welfare, \(W(\lambda)=\max_a \int b\,U(a(t,b))\,d\lambda(t,b)\), subject to \(\int a(t,b)\,d\lambda(t,b)\leq 1\) and \(0\leq a(t,b)\leq 1\). This is per-capita resource allocation: a finite approximation has \(N\) agents and \(N\) units of capacity. Let \(P_\lambda(t,b)\) be the tagged agent’s limiting Clarke externality payment: the first-order loss in the other agents’ optimized reported welfare when an infinitesimal agent of type \(t\) and bid \(b\) is added, excluding that agent’s own reported value. Define it by the right derivative of the corresponding leave-one-in calculation. This is the continuum counterpart of the paper’s finite difference payment.

Restrict the mirror to anonymous linear rebates in their natural large-population form. Choose an integrable weight function \(\rho\) on \([0,1]\). If \(Q_{\nu_\lambda}\) is the decreasing bid quantile function, each agent receives the limiting rebate \(R_\rho(\nu_\lambda)=\int_0^1 \rho(q)Q_{\nu_\lambda}(q)\,dq\). This comes from finite rebates whose coefficient at rank \(j\) is approximately \(\rho(j/N)/N\); removing one agent changes the limiting quantile distribution by zero mass.

The problem is to find a rebate weight \(\rho\) that satisfies, for every admissible \(\lambda\), weak budget balance, \(\int P_\lambda(t,b)\,d\lambda(t,b)-R_\rho(\nu_\lambda)\geq0\), and voluntary participation for every type and bid, \(v_t(a_\lambda(t,b))-P_\lambda(t,b)+R_\rho(\nu_\lambda)\geq0\). Among such weights, minimize the worst-case retained-surplus ratio, \(\sup_{\lambda:W(\lambda)>0}\bigl(\int P_\lambda\,d\lambda-R_\rho(\nu_\lambda)\bigr)/W(\lambda)\). A solution is a feasible, explicitly represented \(\rho\) whose ratio is within \(\varepsilon\) of the infimum. This keeps the paper’s scalar reports, surrogate-welfare allocation, pivotal-payment logic, anonymity, feasibility, participation, and Moulin-style objective. The new object is the population distribution; the quantile rebate is the limit of the paper’s rank-based rebate.

My forecast is Class A for approximation at fixed type support, though only tentatively: a quantile-grid formulation may reduce the problem to finite convex programs, with complexity depending on \(\tau\) and \(\varepsilon\). That is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 6. Further questions include whether the directional externality payment is the limit of the finite SSVCG payment; whether finite rebate coefficients have a useful limit; and whether the theorem’s approximation constants remain controlled as \(N\) grows. This mirror covers the paper’s worst-case rebate-design result and its deterministic constraint-approximation method. It does not mirror the equilibrium results attributed to Johari and Tsitsiklis or the paper’s other proposed extensions.

The weakest point is the population limit itself. A finite agent’s pivotal payment is a leave-one-out difference; the mirror uses its derivative. The paper gives no proof that this derivative preserves the relevant strategic or budget properties. Nor does Theorem 6 provide bounds uniform in \(N\): its constraint space is \(N\)-dimensional, while the limit has a function-valued rebate. So the theorem motivates the mirror and its approximation question, but does not establish that the mirror is tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed mirror’s capacity scaling makes its specific Theorem 6 anchor collapse. Its society has mass \(1\), allocations satisfy \(a(t,b)\leq 1\), and total capacity is \(1\). Giving every agent allocation \(1\) is feasible, so the strictly increasing surrogate utility leaves no resource scarcity. In the stated finite approximation—\(N\) agents and \(N\) units—the same is true: every agent gets \(1\), and each Clarke payment is zero. The corresponding worst-case rebate problem has the trivial optimum \(\rho=0\). Defining \(P_\lambda\) instead by *adding* an infinitesimal agent can produce a nonzero insertion effect at this boundary, but that is not the limit of the paper’s leave-one-out payment.

A better version would use capacity \(\kappa N\) for \(N\) agents, with \(0<\kappa<1\), and \(\int a\,d\lambda\leq\kappa\) in the limit. That preserves scarcity. But it exposes a second mismatch. With a fixed catalogue of \(\tau\) complete types, identical agents in the natural pure-equilibrium regime have the same bid, so the equilibrium bid distribution has at most \(\tau\) levels. The paper’s \(n\)-rank uncertainty then largely disappears from the equilibrium objective. If the mirror instead takes a worst case over arbitrary within-type bid distributions, as the proposed \(\lambda\) does, it has changed that objective from equilibrium-based worst-case design to robustness over all reports. That may be a useful problem, but Theorems 5 and 6 do not establish its complexity or approximation guarantees; their constants are not shown to stay controlled as \(n\) grows.

I would not argue that the paper has no computational result to mirror: its uncertain-program approximation is computational content, and its prior work on divisible goods is not an existing continuous-population solution. The case against is narrower: Theorem 6’s rank-based result does not transfer to the proposed fixed-type population model without either becoming trivial under the stated capacity or changing the worst-case criterion under a scarcity-preserving repair.

That is a solid objection to this anchor as stated, but it does not defeat every plausible mirror. A \(\kappa<1\) fluid model with finitely represented types and rebates could still pose a worthwhile limit and approximation problem. I can’t honestly support the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile continuous mirror.
