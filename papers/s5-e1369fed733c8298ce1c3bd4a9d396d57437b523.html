The continuous mirror question

Given \(τ\) types with positive rational masses \(μ_t\) summing to \(1\) and strictly positive rational valuation vectors \(v_t\) over \(m\) resources, compute per-person bundles \(x_t\in[0,1]^m\) maximizing \(λ\), subject to \(λ\leq\sum_j v_{tj}x_{tj}\) for every type and \(\sum_tμ_t x_{tj}=1\) for every resource. Also compute rational anonymous prices \(p_j\) and per-person type budgets \(b_t=p\cdot x_t\) such that every affordable bundle \(z\in[0,1]^m\) with \(p\cdot z\leq b_t\) satisfies \(v_t\cdot z\leq v_t\cdot x_t\). Is this computable in time polynomial in \(m,τ\), and the input bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A continuum market with finite support over consumer types: type \(t\) has mass \(μ_t\), valuation \(v_t\), and a common per-person bundle \(x_t\); each resource has supply \(1\) per unit of population. Maximize the worst type’s per-person utility, with mass-weighted resource clearing, and output anonymous prices and type budgets supporting the bundles.

What the mirror covers

Theorem 4.3 is covered by the type-mass max-min problem. Proposition 3.17 has a separate mirror when types share both valuations and prescribed bundles; the proposed mirror does not establish its full arbitrary-allocation scope or cover the special-case running times in Theorems 4.4 and 4.5.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper’s support construction be adapted to mass-weighted resource constraints in time polynomial in \(m,τ,L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the market’s population, not its divisible goods. Imagine a large utility or public-resource market serving \(N\) consumers in a few repeated cohorts: all members of a cohort have the same additive valuation vector, and the allocation plan gives them the same per-person bundle. Let \(\mu_t\) be cohort \(t\)’s share of the population, with \(\tau\ll N\) cohorts. Normalize each resource’s supply to one unit per unit of population. The goods remain divisible as in the paper; the continuous object is the distribution of consumers over types.

My lead anchor is Proposition 3.17, proved in this paper. It says that for any Pareto-optimal allocation, there are anonymous item prices and agent budgets that support that allocation, and that the prices and budgets can be computed efficiently. Supporting means each agent’s assigned bundle is among their utility-maximizing affordable bundles. This is the paper’s main result in its most direct form.

Call the mirror CE-Support\(_\infty\). Its input is \(m\) resources and a support list of \(\tau\) types, each with rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), a strictly positive rational valuation vector \(v_t\), and a prescribed rational per-person bundle \(a_t\in[0,1]^m\). The target allocation is promised Pareto efficient, with \(\sum_t\mu_t a_{tj}=1\) for each resource \(j\). Because the support problem distinguishes agents by both their valuations and their requested bundles, a type here records the pair \((v_t,a_t)\). The task is to output rational anonymous prices \(p_j\) and a budget \(b_t\) for each type, so that \(p\cdot a_t=b_t\), and every affordable bundle \(z\in[0,1]^m\) with \(p\cdot z\le b_t\) satisfies \(v_t\cdot a_t\ge v_t\cdot z\). The desired running time is polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the binary input length \(L\), including the masses.

I expect this question to be tractable. The paper’s cycle elimination, tree pricing, and price-scaling LP give a concrete route to adapting the support construction to weighted cohorts. That adaptation needs to handle mass explicitly; it is not already proved by Proposition 3.17. The useful computational question is whether the construction can operate on the \(\tau\) cohort types rather than expanding the market into \(N\) named consumers.

A second anchor, also proved here, is Theorem 4.3. It says that a max-min allocation can be computed efficiently, together with item prices and agent budgets that make the allocation a best response for every agent. Its mirror is MaxMin\(_\infty\): given rational masses \(\mu_t\) and strictly positive rational valuation vectors \(v_t\), find per-type bundles \(x_t\) and a value \(\lambda\) maximizing \(\lambda\), subject to \(x_{tj}\in[0,1]\), \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le1\), and \(\lambda\le\sum_jv_{tj}x_{tj}\) for every supported type. Also output prices and type budgets supporting those bundles in the same demand sense. I expect this to be tractable: the max-min allocation is an \(O(\tau m)\)-size LP, and the further question is whether the support construction has the same type- and bit-length dependence. It also invites questions about equal-budget support and how the required budget diversity depends on the target allocation.

These are algorithmic anchors, not hardness anchors: the paper gives no named NP-hardness or other hardness classification to mirror. So this case does not claim that a discrete hardness result dissolves in the continuum. It asks whether the paper’s efficient equilibrium-support and Rawlsian-allocation results extend to a high-multiplicity representation of the population.

The main weakness is the regime restriction needed to keep \(\tau\) small. For arbitrary-allocation support, consumers in one type must share a prescribed per-person bundle as well as a valuation vector; if the target varies idiosyncratically, types may proliferate until \(\tau\) is near \(N\). The mirror also scales resource supplies with population, which is plausible for a growing market but changes the paper’s unit-supply normalization. Those choices narrow the regime, but preserve the authors’ underlying question: can an arbitrary Pareto-efficient market allocation be supported by anonymous prices and assigned budgets? This does not claim novelty from fractional goods or continuous optimization alone. It makes the society itself a mass distribution and asks for the computational consequences of that representation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to Proposition 3.17’s generality. Its input is an allocation \(x\), and its construction tracks agents individually. To compress it, the proposed mirror puts both a valuation vector and a prescribed bundle in each type. That gives a valid repeated-cohort regime when many consumers share both, but it does not compress arbitrary Pareto-optimal allocations: if similar consumers receive different bundles, the type count can grow to \(N\). Grouping only by valuation does not fix this. A common type budget and prices cannot generally support different utilities for identical consumers, even though such allocations can be Pareto optimal. So the headline result’s full scope does not carry over to a population described only by preference types.

That objection does not defeat the better, cohort-level version. Repeated consumers with the same valuations and assigned bundles are a coherent high-multiplicity instance, and the mass-weighted feasibility constraints make sense. The paper’s algorithm is written for individual agents, so a polynomial-time result in \((m,\tau,L)\) still needs to be established. But the proposed setting is not ruled out by the dependence on identity; it restricts the target allocation to a regime where identity does not matter.

Theorem 4.3 is harder to oppose. Its max-min mirror is a well-posed LP over per-type bundles, with \(\lambda\) constrained for each supported type and resource use weighted by \(\mu_t\). When every supported type has positive mass, minimizing over types matches minimizing over the represented population. Divisible goods and per-capita supplies are coherent model choices here. The resulting problem may be a straightforward high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s finite-agent algorithm, but that is not a substantive objection under this programme’s standard.

So the negative case is weak. Proposition 3.17 has a real limitation: its arbitrary-allocation claim does not compress unless the target allocation itself has repeated structure. But the paper offers a natural repeated-cohort regime, and Theorem 4.3 yields an especially clean population mirror. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror survives.
