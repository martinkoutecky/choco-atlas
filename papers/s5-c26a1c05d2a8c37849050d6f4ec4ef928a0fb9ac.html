The continuous mirror question

Given a unit-mass society of one interchangeable type, a measurable coalition \(C\) of mass \(q\in(0,1]\), common singleton value \(u\), and transferable coalition value \(V\ge qu\), find a measurable payoff \(x:C\to\mathbb R\) with \(x(a)\ge u\) and \(\int_C x(a)\,da=V\) that maximizes \(\frac1q\int_C\log(x(a)-u)\,da\) when \(V>qu\); when \(V=qu\), return \(x(a)=u\).

The model it lives in

One type \(t\) has society mass \(\mu_t=1\). A coalition is a measurable subset \(C\) of mass \(q\), with singleton value \(u\) and coalition value \(V\); the decision is a measurable per-capita payoff \(x\), and the objective is to maximize the geometric mean of surplus payoffs subject to individual rationality and total payoff \(V\).

The objection that survived

The proposed optimization drops the finite-agent concession protocol, so Theorem 2.8 does not establish that the protocol itself has this nonatomic behavior.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow, and the paper has no numbered result that meets your anchor rule strictly. Its only theorem is Theorem 2.8, proved in this paper: for a coalition \(C\), Endriss’s protocol with the PI strategy and egocentric concessions is equivalent to giving each member their singleton value plus an equal share of \(C\)’s surplus. That is a useful algorithmic simplification, but it asserts no complexity class. The \(\Delta_2^P\)-completeness statement about checking the \(\epsilon\)-core is cited from [8], not stated as a numbered result here; the protocol’s performance results are experimental. So I would not claim this paper establishes a Class A, B, or C result.

The best narrow mirror I can offer treats Theorem 2.8 as a weak algorithmic anchor. Consider a continuum of interchangeable prosumers in a microgrid: each has the same singleton value \(u\), and a coalition of mass \(q\) produces total value \(V(q)\), with \(V(q)\ge qu\). The type is the complete payoff-relevant prosumer description; mass \(q\) is the share of the population in the coalition. A plausible regime has many prosumers and one such type, so \(\tau=1\).

Call the problem Continuous Coalition Surplus Division. Given rational \(q>0\), \(u\), and \(V(q)\), find a measurable payoff \(x(a)\) for coalition members that is individually rational, satisfies \(\int_C x(a)\,da=V(q)\), and maximizes the geometric mean of their surplus payoffs,
\[ \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{q}\int_C \log(x(a)-u)\,da\right). \]
A solution is \(x(a)=u+(V(q)-qu)/q\) almost everywhere; if the surplus is zero, \(x(a)=u\). This is the continuum counterpart of the equal-surplus division in Theorem 2.8. It is tractable by a closed-form calculation, assuming \(V(q)\) can be evaluated efficiently. That tractability is a consequence of the proposed mirror’s structure, not a complexity result proved by the paper.

The mirror covers only coalition-internal surplus division. It does not mirror the paper’s empirical claim that WRC-Coalitions performs well, or the distributed search over coalition structures. Further questions include how division changes with multiple types and differing singleton values, and whether coalition formation and \(\epsilon\)-core stability admit a continuous computational treatment.

The weakest point is substantial: Theorem 2.8 is a subsidiary allocation result, and the continuous question is correspondingly small. The paper’s central contribution is a protocol for finite-agent coalition formation, tested on games with 8 and 10 agents. Extending that protocol to a continuum would require a model of mass-based coalition structures and a corresponding negotiation process that the paper does not provide. There is also a normalization issue to clarify for heterogeneous singleton values: the protocol defines PI using products of gains, while the proof proceeds as if the negotiation product were over surplus shares. The single-type mirror avoids that ambiguity, but does not resolve it for the theorem’s general statement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its only theorem, Theorem 2.8, claims an equivalence between a finite-agent bargaining protocol and equal division of coalition surplus. The \(\Delta_2^P\)-completeness result for checking the \(\epsilon\)-core is cited from other work, and the protocol’s performance claims are experimental. So the paper supplies no computational result for ChoCo to continuize.

The proposed Continuous Coalition Surplus Division is a coherent population model, but it does not turn Theorem 2.8 into a computational question. It replaces the theorem’s negotiation process with an optimization over measurable payoffs, while leaving out coalition-structure search, concessions, and the agents’ proposal histories—the paper’s main contribution. It asks for an aggregate allocation rule, not for a complexity or algorithmic result about the protocol. There is also a caveat in the theorem’s stated generality: PI is defined using products of total gains, while the proof applies the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality to surplus shares. Equal singleton values make the proponent’s symmetric case safer, but also narrow it to a standard equal-division setting.

A stronger mirror might model a large microgrid with finitely many prosumer types and masses, give each coalition value as a function of its type-mass vector, and compute a welfare-maximizing or \(\epsilon\)-stable allocation. That would make mass meaningful. But it requires an anonymous value function and mass-based deviations that the paper does not define. Its game instead permits arbitrary values \(v(C)\) for named subsets, and its protocol tracks individual proposals and blacklists. The authors’ random games likewise assign values to coalitions, not to type-mass vectors. Building the typed model could be worthwhile research, but its central model and computational question would need to be supplied from outside this paper.

The negative case is therefore strong against this paper as a ChoCo anchor: it offers no named computational result to mirror, and its theorem supports only a narrow bargaining identity. The universal claim is less secure. The paper’s microgrid motivation makes a high-multiplicity typed model plausible; what is missing here is a reason to call that model a continuous mirror of the paper’s results.
