What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers Theorem 1's party representativeness indicators and their use to compare candidate profiles. It leaves the coalition analysis, including Theorem 2, and the broader election-procedure proposals aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s evaluation of how well party positions represent public opinion. The regime is a large electorate answering a fixed agenda of policy questions. A voter type is their complete yes/no answer profile, \(\mathbf t\in\{-1,+1\}^{Q}\); its mass \(\rho_t\) is the fraction of the electorate with that profile. In a national electorate, the number of voters can be in the millions while the number of distinct profiles is bounded by \(2^{|Q|}\), and repeated profiles are plausible. The agenda weights \(\omega_q\) describe the importance assigned to questions; they are separate from population mass.

My anchor is Theorem 1, “Computing the indicators and their geometric interpretation,” proved in this paper and used to calculate the party indicators. It is an exact computational result, though not a theorem classifying the problem as polynomial-time or hard. Given a society’s per-question opinion balance \(a_q\), candidate answer matrix \(B=(b_{qc})\), and normalized agenda weights \(\omega\), it computes the representativeness matrix, popularity, and universality as
\[ R=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\operatorname{diag}(a)B,\qquad P=\omega^\top R=\tfrac12+\tfrac12(\omega\odot a)^\top B, \]
\[ U=\omega^\top\operatorname{round}(R) =\tfrac12+\tfrac12\omega^\top\delta_a +\tfrac12(\omega\odot\operatorname{sign}(a))^\top B. \]
Here \(\delta_a\) marks questions on which public opinion is tied. Thus popularity is the weighted average share represented, and universality is the weighted frequency with which a candidate represents at least half the society. The theorem also gives the geometric interpretation: candidates’ popularity and universality are determined by projections of their opinion vectors onto the weighted balance vector and weighted majority vector, respectively, with a tie correction for universality.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous Manifesto-Representativeness Evaluation. An instance contains a finite agenda \(Q\) with rational weights \(\omega_q\) summing to one; candidate positions \(b_{qc}\in\{-1,+1\}\); and a list of the \(\tau\) voter profiles with positive rational masses \(\rho_t\) summing to one. For each candidate, compute the exact popularity and universality and return the candidates attaining the maximum under either measure. For a profile distribution, the balance is \(a_q=\sum_t\rho_t t_q\), and the share represented on issue \(q\) is
\[ r_{qc}=\sum_{t:t_q=b_{qc}}\rho_t =\tfrac12+\tfrac12a_qb_{qc}. \]
Those shares give precisely the indicators in Theorem 1. The problem is tractable by direct aggregation in time polynomial in \(|Q|,\tau\), the number of candidates, and the rational input length. This is my lead mirror; I would expect it to be tractable.

The fit to the paper’s question is strong: the candidates are the parties, their positions are their manifesto answers, and the electorate’s mass determines which positions represent more people and when they represent a majority. The paper already works with poll percentages and vote shares, so describing the electorate by mass is natural. For an ordinary discrete election with \(n_t\) voters of each type, setting \(\rho_t=n_t/n\) reproduces the same indicators exactly. This is a faithful formalization of the paper’s party-evaluation results, including its comparison with the DGB.

There is no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result in the paper. Theorem 1 is my anchor because it states the computation the paper actually performs; it should not be mistaken for a complexity classification. I would scope this mirror to the evaluation of parties and other candidate profiles. I would not stretch it to the coalition uncertainty analysis or to the proposed election procedure.

The weakest point is that the continuous representation adds little computational substance: Theorem 1 depends only on per-question opinion margins, not on the joint distribution over complete voter types. Any type distribution with the same margins gives the same scores. Moreover, the paper’s polls come from different sources and dates, so a convincing high-multiplicity instance would use aligned opinion measurements for one electorate. These limitations make the mirror thin, and this paper is not evidence that continuity dissolves discrete hardness. Still, the underlying question—how much of a large society’s mass each political profile represents—is one its authors should recognize. It also invites a precise follow-up: how stable are candidate rankings when estimated opinion margins lie near the \(50\%\) threshold?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is already stated in terms of continuous population shares. It takes the per-issue opinion balance \(a_q\), candidate positions, and issue weights as inputs; its representativeness values are fractions of society. The poll respondent counts have already been reduced to percentages before the theorem is applied. So the closest anchor is not a discrete-electorate result waiting for a high-multiplicity relaxation. It is a formula for aggregate opinion data that the paper already treats continuously.

The proposed profile distribution \(\rho\) is a sensible way to describe a large electorate, but for this theorem it adds no operative information. Its only relevant projection is
\[ a_q=\sum_{t:t_q=1}\rho_t-\sum_{t:t_q=-1}\rho_t. \]
All distributions with the same issue margins give the same popularity and universality scores, regardless of how opinions on different issues are correlated. Supplying \(\rho\) therefore re-encodes the inputs to Theorem 1; it does not create a new population-level computational question. Returning the highest-scoring candidate adds an argmax wrapper, but the paper already computes and ranks the candidates. Theorem 1 is a formula result, not a complexity result about a computational problem.

The strongest upgrade would use the paper’s proposed election procedure: take a full distribution of voter profiles and adjust party seats according to universality. But the adjustment still depends only on those per-issue margins and majority signs, and the paper already demonstrates it from aggregate percentages. A question about whether those rankings survive uncertainty in poll estimates could be interesting, but that introduces a survey-noise model; it does not make the population continuous. Likewise, the coalition calculations concern how party seat weights shape coalition positions, not how citizen mass is distributed across types.

I would not argue that a large electorate is an implausible high-multiplicity regime, or that grouping identical answer profiles distorts the model. Those are not good objections here. The negative case is narrower: the one named result that looks computational is already an aggregate-share calculation, and expanding its input to a voter-type distribution leaves its content unchanged. That makes the proposed mirror a poor contribution to a programme about computational questions opened up by continuization. Still, the case against *any* worthwhile mirror is weak: the exact type-mass formulation is coherent, and an analyst who values explicit high-multiplicity formulations may reasonably count it as a useful mirror.
