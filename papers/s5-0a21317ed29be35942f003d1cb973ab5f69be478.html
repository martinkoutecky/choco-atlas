The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest case anchored in a computational result: the paper contains no named result asserting a complexity or algorithmic claim.

The closest candidate is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that, for any preference domain \(D\) with \(A \subseteq D \subseteq S\), a family of voting rules is strategy-proof, false-name-proof, unanimous, anonymous, and neutral if and only if it is either voting by quota one or voting by full quota. This is a substantive characterization, but it classifies rules by axioms; it does not establish that a computational problem is tractable or hard. Corollary 1 strengthens the false-name condition to strong false-name-proofness, with the same classification, and is axiomatic for the same reason.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting nearby: a large anonymous online poll over a fixed set \(K\) of issues, where types are complete separable preferences over \(2^K\) and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters of each type. But a continuum version of Theorem 1 would ask for an axiomatic characterization of rules on distributions. That is outside ChoCo’s stated scope, and the paper gives no computational anchor for turning it into a mirror problem.

The weakest point is decisive: the paper’s central contribution is an axiomatic result, and its false-name manipulation notion relies on adding voters to a finite society. I would not present a continuum characterization inspired by Theorem 1 as a continuous computational mirror of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes entire families of voting rules by incentive and fairness axioms; Corollary 1 and Propositions 2–4 establish further axiomatic implications. None studies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. There is no input representation, optimization objective, or complexity claim for a continuous version to inherit.

The best rescue is a large anonymous online poll, with voter types represented by a distribution over separable preferences, and a computational question such as the minimum mass of forged ballots needed to change the selected subset. That is a plausible adjacent problem, and the online-poll setting makes a high-multiplicity population credible. But it adds the computational task the paper never posed. Its central result asks which rule families resist false-name manipulation by individual voters; a mass-based attack question would need its own objective and model. Even if that mass reinterpretation is one the authors would recognize, it does not turn Theorem 1 into a computational result.

A continuous characterization of strategy-proof and false-name-proof rules on distributions would be closer to the paper, but it would remain an axiomatic characterization, outside ChoCo’s scope. The honest limit of this negative case is that it cannot rule out every worthwhile new project about mass-based manipulation. It can say that such a project would be adjacent work, not a continuous computational mirror of a result in this paper.
