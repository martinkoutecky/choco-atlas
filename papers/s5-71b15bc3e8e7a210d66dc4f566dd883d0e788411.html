The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), ranking types \(T\), a rational mass vector \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), and fixed parameters, find finite multisets \(M_1,M_2\) and a mixing probability \(\eta\) such that \(U(M_1)\) and \(U(M_2)\) satisfy the stated RepApx conditions and \(D=\eta U(M_1)+(1-\eta)U(M_2)\) has \(\sup_{d\in\mathcal D(\mu)}\frac{\mathbb E_{c\sim D}[\operatorname{SC}_{\mu,d}(c)]}{\min_{i\in C}\operatorname{SC}_{\mu,d}(i)}<3\), where \(\mathcal D(\mu)\) is the family of compatible pseudometric witnesses.

The model it lives in

Types are ranking cohorts with masses \(\mu_t\); the hidden common-space metric is an adversarial witness. Decision variables are constant-size candidate multisets and \(\eta\), with objective to find a mixture whose robust expected social-cost ratio is below \(3\).

The objection that survived

A ranking does not record the voter-to-candidate distances used by social cost, so the proposed \(\mu\) is not literally a distribution over complete agent types; treating metric refinements as one type needs justification or a canonical biased-metric reformulation.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s bounded-randomness metric-voting problem. The continuity belongs to the electorate, not to the candidates, lotteries, or metric space.

Let \(C\) be the candidate set and \(T\) the set of strict rankings of \(C\). A continuous society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters with ranking \(t\). Thus

\[ s_\mu(a\succ b)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\mathbf 1[a\succ_t b]. \]

All pairwise and setwise quantities in the paper are defined by replacing voter averages with these \(\mu\)-weighted sums. The population regime is a large election with \(n\gg |{\rm supp}(\mu)|\): for example, millions of citizens choosing among a fixed slate, with repeated ballot types generated by geographic, institutional, or demographic cohorts. Individual voter identities are irrelevant to the rule, which observes only rankings. The hidden metric remains an adversarial witness, exactly as in the paper; one can allow arbitrary metric refinements within each ranking type, provided the type marginal is \(\mu\).

For a distribution \(D\) over candidates, define

\[ \operatorname{dist}_\mu(D) = \sup_{d} \frac{\mathbb E_{c\sim D}[\operatorname{SC}_{\mu,d}(c)]} {\min_{i\in C}\operatorname{SC}_{\mu,d}(i)}, \]

where the supremum ranges over pseudometrics consistent with the rankings and \(\operatorname{SC}_{\mu,d}\) is average social cost over the continuous population. A bounded-randomness outcome is a uniform distribution over a multiset \(M\) of candidates, so the action variable is a finite candidate roster, possibly with repetitions.

My lead continuous problem is:

Continuous Bounded-Metric-Distortion Design. Given \(C\), \(\mu\), and fixed constants \(k,\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\widetilde\beta\), find multisets \(M_1,M_2\) and a mixing probability \(\eta\) such that \(U(M_1)\) is an \(\varepsilon_1^2\)-RepApx Maximal Lottery, \(U(M_2)\) is an \((\varepsilon_2,k,\frac12+\widetilde\beta)\)-RepApx Pruned Lottery, and

\[ D=\eta U(M_1)+(1-\eta)U(M_2) \]

satisfies \(\operatorname{dist}_\mu(D)<3\). A solution is the two explicit bounded rosters and \(\eta\), not merely an abstract existence claim. The roster sizes must be bounded independently of the population size and, in the paper’s construction, independently of \(m\).

The lead anchor is Theorem 7.6 (Mixing Theorem), proved in this paper. It states that there exist \(\mu,\widetilde\beta,\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2\in(0,1)\) and \(k\in\mathbb N^+\) such that the following rule has metric distortion strictly less than \(3\): with probability \(\mu\), run an \(\varepsilon_1^2\)-RepApx Maximal Lottery; with probability \(1-\mu\), run an \((\varepsilon_2,k,\frac12+\widetilde\beta)\)-RepApx Pruned Lottery. Here the theorem’s \(\mu\) is a mixing probability, not the society distribution.

This is a direct and credible mirror. The paper’s proofs use only quantities such as \(s_{a\succ b}\), \(s_{I\succ j}\), and averages of multiset-comparison probabilities. Every one of these is linear in the voter profile and therefore has an exact \(\mu\)-version. A finite election with \(n_t\) voters of type \(t\) is precisely the rational society \(\mu_t=n_t/n\); conversely, any rational \(\mu\) can be realized by duplicating types. The result is therefore a genuine high-multiplicity reformulation, not a tractable weakening.

I would expect this problem to be Class A for fixed accuracy parameters. The weighted tournament and all relevant comparison probabilities can be computed from an explicit \(\mu\) in time polynomial in the number of represented types and the input bit length. The paper’s Stable Lottery computation is polynomial, and the required rosters have constant size. Since the paper itself proposes enumerating constant-size multisets, the search is polynomial in \(m\) for fixed parameters, though the dependence on \(\varepsilon^{-1}\) and \(k\) is parameterized rather than uniformly polynomial.

The main further question is the true continuous optimization problem

\[ \operatorname{OPT}_N(\mu) = \inf\{\operatorname{dist}_\mu(U(M)): |M|\le N\}. \]

What is the smallest \(N\) that guarantees \(\operatorname{OPT}_N(\mu)<3\) for every continuous society? The paper conjectures \(N=2\) in the finite setting; the continuous version makes that conjecture a clean high-multiplicity question.

A second, more focused anchor is Theorem 3.4 (Existence of Small-Support \(\varepsilon\)-RepApx Stable \(k\)-Lotteries), proved here. It states that for every constant \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), every \(k\in\mathbb N\), and every election, sampling

\[ q=\left\lceil \frac{\pi}{2}k^2\varepsilon^{-2}\right\rceil \]

candidates independently from a Stable \(k\)-Lottery and taking the uniform distribution over the resulting multiset produces an \(\varepsilon\)-RepApx Stable \(k\)-Lottery with positive probability. More generally, it produces a \((1+\gamma)\varepsilon\)-RepApx Stable \(k\)-Lottery with probability at least \(\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}\).

Its continuous mirror is:

Continuous Representative Stable-Lottery Search. Given \(C\), \(\mu\), \(k\), and \(\varepsilon\), compute a Stable \(k\)-Lottery \(D_{\mathrm{SL}}\), and find a multiset \(M\) of \(q\) candidates such that \(\operatorname{Supp}(U(M))\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(D_{\mathrm{SL}})\) and, for every candidate \(a\),

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\Pr[a\succ_t U(M)^k] \leq \frac{1}{k+1}+\varepsilon. \]

The probability inside the sum is exactly the paper’s multiset comparison probability, with \(k\) independent draws from \(U(M)\). The expected way this goes is Class A: sampling gives a Las Vegas algorithm with constant expected restart overhead, and all voter averages become explicit \(\mu\)-weighted sums. The important open questions are whether the \(k^2\varepsilon^{-2}\) support bound is tight in the continuous regime, and whether its dependence can improve when \(\mu\) has small support or special structure.

The paper’s cited Theorem 2.8 supplies the appropriate robustness certificate: replacing finite voter averages by \(\mu\)-averages, a distribution \(D\) has distortion at most \(1+2\lambda\) exactly when, for every biased-metric vector \(x\), the corresponding continuous functions satisfy \(L_\mu(D)\leq\lambda R_\mu\). Thus the mirror remains computationally meaningful: its core is a robust optimization problem over a population distribution, not merely a restatement about fractional outcomes.

The weakest point is that the paper contains no named NP-hardness, membership-in-\(\mathrm P\), or parameterized-complexity theorem. Its anchors are constructive distortion and small-support results, not a complexity classification. Moreover, for this paper the finite-to-continuous passage is almost exact, so the mirror is unlikely to produce a surprising new theorem by itself. But that is a limitation of novelty, not of legitimacy. The paper’s actual question is already naturally high-multiplicity, and the continuous version exposes the computational problems that remain: optimal bounded support, minimum support for beating \(3\), and the complexity of robust lottery design as a function of \(m\), \(|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\), and the accuracy parameters.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper does not actually have a population-level computational object to continuize.

The proponent treats a ranking as a complete voter type. That is not true in the paper’s metric-distortion problem. A ranking is only the information revealed to the voting rule; the objective depends on the hidden distances \(d(v,c)\). Two voters with the same ranking may have completely different distances and therefore different contributions to social cost. Under ChoCo’s definition of type, \(\mu\) over rankings is not a distribution over complete agent types.

There are only two repairs. If metric information is included in the type, the type space is generally continuous and globally constrained by the requirement that all voters and candidates lie in one pseudometric space; the finite-\(\tau\) high-multiplicity model disappears. If metric information remains latent and adversarial, then \(\mu\) is only a distribution over observations, followed by a worst-case quantification over unrepresented microstates. That is a robust weighted-profile problem, not a high-multiplicity relaxation of the metric instances themselves.

This defeats the proposed reading of Theorem 7.6. The theorem already works for every finite ordinal profile and every compatible metric, and every quantity used in its proof is normalized by \(n\). The paper’s own search procedure enumerates candidate multisets and has no dependence on voter identities or population size. Replacing

\[ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in V} \]

by

\[ \sum_t \mu_t \]

therefore does not create a new population regime; it merely writes the paper’s existing sufficient statistics as a weighted profile. The proposed \(\operatorname{OPT}_N(\mu)\) may be an interesting question, but its substantive variable is the candidate roster size \(N\), hence bounded randomness and outcome design—not continuization of the electorate.

The same problem defeats Theorem 3.4. Its sampling is over candidates drawn from a Stable Lottery. The DKW argument uses a uniform interval to represent candidate draws, not a continuum of voters. The voter population appears only as an outer average, so the theorem transfers to \(\mu\) verbatim with the same \(q=\Theta(k^2\varepsilon^{-2})\) bound. A sharper question about the minimum support \(q\) is a support-design problem in the outcome space, not a population-continuous computational problem.

Theorem 2.8 does not rescue the mirror. The integral over \(t\) and the vector \(x\) describe the adversarial metric witness. They are continuity in the metric certificate, not in the society. Writing \(L_\mu(D)\leq \lambda R_\mu\) simply replaces an empirical expectation by a weighted expectation; it does not introduce a mass-transfer, population-control, or high-multiplicity optimization problem.

Thus none of the three anchors supplies the programme’s characteristic phenomenon: a computational problem whose discrete difficulty or formulation concerns individual agents and whose continuous version treats repeated complete types as mass. The paper’s rule is already profile-aggregated, while its metric data are hidden rather than type-level. The resulting “mirror” is either a notational restatement of the paper or a new candidate-support problem with a continuous coefficient vector.

This negative case is not airtight. If ChoCo accepts revealed rankings as the relevant types despite hidden metric heterogeneity, then the proponent’s \(\mu\)-model is mathematically legitimate, and \(\operatorname{OPT}_N(\mu)\) is a reasonable open question. But under the programme’s stricter definition of complete type, the paper offers no convincing population continuization; its apparent continuity lies in normalized profiles, candidate lotteries, and metric witnesses instead.
