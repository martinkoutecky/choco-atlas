The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\le7\) alternatives and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings with \(|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\le3\), can one find locations in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) for the alternatives and each positive-mass type that realize every ranking in the support by Euclidean distance?

The model it lives in

Use rankings as types with masses \(\mu_t\); choose shared alternative locations \(p_c\in\mathbb{R}^2\) and voter locations \(z_t\in\mathbb{R}^2\). The feasibility constraints require \(\|p_a-z_t\|_2<\|p_b-z_t\|_2\) exactly when type \(t\) ranks \(a\) above \(b\); there is no optimization objective.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3 supplies no worst-case runtime or guaranteed embedding algorithm beyond its bounded exhaustive experiment with a heuristic.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's regime of at most three supported rankings and at most seven alternatives, with arbitrary rational masses. It does not address recognition for larger supports or alternative sets.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can embeddings be found by a provably terminating algorithm with an explicit running-time bound, and does universal representability extend to larger support or more alternatives?

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant positive case from this paper: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifying a computational problem as hard or tractable.

The nearest result is Theorem 3, proved here through a computational experiment: every profile with at most three voters and at most seven alternatives is 2-dimensional Euclidean. A possible lift would take a society \(\mu\) over complete ranking types, with support size \(\tau\le 3\) and \(m\le 7\), and ask whether there are shared locations for the alternatives and a voter location for each supported type that realize every ranking in the support. Theorem 3 says the answer is yes for every such society, regardless of its rational masses.

That lift is too weak to serve as the requested mirror. Its answer depends only on which types have positive mass; the masses themselves do no work. It restates a small-profile geometric existence result and supplies no computational question about a continuous population. The introduction’s complexity statements about recognizing Euclidean preferences are citations to other papers, not named results established here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor, Theorem 3, establishes a geometric fact: every profile in a specified small range has a 2-dimensional Euclidean embedding. Its proof uses a randomized heuristic and an exhaustive computational study. It does not give a runtime bound or classify the complexity of recognizing Euclidean profiles. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 likewise give embedding constructions, not complexity results. The paper mentions recognition complexity through citations, but those results are not this paper’s findings.

The direct population lift is well-defined: put mass \(\mu_t\) on each ranking type and ask whether one placement of the alternatives, together with a voter location for each supported type, realizes all positive-mass rankings. But the masses never enter the constraints. The paper also assumes voters have distinct rankings, so its profiles already correspond to sets of types: repeated voters can be compressed to one type without changing realizability. This lift therefore supplies no new mass-based computational question, and Theorem 3 itself supplies no algorithmic result for it to mirror.

The stronger candidate is a mass-sensitive fit problem: choose a shared alternative placement and maximize the total mass of types whose rankings it realizes, or minimize the mass that must be excluded to obtain a Euclidean profile. That makes population mass consequential and could be useful for measuring how well spatial models fit a large electorate. But it adds an optimization objective and an allowance for misfit that the paper never studies. Its theorems do not characterize partial realizability, weighted deletion, or approximation. It may be a worthwhile new problem; it is not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a source of a continuous computational result: its contributions are representability results, and multiplicities add no information to exact representability. The case is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile follow-on scenario exists. A weighted fit problem is plausible, and I cannot rule out its value; the honest objection is that it would be a new line of work rather than the continuization programme’s computational mirror of this paper.
