What the mirror covers

The proposed anchor covers Proposition 3's unique-equilibrium characterization; it leaves the incentive comparisons, sophistication result, mechanism comparisons, and appendix cases without a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational complexity result: no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifies a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, or otherwise computationally bounded. Its named results concern incentives and equilibrium properties.

The closest candidate is Proposition 3, proved here: under Assumptions 1 and 2, for any problem, deduction rule, and division of students into sincere and sophisticated students, the preference revelation game has a unique Nash equilibrium outcome, equal to deferred acceptance under the augmented priority scores. That is a useful characterization, but the proposition does not classify the complexity of computing the outcome. Treating it as an “in P” anchor would go beyond what the paper establishes.

A continuum version would also risk changing the question: if a single student has zero mass, an individual deviation may no longer affect the assignment, while positive-mass group deviations introduce a different strategic model. So I cannot honestly give an anchor-based continuous mirror for this paper under the stated criteria.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper names no computational problem whose complexity it classifies. Its results concern manipulability and equilibrium properties. Proposition 3 is the best candidate: it characterizes the unique finite-game equilibrium outcome as deferred acceptance under augmented priorities. But it does not analyze the complexity of computing that outcome, or propose an algorithmic problem beyond the characterization.

A mass version of Proposition 3 is easy to state: given rational masses of students by preference, priority score, and sophistication, and school capacities scaled to the population, compute the equilibrium assignment of mass. High school placement is a credible high-multiplicity setting, so lack of repeated types is not a convincing objection. The harder issue is whether this is still the paper’s equilibrium question. Its Nash condition concerns a particular student’s deviation and resulting assignment. In an aggregate mass model, one student has measure zero, so the mass outcome cannot register that deviation. A nonatomic model could give a representative student utility at priority cutoffs, but then it studies price-taking best responses against fixed aggregate thresholds. Making a whole type’s mass strategic restores effects on other students, but changes the player from an individual to a coalition. Neither construction simply carries Proposition 3’s finite-game object into the continuum.

One could study how the equilibrium assignment behaves as finite cohorts approach a mass limit. That is a coherent research question, but it is a new limit theorem, not a continuous computational mirror anchored in a named complexity result. The other propositions do not supply a stronger computational anchor: they compare incentives or welfare across mechanisms.

So the no-mirror case is strongest as a claim about this paper’s fit with ChoCo’s complexity programme, not as a claim that school assignment lacks a sensible high-multiplicity regime. That regime is plainly plausible; what is missing is a named computational result that survives the change in strategic unit.
