What the mirror covers

No result has a qualifying computational mirror; Theorems 1 and 2 are analytic asymptotic equilibrium results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a large, common-interest fact-finding group: many reviewers assess the same hidden binary state, receive independent noisy signals, and vote under a shared decision rule. A type is a reviewer’s signal, \(g\) or \(i\); mass is the fraction of reviewers with each signal; and the decision variable is how much of each type votes to convict. The group convicts under a majority rule if convicting mass reaches \(1/2\), or under unanimity if it is all the mass. Each reviewer’s objective is the paper’s expected utility from the group decision, given their signal and beliefs. A large reviewer pool could have \(n\) agents but only \(\tau=2\) types, so the high-multiplicity regime is plausible.

But the paper contains no named computational result of the kind required for an anchor: it establishes no problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Its nearest named results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved here. Theorem 1 says that, for fixed \(\lambda<1\), in any Logit AQRE without a straw poll, the probability of acquittal exceeds \(1-\delta\) for all sufficiently large juries, regardless of the state. Theorem 2 gives the same conclusion for unanimous juries with a straw poll. These are asymptotic results about equilibrium behavior, not computational-complexity results. The paper’s numerical work on QRE does not establish an algorithmic complexity claim.

Those theorems support a nearby *analytic* population limit: as the jury grows, signal and action frequencies can be described by masses rather than individual votes. They do not, however, anchor a continuous computational problem. In a direct atomless model, one reviewer has no effect on the outcome, so the pivotality that drives the paper’s strategic voting can disappear. I would therefore not present “compute the continuum Logit equilibrium” as the paper’s mirror result: it is a new question, and these theorems do not say it is tractable or hard.

The best positive reading is that the authors would recognize the large-group, two-signal mass model as a natural extension of their jury problem, especially given their asymptotic results. Its weak point is decisive under this assignment: the paper has no qualifying computational anchor. I can’t honestly give a continuous problem per anchor or classify one as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own admission is decisive: this paper proves no named computational result. Theorem 1’s large-jury result and Theorem 2’s straw-poll result concern limiting equilibrium behavior; neither classifies an algorithmic problem by complexity. They can motivate a continuous *analytic* model, but that is not the computational gap ChoCo is meant to fill.

The proposed two-signal mass model does not rescue either theorem as a computational anchor. In the unanimous-jury results, a Logit response leaves each juror a positive chance of acquitting. In an atomless population, that becomes a positive mass of acquittals, so unanimity fails and the jury acquits. The straw poll does not restore individual pivotality: a single reviewer still cannot change either vote mass. This is a meaningful limit of the finite game, but its computational question is essentially absent—the outcome collapses to acquittal. Replacing unanimity with a mass threshold might avoid that collapse, but it changes the decision rule and no longer mirrors these results.

There is a further mismatch in the proposed type model. The paper’s signals are private, and the strategic incentives turn on the chance that a particular juror is pivotal. If the signal masses are supplied as the known society, the model exposes aggregate information that the paper’s jurors do not observe. If they are instead treated as a prior over privately realized signals, the model must retain stochastic finite-population events; it is no longer simply the given society distribution. Either way, the atomless version removes the pivotal events that drive the theorems.

One could ask for the QRE of a continuum fact-finding group. That would be a new equilibrium-analysis problem, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. With two signals and two actions, it also offers little of the algorithmic landscape ChoCo targets; adding richer types, interventions, or costs to create such a landscape would supply new structure and a new problem.

A large reviewer pool may be a plausible setting for the underlying model, so the negative case does not rest on claiming that multiplicity is impossible. The stronger point is that neither named theorem anchors a worthwhile continuous *computational* mirror. I cannot rule out every useful future problem built from this model, but the proponent has not identified one that survives as a mirror of this paper’s results.
