The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a fully compliant positive case from this paper: it has no named computational-complexity result. The numbered theorems characterize the existence and form of uniformly most powerful (UMP) statistical tests; they do not show that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classify its complexity. The paper even leaves efficient computation of the proposed tests as future work.

The closest mathematical anchor is Theorem 8, proved here. It says that in Condorcet’s model, for \(m\ge2\), \(n\ge2\), and any \(0<\phi<1\), there is a level-\(\alpha\) UMP test of \(H_0=B(A)\setminus R_{a\succ\mathrm{others}}\) against \(H_1=R_{a\succ\mathrm{others}}\). The test rejects when its likelihood ratio
\[ \frac{m-1}{\sum_{b\ne a}\phi^{w_{P_n}(a\succ b)}} \]
crosses a threshold, with randomization at the threshold as needed. That is a statistical-optimality result, not a complexity result.

A plausible continuous setting would be a large crowdsourcing panel ranking a small menu. Let each type \(t\in T=B(A)\) be a worker’s complete reported binary relation, and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of workers reporting it. With \(N\) workers and modest \(m\), \(N\) could greatly exceed the number \(\tau\) of observed types, which is at most \(|T|=2^{\binom m2}\). The decision would still be whether \(a\) is a winner.

One precise candidate is: given \(A,\phi,\kappa,a,\alpha\), set \(\pi_h(t)\propto\phi^{KT(t,h)}\) and model the observed mass vector as \(\mu\sim\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\kappa\pi_h)\). Find a critical function \(f:\Delta(T)\to[0,1]\) with
\[ \sup_{h\in H_0}\mathbb E_h[f(\mu)]\le\alpha \]
that maximizes \(\mathbb E_h[f(\mu)]\) for every \(h\in H_1\), or certify that no such UMP function exists. Its question preserves the paper’s aim—robust winner testing over competing ground truths—while making the observed society a distribution over report types. I would leave its complexity classification open; Theorem 8 does not establish one.

The weak point is substantial: the Dirichlet observation model is a co-modification, not Xia’s iid sampling model. With a truly infinite iid population, the observed mass vector would equal \(\pi_h\), removing the sampling uncertainty the paper’s tests address. With finite iid samples, empirical masses remain on a discrete grid. So this is a plausible research question near the paper, but it is not an honest complexity mirror anchored by one of its named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s own diagnosis is decisive for a mirror of this paper: Theorem 8 characterizes a UMP statistical test; it makes no claim about the complexity of a computational problem. The paper explicitly leaves efficient computation of its tests as future work. The theorem gives an optimality result, not an algorithmic one for ChoCo to continuize.

The most direct population mirror also removes the uncertainty that makes Theorem 8 interesting. Let each type be a binary relation \(t\in B(A)\), and let the continuous society have masses \(\mu_t=\pi_h(t)\) under ground truth \(h\). For \(0<\phi<1\), \(\pi_h(t)\propto\phi^{KT(t,h)}\) has a unique maximum at \(t=h\). Thus the exact mass vector identifies \(h\). Since \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) are disjoint, the test that rejects exactly when \(\mu=\pi_h\) for some \(h\in H_1\) has size zero and power one. There is no nontrivial UMP trade-off left to classify.

Finite samples retain that trade-off, but their empirical mass vectors lie on the \(n\)-grid and preserve the paper’s discrete sampling experiment. Encoding the profile by type counts may make it a high-multiplicity problem; it does not make the observed society continuous in the relevant limiting sense. The proposed Dirichlet model is the strongest alternative, but it adds a random population composition to preserve uncertainty. That may be worth studying as a new statistical model; it is not a consequence of continuizing the paper’s i.i.d. population, and a complexity question about its tests would be new work rather than a mirror of Theorem 8.

The negative case is strong for a mirror of the stated results. It cannot rule out every nearby model someone might invent: if “mirror” permits changing the data-generating experiment, a Dirichlet-based computational problem could be worthwhile. That is where the universal claim is weakest.
