The case FOR (proponent)

Under ChoCo’s strict anchor rule, this paper has no eligible computational anchor. Its named results are axiomatic:

  • the main characterization theorem (the restated robustCharacterization) says that robustness, anonymity, unanimity, reinforcement, and right-biased continuity characterize position-threshold rules;
  • Proposition sincerity connects robustness with strong uncompromisingness and strategyproofness;
  • Theorem 1 says that the endpoint-median rule is the unique position-threshold rule satisfying majority criterion and strong unanimity;
  • Lemma 1 characterizes compatibility of weight and threshold vectors.

None asserts polynomial-time solvability, hardness, parameterized complexity, approximation, or an algorithmic formulation used computationally. Thus there are zero compliant anchors, and I cannot honestly supply the required “one continuous problem per anchor” case.

The strongest nearby positive construction, if the anchor requirement were relaxed, would be this. Let the ordered alternatives be \(A=\{x_1,\ldots,x_m\}\), and let voter types be intervals \(I=[x_\ell,x_r]\), of which there are \(\tau=m(m+1)/2\). A continuous society is a distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(\Lambda)\), where \(\mu_I\) is the population share reporting interval \(I\). This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: many citizens may share the same acceptable band of dinner times, policy positions, or political candidates, with \(n\gg m\) and \(\tau\) moderate.

For fixed \(\alpha\) and \(\theta\), define

\[ \Pi_{\alpha,\mu}(x_j)=\sum_{I\in\Lambda}\mu_I\pi_\alpha(I,x_j). \]

The continuous winner problem is: given rational \(\mu\), output the left-most alternative

\[ F_{\alpha,\theta}(\mu) = \max_{\succ} \left\{ x_j:\Pi_{\alpha,\mu}(x_j)\geq \theta_j \right\}. \]

For the endpoint-median rule, \(\alpha_j=\theta_j=\tfrac12\); equivalently, each unit of interval mass is split equally between its two endpoints and the median endpoint is selected. This problem is plainly tractable by direct aggregation in polynomial time in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the encoding length of \(\mu\).

The construction is mathematically natural, and the authors would likely recognize it as the continuum analogue of their variable-electorate interval model. But it mirrors an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result. Moreover, the paper’s “robustness” is a local axiom about changing one interval, not ChoCo’s winner-robustness or optimization-under-perturbations question. That is the decisive weakness: this is a good continuous social-choice model, but not yet a qualifying continuous computational mirror of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong here: the paper offers no computational result to continuize. Its named theorems—robustCharacterization, Proposition sincerity, Lemma 1, and Theorem 1—are axiomatic characterizations or incentive-theoretic implications. None defines an algorithmic problem, proves complexity, gives an approximation guarantee, or studies optimization over profiles. Under ChoCo’s anchor rule, there are therefore no eligible anchors, and the required mirror cannot be a mirror of this paper’s results.

The most charitable reconstruction would take interval types \(I=[x_\ell,x_r]\) as population types and a society \(\mu\in\Delta(\Lambda)\), with \(|\Lambda|=m(m+1)/2\), then ask for the winner of a fixed position-threshold rule:

\[ F_{\alpha,\theta}(\mu) = \max_{\succ} \left\{ x_j: \sum_{I\in\Lambda}\mu_I\pi_\alpha(I,x_j)\geq\theta_j \right\}. \]

This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime: many people may share an acceptable band of times, policies, or candidates. But it is not a computational mirror of a result in the paper; it is a newly invented evaluation problem. Moreover, it reduces immediately to \(m\) weighted sums over \(O(m^2)\) interval types, followed by threshold comparisons. There is no hidden exponential type space, pricing problem, perturbation optimization, or population-dependent combinatorial task of the kind ChoCo is designed to expose. The endpoint-median case is even more direct: aggregate endpoint mass and take a median.

One could instead continuize the axioms themselves—asking, for example, whether reinforcement or robustness holds on \(\Delta(\Lambda)\). That merely rewrites the paper’s axiomatic setting in measure notation. It does not introduce a computational question, and it risks confusing population continuity with the paper’s existing “continuity” axiom, which concerns cloning profiles and tie-breaking rather than a continuous society.

The case is not that interval populations are nonsensical. They are perfectly credible, and a future paper could attach a genuine optimization or robustness problem to them. The point is narrower but decisive: doing so would create a new computational-social-choice project rather than mirror this paper. Nothing in the paper supplies a qualifying computational anchor, and the obvious continuous reconstruction is a routine finite aggregation exercise. Thus, on the programme’s stated terms, there is no worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
