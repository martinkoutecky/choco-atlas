The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_a\), a coalition-size bound \(\alpha\), a per-capita coalition-count bound \(\kappa\), and unknown symmetric type-pair utilities \(w_{ab}\), does there exist a learner with expected regret \(o(T)\) against the minimum achievable maximum pairwise envy? In each round it chooses masses \(x_q\) over nonempty integer group compositions \(q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{\tau}\) with \(\sum_a q_a\le\alpha\), subject to \(\sum_q q_a x_q=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_q x_q\le\kappa\), then receives one independent sub-Gaussian report for each type pair that co-occurs in positive mass (including \((a,a)\) when a group contains at least two type-\(a\) members). For a type-\(a\) person in composition \(q\), own utility is \(\sum_c(q_c-\mathbf{1}[c=a])w_{ac}\); replacing a type-\(b\) member of composition \(r\) gives utility \(\sum_c(r_c-\mathbf{1}[c=b])w_{ac}\). The envy objective is the maximum positive difference between these utilities over types and positive-mass compositions. Can the learner achieve this regret with an \(f(\tau,\alpha)\operatorname{poly}(L,T,1/\varepsilon)\)-time \(\varepsilon\)-accurate implementation, where \(L\) is the input bit length?

The model it lives in

A population distribution over \(\tau\) complete interaction types, with mass \(\mu_a\) per type and shared symmetric pair utilities \(w_{ab}\). Decision variables \(x_q\) give mass per person assigned to each integral group composition, subject to the type-mass and per-capita group-count constraints. The objective and regret benchmark are the maximum replacement envy over compositions with positive mass; each round’s fixed audit supplies noisy reports for co-occurring type pairs.

The objection that survived

With the paper’s all-within-coalition feedback rule, repeated types yield many reports per type pair and noise washes out as population size grows; the fixed audit preserves learning but changes the observation rule.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s instance-independent regret guarantee for online minimax-envy learning; it leaves the instance-dependent bound and the paper’s finite-agent initialization analysis aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a learner achieve sublinear expected envy regret with an \(f(\tau,\alpha)\operatorname{poly}(L,T,1/\varepsilon)\)-time implementation, without an oracle for selecting the next allocation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4, proved by Cohen and Agmon, with detailed proof in their supplementary material [25]. Under Assumption 1, for any input and with \(\gamma=2\), LCB-EM-CF’s instance-independent envy regret satisfies, for \(\beta>2\),
\[ R_T\le 1.5T^{2/3} \left(16\Delta_{\max}n^4(\sqrt{2\beta}+2)^2\sigma^2\log T\right)^{1/3} +4n^2\Delta_{\max} +\frac{2n^2(n-1)\Delta_{\max}}{\beta-2}, \]
where \(\Delta_{\max}\) is the largest envy gap between a non-minimax-envy partition and a minimax-envy partition. In particular, for fixed instance parameters, the bound is sublinear in \(T\), of order \(T^{2/3}(\log T)^{1/3}\), and does not depend on the minimum suboptimality gap. This is a guarantee about learning to minimize the maximum envy from partial, noisy feedback; it is not a polynomial-time guarantee for choosing each partition.

Here is a continuous problem I would put to a prover: Continuous-Type Online Minimax-Envy Learning. Consider a large population of \(N\) people in a repeated project-grouping system—for example, a large training programme whose participants share a small set of role or collaboration profiles. Let \(\tau\) be the number of profiles, with \(N\) in the thousands or more and \(\tau\) in the tens or low hundreds. A type \(a\) is a complete interaction profile: every person of type \(a\) has the same symmetric utility \(w_{ab}=w_{ba}\) toward every type \(b\), and the same feedback-noise law. This retains the paper’s pairwise preferences and envy criterion while making high multiplicity an actual feature of the instance.

Let \(\mu_a>0\) be the known fraction of the population of type \(a\). Coalition size is capped at \(\alpha\), while the number of groups is capped at \(\kappa N\), for a per-capita group limit \(\kappa\) with \(\alpha\kappa\ge1\). A feasible group composition is an integer vector \(q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\tau}\) with \(1\le\sum_a q_a\le\alpha\). The action is a nonnegative mass \(x_q\) of groups of each composition, measured per person in the population, satisfying
\[ \sum_q q_a x_q=\mu_a\quad\text{for every }a, \qquad \sum_q x_q\le\kappa. \]
Thus people remain in integral-sized groups; it is the population split across group compositions that is fractional.

For a type-\(a\) person in a group of composition \(q\), utility is
\[ U_a(q)=\sum_c(q_c-\mathbf 1[c=a])w_{ac}. \]
If that person considers replacing a type-\(b\) person in a group of composition \(r\), their envy is
\[ \left[U_a(r-e_b)-U_a(q-e_a)\right]_+. \]
Define \(E(x,w)\) as the maximum of this quantity over types \(a,b\) and compositions \(q,r\) with \(x_q,x_r>0\), \(q_a>0\), and \(r_b>0\). The benchmark \(E^*(\mu,w)\) is the minimum of \(E(x,w)\) over feasible mass allocations. This is the paper’s minimax pairwise envy objective, with coalition counts scaled to the population.

Each round, the learner chooses a feasible \(x^t\), then receives noisy feedback only for type pairs that actually co-occur in positive mass. To keep the paper’s partial, noisy feedback meaningful at continuum scale, I would use a fixed per-round audit: one independent \(\sigma^2\)-subgaussian report around \(w_{ab}\) for each co-occurring type pair. The question is whether there is a learner that uses those reports to output feasible allocations with
\[ \mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^T\big(E(x^t,w)-E^*(\mu,w)\big)\right]=o(T), \]
and runs in \(f(\tau,\alpha)\operatorname{poly}(L,T,1/\varepsilon)\) time for an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate implementation, where \(L\) is the input bit length. That is the continuous analogue of the underlying learning problem, rather than a question about an encoding.

I expect this bounded-type version to be tractable in the parameterized sense, hence a Class A candidate. There are finitely many integral group compositions, and envy depends on an allocation through which compositions have positive mass. For any proposed support of compositions, feasibility is a linear mass-allocation question; enumerating supports may cost a large function of \(\tau\) and \(\alpha\), but that leaves room for an FPT algorithm. The feedback still has to be used to learn the type-pair utilities, so this does not make the learning problem disappear. I would not claim polynomial time in unrestricted \(\tau\): the composition-support search may retain substantial combinatorial difficulty.

The authors should recognize the mirror: it keeps repeated group formation, bounded group sizes and counts, pairwise replacement envy, noisy feedback from interactions, and regret against a minimax-envy benchmark. The changes—type-level utilities, fractional masses of group compositions, and a per-capita group cap—are consequences of making the population the continuous object. This covers Theorem 4’s online minimax-envy learning result, not the paper’s entire model or all hedonic games.

The weak point is material: the paper permits an essentially arbitrary utility for each pair of named individuals, whereas this mirror assumes many people share a complete type profile. Also, the paper’s regret theorem assumes an oracle for choosing a partition that minimizes estimated envy; it does not establish the computational cost of that step. The continuous question therefore asks whether a parameterized allocation method can supply the missing computation. And the audit model is a deliberate co-modification: if one literally observes every interaction in an infinite population, averaging can erase the paper’s noise. Those limits narrow the claim, but leave a recognizable and concrete continuous version of the paper’s central problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is a real anchor: it proves a sublinear-regret guarantee for an online algorithm, so the objection cannot be that the paper has no computational result. The harder question is whether that guarantee survives as a meaningful *learning* problem for a continuous population.

With bounded coalition size and a fixed number \(\tau\) of repeated types, it may not. If a positive mass of each type is placed in groups together, a finite population of size \(N\) produces \(\Theta(N)\) noisy reports for that type pair in a round. Their average has variance \(O(1/N)\), so in the continuous limit the learner learns the pair’s utility essentially exactly on its first co-occurrence. Covering the \(\binom{\tau}{2}\) type pairs then takes a finite number of rounds. The \(n^2\)-scale uncertainty that motivates the paper’s initialization and regret analysis disappears.

A fixed audit budget, as proposed, preserves uncertainty, but it changes the feedback process: reports no longer arrive for every realized within-coalition pair. That is a plausible model for a teacher with limited observation capacity, and the authors might recognize it. Still, the result would concern learning under a new sampling budget, rather than the population limit of the paper’s observation rule. Under the paper’s rule, noise washes out; under the audit rule, the audit design becomes part of the problem.

There is a second mismatch. Theorem 4 assumes an oracle for choosing the partition that minimizes estimated envy. It proves a statistical guarantee, not a running-time bound for that choice. In the proposed type model, there are finitely many group compositions \(q\). One can enumerate their supports, determine each support’s envy, and test feasibility with a linear program. This gives an \(f(\tau,\alpha)\operatorname{poly}(L)\) algorithm directly. That may be too large to use, and a polynomial-time result in \(\tau\) could still be interesting, but the advertised parameterized target follows from finite support enumeration rather than a new continuous-optimization technique. The mirror therefore either makes the learning theorem largely vacuous in the limit or redirects attention to a finite composition-selection problem.

This is not an airtight negative case. Large cohorts with a small set of shared interaction profiles are sensible, and a fixed reporting budget could make the online question worthwhile. The strongest case against is narrower: the natural limit erases the theorem’s noisy-learning challenge, while the co-modification that restores it also changes the learning model and leaves a finite, parameterized allocation problem. That makes this a weak candidate for a *continuous* mirror of Theorem 4, rather than a convincing claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
