The continuous mirror question

Given a finite role-type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), a symmetric acceptable relation on distinct types, and weak incomplete rankings \(\succeq_t\) satisfying the paper's narcissistic, single-peaked, and tie-sensitive single-crossing promises under a common order \(\rhd\), decide whether there exists a symmetric mass matching \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\ge 0\) with \(x_{tt}=0\) and \(\sum_u x_{tu}\le \mu_t\), such that for every mutually acceptable pair \(t,u\), it is not the case that both \(D_t(u)>0\) and \(D_u(t)>0\), where \(r_t=\mu_t-\sum_v x_{tv}\) and \(D_t(u)=r_t+\sum_{v:u\succ_t v}x_{tv}\); output \(x\) when one exists.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic, role-complementary stable-roommates market in which type \(t\) carries mass \(\mu_t\), \(x_{tu}\) is paired mass, and stability forbids positive mutually improving mass for any acceptable pair.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is anchored in Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper:

Stable Roommates with incomplete preferences and ties remains NP-complete, even when preferences are narcissistic, single-peaked, and tie-sensitive single-crossing.

The theorem reduces bounded-degree Independent Set. For a graph \(G=(U,E)\) and integer \(k\), the construction creates ten vertex agents for every \(u_i\in U\) and two five-agent selector gadgets for each of \(k\) selector positions, hence \(10|U|+10k\) agents. The selector gadgets force selected agents to attach to vertex gadgets; the vertex gadgets force two selected vertices to conflict exactly when the corresponding graph vertices are adjacent. Thus a stable matching exists if and only if \(G\) has an independent set of size \(k\). The constructed profile is simultaneously narcissistic, single-peaked, and tie-sensitive single-crossing.

My lead mirror is the following extension, which I would call Continuous Typed Stable Roommates with Ties.

There is a finite set \(T\) of roommate-role types and a rational population distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population of type \(t\). A type records the complete preference and compatibility information relevant to matching. The acceptable type relation is symmetric and may be incomplete; ties are allowed. As in the paper, the profile is promised to be narcissistic, single-peaked, and tie-sensitive single-crossing with respect to a common order \(\rhd\) on \(T\).

To preserve the paper’s loopless acceptability convention, this version is for a role-complementary market in which two agents of exactly the same role are not eligible to form a pair. This is natural, for example, in a large project-pairing scheme where pairs must combine distinct certifications or work roles. The formal self-type remains the narcissistic top choice, but it is not an assignable partner, just as an agent cannot be matched to itself in the finite problem.

A solution is a symmetric mass matching \(x\): for each distinct acceptable pair \(t,u\), \(x_{tu}=x_{ut}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents paired with type \(u\) agents, with

\[ \sum_{u}x_{tu}\le \mu_t. \]

The residual \(r_t=\mu_t-\sum_u x_{tu}\) is unmatched mass. For a potential partner type \(u\), define the dissatisfied mass of type \(t\) by

\[ D_t(u)=r_t+\sum_{v:\,u\succ_t v}x_{tv}. \]

The matching is weakly stable if, for every mutually acceptable pair \(t,u\), it is not the case that both \(D_t(u)>0\) and \(D_u(t)>0\). In words, there may not be positive mass on both sides who would strictly prefer one another to their current assignments. The computational question is whether such an \(x\) exists, and, if so, to output it.

This is recognizably the authors’ problem: the objects remain acceptable partners, ties, blocking pairs, stable matchings, and the same three structural restrictions. Only the population representation changes from named individuals to large cohorts. A natural regime is \(N\gg\tau\): many residents or project participants, but only \(\tau\) recurring complete role types, with \(\mu_t=n_t/N\).

I expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness should transfer, because the Independent Set combinatorics reside in the compatibility graph among roles, not in the number of copies of each role. The main proof obligation is nontrivial: a fractional stable mass matching might split a selector cohort among several vertex cohorts. One would need a support-preserving rounding or gadget lemma showing that every feasible mass solution yields an integral stable witness, or else characterize precisely how fractional selector mass changes the reduction. This could reveal a continuum-specific tractable relaxation, but that is exactly a worthwhile continuous question rather than a change of subject.

The weakest point is the same-type convention. If same-type agents are freely allowed to match, narcissism makes the mirror dangerously close to trivial: each cohort can often match internally, eliminating the paper’s finite “self is preferred but self-matching is impossible” mechanism. Thus this is best described as a faithful typed extension of Theorem 4.4, not an unquestionable direct clone quotient. The extension is still author-recognizable because incomplete acceptability is central to their theorem, and the no-within-role rule is an explicit compatibility relation rather than a fractional outcome-space trick.

A cleaner, though scientifically thinner, second anchor is Proposition 2.10, proved in the paper:

Deciding whether an incomplete preference profile without ties is single-crossing, equivalently tie-sensitive single-crossing, or single-peaked is NP-complete.

The continuous version is Type-Order Recognition. The input is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over finitely many preference types \(T\), where each type gives a strict, possibly incomplete ranking of the common type set. The question is whether there exists a linear order \(\rhd\) of the types such that the support profile is single-crossing, or alternatively single-peaked, exactly under the paper’s definitions. A solution is the witnessing order \(\rhd\).

This is a direct high-multiplicity mirror: millions of agents may belong to a small number of recurring preference categories, and the task is to determine whether their society has a latent one-dimensional order. The masses do not alter the answer as long as they are positive, so clearing denominators recovers the corresponding finite profile. Proposition 2.10’s Betweenness reduction therefore transfers immediately, giving an expected Class B result. It has less continuous-optimization content than the stable-mass problem, but it survives the same-type objection and establishes that the paper contains at least one unambiguous population-level continuous question.

I would not use Theorem 3.5 as an additional anchor. Its finite result—existence and \(O(n^2)\)-time construction of a stable matching for complete, narcissistic, single-peaked profiles with ties—does not survive naturally when same-type partners are permitted, because narcissistic cohorts can match internally. The proposed mirror therefore covers Theorem 4.4 emphatically, with Proposition 2.10 as a robust secondary mirror, rather than pretending to continuize the whole paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed mirror of Theorem 4.4 changes the problem at its most important point: it replaces individual agents by roles.

In the paper, narcissism is intrinsically individual. Agent \(i\) ranks \(i\) above every other acceptable agent. Hence two agents \(i\) and \(j\) cannot be the same type in the programme’s precise sense: their complete preference descriptions differ because each treats a different named individual as its top choice. The gadgets in the Independent Set reduction therefore contain many distinct agents, not repeated high-multiplicity types.

There are only two ways to repair this. If agents of the same role may match one another, then narcissism makes the continuous problem essentially trivial: match every type internally. Since each type regards its own type as best, no cross-type pair can block. If same-role matching is forbidden, as the proponent proposes, then “narcissism” no longer means preference for oneself; it means preference for a formal role-class that is deliberately unavailable. That may define a reasonable new role-complementary market, but it is not a straightforward high-multiplicity version of the paper’s stable-roommates problem.

More importantly, the hardness mechanism does not survive the mass relaxation. The reduction relies on indivisibility and parity. In the five-agent selector cycle, an odd number of agents forces somebody to remain unmatched, which creates a blocking pair. With unit mass on five corresponding types, one can assign mass \(1/2\) to every cycle edge. Every type is then fully matched, despite the odd cycle. The same phenomenon lets selector mass split across several vertex types and turns the independent-set condition into a fractional packing condition. The paper’s proof supplies no rounding principle, and ordinary fractional matching has no reason to recover an integral independent set.

One could impose that every type must be paired wholly with one other type, or that selector mass cannot split. But that is precisely a type-level integral matching problem. It restores the indivisibility that the continuum was supposed to remove; it is not the proposed mass-matching relaxation. Thus Theorem 4.4 gives a strong finite hardness result, but not a convincing continuous-population anchor without a new, substantial model and a new reduction.

Proposition 2.10 is harder to defeat. The proposed Type-Order Recognition problem is formally well-defined: given \(\mu\), ask whether the positive-mass types admit a single-peaked or single-crossing order. It is also a legitimate high-multiplicity reformulation of recognition, and the Betweenness reduction can be transferred to the support.

The negative point is that \(\mu\) does no work at all. The question is exactly

\[ \exists\,\rhd\text{ on }\operatorname{supp}(\mu) \]

with the paper’s finite recognition condition. Duplicating a type, changing its mass, or replacing a population by an arbitrarily large cohort leaves the answer unchanged. This is not a continuous matching, control, or optimization problem; it is the original finite support-recognition problem with multiplicities discarded. A weighted violation objective could make mass operational, but that would be a new problem rather than a mirror of Proposition 2.10.

So the negative case successfully undermines the proposed stable-roommates mirror, especially its treatment of narcissism, parity, and fractional matching. But it is not an airtight universal rejection. If the programme counts mass-insensitive recognition problems as worthwhile continuous mirrors, Proposition 2.10 survives and is enough to green the paper. The honest conclusion is that the best case against is substantial for Theorem 4.4 but weak against the proponent’s secondary anchor.
