What the mirror covers

The attempted mirror concerns Proposition 2's core-nonemptiness claim; it does not cover Proposition 1, the endogenous-quality case, or the empty-core example.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest foothold is Proposition 2, proved in this paper: for a finite \(n\)-firm market with equally spaced qualities, the partition-function game’s delta (projection) core is nonempty. The authors construct an allocation of the grand-coalition profit using each firm’s payoff from a singleton deviation, then show it withstands coalition deviations. That constructive proof could motivate a search for a continuous payoff density over firm types.

But Proposition 2 is an existence result, not a named computational result: it gives no complexity classification or algorithmic claim. There is no named computational result in the paper to anchor a continuous-complexity mirror to. Proposition 1 concerns which variants cartels produce and is cited to Gabszewicz et al. (2016); it does not supply such an anchor either.

A possible follow-on would give mass \(\mu_t\) to firms of each quality type \(t\), then seek per-firm payoffs \(x_t\) whose total equals the grand cartel’s worth and whose sums for every coalition cover that coalition’s worth. But the paper does not define the needed mass-market worth function. Its model relies on finitely many firms with distinct, ordered qualities, and neighboring variants determine market shares. Repeated firms of the same quality alter that price game; taking a limit over unique qualities instead makes the number of types grow with the number of firms. The paper’s continuous consumer distribution does not solve this gap, since consumers are not the players in its core.

So the honest positive case is limited: Proposition 2 offers a constructive structural result that could inspire a new continuum market model. It does not, on its own, yield a well-defined continuous problem that the authors’ result already anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the paper’s strongest anchor, but it proves a mathematical existence claim: with finitely many firms and equally spaced qualities, a payoff allocation exists in the projection core. The proof constructs one explicitly. Proposition 1 characterizes which products cartel members sell, relying on an earlier result; Sections 3.1 and 3.2 give further existence claims and an emptiness example. None states a complexity result or poses an algorithmic problem. The paper gives no input model or computational target to continuize.

A stronger proposal would ask whether a stable allocation exists, or how to compute one, when firms have a distribution of quality types. That is a coherent research direction, but it changes the object under study. Consumers are already modeled continuously, yet they are not the players in the core; the strategic players are the finite set of firms. Giving mass to firm types is not a direct analogue of giving mass to voter types. Firms at the same quality sell identical products: if they price independently, their competition changes the payoff game; if they coordinate, the model has folded them into a cartel. A continuum of distinct quality levels avoids duplicate products, but then the model needs a new price equilibrium and coalition-worth function. The paper’s finite ordering, neighboring variants, and cartel payoff calculations do not supply those definitions. A payoff density with constraints for every measurable coalition might be interesting, but it would be a new continuum industry model, not a computational mirror of Proposition 2.

Nor does taking more and more distinct firms settle the issue. The per-firm payoffs used to construct the paper’s allocation shrink as quality gaps shrink, while the grand cartel’s profit remains tied to the top-quality product. The limit therefore needs a substantive choice about how firms, products, and market demand scale. That choice may yield a useful model, but the paper gives no reason to prefer one scaling or limit question as its continuous counterpart.

So the negative case is strongest at the programme’s scope boundary: there is no named computational result here to mirror, and the paper’s existing continuity belongs to the consumer distribution and the quality dimension, not to a computationally studied population of players. I cannot establish that no continuum-firm coalition problem would be worthwhile; a carefully built one could be. The weaker but better-supported conclusion is that it would launch a new research question, rather than fill the computational gap this paper leaves behind.
