The continuous mirror question

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be finite supports of complete preference types over opposite-side categories, including acceptability, with men's types allowed ties and women's types strict. Give each side total mass \(1\), with binary-rational type masses \(\mu_x\) and \(\nu_y\). A flow \(\pi_{xy}\geq 0\) has row and column sums bounded by those masses; it is weakly stable if no mutually acceptable pair \((x,y)\) has positive mass on each side assigned below the other, counting unmatched mass as below every acceptable type. Let \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu,\nu)\) be the maximum total matched mass of a stable flow. Is there a polynomial-time anonymous algorithm returning a stable flow of mass at least \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu,\nu)/1.5\), such that for every man type \(x\) with \(\mu_x>0\), the truthful lottery \(\pi_{xy}/\mu_x\) stochastically dominates the limiting lottery after moving \(\epsilon\) mass from \(x\) to any reported type \(x'\) and taking \(\epsilon\downarrow0\)? Require these limits to exist. For every \(0<\epsilon<1.5\), is it impossible for any such man-strategy-proof stable mechanism to guarantee \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu,\nu)/(1.5-\epsilon)\)?

The model it lives in

A cohort matching market with finite supports of complete preference types and binary-rational masses on each side. The decision object is an anonymous mass flow \(\pi\), where each person still receives one whole partner and \(\pi\) records the mass assigned between types. The objective is to maximize matched mass among weakly stable flows; incentives compare each type's partner lottery under truthful reporting with its limiting lottery after an infinitesimal report change.

The objection that survived

The finite lower-bound gadget changes one man's access to a named partner; an infinitesimal report may not change a macroscopic flow or force a different stable assignment, so the paper gives no demonstrated transfer of its \(1.5\) impossibility.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s one-sided-ties result, Theorem 1.4. The paper studies a computational mechanism-design question: how large a stable matching a strategy-proof mechanism can guarantee. It does not give a numbered theorem asserting NP-hardness or membership in a complexity class, so I would not use its prose citation of MAX SMTI’s NP-hardness as an anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large, centralized placement market: for example, recurring cohorts of applicants and positions whose members rank a bounded set of counterpart categories in the same way. Each type is a complete preference list over those categories, including acceptability and ties. With \(N\) people on each side, there may be only \(\tau\) distinct types, where \(\tau\ll N\). The paper’s one-sided-ties case fits a market where applicants are indifferent among positions in a category but positions have strict rankings over applicant categories. Grouping exact preference types is the high-multiplicity assumption; it does not make named individuals with different preferences interchangeable.

Here is the continuous object I would ask the authors to study. Give each side total mass \(1\), distributed over finite supports of types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_x\) and \(\nu_y\). A matching is a mass flow \(\pi_{xy}\geq 0\), where \(\pi_{xy}\) is the mass of type \(x\) matched to type \(y\), with row and column sums bounded by \(\mu_x\) and \(\nu_y\). This flow represents a population of people each receiving one whole partner: it records how the atomless cohorts are divided among partner types; it does not give any individual a fractional partner.

Call the flow weakly stable if there is no mutually acceptable type pair \((x,y)\) for which positive mass of \(x\) strictly prefers \(y\) to its assigned partner (or is single), and positive mass of \(y\) strictly prefers \(x\) to its assigned partner (or is single). Let \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu,\nu)\) be the largest total matched mass among stable flows. A mechanism maps each pair of type distributions to a stable flow. For incentives, read each type’s row as the partner lottery of a uniformly sampled member of that cohort. A negligible agent’s false report is modeled by moving mass \(\epsilon\) from their true type to the reported type, then taking the limit as \(\epsilon\to0\). Require truthful reporting to stochastically dominate every resulting limit lottery, according to the agent’s ranking. This recovers the paper’s no-profitable-individual-deviation condition when assignments are deterministic, while giving it a precise ordinal meaning for mass flows.

Lead question — continuous MAX SMTI-1TM, with a man-strategy-proof \(1.5\) guarantee. For every such profile in which only men’s types may have ties, does there exist a stable mechanism satisfying
\[ \sum_{x,y}\pi_{xy}\geq \frac{\mathrm{OPT}(\mu,\nu)}{1.5}, \]
and, for every \(\epsilon>0\), is there no man-strategy-proof stable mechanism that always guarantees at least \(\mathrm{OPT}(\mu,\nu)/(1.5-\epsilon)\)?

The anchor is Theorem 1.4, stated in the paper and proved there through Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2. It says that MAX SMTI-1TM admits a man-strategy-proof \(1.5\)-approximate-stable mechanism, but no man-strategy-proof \((1.5-\epsilon)\)-approximate-stable mechanism exists for any positive \(\epsilon\). The constructive proof builds a one-sided-ties instance, applies man-oriented Gale–Shapley to a transformed instance, and proves stability and the \(1.5\) bound; its strategy-proofness argument uses the cited strategy-proofness of Gale–Shapley. The paper also says its positive algorithms run in linear time and that the results extend to coalitions. Its lower bound is proved in the paper.

I would expect the upper-bound mechanism to have a mass version, but the key question is whether its strategy-proofness and approximation proof survive fractional splitting. I lean toward the lower bound surviving too: the manipulation in the proof uses a small, fixed collection of preference types, so it is not obviously driven by population size. In ChoCo terms, that suggests a B-like transfer of the approximation barrier, while leaving open whether continuous mass procedures make the constructive side simpler. The decisive further question is whether a mechanism can use type-level splitting to improve on \(1.5\) while retaining the limiting stochastic-dominance incentive condition.

A second, broader anchor is Theorem 1.2, also proved in the paper through Lemmas 2.1 and 2.2. It says MAX SMTI admits a man-strategy-proof \(2\)-approximate-stable mechanism and a woman-strategy-proof \(2\)-approximate-stable mechanism, but for every positive \(\epsilon\) admits neither a man-strategy-proof nor a woman-strategy-proof \((2-\epsilon)\)-approximate-stable mechanism. The positive construction breaks ties by a fixed rule and applies Gale–Shapley; the lower bounds use small manipulation examples. Its continuous question is the same one above, now allowing ties on both sides: for every pair of finite-support type distributions, do there exist separate stable mechanisms, one for each side, guaranteeing at least \(\mathrm{OPT}/2\) under that side’s incentive condition, with no mechanism on that side guaranteeing \(\mathrm{OPT}/(2-\epsilon)\) for any \(\epsilon>0\)? I expect the same approximation barrier to transfer for the same reason, but the fractional-assignment question remains open. This is a broader supporting mirror; Theorem 1.4 is the stronger lead because it isolates the paper’s main restricted-market result.

The authors could plausibly recognize these as their questions: the agents, preferences, stability condition, matching objective, and one-sided incentives are still theirs. The flow is the population-level accounting of integral matches, and cohort rankings explain why ties arise. I would not claim that the paper’s proofs already answer either continuous question.

The soft spot is incentive semantics. An atomless person cannot change the aggregate distribution by reporting differently, so copying the finite definition without specifying what happens to a negligible reporter would make strategy-proofness vacuous or ambiguous. The limiting report-and-lottery definition above addresses that, but it is a substantive extension of their model. Fractional division across types may also create options absent from the paper’s integral matching outputs. That is why the continuous theorem must be proved afresh—even though the paper’s finite manipulation gadgets give a credible reason to expect its tight ratios to persist.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that the tight bounds concern strategic incentives, while the paper’s computational contribution is limited: its positive mechanisms run in linear time, and its negative results make no runtime assumption. Theorems 1.2 and 1.4 establish tradeoffs between strategy-proofness and matching size, not complexity bounds for computing a solution. A continuous version could be interesting mechanism design, but it would sit at the edge of ChoCo’s stated focus on computational questions.

There is also a real modeling gap. In the paper, a man ranks particular women and may change his own list; stability and manipulation concern identifiable people and pairs. A mass flow over preference types records how much of each type is matched to each other type. It does not, by itself, say which individual receives which partner or how one person’s changed report affects that person’s assignment.

The proposed vanishing-mass definition does not follow from the flow alone. If a report type already has positive mass, the per-capita assignment for that row after adding an \(\epsilon\)-mass reporter converges to the row’s pre-existing assignment as \(\epsilon\to0\). That can define an interim incentive condition, but only after adding assumptions about anonymity, how assignments are distributed within a type, and the mechanism’s behavior for reports outside the support. Those choices are part of a new mechanism model. The paper’s deterministic strategy-proofness condition does not select them.

This is the pressure point for Theorem 1.4. Its lower bound uses an individual’s deletion of a named option to force a different stable matching and improve his partner. In a category market, deleting one named woman may have no counterpart: the report may instead remove a whole category, or a negligible amount of access to it. The proposed stochastic-dominance condition also compares lotteries over partner types, whereas the theorem compares partners under an individual’s ordinal list. A mass-flow mirror can be formulated, but neither the paper’s theorem nor its gadget determines that formulation or transfers its incentive claim.

Theorem 1.2 has the same gap on both sides. Fixed tie-breaking and Gale–Shapley give its positive mechanisms; its lower bounds again use named participants and individual list changes. Aggregating both genders into types makes the assignment a flow and requires a rule for how each type’s members share its partner-type distribution. Extending the proposed incentive semantics to both sides does not remove that modeling choice.

These are serious reasons to say the proposed mirrors are not direct continuizations of the theorems. They do not establish the universal negative the assignment asks for. A large placement market with many applicants and positions sharing rankings over categories is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and a type-level flow with explicit report-conditioned allocation rules could be a coherent question the authors would recognize. Theorems 1.2 and 1.4 therefore leave open worthwhile continuous questions. The honest negative case is weak here: it can show that the proponent’s formulation needs substantive extra machinery, but it cannot persuasively show that every sensible version fails.
