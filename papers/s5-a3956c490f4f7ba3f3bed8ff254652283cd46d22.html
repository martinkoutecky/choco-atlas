What the mirror covers

No computational result is mirrored. The majority-pattern characterization and the other geometric results already concern distributional profiles; Section 7 also studies finite electorates through Stearns numbers.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible continuous problem is majority-pattern attainability. Let \(K\) be a finite agenda, \(X\subseteq\{\pm1\}^K\) the admissible complete judgements, and \(y\in\{\pm1\}^K\) a target pattern. Ask whether there are masses \(\mu_x\ge0\), summing to \(1\), such that
\[ y_k\sum_{x\in X}\mu_x x_k>0\qquad\text{for every }k\in K. \]
The solution is a population distribution whose propositionwise strict majority is \(y\). A plausible regime is a large electorate with many citizens but relatively few distinct judgement types.

The trouble is that this is already the paper’s model: it defines a profile as a probability measure \(\mu\) over admissible judgements, with \(\mu(x)\) the fraction of voters holding judgement \(x\). Its Theorem 1.3(a), proved in the paper, says exactly that \(\operatorname{maj}(X)=\{y\in\{\pm1\}^K:O_y\cap\operatorname{conv}(X)\ne\varnothing\}\). For explicitly listed \(X\), that yields an LP feasibility formulation, but the theorem itself does not assert a complexity classification. The paper’s other numbered results are likewise geometric or combinatorial; none asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or a similar class.

So there is a sensible continuous-population question here, but presenting it as a new continuous mirror would restate a model the paper already uses. I cannot give the requested anchor-based positive case honestly: the paper contains no named computational result of the required kind.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole anchor is majority-pattern attainability: given \(X\) and a target \(y\), does some society make \(y\) the strict propositionwise majority? That is already the paper’s central model. It defines a profile as a probability distribution \(\mu\) over admissible judgements, with \(\mu(x)\) the fraction of society holding judgement \(x\), and considers all such profiles. Theorem 1.3(a) characterizes exactly which patterns those distributions realize: \(y\in\operatorname{maj}(X)\) iff \(O_y\cap\operatorname{conv}(X)\ne\varnothing\). There is no population limit to take; the anchor restates a question the paper already poses and answers geometrically.

A computational version needs an input representation for \(X\). If \(X\) is explicitly listed, the decision is linear feasibility in the masses; a feasible witness can be supported on at most \(|K|+1\) judgements. If \(X\) is given succinctly, the pricing problem may be interesting, but its complexity concerns that representation of the property space. The population is already continuous in the paper. Supplying \(\mu\) as input makes majority calculation direct; adding conversion costs or a margin objective could yield new optimization problems, but those are new questions rather than mirrors of a computational result in this paper.

The paper has no numbered complexity result to transfer. Section 7’s Stearns numbers concern finite voter counts; a continuous margin objective would be a sensible related study, but it changes the question and stays within the paper’s existing distribution model. The negative case is therefore strong against the proposed anchor as a continuous mirror. It is weaker as a universal claim that no adjacent computational project could be worthwhile: the paper may motivate such projects, but its results do not supply a discrete computational problem for continuization to mirror.
