The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a rational quota \(q\), additive feature contributions \(a_j(t)\), and a DNF strict partial order \(C\) over profiles, describe exactly all allocations \(y\) with \(0\le y_t\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t y_t=q\) for which no other feasible allocation \(z\) satisfies \(C(p(z),p(y))\), where \(p_j(y)=\sum_{t\in T}y_ta_j(t)\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete voter descriptions, including rankings and relevant attributes; \(\mu_t\) is each type’s population mass, and \(y_t\) is its selected panel mass. Additive features determine panel profiles, and the task is to represent all undominated feasible profiles and their allocations.

The objection that survived

The paper motivates fixed-size selections such as limited poll samples and predetermined admission classes, while the mirror selects a share \(q\) whose size grows with the population; the proponent does not establish that this is the more practical regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers profile-preserving grouping and best-panel selection for additive relevant features. It leaves aside non-additive features, the full SQL feature language, and the paper’s experimental performance claims.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the exact complexity of describing all undominated panels as the type count, feature dimension, and preference formula vary?
  • For fixed feature dimension and formula, is the problem tractable, and how do discrete best \(k\)-subsets approximate the \(q\)-mass frontier as the population grows?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for a large poll panel or citizens’ assembly. Imagine a population of \(N\) citizens with complete preference rankings over \(m\) candidates, plus any attributes used to assess the panel. Citizens with the same relevant description form a type. If there are \(\tau\) types and \(\tau \ll N\), describe the society by rational masses \(\mu_t\). The decision is to select a panel of mass \(q\), where \(q\) is a fixed share of the population. This fits the paper’s stated use of fixed-cardinality set preferences for representative polls, while making the population itself the continuous object.

The paper contains no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or similar. Its strongest named computational anchor is Theorem 2, proved here with a sketch. In full: for a DNF profile-preference formula \(C\) of the form in Equation (2), a relation \(r\), and \(k<|r|\), if all features relevant to \(C\) are additive, then the exchangeability relation represented by the M-relation in Definition 14 is the optimal exchangeability relation over \(r\). That means the paper’s grouping by additive feature contributions is the coarsest safe grouping: substituting one tuple for another in any otherwise identical \(k\)-subset preserves the relevant profile exactly when the grouping allows it.

I would mirror that result with the Continuous Best-Panel Profile problem. Its instance is a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), a rational panel quota \(q\), additive feature contributions \(a_j(t)\in\mathbb Q\) for \(j=1,\ldots,d\), and a DNF strict partial order \(C\) over feature profiles. A feasible panel is a vector \(y\) with \(0\leq y_t\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t y_t=q\); its profile is \(p_j(y)=\sum_t y_ta_j(t)\). The task is to return all feasible panels \(y\) for which no feasible \(z\) satisfies \(C(p(z),p(y))\). A solution can be an exact finite Boolean description using rational linear inequalities, with the type-mass allocations retained so that the actual panels are represented.

This is a plausible mirror of the authors’ problem: they ask which fixed-size groups have the best profiles, and the continuous question asks which fixed-share cohorts have the best profiles. Types with identical relevant additive contributions can be merged by adding their masses, just as Theorem 2 permits the M-relation to merge exchangeable tuples. I tentatively expect Class A for fixed feature dimension and a fixed preference formula, using polyhedral methods. For variable dimension and formula size, the exact frontier may be Class C: the combinatorics can come from the preference formula and the geometry of the frontier, rather than from population multiplicity. That is a forecast, not a result established by this paper. Further questions include whether the problem is FPT in \(d\) and formula size, and how closely the discrete best panels approximate the continuous frontier as the population grows.

A second, narrower anchor is Theorem 3, stated and used here, with no outside source attributed; the supplied text omits its proof details. It says that if \(o\) is an M-relation corresponding to \(r\), then every \(k\)-subset of \(r\) has a \(k\)-multisubset of \(o\) with the same profile projected onto the relevant features, and every such \(k\)-multisubset has a corresponding \(k\)-subset of \(r\) with that same projected profile.

Its continuous counterpart is the Continuous Target-Profile Attainability problem. Given \(T,\mu,q\), additive contributions \(a_j(t)\), and a target profile \(p^\ast\), decide whether some feasible panel \(y\) satisfies \(\sum_t y_ta_j(t)=p^\ast_j\) for every \(j\), and return such a \(y\) when one exists. This is a rational linear feasibility problem, so I expect Class A, solvable by linear programming in the input size. It mirrors the paper’s profile-generation step, rather than the whole best-subset query.

The scope is the paper’s additive-feature, DNF-preference results. The mirrors do not cover non-additive features such as distinct-vendor counts, nor the full SQL feature framework. The soft spot is that the paper’s objects are tuples in a finite relation, while the mirror treats citizens as a population and admits fractional type-level selection. Scaling the panel quota with the population is essential: a fixed-size committee would not be a meaningful positive-mass limit. Still, the poll-panel scenario is a natural version of the paper’s own representative-sample example, and its M-relation theorems provide a concrete bridge for aggregating many similar citizens without losing the relevant profiles.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The two anchors are the paper’s strongest computationally relevant results, but neither is a complexity result. Theorem 2 characterizes a safe way to group tuples for an optimization; Theorem 3 says that this grouping preserves the attainable profiles of discrete \(k\)-subsets. The paper gives algorithms and experiments, but no named tractability or hardness classification for the best-subset problem. So these anchors do not directly open the kind of complexity question ChoCo is built to chart.

Theorem 2’s proposed mirror has a narrow interpretation. For additive features, exchangeability is equality of the tuples’ relevant per-item contributions: replacing one with another leaves every relevant profile unchanged. A continuous type model can aggregate such tuples by their feature vectors and add their masses. That is a clean quotient, but it is not a continuous analogue of the theorem’s result about computation; the new question starts when one asks which fractional panels are best. The stronger version—optimize over all feasible type-mass allocations—describes a different object, a polytope of attainable profiles. For a linear objective, optimizing over it is an LP. Studying its full Pareto frontier could be interesting, but that is a new optimization problem, not a continuation of Theorem 2’s exchangeability result.

Theorem 3 likewise concerns an exact correspondence among finite objects: discrete subsets, bounded-multiplicity multisubsets, and their profiles. Replacing integer multiplicities by real masses gives a natural profile-attainability question, but it replaces the finite correspondence with membership in the linear image of a bounded simplex. That is a legitimate LP question; it does not preserve the theorem’s account of which discrete subsets exist. The best-subset version faces the same scaling choice: keep \(k\) fixed as the population grows, and the selected share \(k/N\) tends to zero; keep a positive share \(q\), and the selection quota scales with the population.

The strongest repair is the proponent’s panel scenario, or proportional admissions: citizens form the population, types include their rankings and relevant attributes, and the task selects a share \(q\) with the best profile. This is coherent, and the paper’s poll-sampling and admissions examples give it a foothold. But its practical fit is the negative case’s weak point. The paper describes interviewing a limited number of people and admitting a predetermined class size; those are fixed-size selections. A panel containing \(qN\) people as \(N\) grows is a bulk cohort-selection problem. It may be worthwhile on its own, but it is not clearly the computational problem this paper studies. It also selects a subpopulation by an organizer’s set preference, rather than changing an election outcome in a society whose ballots are the continuous object.

I would therefore reject both theorem-specific mirrors as anchors for this paper’s computational contribution. I would not claim the universal negative is airtight: proportional admissions or a bulk citizens’ panel could be a sensible continuous mirror if the authors accept that scaling regime. The case against rests on how much of the actual setting must change to get there, not on a claim that such a problem is invalid or that its answer would be uninteresting.
