The continuous mirror question

Given a finite outcome set \(Y\), types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) and rational beliefs \(q_t\), and fixed type-level reports and wagers of the other forecasters, compute a designated type’s optimal prediction and per-agent wager under WALLA I or II’s population baseline, and the resulting pooled forecast. The population baseline is the wager-weighted average over types; the single forecaster’s mass is negligible, as in the limit of finite populations with many agents per type.

The model it lives in

A continuum of forecasters represented by a finite support \(T\), with mass \(\mu_t\) and complete type \(t\) encoding its belief and relevant mechanism parameters. Each type uses a prediction \(p_t\) and per-agent wager \(w_t\); the designated type maximizes expected WALLA payout against the population baseline, and the platform pools reports using the wagers.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the one-shot optimal prediction and wager results of Theorems 3.2 and 3.3. It leaves the full equilibrium computation, repeated-question wager learning, and empirical aggregation results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is an extension of the paper’s finite-player equilibrium model to a large forecasting crowd. But under your anchor rule, this paper has no eligible named computational-complexity result: it does not classify a problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar.

The nearest results are Theorem 3.2, Theorem 3.3, and Corollary 3.6, all proved in the paper. Theorem 3.2 says that an agent’s belief \(Q_i(Y=\cdot\mid F_i)\) maximizes her conditional expected payout regardless of opponents’ reports and wagers, uniquely when her wager is positive. Theorem 3.3 says that, for a fixed prediction, her best-response wager is \((A_i/(2c_3))^+\), where \(A_i\) is her expected score advantage over the leave-one-out baseline. Corollary 3.6 says that with heterogeneous beliefs, truthful predictions together with those best-response wagers form a Nash equilibrium. These are mechanism and equilibrium guarantees, not complexity classifications. I would not present them as compliant anchors.

There is nonetheless a plausible population regime in the paper’s own setting: a forecasting service or market with many independently operated agents drawn from a small number of model-and-evidence configurations. For a given question, many agents might use the same base model and receive the same standardized evidence packet, while others receive no packet. Then the number of agents \(N\) could be much larger than the number of complete behavioral types \(\tau\). A type must include everything relevant to the one-shot problem—its belief over outcomes, information access, risk neutrality, scoring rule, and mechanism parameters. For this instance, its belief can be represented by a rational vector \(q_t\in\Delta(Y)\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of participants of type \(t\).

The candidate question, which I would call Finite-Support Continuum-WALLA Equilibrium, is: given a finite outcome set \(Y\), finite type support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), beliefs \(q_t\), the Brier score, \(c_3>0\), and a choice of WALLA I or II, find an \(\epsilon\)-Nash equilibrium in type-symmetric strategies and the resulting pooled forecast. Each type chooses a prediction \(p_t\) and a per-participant wager \(w_t\); its agents maximize expected net payout under \(q_t\). With \(W=\sum_t\mu_t w_t>0\), the population baseline for WALLA I is \(b^I(y)=\sum_t\mu_t w_t s(p_t,y)/W\); for WALLA II it is \(b^{II}(y)=s(\bar p,y)\), where \(\bar p=\sum_t\mu_t w_t p_t/W\). A type’s expected payout is \(w_t(\sum_y q_t(y)[s(p_t,y)-b(y)]-c_3w_t)\), and the platform’s forecast is \(\bar p\). A solution supplies the type-level actions and forecast. The finite counterpart has \(n_t\) agents of each type and masses \(n_t/N\); with positive total wager, its leave-one-out baselines approach the population baselines as the type counts grow.

This candidate extends the equilibrium question of Corollary 3.6, supported by the dominant-strategy and wager best-response results of Theorems 3.2 and 3.3. It covers the one-shot strategic equilibrium and aggregate forecast. It does not cover the paper’s empirical performance claims, neural wager training across repeated questions, or continuity of the outcome space.

For Brier scoring with fixed \(|Y|\), my tentative expectation is Class A: truthfulness pins positive-wager predictions to \(q_t\), and the wager equations appear reducible to a fixed-dimensional system of aggregate moments, even as \(\tau\) grows. That is a research hypothesis, not a result of this paper. With \(|Y|\) as part of the input, I would leave the classification open; continuum-specific hardness is a possible question. There is no hardness result here that would transfer from a discrete problem.

The best evidence that the authors might recognize this as their problem is their homogeneous-model/private-context example and Corollary 3.5, as well as their heterogeneous-belief equilibrium result. The weakness is that their experiments involve only 4–12 model instances, and many copies of the same model with the same evidence may be redundant rather than independent strategic participants. More fundamentally, the proposed continuum-equilibrium computation is a new complexity question. The paper motivates it, but supplies no required complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper never states a computational problem whose complexity it studies. Theorem 3.2 is a truthfulness guarantee; Theorem 3.3 gives a closed-form best response once the baseline is fixed; Corollary 3.6 establishes a finite-player equilibrium. None asks for an algorithm or classifies its running time. Under the programme’s stated criterion, these are poor anchors for a complexity landscape.

The proposed continuum equilibrium is the strongest repair. A society of finitely many types with rational masses and beliefs is a coherent high-multiplicity model, and the paper’s homogeneous-model example gives it some footing. But computing that equilibrium would be a new computational task built around Corollary 3.6, rather than a continuous version of a computational task the paper poses. For the paper’s repeated-question learning setting, a type would also need to preserve its question-dependent private information and wager policy; reducing it to a fixed belief vector describes only the one-question equilibrium problem. That makes the candidate a narrow theoretical extension, not a mirror of the paper’s empirical contribution.

This is a scope objection, not a claim that the equilibrium problem is uninteresting or that its answer would be easy. The candidate is formally coherent, and many forecasters sharing a small number of standardized information states could make its population regime plausible. The paper’s small pool of LLMs weakens that story, but does not rule it out; identical model instances with standardized contexts are at least a recognizable version of the authors’ own homogeneous-agent example.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a source of computational results for the programme. It is weak against the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: the finite-support equilibrium question may be worth asking on its own, and I cannot honestly rule out the authors recognizing it as their problem.
