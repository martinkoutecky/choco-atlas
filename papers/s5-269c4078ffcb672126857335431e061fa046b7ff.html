The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited by the anchor requirement: the paper has no numbered result asserting a computational complexity classification—nothing about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 3 gives the identity \(I_i[M_\rho f]=\frac{1+\rho^2}{2}I_i[f]\); Theorem 6 gives \(W(M_\rho f)=\rho W(f)\). They are named mathematical results, but neither classifies the complexity of computing or optimizing an underlying problem. The paper also describes a dynamic program for exact majority accuracy, but does not present it as a numbered complexity result.

There is a plausible population-level setting behind those results. In a large, anonymous two-candidate referendum, take the types to be the two votes, \(+1\) and \(-1\), with masses \(p\) and \(1-p\). Randomized response maps the \(+1\) mass to \(q=\rho p+(1-\rho)/2\). This is a natural continuous description of ballot shares, but it does not yield an anchored computational mirror: with nonatomic mass, independent noise averages to the deterministic share \(q\), and an individual has zero mass. The paper’s individual-influence question therefore degenerates in this limit. Under the impartial-culture assumption, the realized share also converges to \(p=1/2\), so per-capita welfare loses the finite-profile variation studied in Theorem 6.

The weakest point is thus central: the most natural low-support regime preserves the paper’s referendum setting, but not the substance of its influence analysis or a nontrivial computational question. I would not turn either theorem into a purported complexity anchor or hand a per-anchor mirror problem to a prover. There are no eligible anchors here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 3 and Theorem 6 are identities about expectations under the uniform product distribution: \(I_i[M_\rho f]=\frac{1+\rho^2}{2}I_i[f]\) and \(W(M_\rho f)=\rho W(f)\). Proposition 4 identifies the welfare-maximizing rule; Proposition 2 gives a privacy bound. None states a complexity classification for a specified computational problem. More fundamentally, the paper treats \(f\) as an arbitrary Boolean function without specifying how it is represented. The complexity of computing a quantity from \(f\) would depend on that representation. Adding population masses would not supply the missing computational problem.

The best population setting is an anonymous two-candidate referendum, with masses \(p\) and \(1-p\). It preserves the electorate and makes mass meaningful. But it does not preserve Theorem 3’s individual question: a single voter has zero mass. One could replace individual influence with susceptibility to moving a positive mass between vote types. That is a defensible new quantity, but for majority it is concentrated at the threshold, while the theorem’s finite-\(n\) influence measures the probability of being pivotal. Recovering that pivotality requires keeping finite-population fluctuations or adding a fluctuation-scale limit. Either move changes the object being studied. Restricting to anonymous rules also drops the theorem’s claim about arbitrary \(f\); encoding the roles needed for arbitrary rules can instead leave only one voter per type.

Theorem 6 admits a more natural mass-level reformulation: define per-capita welfare for a vote distribution and pass randomized response through it. But the paper’s welfare averages over labeled profiles under impartial culture; it is not an optimization problem over population distributions. Proposition 4’s closest mass analogue chooses the outcome that agrees with the larger share. Even if that is a worthwhile population statement, turning it into a computational problem requires adding a task—such as optimizing a rule or choosing a privacy mechanism—that the paper does not study. The fact that such a task might be easy would not disqualify it; the problem is that it would be a new task rather than a mirror of a computational result here.

The dynamic program for exact majority accuracy is the strongest possible salvage because it is an algorithm. Its recurrence computes finite-\(n\) profile fluctuations. With fixed continuum masses, randomized response maps the vote share to another share, so the finite-sample accuracy question disappears; retaining those fluctuations leads to a limit analysis, not a complexity result over continuous societies. The program can certainly inspire new mass-based questions about privacy and welfare, but the paper supplies no computational anchor for them. I would not send any of these as a per-result mirror. The negative case is strong for direct continuization; it cannot rule out a valuable new project inspired by the paper, but that would be a different claim.
