The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) types with rational masses \(\mu_h\) summing to \(1\), per-household demands \(d_h\), piecewise-constant utilities \(v_{hj}\) for full connection over each of \(p\) intervals, and per-capita supply \(s\), choose connection shares \(\lambda_{hj}\in[0,1]\) to maximize \(r\), subject to \(\sum_h \mu_h d_h\lambda_{hj}\le s\) for each interval \(j\) and \(\sum_j v_{hj}\lambda_{hj}\ge r\) for each type \(h\). Return individual on/off schedules realizing these shares.

The model it lives in

A finite LP over type-time connection shares, with mass-weighted supply constraints and max-min type utility; rotating service among each type's members realizes the shares through individual on/off schedules.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the egalitarian welfare-optimization aspect of Theorem 4.1. It does not mirror the \(n-1\)-cut lower bound or establish that PPA-hardness transfers to a small-support population; it also leaves the paper's \(q\)-times bin-packing questions untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can an optimal type-mass welfare allocation be implemented with a shared finite cut partition whose size is bounded by \(\tau\), \(p\), and the input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is the paper’s egalitarian electricity division problem, with household profiles represented by a nonatomic population. This preserves the individual fairness objective while making the population continuous.

Suppose a large distribution region has \(N\) households but only \(\tau\) standardized demand and utility profiles, with \(N\gg\tau\). A type \(h\) records a household’s complete profile: its fixed demand \(d_h\) and its additive utility across \(p\) time intervals. The mass \(\mu_h\) is the fraction of households of that type, with \(\sum_h\mu_h=1\). This is plausible where connection classes and recurring appliance-use patterns produce many households with the same profile. If each household has a unique profile, then \(\tau\) approaches \(N\) and the high-multiplicity case is less useful.

Here is a precise mirror, Type-Mass Egalitarian Electricity Division. The instance gives the rational masses \(\mu_h\), demands \(d_h\), utilities \(v_{hj}\) for receiving electricity throughout interval \(j\), and per-household supply \(s\). For a finite population of \(N\) households, this corresponds to total supply \(Ns\).

Choose fractions \(\lambda_{hj}\in[0,1]\), where \(\lambda_{hj}\) is the fraction of type \(h\) connected at any instant in interval \(j\). Feasibility requires
\[ \sum_h \mu_h d_h\lambda_{hj}\le s \quad\text{for every interval }j. \]
The objective is to maximize \(r\) subject to
\[ \sum_j v_{hj}\lambda_{hj}\ge r \quad\text{for every type }h. \]
A solution consists of \(r\), the fractions \(\lambda_{hj}\), and a rule assigning actual on/off schedules to households.

Those fractions do not give households fractional electricity. Within each interval, label the members of each type by \(\theta\in[0,1)\). At normalized time \(u\in[0,1]\), connect the members satisfying \((\theta+u)\bmod 1<\lambda_{hj}\). At every instant exactly a \(\lambda_{hj}\) fraction of that type is connected; over the interval, every member gets exactly that fraction of its duration. Thus each household has an integral on/off schedule and receives the same utility as every other household of its type. With piecewise-constant utilities, the resulting welfare problem is a linear program with \(O(\tau p)\) variables and constraints, solvable in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(p\), and the input bit length \(L\).

The anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that egalitarian electricity division among \(n\) finite agents may require \(n-1\) cuts and is PPA-hard to compute. The proof given here reduces from 2-consensus division: it uses supply \(n-1\), \(n-1\) agents with demand \(1\) and valuations \(v_1,\ldots,v_{n-1}\), and one agent with demand \(n-1\) and their average valuation. The construction makes the two maximal feasible sets the group of \(n-1\) small-demand agents and the single large-demand agent. The paper invokes prior results for the consensus-division hardness and cut lower bound, including Alon (1987) and Filos-Ratsikas and Goldberg (2018). The finite-agent theorem itself is proved here by reduction.

I expect this mirror to be Class A: tractable. The important change is that a type’s mass can rotate through service schedules, so every household in the type receives the same utility while the aggregate supply constraint holds at every instant. The paper’s consensus-division reduction does not by itself establish PPA-hardness for this mirror: its finite agents become non-atomic cohorts, which can be divided among schedules. This is a computational mirror of the theorem’s welfare-maximization result, not a claim that its PPA-hardness transfers.

The cut claim needs separate treatment. The rotation rule is compactly described, but its cut times can vary across the continuum of households. A natural follow-up is whether welfare-optimal allocations admit a shared finite cut partition whose size is bounded in \(\tau\), \(p\), and \(L\). I would not use the theorem’s \(n-1\) cut lower bound as evidence either way.

My weakest point is the rotation assumption. It requires sufficiently fine switching within intervals of constant utility. If operational constraints impose minimum on/off durations or forbid rapid switching, the LP may describe an unattainable allocation. The mirror still preserves the paper’s stated demands, additive utilities, supply constraint, and egalitarian objective; it leaves the separate practical concern about minimizing cuts open.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this mirror is that the proposed LP changes the allocation object that Theorem 4.1 studies. Its variables \(\lambda_{hj}\) describe the fraction of type \(h\) connected during interval \(j\). The rotation rule can give each household an integral on/off schedule, but different households switch at different times. Across a continuum of households, that need not produce a finite shared cut partition. So the LP does not settle the theorem’s cut claim; adding a finite-cut requirement would need a different model and output measure.

There is also a gap between the theorem’s hard instances and the proposed high-multiplicity regime. Its reduction uses independently chosen utility functions, making the agents distinct types when type includes the complete utility profile. In the electricity setting, grouping households by demand alone would be invalid: their utility profiles affect the egalitarian objective, and approximating profiles can change which household determines the minimum. This makes a small-support regime less immediate than the proponent suggests.

But neither objection defeats the welfare mirror. For finitely encoded, piecewise-constant utilities, the type-mass formulation is a finite LP, and the rotation construction explains how to give each household an integral schedule with the promised utility. The theorem explicitly defines the computational problem as maximizing egalitarian welfare; that question remains even if cut minimization is set aside. A repeated-profile household regime is also plausible, and the LP’s tractability would itself be a meaningful result—not a reason to dismiss the question because its answer is easy.

So I cannot honestly make the requested universal negative stick. The cut result is not mirrored by the proposed construction, and the regime behind the welfare LP needs justification. But Theorem 4.1’s welfare-computation claim survives as a worthwhile continuous-population question.
