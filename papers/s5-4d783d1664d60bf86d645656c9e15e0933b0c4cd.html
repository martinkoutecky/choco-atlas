What the mirror covers

No mirror covers a result; Proposition 4.3 and the paper’s other propositions analyze partitions, cost allocations, and secession-proofness in an already continuous population.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper studies exactly the kind of population-level object ChoCo cares about. An individual’s type is her ideal location \(t\); mass is Lebesgue measure over \([-\theta,\theta]\) or \(\mathbb R\). The choices are a partition into jurisdictions, a project location for each jurisdiction, and a contribution function \(x(t)\). A coalition can secede if its members’ current aggregate cost exceeds the cost of a new project plus their minimum possible transportation cost. A large urban population spread across locations is a plausible regime for this model.

The closest named result in substance is Proposition 4.3, proved in this paper. It says that on \(\mathbb R\), a pair \((P,x)\) is secession-proof if and only if \(P\) is the partition into optimal intervals of length \(2\sqrt g\) and \(x\) is the Rawlsian allocation. This is a sharp, useful result about the continuous population and would make a natural benchmark for a later computational study.

But it is not a qualifying anchor under your instructions. The proposition characterizes stable allocations; it does not assert that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classify its complexity. The same applies to the paper’s other numbered propositions, including Proposition 2.5’s characterization of efficient partitions. There are no named computational-complexity results to anchor a mirror on, so there are no per-anchor problems or trichotomy predictions I can honestly provide.

The weakness is fundamental: the paper’s population is already continuous, and its results are analytical rather than computational. A complexity study of finding or checking secession-proof allocations for rational finite-support populations could be a plausible ChoCo project, but that would be a proposed extension of the paper, not a continuous mirror anchored in one of its computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 4.3 is the proponent’s only plausible anchor, and it does not supply the kind of result ChoCo is meant to mirror. It characterizes stable allocations; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The paper has already made the population continuous: its agents are locations under Lebesgue measure, and its allocation is a measurable function. So a computational study would have to add an encoded input and an algorithmic task that the paper never specifies.

The best attempt would be to ask for a secession-proof allocation from a finite description of a society. But the paper’s unbounded case supplies no such input: it fixes the uniform population on \(\mathbb R\) and the cost \(g\), while the object to characterize is an allocation over that whole line. Encoding a finite list of location types and rational masses would make an algorithmic question possible, but it would replace the paper’s Lebesgue society with a different instance family. The authors might recognize that as a worthwhile extension; it is not a continuous mirror of Proposition 4.3. Proposition 2.5 and the bounded-case characterization in Proposition 4.4 have the same limitation: they are analytic results about the paper’s continuous model, not computational results with an encoded family of instances.

That is a strong case against a *mirror of this paper*, but not proof that no related ChoCo project could be worthwhile. A finite-support model with divisible masses could plausibly make secession-proof allocation algorithmic and meaningful. Its weakness as a negative case is precisely that the paper’s broader topic may support such a new question. The case against is narrower: none of the paper’s named results anchors it, and making one do so requires supplying a computational problem and input model the paper does not study.
