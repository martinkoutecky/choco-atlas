The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type graph \(H=(T,E,D)\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, and a target type \(t\), define \(V_H(x)\) as the maximum matching worth over pair masses \(y\geq 0\): cross-type pairs use edges in \(E\), same-type pairs are allowed for types in \(D\), and each type's incident pair mass (counting a same-type pair twice) is at most its mass \(x_a\). Under the promise that the dual minimizing \(\sum_{a\in T}\mu_a z_a\), subject to \(z_a\geq 0\), \(z_a+z_b\geq 1\) for \(\{a,b\}\in E\), and \(2z_a\geq 1\) for \(a\in D\), has a unique optimum, compute its coordinate \(z_t\), equivalently the integrated marginal worth \(\int_0^1 \partial_t V_H(s\mu)\,ds\).

The model it lives in

Use a finite graph of compatibility types with rational population masses. Decision variables are aggregate cross-type and same-type pair masses, each constrained by type supply; maximize total pair mass to define matching worth. The target payoff is the marginal worth along proportional population growth, which under the unique-dual-optimum promise equals the target coordinate of the fractional vertex-cover dual optimum.

The objection that survived

The proposed continuous payoff has not been proved to be the limit of finite Shapley values in the replication regime; controlling discrete marginal contributions remains open.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's exact Shapley computation question in a high-multiplicity compatibility-type regime. It does not cover the paper's special-graph algorithms or its FPRAS.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove whether the per-agent Shapley values in finite replicated instances converge to the proposed path-marginal payoff, and determine the convergence rate.
  • Define and analyze a canonical payoff when the dual optimum is not unique.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s Theorem 3, proved in the paper: computing the exact Shapley value of a specified player in an unweighted matching game is #P-complete. In this setting, the raw value counts the permutations in which that player is pivotal; dividing by \(n!\) gives the Shapley value. The theorem’s hardness reduction is from counting vertex subsets that induce perfectly matchable subgraphs.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large market or consortium in which agents form unit-value bilateral partnerships, and each agent can join at most one. Many agents may share a role: every member of a role has the same compatibility with every other role, and the same compatibility with peers in their own role. For example, a platform could have millions of participants but only hundreds of compatibility profiles. A type is that full compatibility profile; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with type \(t\). The type graph can still be arbitrary, so this does not limit the model to a few special compatibility patterns.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Give a finite type graph \(H=(T,E,D)\), where \(E\) lists compatible pairs of distinct types and \(D\) lists types whose members can pair with one another. Give rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a target type \(t\). For any population vector \(x\), define its matching worth by
\[ V_H(x)=\max \left\{\sum_{\{a,b\}\in E}y_{ab}+\sum_{a\in D}y_{aa}: y\geq 0,\ \sum_{b:\{a,b\}\in E}y_{ab}+2y_{aa}\leq x_a\ \text{for every }a \right\}. \]
Here \(y_{ab}\) is the mass of pairs formed between those types; a same-type pair uses two units of that type’s mass. Define the continuous per-agent Shapley payoff along the proportional-growth path as
\[ \psi_t(H,\mu)=\int_0^1 \partial_t V_H(s\mu)\,ds. \]
The proposed problem, Continuous Shapley-Matching, asks for \(\psi_t(H,\mu)\) exactly. To make the payoff canonical in the first version, promise that the following dual LP has a unique optimum:
\[ \min \sum_{a\in T}\mu_a z_a \quad\text{subject to}\quad z_a\geq 0,\quad z_a+z_b\geq 1\ (\{a,b\}\in E),\quad 2z_a\geq 1\ (a\in D). \]
The required answer is the coordinate \(z_t\) of that optimum. Since the worth is homogeneous and piecewise linear, uniqueness makes its derivative along the ray \(s\mu\) equal to \(z_t\) for \(s>0\). Thus the integral is exact, and the payoff for the whole type is \(\mu_t z_t\). The dual LP also gives efficiency: \(\sum_t\mu_t z_t=V_H(\mu)\).

I expect this promised problem to be tractable: it is a rational LP of size polynomial in the number of types and compatibility edges, with bit complexity polynomial in the input length. The bridge to Theorem 3 is that replicating each type \(q\mu_t\) times gives a finite unweighted matching game with complete compatibility between the corresponding role classes. As the replication grows, the normalized matching worth becomes the fractional matching worth above. On instances with a unique dual optimum, the continuous marginal prices give a natural limit of the finite per-agent Shapley payoffs. The LP’s fractional pair masses are an aggregate of integral matches among many indistinguishable agents; they are not fractional matches assigned to any one agent. That co-modification follows from continuizing the population.

The authors could recognize this as their problem because the coalition worth remains maximum matching size, and the target remains the marginal worth of an agent to a coalition. The continuous question asks for that payoff per unit of type mass. It covers Theorem 3 in this high-multiplicity regime; it does not claim to mirror every graph instance in the theorem or the paper’s separate FPRAS result.

The soft spot is the uniqueness promise. At a mass vector with multiple optimal dual prices, the radial derivative need not give an efficient allocation if each coordinate is chosen independently. A fuller mirror should define the canonical payoff there, most naturally by proving and analyzing the limit of the finite replicated Shapley values. That tie case—and the rate at which the finite values approach the continuous one—are the next questions. The positive case I trust is narrower: for generic, smooth type populations, exact Shapley computation has a concrete continuous form and an LP solution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole anchor is Theorem 3: exact Shapley computation for unweighted matching games is #P-complete. The strongest objection is that the proposed LP does not, by itself, compute the paper’s Shapley value. The paper averages marginal contributions over random arrival orders; \(\psi_t\) is a derivative along proportional growth. The dual LP computes a fractional vertex-cover price. Calling that price a continuous Shapley value requires showing that it is the limit of finite Shapley values in the proposed high-multiplicity regime. The unique-optimum promise makes the derivative well-defined, but does not itself prove that limit.

That gap looks repairable. Fix a type graph \(H\), specify same-type compatibility, and replicate each type according to its rational mass. In a random ordering, the predecessor population at any fixed rank fraction should concentrate around the corresponding multiple of \(\mu\). With a unique dual optimum, the LP price is stable around that ray. Those facts make convergence to \(z_t\) plausible, though a proof would need to control the discrete marginal contributions as well as the matching worth. The market interpretation is coherent: agents with the same compatibility profile are genuine types, and fractional pair masses aggregate integral matches.

So the negative case does not defeat this anchor. The missing convergence theorem is a real qualification on the proponent’s claim, especially for extending it through dual ties. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. For unique-optimum instances, the proposed problem is a concrete, polynomial-time continuous allocation question, and the paper’s #P-hardness result gives it a clear motivation. I cannot honestly argue the universal negative case here; the strongest skeptical position is to treat the mirror as promising but not yet validated as a Shapley limit.
