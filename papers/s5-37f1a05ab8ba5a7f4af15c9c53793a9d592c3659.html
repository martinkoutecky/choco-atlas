The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its unnumbered Theorem*—attributed to Proposition 3 of Manea (2009) and reproved here—states that, for a given preference profile, the RSD assignment is not SD-efficient if and only if some ex post assignment is not SD-efficient. Lemmas 1–3 support that structural characterization. None asserts a complexity classification such as polynomial-time solvability or NP-hardness.

One could ask whether an analogous equivalence holds for a continuum of preference types and scaled object supplies. But that would be a new efficiency-characterization question, not a continuous version of a computational result established in this paper. Under the requested anchor criterion, there are no eligible anchors and therefore no qualifying per-anchor continuous problems to propose.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right that the paper proves no complexity classification. But that fact does not rule out a computational mirror of its named Theorem*: the theorem supplies a specific predicate to study—whether RSD’s assignment is SD-efficient—and an equivalence involving the existence of an inefficient ex-post assignment. A continuous version of those decision questions can be computational even though this paper does not analyze their complexity.

For example, take \(\tau\) preference types with rational population shares \(\mu_t\), and \(m\) object classes with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), where \(\sum_j q_j=1\). Replicate the population and object supplies together, as in a large housing or school allocation market. Each unit of mass still receives one integral object; fractional entries in the type-level allocation record what fraction of a type receives each class. Define continuum RSD by the limiting random-priority process. Then ask, given \((\mu,q)\), whether that allocation is SD-efficient and whether some ex-post-efficient allocation is SD-inefficient. SD improvement can be defined to benefit a positive mass of agents, which gives the continuum version a meaningful strict-improvement test.

The natural-mirror objection is that this is “a new efficiency-characterization question.” But the population is precisely what has been continuized, and the questions can be posed as algorithmic problems over rational, finite-support inputs. Their computational study could ask for complexity in \(m\), \(\tau\), and input bit length, and could use cycle tests or optimization over type-level allocation variables.

There is also a real asymptotic issue to investigate. In finite RSD, every priority ordering has positive probability, which is why the paper’s support argument reaches every Pareto-optimal deterministic assignment. Under replication, some orderings may become vanishingly rare; the limit may not preserve that full-support property. Whether the theorem’s equivalence survives is therefore not settled merely by restating the finite proof. The model is sensible, the question is recognizable, and the limit could expose a genuine boundary. So the lack of a complexity theorem in this paper weakens the case for a *direct computational-result mirror*, but it does not support the stronger claim that there is no worthwhile continuous mirror.
