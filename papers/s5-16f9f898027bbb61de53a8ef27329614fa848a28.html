The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result, so it has no anchor that satisfies your rule. Its closest computational contribution is LaRSA: the paper says it enumerates rounded degressively proportional (RDP) allocations and returns each country’s minimum and maximum seat count. It reports roughly six hours for 27 states, nearly six billion feasible allocations, and 3.6 billion allocations with no country losing seats. But LaRSA is attributed to Łyko and Rudek (2013), and these findings are not stated as a numbered result here. The paper also applies a squared-distance criterion to select an allocation, but that is not a qualifying anchor either.

The strongest candidate mirror I can make is High-Multiplicity RDP Seat Selection, motivated by that unnumbered computation. Let types be classes of seat-receiving jurisdictions with the same relevant attributes, including per-jurisdiction population \(p_t\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of jurisdictions of type \(t\), and let \(q_t\) be its rational target quota—for the paper’s proposed criterion, \(q_t=A(p_t)\). Given seat bounds \(m,M\) and a per-jurisdiction seat budget \(\bar H\), choose an integer seat count \(s_t\) for each type to minimize
\[ \sum_t \mu_t(s_t-q_t)^2 \]
subject to
\[ \sum_t\mu_t s_t=\bar H,\qquad m\le s_t\le M, \]
and, after ordering types by population,
\[ s_t\le s_{t+1},\qquad \frac{p_t}{s_t}\le\frac{p_{t+1}}{s_{t+1}}. \]
A solution is a minimizing seat vector, or a report that none exists. With 27 distinct types of mass \(1/27\) and \(\bar H=751/27\), this recovers the paper’s RDP selection objective and constraints.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large federation allocating seats among many electoral districts with a small number of recurring jurisdiction types. The districts are the agents; mass records the share of districts in each type; each district still receives an integer number of seats. This keeps the paper’s apportionment question intact while allowing the number of districts to greatly exceed the number of types. For the paper’s actual 27-country case, however, there is no such gap: the units are few and distinct.

I expect this candidate to follow hardness transfers if the corresponding discrete optimization is hard. A finite profile with \(n_t\) jurisdictions of type \(t\) is represented by \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and rational masses can be scaled back to integer multiplicities. That makes the continuous version an exact high-multiplicity representation, rather than a source of automatic tractability. The paper establishes no hardness or algorithmic classification, though. The next questions would be whether the optimization admits an algorithm polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length, and how feasibility, per-type seat ranges, and no-loss constraints behave under that representation.

The weak point is the scenario and the absent anchor. The article allocates seats to 27 member states; it does not study repeated jurisdiction types or computational complexity. The district scenario is a plausible extension of its allocation problem, but not a result the paper itself proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. Its main computation applies LaRSA, an algorithm attributed to earlier work, to the fixed 27-state case. The reported billions of feasible allocations and the proposed minimizing allocation are instance findings, not a theorem about the complexity of a problem. The squared-distance criterion could motivate a new problem, but the paper gives no complexity result for it.

The proposed high-multiplicity version does not fix that gap. Treating jurisdictions as agents with exact population types is coherent in a large federation, but it changes the paper’s setting. With the Treaty’s fixed total of 751 seats and minimum of 6 per state, there can be at most 125 states. An unbounded regime therefore requires scaling the total seat supply, which makes this a generalized apportionment problem rather than the paper’s post-Brexit problem. Treating citizens as the agents does not preserve the allocation question: seats are assigned to whole jurisdictions, not to individual citizens.

There is a further limit to the proposed type-based relaxation. Under rounded degressive proportionality, two jurisdictions with the same population must receive the same number of seats: the seat counts must be nondecreasing, while the population-to-seat ratios must also be nondecreasing. So a continuum cannot split one exact jurisdiction type across different integer seat bundles while preserving RDP. A measure over jurisdiction sizes with an integer allocation at each size is a possible generalization, but it is not the paper’s result, and grouping different population values into one type would change the RDP constraints.

That makes the paper a poor anchor for this programme: the proposed mirror is a new high-multiplicity apportionment question, not a continuous version of a computational result established here. I cannot honestly turn that into the stronger claim that no scenario could make such a question worthwhile. A federation with many recurring jurisdiction types could support a sensible study; the negative case is strong against this paper as evidence for one, but weak against that broader possibility.
