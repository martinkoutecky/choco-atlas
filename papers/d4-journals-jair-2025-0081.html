What the mirror covers

The proposed model attempts to mirror the existence question in Theorem 1 and the stable-outcome search problem in Theorem 5. It leaves the social-welfare, dynamics, and price-of-anarchy results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

A credible mirror is the nonatomic version of Nash stability in the paper’s volunteer scenario: a large pool of volunteers chooses among a small number of charities or organizations. A type is a volunteer’s complete skill set; its mass is the fraction of the pool with that profile. For example, a region might have many thousands of volunteers but only a few dozen recurring training profiles. Different members of one type may join different organizations, so type mass describes the population’s choices, not a mixed strategy for an individual.

Here is a precise model. The input is a finite skill set \(S\), tasks \(r\) with required skill sets \(D_r\subseteq S\) and positive rational weights \(w_r\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over skill types \(A\subseteq S\), and a bound \(q\) on the number of coalitions. An allocation is \(x_{A,j}\geq 0\), the mass of type \(A\) in coalition \(j\), with \(\sum_{j=1}^q x_{A,j}=\mu_A\). Write \(\rho_{s,j}=\sum_{A\ni s}x_{A,j}\) for the mass holding skill \(s\) in coalition \(j\). Task \(r\) is performed there exactly when \(\rho_{s,j}>0\) for every \(s\in D_r\).

The per-capita-rescaled utility of type \(A\) in coalition \(j\) is

\[ U_{A,j}(x)= \sum_{\substack{r:\ \rho_{s,j}>0\ \forall s\in D_r}} \ \sum_{s\in D_r\cap A} \frac{w_r}{|D_r|\rho_{s,j}}. \]

This replaces the paper’s count of skill-holders with their population mass. The common rescaling preserves payoff comparisons: in a population of size \(n\), multiplying the paper’s utility by \(n\) gives this expression when masses are counts divided by \(n\).

The lead question, Nonatomic Nash-Stable Hedonic Skill Game, asks whether an input admits an allocation \(x\) such that

\[ x_{A,j}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ U_{A,j}(x)=\max_{k\le q}U_{A,k}(x) \]

for every type and coalition. A solution is the mass allocation itself; a “yes” answer can be witnessed by an exact allocation. This is a population-level best-response condition: a member of a type assigned positive mass to an organization must not prefer another organization at the current population allocation.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for general hedonic skill game instances, deciding whether a Nash-stable outcome exists is NP-complete when \(q\geq 3\). The authors prove it by reducing from PARTITION. For each integer \(a_i\), they create an agent with skills \(\{s_i,s'_i,s''_i\}\), two further agents with skills \(\{s_0,s_1,\ldots,s_n\}\), and \(q-2\) agents with those latter skills plus \(s_{n+1}\) and all \(s''_i\). They add weight-\(4a_i\) tasks requiring \(\{s_i,s'_i\}\), weight-\(0.4\) tasks requiring \(s''_i\), and a task of weight \(4B-0.4\) requiring \(\{s_0,s_{n+1}\}\). The constructed game has a Nash-stable outcome exactly when the \(a_i\) can be split into two groups summing to \(B\).

For the continuous question, I expect Class A: it always has a solution, obtainable by putting all mass in one coalition. In that allocation every performable task is performed by the grand coalition, and each type’s utility is nonnegative. An infinitesimal member moving to an empty coalition does not create positive skill mass there, so earns zero; no member has a strictly profitable deviation. The PARTITION reduction’s integer balancing condition disappears when population mass can be divided.

A second, separate anchor is Theorem 5, also proved here: computing a Nash-stable outcome with singleton tasks is PLS-complete when \(q=2\). The paper reduces from Max Cut Flip: each graph vertex becomes an agent, each edge becomes a skill held by its endpoints and a singleton-skill task weighted by the edge weight, and the two coalitions represent the cut’s two sides. A Nash-stable outcome maps to a cut where flipping no vertex improves the cut. The corresponding continuous search problem, Nonatomic Singleton-Task Equilibrium, has the same mass-allocation input and equilibrium condition, with singleton-skill tasks and \(q=2\); its solution is an allocation \(x\). It too has an immediate Class A algorithm: place all mass in the first coalition. This anchor independently shows how an atomic local-search obstruction can disappear in the population limit.

The mirror covers the existence and computation of stable outcomes in Theorems 1 and 5. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s social-welfare or price-of-anarchy results. The skill sets, task technology, reward-sharing formula, and coalition choices are preserved, so this is a recognizable nonatomic version of the authors’ problem. The many-volunteer, few-training-profile regime is also a plausible high-multiplicity setting for that version.

The soft spot is consequential: an infinitesimal mover cannot activate a task by being its first skill-holder. The discrete paper’s agent can do exactly that, and that pivotal contribution is central to both hardness constructions. The all-in-one equilibrium therefore gives a clean continuous analogue, but it does not preserve the discrete game’s strategic bite. A useful next question is how its equilibria relate to finite populations—especially whether they yield approximate discrete stability, or whether the pivotal effect of one person persists under every reasonable scaling.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The volunteers scenario is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, so I would not reject the mirror on grounds that volunteers cannot be grouped into types. The difficulty is more specific: the paper’s Nash stability depends on an individual changing which skills are present in a coalition. In an atomless population, one individual has zero mass. Moving them changes neither \(\rho_{s,j}\) nor whether a task is performed. The per-capita rescaling preserves payoff comparisons; it does not restore that individual’s effect on the coalition.

That makes the proposed mirror of Theorem 1 a poor computational analogue. Its PARTITION reduction relies on indivisible agents whose moves affect skill coverage and whose utilities enforce an integer balance. With the proposed mass allocation, types can split across coalitions, while individual deviations cannot activate tasks. In fact, putting all population mass in one coalition is stable: every task feasible for the population is performed there, and a zero-mass move to an empty coalition earns nothing. This is not merely a surprising answer to the continuous question; it reflects that the limit has removed the pivotal action on which the discrete existence problem rests. Treating an entire type’s mass as one indivisible deviator might restore pivotal moves, but then identical volunteers must act as a coordinated bloc. That is a different game, not the nonatomic limit of individual choice.

The same issue defeats the proposed mirror of Theorem 5. In the Max Cut Flip reduction, a vertex’s move changes which edge skills are present on each side. In a continuum, an individual’s move changes no skill mass, while splitting the mass associated with a vertex gives a fractional assignment rather than a vertex flip. The all-in-one allocation is again stable for singleton tasks and \(q=2\). Keeping vertices as indivisible positive-mass players would preserve the flip, but would retain atomic strategic units instead of making the population itself continuous.

The underlying technology also weakens a natural alternative anchor in the paper, Theorem 6 on social-welfare maximization. If every skill has positive mass and \(q\) is finite, one can divide each type’s mass so that every skill appears in every coalition. Then every globally feasible task is performed in all \(q\) coalitions, however little mass is assigned to each. The discrete limit on task repetitions, set by how many agents possess each skill, disappears. This points to a broader mismatch in this model: tasks have a zero threshold for skill presence, while the continuum permits arbitrarily small positive allocations.

One could require positive mass in every coalition, impose minimum staffing levels, or study deviations by positive masses of agents. Those choices may produce interesting computational questions, but each repairs the problem by adding a threshold or changing unilateral Nash deviations into group deviations. Neither is supplied by the paper’s model. For its stated tasks and solution concept, I find the case against the two anchors strong: atomless Nash stability discards the individual pivotality they study. I cannot make the broader claim airtight for every redesigned volunteer model; quota-based tasks or coordinated migration might be worthwhile subjects, but they would be new models rather than continuous mirrors of these results.
