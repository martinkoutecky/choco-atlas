The continuous mirror question

Given \(K\) arms and \(\tau\) types with known positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, each type has an unknown reciprocal preference matrix \(P_t\) with a Condorcet winner \(a_t^\star\). Each round, a learner chooses two policies \(\pi,\pi' \in \Delta_K\), samples an arm from each, and receives one independent preference response from each type. Define \(u_t(\pi)=\sum_{a=1}^K \pi(a)\,2P_{t,a,a_t^\star}\) and \(\Phi_\mu(\pi)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\log u_t(\pi)\right)\), with zero welfare if any positive-mass type has zero utility. What is the minimax expected cumulative regret over horizon \(H\), as a function of \(H,K,\tau,\mu\), and the preference gaps, and can an algorithm attain the resulting rate?

The model it lives in

A finite-support society of preference types with masses \(\mu_t\), where each type has its own unknown pairwise preferences and contributes one response per round. The learner chooses policies over arms to maximize the weighted geometric mean \(\Phi_\mu(\pi)=\prod_{t=1}^{\tau}u_t(\pi)^{\mu_t}\); the objective is to determine minimax regret and an algorithm attaining its rate.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the regret lower-bound question from Theorem 4.1. It leaves the upper guarantees in Theorems 4.2 and 4.3 unproved for weighted welfare, as well as the rate’s dependence on unequal masses.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the minimax rate depend on small or unequal type masses?
  • How does the rate change if feedback is sampled in proportion to mass or scales with population size?
  • Can winner identification and welfare optimization be combined to improve the stated upper bounds?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is an online one: a large recommendation platform serves a population with a small number of stable preference segments. A type is a segment’s complete pairwise preference matrix \(P_t\), including its Condorcet winner. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the served population in that segment. The number of people \(N\) can be much larger than the number of types \(\tau\). Each round, the platform chooses two policies over the finite set of arms and gets one preference response from a representative evaluator of each type. The policy is judged by its welfare across the whole population, with segment size determining each segment’s weight.

My anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper (Appendix A.1). It says that for even \(D,K\ge4\), under the assumed existence of a Condorcet winner for every user and the paper’s Condorcet-relative score, every online algorithm has expected total regret at least \(\Omega(T^{2/3}\min(K,D)^{1/3})\) on some instance. This is a statistical regret lower bound: the hard instances force a learner to choose between costly exploration and sustained welfare loss. It is not a claim of NP-hardness or a static running-time lower bound.

The continuous problem I would hand to a prover is Continuous-Type Fair Dueling Bandits. An instance has \(K\) arms, horizon \(H\), a finite support \(T\) of preference types, and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. Each type has an unknown reciprocal preference matrix \(P_t\) with a Condorcet winner \(a_t^\star\). On a round, the learner chooses two policies \(\pi,\pi'\in\Delta_K\), samples an arm from each, and receives one independent pairwise response from each type. Define
\[ u_t(\pi)=\sum_{a\in[K]}\pi(a)\,2P_{t,a,a_t^\star}, \qquad \Phi_\mu(\pi)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log u_t(\pi)\right), \]
with zero welfare when a positive-mass type has zero utility. The regret is the sum over rounds of \(\Phi_\mu(\pi^\star)-(\Phi_\mu(\pi)+\Phi_\mu(\pi'))/2\), where \(\pi^\star\) maximizes \(\Phi_\mu\). The question is to characterize the minimax expected regret as a function of \(H,K,\tau,\mu\), and the preference gaps, and give an algorithm attaining that rate. A solution could establish matching upper and lower bounds, or identify which parameters prevent a match.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: the platform serves many people, but their preference models fall into a modest number of segments; a fixed evaluation panel samples those segments while population masses determine fairness. The mass-weighted geometric mean is the natural continuous version of the paper’s NSW objective. If \(D\) distinct types have equal mass \(1/D\), then \(\Phi_\mu\) is the \(D\)-th root of the paper’s product welfare, so it selects the same optimal policies. On that uniform, finite-support subfamily, Theorem 4.1 gives a lower bound for the continuous question too: since \(x^{1/D}-y^{1/D}\ge(x-y)/D\) for \(1\ge x\ge y\ge0\), its regret lower bound implies \(\Omega(H^{2/3}\min(K,\tau)^{1/3}/\tau)\) for the normalized welfare when \(\tau=D\). Thus I expect a B-style transfer on this subfamily, while the dependence on unequal masses remains open.

The mirror covers the paper’s regret lower bound, not its entire contribution. Fair-ETC and Fair-\(\epsilon\)-Greedy suggest candidate algorithms for the continuous problem, but their stated upper-bound theorems are not already proofs for mass-weighted welfare. Further questions include how the rate depends on small segment masses, whether knowing the masses or Condorcet winners changes it, and whether exploration can jointly identify winners and optimize welfare. Given known utilities, maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\log u_t(\pi)\) is a concave optimization problem over the simplex; the paper’s anchor is therefore about the cost of learning, not a hard optimization oracle.

My weakest point is the observation model. The paper receives a feedback bit from every named user each round. The mirror uses a fixed, type-stratified panel; querying a continuum of people at once would change the statistical problem, while querying one representative per type gives a sensible fixed-budget evaluation setting. A referee might regard that change as too large for a direct transfer, especially for repeated types whose individual responses would otherwise supply extra samples. The case survives as a plausible continuous-population version of the paper’s fair preference-learning question, but the lower bound above transfers directly only on the equal-mass, one-type-per-evaluator subfamily.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor the proponent raises is Theorem 4.1. Its lower bound concerns statistical regret: how much an algorithm must lose while learning unknown preference matrices. The paper’s upper-bound theorems are regret guarantees too; none gives a running-time or complexity classification for a computational social choice problem. The proposed mirror asks for minimax regret and a rate-optimal learning algorithm, so it carries that statistical question into a weighted model. That may be useful online-learning research, but it does not supply the kind of complexity landscape ChoCo is set up to chart.

There is a more basic obstacle to treating the proposed model as a high-multiplicity limit of this paper. Each round, the paper receives a response from every user. If many people share a type and all respond, repeated types supply many independent observations each round; as the population grows, learning the type’s preference matrix becomes arbitrarily fast. A fixed panel with one response per type avoids that collapse, but fixes the feedback budget while population size grows. Sampling evaluators according to population mass gives yet another learning problem, with rare types observed less often. The panel model is coherent, and its equal-mass finite case can inherit Theorem 4.1’s lower bound after the welfare normalization. But that transfer retains the paper’s finite feedback protocol; it does not show that the theorem’s hard regime persists under population multiplicity.

The better version of the mirror—unequal masses, a fixed query budget, and regret rates depending on the masses—could pose a worthwhile statistical question. Its difficulty would be how queries reveal unknown preferences and how the weighted welfare values errors. With preferences known, however, maximizing the weighted log welfare, \(\sum_t \mu_t\log u_t(\pi)\), is a concave optimization over the \(K\)-arm simplex. The population continuum creates no apparent pricing problem or computational bottleneck for continuous optimization. The proposed problem’s central contribution would be its sampling model and regret analysis.

So the strongest case against this anchor is about fit: it is not a computational-complexity result of the kind the programme targets, and the claimed population limit requires a choice of feedback protocol that changes the learning problem. That case is weak as a universal claim. A large platform with stable preference segments and a limited evaluation panel is plausible, and the resulting weighted-regret problem could be worthwhile in online learning. I cannot honestly argue that no such mirror deserves study; I can argue that this anchor does not establish a compelling ChoCo complexity mirror.
