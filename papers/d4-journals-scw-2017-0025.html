The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is that taxation is a natural high-multiplicity setting: in a large payroll or tax system, many taxpayers may share one taxable-income level, so the number of people can be far larger than the number of distinct income types. A type can be an income \(c_t\), and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of taxpayers at that income. Given per-capita post-tax resources \(E\), a continuous allocation is a vector \(x_t\) with \(0\le x_t\le c_t\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=E\). The decision is the post-tax income assigned to each type; the paper’s rules select that allocation rather than optimize an objective.

For example, a precise candidate is High-Multiplicity Leveling-Tax Evaluation: given finite rational data \((\mu_t,c_t)\) and rational \(E\), compute \(x_t=\min(c_t,\lambda)\), where \(\lambda\) is chosen so that \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=E\). This is a plausible population-continuous version of a rule discussed in the paper, and the type-level output is exactly what the rule assigns. Its expected complexity is tractable. But the paper does not state or prove that complexity result, so this is a proposed mirror, not an eligible anchor.

There are no named computational-complexity results in the paper: no theorem or proposition establishes that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that a taxation rule satisfies continuity, equal treatment of equals, consistency, and composition down if and only if it is a generalized equal-sacrifice rule. Theorem 2 characterizes the rules satisfying those four axioms plus homogeneity: the leveling tax or constrained equal-sacrifice rules with the stated power or logarithmic utility indices. Theorem 3 (Young, 1987a) and Theorem 4 (Young, 1988) are cited axiomatic characterizations. These results classify rules; they do not analyze algorithms or complexity. None can honestly serve as the requested anchor.

The weak point is therefore decisive for this task: the paper’s contribution is axiomatic, and continuizing an axiomatic question while the society remains finite is explicitly outside ChoCo’s scope. A continuous-population tax-allocation computation is plausible and distinct from merely observing that money is divisible, but this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. The positive case can establish a credible research direction, not an anchored continuous mirror of a result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

On the required anchor standard, the case against is strong: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes rules by axioms; Theorem 2 adds homogeneity; Theorems 3 and 4 are cited axiomatic characterizations. The paper’s “continuity” is continuity of a rule as the data of a finite-population problem change, not a continuous-population model. None of these results asks or answers an algorithmic or complexity question.

The proponent’s High-Multiplicity Leveling-Tax Evaluation is the best candidate, but it is a new computational question about a rule named in the paper, not a mirror of a result the paper establishes. Generalizing it to arbitrary generalized equal-sacrifice rules would also require specifying how the partition and functions are encoded as finite input. That could define a separate project; it cannot supply the missing anchor. This objection does not depend on the problem being easy or on what its complexity turns out to be.

The broader claim that no continuous mirror could be worthwhile is much harder to defend. Taxpayers with the same income are a plausible high-multiplicity type, and the rule’s allocations do not depend on their individual identities. There is no obvious population-limit degeneracy either. So the honest negative finding is narrower: this paper offers no computational result for ChoCo to continuize. A computational study of tax-rule evaluation might be worthwhile, but the paper’s axiomatic theorems do not anchor it.
