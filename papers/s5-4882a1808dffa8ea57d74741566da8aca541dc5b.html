There is still a plausible *prospective* mirror of the paper’s stable shift-allocation problem. Consider a large organization assigning recurring shifts. A type \(t\) is a complete employee profile: a ranking of the shift categories and the utility scores that determine how each shift ranks applicants. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of employees of type \(t\), and let \(q_s\) be the fraction of positions in shift category \(s\), with both distributions summing to \(1\). In a plausible regime, many employees share contract and preference profiles, so the population is in the tens of thousands while the number \(\tau\) of distinct types is far smaller.

Call the problem Type-Optimal Stable Shift Allocation\(_\infty\). Given the rational masses \(\mu_t\), shift capacities \(q_s\), employee rankings, and shift priorities over types, compute assignment masses \(x_{t,s}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}=q_s\). Require stability: there must be no type–shift pair where positive mass of type \(t\) is assigned to shifts it ranks below \(s\), while \(s\) has capacity vacant or assigned to lower-priority types. Among stable assignments, return the employee-optimal one: each type receives the most preferred rank distribution achievable in any stable assignment. The total utility \(\sum_{t,s}x_{t,s}u_{t,s}\) can be reported, as the paper reports utility totals, but the core solution condition is stability and employee optimality.

The most plausible regime is a large hospital or other 24/7 organization with a recurring rota: shift capacities grow with the workforce, while many staff share contract-defined availability, priorities, and preference profiles. That is a genuine high-multiplicity story, although it goes beyond the paper’s ten-person illustration. With finitely many shift categories and type profiles, a mass version of deferred acceptance looks tractable: types propose in preference order, and each shift retains the highest-priority mass up to capacity. I would expect this mirror to be Class A, with running time polynomial in the number of types, shift categories, and input bit length. Further questions include whether the paper’s Boston mechanism has a natural mass analogue, how welfare varies across stable allocations, and when a fractional allocation can be rounded to a finite workforce without changing the outcome.

The weak point is also the decisive one for this assignment: this is an extrapolation from an algorithm the paper describes, not a mirror of a qualifying named result. The paper gives no complexity theorem for the underlying matching problem, and the stable-allocation formulation above adds a type-level optimality question that its example does not formally analyze. The positive case is therefore a credible research direction, but it cannot meet the requested anchor requirement.
