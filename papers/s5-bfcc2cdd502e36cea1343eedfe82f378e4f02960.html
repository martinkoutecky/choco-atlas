The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of schools \(C\), a finite support of student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_c\), each type’s school preferences and priority attributes, and finite-stage monotone, non-excessive-reduction choice protocols encoded by rational piecewise-affine rules, determine whether there is an exact algorithm polynomial in \(|C|\), \(|T|\), input bit length \(L\), and protocol size that computes the unique student-optimal procedurally stable mass assignment. Does the induced mechanism remain group-strategyproof against positive-mass coalitions?

The model it lives in

Each type has identical preferences and all school-relevant characteristics; each school has a finite-stage choice protocol acting on mass, with capacities scaled to population size. The variables \(y_{t,c,s}\) record the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(c\) at stage \(s\), plus unmatched mass. Each individual still receives one indivisible school place or remains unmatched; the type-level assignment may be fractional.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe in is for Theorem 2, proved in this paper in Section 6. The paper has no named result classifying a problem as NP-hard, in P, or in a parameterized class. Theorem 2 is instead a constructive matching result: its proof transforms the problem into an ordinary college admissions problem and computes the outcome using student-proposing deferred acceptance.

Theorem 2 says: (i) if all choice protocols are monotonic, then for every student preference profile there exists a unique student-optimal procedurally stable matching; and (ii) if the protocols are also non-excessive-reduction, the mechanism selecting that matching is group-strategyproof for students. The theorem is proved here, using known results about deferred acceptance for the transformed problem. I would use this as the sole anchor. Its school-choice application is especially apt: the paper itself discusses sibling access and distributing spare capacity between student groups.

My lead mirror is Continuous Complex-Constraint School Choice. Consider a large school district with \(N\) students, a finite set of schools \(C\), and a finite support of student types \(T\). A type specifies a student’s strict school preferences and every characteristic used by the schools’ choice rules—for example, sibling status, sex, and priority category at each school. Thus members of a type are interchangeable for the purposes of the mechanism. The input gives each type’s rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Each school has capacity \(\kappa_c\) per unit of population, so its capacity in a district of size \(N\) is \(N\kappa_c\).

Each school also has a finite-stage choice protocol: a strict priority order over types at each stage, a first-stage capacity, and rational capacity functions for later stages, depending on seats left unused earlier. Encode those functions as finite rational piecewise-affine rules and require monotonicity and the paper’s non-excessive-reduction condition, extended to rational masses. A school could, for example, consider sibling types first and divide any resulting spare capacity between male and female applicants.

The requested solution is a mass assignment \(y_{t,c,s}\): the amount of type \(t\) assigned to school \(c\) in stage \(s\), plus the amount left unmatched. Every individual still receives one indivisible school place or remains unmatched; \(y\) records how the population of each type is distributed. The assignment must respect each type’s mass, school capacities, and stage eligibility. It is procedurally stable if no type mass prefers another school that would accept a positive amount of it at some stage—because that stage has spare capacity or holds lower-priority mass—and anyone assigned at a later stage could not have obtained a place at an earlier one. Among stable assignments, student-optimality can be defined by requiring that, for each type, at least as much mass as in any other stable assignment gets one of its top \(k\) schools, for every rank cutoff \(k\). The problem is to compute that unique student-optimal mass assignment for each report profile, and thus define the associated mechanism. Group-strategyproofness means that no positive-mass coalition can jointly misreport so that every member gets a strictly preferred school.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). A mass version of student-proposing deferred acceptance can operate on the \(\tau\) types, letting a school accept or reject portions of a type at a priority cutoff. Each type can advance through its finite preference list, while the protocol functions use exact rational arithmetic. The concrete algorithmic question is whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(|C|\), \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and the protocol description size—without expanding a type of mass \(1/D\) into \(D\) separate students. The paper’s theorem motivates the outcome and mechanism; it does not already establish this continuous algorithm or its running time.

This is a population continuization: the type masses and capacities scale with the district, while the assignment each student receives remains indivisible. It also gives the theorem a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A district with many thousands of students but a much smaller number of recurring preference and priority profiles is a sensible setting, provided those profiles really capture every feature the mechanism uses. The authors should recognize the question: it retains their student-optimality, procedural-stability, and truthful-reporting goals, and their own school-choice discussion supplies the institutional example.

The weak point is whether the type support stays small without distorting the actual priority system. If consequential grades, lottery numbers, or other criteria distinguish nearly every applicant, then the complete type definition can make \(\tau\) nearly \(N\), eliminating the compression. The mirror is strongest for school systems whose operative rules genuinely use recurring priority classes; it should not be sold as a continuous account of the paper’s German admissions example if those applicants’ fine-grained scores must remain part of their types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against starts with the anchor itself: Theorem 2 is not a complexity result. It establishes existence, student optimality, and group strategyproofness, then obtains those properties by transforming the problem and applying known deferred acceptance results. It gives no computational problem with a stated input model, running time, or hardness classification. The proposed polynomial-time mass algorithm would therefore be a new algorithmic extension of the theorem, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

There is also a real modeling cost in the proposed finite-support version. The paper gives each university a strict ranking of individual applicants, and defines each type as identical in every feature the mechanism uses. If a type must include exact priority scores or positions in those rankings, many applicants will become singleton types and the intended compression disappears. If the model instead merges applicants into priority categories, a school may split a type at a cutoff. Then individual strategyproofness is vacuous for measure-zero agents, while strategyproofness against positive-mass coalitions is a newly chosen strategic notion; Theorem 2 does not establish it for that model. A district using only a few priority categories could avoid this problem, but that is a narrower institutional regime than the paper’s general strict rankings.

Continuum school choice and continuum deferred acceptance are already established models: for example, Grigoryan describes a continuum of applicants and finitely many schools, with preferences and priority scores represented by a distribution and DA characterized through cutoffs. That work studies analytic properties and convergence to finite markets, so it does not answer the computational question here or count as a collision under ChoCo’s rules. It does make the proposed mass-DA mechanism look like an extension of familiar continuum matching machinery, rather than a computational problem the paper itself brought into view. [Grigoryan, “A continuum model as a limit of large finite matching markets”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.3982/TE5478)

This is a limited negative case. A district with recurring preference profiles and genuinely coarse priority classes is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and extending complex-constraint DA to that regime could be worthwhile. I cannot honestly rule out that mirror in every scenario. My strongest objection is that the proposed anchor does not itself supply the computational problem, and its clean finite-type version changes how the paper’s strict priority structure works. The case against is therefore weaker than the case for.
