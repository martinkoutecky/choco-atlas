The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(S\), finite complete student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), true school rankings, immutable category maps \(a_s(t)\) and scores \(\sigma_s(t)\), per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), and rational non-increasing piecewise-linear adjustments \(\alpha_{s,a}\), does there exist an exact polynomial-time direct mechanism with the following properties? For every reported mass profile \(\widehat\mu_{t,R}\), it returns assignment masses \(x_{t,R,s}\) summing to \(\widehat\mu_{t,R}\), respects \(\sum_{t,R}x_{t,R,s}\le\kappa_s\), and is stable at reported rankings: no positive mass assigned to a less-preferred option can move to a preferred school by displacing positive incumbent mass with lower adjusted priority, and no acceptable applicant mass can take an unfilled seat. At school \(s\), type \(t\)’s priority is \(\sigma_s(t)+\alpha_{s,a_s(t)}(z_{s,a_s(t)})\), where \(z_{s,a}\) is the mass of category \(a\) assigned there. The mechanism must also prevent every positive-measure coalition from jointly changing reports so that almost every coalition member strictly improves under their true ranking.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity admissions cohort represented by rational masses over finitely many complete student types. Each type carries its true school ranking, school-specific categories, and scores; assignment variables allocate that mass across schools and the unmatched option, capacities scale with cohort size, and adjusted priorities depend on assigned category mass.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named complexity result establishing a problem is in P, hard, or fixed-parameter tractable. Its closest algorithmic anchor is Theorem 1 (proved here): under the paper’s PD, WO, and DC assumptions on school priorities, the constructed SPDA mechanism \(\bar\phi\) produces a stable matching and is group strategy-proof. Appendix D separately gives a polynomial implementation, with running time \(O(|I|^4|S|)\). There is no hardness result in the paper. So this is a possible algorithmic mirror, but weaker evidence for ChoCo’s complexity-landscape claim than a named complexity theorem would be.

The strongest plausible setting is a large centralized school-admissions cohort. A type would specify a student’s true ranking of schools, school-specific diversity categories, and exam-score values. These attributes must all match within a type. A cohort with many applicants but a much smaller number of combinations of rankings, score bands, and categories is a credible high-multiplicity regime. Mass is the fraction of the cohort with each type. School capacities scale with cohort size to per-capita capacities; each student still receives one school, though the assignment of a type’s mass can be divided among schools.

Lead mirror: Continuous Controlled School Choice with Adjusted Priorities. Given schools \(S\), finite types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), per-capita capacities \(\kappa_s\), and each type’s preferences and immutable priority attributes, construct a direct mechanism. For school \(s\), let \(a_s(t)\) be type \(t\)’s category and \(\sigma_s(t)\) its score. Let \(\alpha_{s,a}\) be a rational, non-increasing piecewise-linear function of the mass of category \(a\) assigned to \(s\). If that mass is \(z_{s,a}\), type \(t\)’s adjusted priority score is

\[ f_{s,t}(z)=\sigma_s(t)+\alpha_{s,a_s(t)}(z_{s,a_s(t)}). \]

The mechanism takes students’ reported rankings and returns a measurable assignment of mass to schools and to the unmatched option. For a report group of mass \(\widehat\mu_{t,R}\), its assignment variables \(x_{t,R,s}\) must satisfy
\[ \sum_{s\in S\cup\{\emptyset\}}x_{t,R,s}=\widehat\mu_{t,R}, \qquad \sum_{t,R}x_{t,R,s}\le \kappa_s. \]
Require the result to be stable at the reported rankings: no positive mass assigned below a school can move there and displace a lower-priority incumbent, and no acceptable applicant mass can take an unfilled seat. Also require mass group strategy-proofness: no positive-measure coalition can jointly misreport so that every coalition member gets a strictly preferred school.

The proposed mirror changes population counts to population shares in the adjustment rule, while keeping the paper’s priority dependence on assigned composition. That is a plausible co-modification: a policy that adjusts priorities by category share is a natural counterpart to one that adjusts them by category count. The paper’s adjusted-scoring framework supports the idea, though its named Theorem 1 is stated for finite populations.

I would provisionally expect this mirror to be Class A for finite type support and piecewise-rational adjustment functions. The paper’s route through substitutable choice functions and deferred acceptance gives a concrete starting point. But a mass version of that mechanism is not automatic: its finite tie-breaking and group-strategy-proofness arguments may not survive splitting a type across schools. The precise computational question is whether an exact mechanism with these properties can be computed in time polynomial in the school count, type support, input bit length, and adjustment-function description size.

Further questions include whether mass deferred acceptance terminates with weak priorities, whether its discrete approximations converge when masses lie on a \(1/N\) grid, and whether a student-optimal mass-stable mechanism exists under the linear-priority condition of Theorem 2.

The weak point is the group-strategy-proofness requirement. Individual strategy-proofness in a nonatomic population can become vacuous because one student has zero mass; requiring strategy-proofness against positive-mass coalitions avoids that, but it is a new property that does not follow from Theorem 1. There is also a support-size risk: if nearly every student has a distinct score or ranking, then the proposed type compression buys little. The positive case therefore rests on a specific admissions regime with repeated, complete types, not on school choice in general.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case should not claim the paper lacks an algorithmic result. Theorem 1 gives a stable, group-strategy-proof mechanism, and Appendix D gives an implementation bound of \(O(|I|^4|S|)\). Theorem 2 adds student-optimality under linear priorities. The limitation is that the paper’s main result is about a mechanism’s incentives and fairness, not a complexity classification for a search or optimization problem.

The harder obstacle is that group strategy-proofness is defined over named students: no coalition, including a single student, can all gain by misreporting. A mechanism that sees only type masses cannot detect one student changing their report. In a sequence of finite populations, it likewise loses deviations by coalitions whose size is \(o(N)\). Individual strategy-proofness therefore becomes vacuous for a distribution-only mechanism. Requiring every coalition of positive measure to be unable to gain, as the proposed mirror does, is a coherent new property, but it tests a different scale of coalition and drops part of the theorem’s guarantee. Restoring sensitivity to small coalitions requires identity-level or atomic information; that defeats compression into a continuous society.

The proposed mirror can still be made credible as a new mass-market model: use exact finite types, scale school capacities per capita, define blocking in terms of mass, and ask for a computable direct mechanism. A cohort with repeated rankings and scores could make the type support small. But this repairs the regime assumptions, not the incentive mismatch. Moreover, applying the finite mechanism’s construction to split type masses requires new choices about how to allocate tied mass and how to interpret deviations that divide a type across reports. Those are substantive parts of the new model, not consequences of Theorem 1.

The exact-type concern strengthens the case without deciding it. School-choice types must capture rankings and school-specific priority attributes, including exam scores. In many admissions settings, exact scores and preference lists produce many distinct types; grouping score bands can change the adjusted priorities. A specially structured cohort could avoid this problem, so it is not a universal objection.

Theorem 2 does not supply an independent escape: a mass version of student-optimality also has to replace individual Pareto comparisons with a measure-level notion, which cannot register improvements confined to a null set.

So the strongest case against is that this paper is a weak ChoCo anchor: its central computationally implemented result depends on individual incentive guarantees that aggregate masses erase, while restoring those guarantees brings identity-level data back into the input. The proposed continuous school-choice problem may be worthwhile as a separate mechanism-design project. I cannot honestly establish that no such scenario could be worthwhile; the negative case is strongest on the mismatch with this programme’s computational aim, not on the impossibility of a meaningful mass model.
