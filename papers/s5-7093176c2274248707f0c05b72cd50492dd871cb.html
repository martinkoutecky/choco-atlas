The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(B\), supported student types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), preference lists and consent status, school priority orders over types, and rational capacity shares \(\kappa_b\), compute the terminal allocation of mass EADAM. Run deferred acceptance in rounds, splitting mass proportionally among history cohorts when a priority cutoff falls within a type; track a cohort’s hold and rejection history, remove the latest-round interrupting pairs involving consenting mass, and rerun until none remain. Is this output computable in time polynomial in the number of schools, \(\tau=|T|\), the number of acceptable type-school pairs, and input bit length \(L\)?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic society with rational mass \(\mu_t\) on each complete student type, where a type records preferences, school priority attributes, and consent. Schools have capacities scaled as shares \(\kappa_b\); deferred acceptance and EADAM operate on mass, with history cohorts split proportionally at capacity cutoffs, and the output is the mass \(x_{tb}\) assigned from each type to each school, plus unmatched mass.

The objection that survived

Strict individual priorities can make the support size \(\tau\) grow with the population, and the type-level priority extension has no demonstrated polynomial bound on the number of history cohorts; the proponent identifies these risks but does not resolve the cohort-growth question.

fatal: False

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the number of distinct history cohorts and EADAM rounds be bounded polynomially in the support size, school count, acceptable type-school pairs, and input bit length?
  • Does proportional splitting at priority cutoffs give a unique aggregate outcome, and which consent and constrained-efficiency properties survive?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 7.1, proved in this paper. It says that, for any stable assignment instance \((G(A\cup B,E),<,q)\) and any specified set of consenting students, the output of EADAM with consent can be computed in \(O(|E|)\) time. The authors establish this using their rotation-based algorithm and its equivalence to EADAM. The paper gives no numbered hardness result, so this algorithmic theorem is the best anchor available.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large school district’s annual cohort. Take \(N\) students, with \(N\) much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct complete student types. A type records a student’s ranked list of acceptable schools, priority category at each school, and consent status. These attributes matter to the matching problem, so students who differ on them belong to different types. The regime is plausible when preference lists and school priorities derive from recurring categories; it is weaker if individualized tie-breaks give nearly every student a different priority profile. Scale each school’s capacity with the cohort, so \(q_b/N\) approaches a per-capita capacity \(\kappa_b\).

I would call the mirror Mass-EADAM with consent. Its input is a finite set of schools \(B\), supported types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), rational capacity shares \(\kappa_b\), the type rankings and priority categories, and the consent bit for each type. Each school has a priority order over types, with members of one type tied. The society is nonatomic: if a capacity cutoff falls within a type’s mass, the school can hold part of it. This is a co-modification forced by treating students as indistinguishable within a type. Individuals still receive one school or remain unmatched.

The question is to compute the terminal assignment produced by the mass version of EADAM. Run deferred acceptance in rounds: unmatched mass proposes to its highest-ranked school still available to it, and each school retains the highest-priority mass up to its capacity. Track held and rejected portions of each type as separate cohorts when their histories diverge. A cohort is interrupted at school \(b\) in a later round if it was previously held there, is now rejected, and \(b\) rejected positive mass in an intervening round. As in EADAM, find the latest round containing an interruption by consenting mass, remove that school from the affected consenting cohorts’ lists, and rerun deferred acceptance. Stop when no consenting mass is interrupted. The solution is the aggregate allocation \(x_{tb}\), where \(x_{tb}\) is the mass of original type \(t\) assigned to school \(b\), plus unmatched mass \(u_t=\mu_t-\sum_b x_{tb}\). There is no welfare objective: the task is to compute the mechanism’s prescribed output.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable in \(s,\tau,e,L\), where \(s=|B|\), \(e\) is the number of acceptable type-school pairs, and \(L\) is the input bit length. With finite support, proposals, priority comparisons, and capacity cutoffs are all operations on rational masses. The central algorithmic question is whether the history cohorts and interruption rounds can be represented with polynomial size in those parameters. Theorem 7.1 does not establish that compression, so the expected classification is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of the discrete runtime. Further questions include whether its aggregate output is unique under all ways of splitting exchangeable mass at cutoffs, whether the discrete replicated instances converge to it, and whether EADAM’s consent and constrained-efficiency properties carry over.

This mirror covers Theorem 7.1 and its EADAM-with-consent output. It does not claim to continuize the legal-assignment lattice, the results on legal matchings, or every result in the paper. I think the authors would recognize the proposed question: it preserves EADAM’s preference lists, priorities, consent decisions, capacities, and assignment output, while grouping repeated students into types.

The weak point is the priority model. The paper ranks named students strictly, while a high-multiplicity type must share its priority attributes with many students. The mirror therefore uses priority classes and allows a school to split mass at a class boundary. EADAM’s interruption history also operates at the individual level, so the mass-cohort definition needs to preserve those histories without creating excessive numbers of cohorts. If the authors require arbitrary individual tie-breaks to remain part of the problem, \(\tau\) may grow with \(N\), and this mirror loses its high-multiplicity advantage.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 7.1 is a real computational result, so the “no named result to mirror” objection does not apply. The strongest case against this anchor is that the paper’s input is difficult to compress without changing the mechanism. Its stable assignment instance gives each school a strict ranking of named students, and EADAM’s interrupting pairs depend on the order and history of individual proposals. A type that preserves a student’s position in every school’s ranking will generally be unique to that student. In that exact model, the support size \(\tau\) grows with the population, so the proposed high multiplicity advantage disappears.

The proposed priority classes address this by replacing strict rankings with ties and allowing a school to split a type’s mass at a capacity cutoff. That is a plausible school choice model, but it leaves EADAM’s history to specify. Once some members of a type are held and others rejected, they must be tracked as separate cohorts: later interruptions depend on which cohort was held, when it was rejected, and whether its members consent. The aggregate assignment \(x_{tb}\) does not retain that information. A faithful algorithm would need to show that these evolving cohorts can be represented with polynomial size in \(\tau\), \(e\), and \(L\). The paper’s \(O(|E|)\) algorithm does not establish that: its \(E\) contains individual student-school pairs, and its rotation and interruption procedures operate on those individuals.

The nearby algorithmic results do not obviously avoid this issue. Theorems 5.5 and 6.2 compute legal assignments or the legal subinstance using the same strict, individual-level matching structure. A mass version would need definitions of blocking and legality for allocations that can split a type. Those may be definable, but the paper’s results do not supply them.

That is the best negative case, and it has a substantial weakness: a mass EADAM with priority classes and fractional cutoffs is a coherent candidate problem, and proving that its histories can or cannot be compressed is itself a computational question within the programme’s scope. The submitted case has not established tractability, but that does not defeat the mirror. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; the exact strict-priority version has little high multiplicity, while the priority-class version remains a credible research question.
