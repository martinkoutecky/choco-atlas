The continuous mirror question

Given a finite conflict graph \(G=(V,E)\), agent types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), unit valuations \(p_t(v)=1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(b_v\), maximize \(z\) by choosing masses \(x_{t,I}\ge 0\) of agents of type \(t\) receiving each independent set \(I\) of \(G\), subject to \(\sum_I x_{t,I}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,I:v\in I}x_{t,I}\le b_v\), and \(x_{t,I}=0\) whenever \(|I|<z\). In particular, on the graph formed from two copies of \(H\) joined completely, with one type of mass \(1\) and \(b_v=\tfrac12\) for every vertex, determine or approximate the maximum \(z\).

The model it lives in

A mass-allocation model with integral independent-set bundles and per-capita item-category supplies. For a fixed utility threshold \(z\), feasibility is a configuration LP whose variables \(x_{t,I}\) assign mass to qualifying bundles; the anchored two-copy instance has optimum \(\alpha(H)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6’s general-graph inapproximability via Independent Set. It does not mirror the paper’s graph-class-specific hardness results, pseudo-polynomial algorithms, or FPTAS.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do the paper’s pseudo-polynomial algorithms for cocomparability, chordal, biconvex bipartite, or bounded-treewidth graphs extend to rational mass allocations with per-capita supplies?
  • What finite-population conditions give a precise correspondence between rational mass allocations and allocations of \(N\) agents when \(N\mu_t\) and \(Nb_v\) are integral?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I see is a single lead: a high multiplicity version of Fair Division Under Conflicts with per-capita supplies. Its main anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper in Appendix A: for every \(k\ge 1\) and \(\varepsilon>0\), it is NP-hard to approximate Fair \(k\)-Division Under Conflicts within a factor of \(|V(G)|^{1-\varepsilon}\), even when every profit function is the unit function. The proof reduces from Independent Set. That homogeneous-agent hardness makes this a better anchor for a population mirror than results that rely on many distinct agent valuations.

Call the mirror Continuous Fair Division Under Conflicts with Per-Capita Supply. An instance has a finite conflict graph \(G=(V,E)\), a finite set \(T\) of agent types, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), additive valuations \(p_t:V\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), and a rational per-capita supply \(b_v\ge0\) for each item category \(v\). Let \(\mathcal I(G)\) be the independent sets of \(G\). A solution chooses a guaranteed utility \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\) and masses \(x_{t,I}\ge0\), where \(x_{t,I}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(I\). Require
\[ \sum_{I\in\mathcal I(G)}x_{t,I}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{I\ni v}x_{t,I}\le b_v, \]
and \(x_{t,I}=0\) whenever \(\sum_{v\in I}p_t(v)<z\). The question is to maximize \(z\); a solution is \(z\) and the bundle-mass allocation witnessing it. Thus bundles remain integral and conflict-free. Only the population is continuous. If the population has \(N\) members, \(b_v\) represents \(Nb_v\) copies of category \(v\), when that quantity is integral.

A plausible regime is a large pool of interchangeable shipping partners handling recurring order categories. The partners share a valuation type, while the conflict graph records which order categories cannot be assigned together to one partner. The goal is to maximize the minimum number of orders any partner receives. Here \(N\) can be large while \(\tau=|T|=1\); order stocks scale with \(N\). This keeps the paper’s integral-bundle and max-min structure while making the high-multiplicity assumption explicit.

The connection to Theorem 6 is exact on its Independent Set construction. Given a graph \(H\), form two copies \(H_1,H_2\) and add every edge between the copies. Use one agent type, unit profit for every vertex, and per-capita supply \(b_v=\tfrac12\) for every vertex. Any feasible bundle lies wholly in one copy, so its size is at most \(\alpha(H)\). Conversely, assign mass \(\tfrac12\) to a maximum independent set in each copy. Every item category used consumes exactly its allowed mass, and the whole population receives utility \(\alpha(H)\). The continuous optimum is therefore exactly \(\alpha(H)\). With \(N\) divisible by two, this describes \(N\) identical agents and \(N/2\) copies of each item category. Exact optimization is NP-hard, and the Independent Set inapproximability underlying Theorem 6 transfers as well.

I expect hardness transfers here: the reduction’s combinatorics live in the conflict graph, not in the number of individually named agents. This is not a claim that every result in the paper has a continuous counterpart. It is a mirror of Theorem 6’s general-graph hardness, and the configuration formulation also points to the relevant algorithmic obstacle: optimizing over independent-set bundles can leave a hard pricing problem.

The weakest point is the supply co-modification. In the paper, each item is a single object allocated at most once; here each category has stock proportional to the population, with copies of a category behaving identically. That is a real modeling choice, though it matches the paper’s own motivation that similar items need not both go to one agent. The two-copy construction is also an engineered hardness instance rather than a typical freight example. The case survives because it preserves each person’s integral, conflict-free bundle and the minimum-utility objective, and the continuous optimum on the anchor construction is provably the same value.

This case covers Theorem 6, not the paper’s full graph-class boundary. It raises useful follow-up questions: do the bipartite hardness results survive the same normalization of supplies? Do the paper’s algorithms for chordal, cocomparability, or bounded-treewidth graphs yield efficient algorithms for the mass-allocation model? And what finite-\(N\) approximation guarantee relates rational mass allocations to discrete high-multiplicity instances?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot honestly defeat the proponent’s anchor under your criteria. The strongest concern is that the mirror replaces the paper’s finite stock of items with category supplies that scale with population. With fixed stock, the minimum utility can collapse as the population grows. But per-capita supply is an allowed co-modification, and the paper itself motivates conflicts through similar or identical items. A large pool of partners handling recurring order categories is a plausible high-multiplicity regime; a single agent type is coherent because those agents share the same valuation.

Theorem 6’s reduction survives that mirror exactly. Take two copies of a graph \(H\), join every vertex in one copy to every vertex in the other, and set each category’s per-capita supply to \(1/2\). Every feasible bundle lies in one copy, so its size is at most \(\alpha(H)\). Assigning half the population a maximum independent set in each copy achieves \(\alpha(H)\). The continuous optimum is therefore exactly \(\alpha(H)\), and the hardness transfers.

The configuration formulation also gives a genuine computational question: for a utility threshold, choose masses of integral, conflict-free bundles subject to supply constraints. Its pricing problem involves optimizing over independent sets. That the graph remains the source of hardness may make the mirror’s answer predictable, but under the programme’s rules, transferred hardness is a meaningful boundary result, not an objection. The paper provides no continuous computational treatment that already answers it.

So the honest negative case is weak: the required supply co-scaling is a real modeling choice, but I cannot show that authors of this paper would reject it, or that it makes the mirror unworthy. This anchor survives.
