The continuous mirror question

Given supported applicant types \(X\) and slot types \(Y\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_x,\nu_y\), type rankings, acceptable type pairs \(E\), and socially connected pairs \(H\subseteq E\), choose match masses \(z_{xy}\geq 0\) subject to \(\sum_y z_{xy}\leq\mu_x\) and \(\sum_x z_{xy}\leq\nu_y\). For each \((x,y)\in H\), let \(d_x(y)=\mu_x-\sum_{y':\,y'\succeq_x y}z_{xy'}\) and \(d_y(x)=\nu_y-\sum_{x':\,x'\succeq_y x}z_{x'y}\); require \(d_x(y)=0\) or \(d_y(x)=0\), and maximize \(\sum_{(x,y)\in E}z_{xy}\). Is this optimum computable in time polynomial in the number of supported types and input bit length?

The model it lives in

A nonatomic, one-to-one matching market over applicant and slot types with binary-rational masses, type rankings, and acceptable and social links between types. The decision variables are matched masses \(z_{xy}\); maximize total matched mass subject to type supplies and the condition that no socially connected pair has positive mass on both sides who strictly prefer each other.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s polynomial-time result, Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that for \((2,\infty)\)-MAX Weakly SMTISS—each man has at most two acceptable partners, each woman may have an unbounded incomplete list, and ties are allowed—the authors’ algorithm finds a maximum-cardinality weakly socially stable matching in \(O(n^{3/2}\log n)\) time, where \(n\) is the total number of agents. That is the paper’s numbered computational theorem, so I would use it as the sole anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large placement market in which applicants and one-to-one placement slots fall into a modest number of cohorts. For example, applicants might share specialty, region, and training profiles, while slots share placement and supervisor profiles. Members of a cohort have the same ranking over placement categories, the same acceptable categories, and the same category-level acquaintance pattern. Applicants list at most two acceptable categories; placement types may rank an unbounded number of applicant types. There are many people and slots, but relatively few distinct cohort types. This is a stylized market, not a claim that every medical matching scheme has this structure.

Here is the corresponding continuous problem. Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be applicant and slot types, with binary-rational masses \(\mu_x\) and \(\nu_y\), normalized so that \(\sum_{x\in X}\mu_x+\sum_{y\in Y}\nu_y=1\). Each type has a weak ranking over acceptable types on the other side. Let \(E\subseteq X\times Y\) be the acceptable pairs and \(H\subseteq E\) the socially connected pairs; these are block-level relations, so every pair of agents in a connected pair of types is acquainted.

For every acceptable pair, choose a mass \(z_{xy}\geq 0\) to match. Respect the type supplies:
\[ \sum_y z_{xy}\leq\mu_x,\qquad \sum_x z_{xy}\leq\nu_y. \]
For a socially connected pair \((x,y)\), define
\[ d_x(y)=\mu_x-\sum_{y':\,y'\succeq_x y}z_{xy'},\qquad d_y(x)=\nu_y-\sum_{x':\,x'\succeq_y x}z_{x'y}. \]
These are the masses unmatched or assigned to a strictly worse type. Require \(d_x(y)=0\) or \(d_y(x)=0\) for every \((x,y)\in H\), then maximize \(\sum_{(x,y)\in E}z_{xy}\). The disjunction says that no socially connected pair has positive mass on both sides who would strictly prefer each other to their current assignments. A solution is a maximum-mass assignment; because the population is nonatomic, each type’s mass can be partitioned to realize it, while each individual still receives one integral match.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable, with an algorithm polynomial in the number of supported types and the input bit length. The paper’s result gives a credible lead: its proof uses deletions, a minimum-weight maximum matching, and subsequent improvements. With type supplies, a prover could investigate whether those operations extend to capacitated flows and bulk transfers. This is a conjecture about the mirror, not a consequence of Theorem 4.1. In particular, the stability condition above is disjunctive, so the mirror is not automatically an LP.

The mirror covers the theorem’s maximum-size weak social stability problem and its asymmetric short-list regime. It does not try to continuize the paper’s other cited results or its open questions about strong and super stability. Further questions include whether the continuous optimum is the limit of replicated finite instances, whether it admits a denominator-independent algorithm, and what changes when applicants may list three types.

The weakest point is the type-level interpretation of the short-list bound. To keep \(\tau\) small, an applicant ranks at most two *types* of slots, and the social graph must also be homogeneous within type pairs. If applicants instead rank two named individuals each and have idiosyncratic acquaintance links, the types may be nearly singletons and the high-multiplicity case disappears. The cohort model remains plausible for markets where people choose among placement categories and slots within a category are interchangeable; it is also the main co-modification a referee would need to accept.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case has only one anchor to address: Theorem 4.1, which gives an \(O(n^{3/2}\log n)\) algorithm for \((2,\infty)\)-MAX Weakly SMTISS. The cited NP-hardness results are prior work, not the paper’s own computational theorem. So the objection has to be about whether this problem has a sensible high-multiplicity regime.

The strongest concern is that the theorem’s bound of two is on acceptable *individuals*, while social stability depends on which named pairs know each other. A type that includes an agent’s full preference list and acquaintance neighborhood cannot usually recur many times in a growing instance: copies of the same man type share the same two named partners, so the matchable supply on the other side stays bounded. Repeating whole local networks does not immediately solve this. Keeping each network’s identity preserves its links but makes the number of types grow with the number of networks; suppressing that identity risks allowing matches or blocking pairs across networks that have no such connection.

The proponents’ category-level model is the strongest escape from this problem. Applicants could rank two placement categories, and social links could be complete between specified applicant and slot categories. Its mass-stability condition is coherent: if both sides retain positive mass that would strictly prefer one another, a positive mass of blocking pairs exists. Nor does maximum cardinality itself require preserving individual identities.

But this escape changes what the theorem’s restrictions mean. “At most two” now bounds categories, not acceptable partners, and a category-level social edge makes every applicant in one cohort acquainted with every slot in another. That is a substantive replacement of the paper’s sparse, person-to-person social network with a block network. It may describe some placement markets, but the theorem does not establish that it is a good account of them, and its algorithm does not itself supply a continuous-optimization method for the resulting capacity problem.

That is the best case against the mirror: the paper’s own sparse social structure fits high multiplicity poorly, while the clean type-level formulation relies on a different social structure. Still, the case is weak against the requested universal claim. A market with interchangeable placement categories and genuine block-level acquaintance is plausible, and the paper’s authors might recognize its continuous version as their problem. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile scenario; the objection is to how directly this particular theorem motivates one.
