The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses μ_t, rational per-claim amounts a_t, and a rational estate per claimant e with 0<e<A:=∑_t μ_t a_t, replicate the society by a factor k and give it estate ke. Determine the limiting Shapley payoff per claimant for each type as k grows through denominator-compatible values; in the designated-type case with a_{t^†}=1, decide whether that limiting payoff is less than ½.

The model it lives in

Types are claimant classes with identical claim a_t, and μ_t is each class’s fraction of the population. The estate scales with population at e per claimant. Define each type’s payoff as the replication limit of its finite-game Shapley payoff; the limit is ψ_t=a_t e/A, and the total allocation to type t per unit of population is μ_tψ_t.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.3 through the Shapley allocation and designated-type threshold in scaled bankruptcy societies, though the normalized SHP predicate becomes automatically true. It leaves Theorem 4.2, the recursive algorithms, and the FPRAS unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • How quickly does the finite-replication Shapley payoff converge to ψ_t, particularly near the bankruptcy threshold?
  • Can convergence bounds yield additive approximation or preprocessing guarantees when the number of claimant types is small?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity bankruptcy game: many claimants, grouped by identical claim amount, divide an estate that grows with the population. For example, a large pool of depositors or unpaid workers may fall into a small number of contract tiers. A type \(t\) is a claim tier, \(\mu_t\) is its population fraction, and \(a_t\) is each claimant’s claim. If \(e\) is the estate per claimant, let \(A=\sum_t\mu_ta_t\), with \(0<e<A\).

This keeps the paper’s underlying question—the Shapley allocation in a bankruptcy game—while making the population continuous. To define the value without inventing a random ordering of infinitely many people, take a replication limit. For denominator-compatible \(k\), make \(k\mu_t\) claimants of each type, each with claim \(a_t\), and an estate \(ke\). The Shapley value per claimant of type \(t\) has a limit as \(k\) grows. In a random ordering, a claimant’s position approaches a uniform \(U\in[0,1]\); the claims before them approach \(UkA\). The claimant contributes their full claim once the accumulated claims pass the bankruptcy threshold, and contributes zero before it. The threshold is crossed at \(U=1-e/A\), so the limiting per-claimant value is \(\psi_t=a_te/A\). The type’s total allocation is \(\mu_t\psi_t\). Equivalently, the continuous characteristic function on coalition masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) is \(V(x)=\max\{0,\sum_ta_tx_t-(A-e)\}\). The continuous problem, Bankruptcy Mass-Shapley, is: given rational \((T,\mu,a,e)\), output the exact per-claimant payoff \(\psi_t\) for every type. Its designated-type decision version asks whether \(\psi_{t^\dagger}<1/2\), with \(a_{t^\dagger}=1\). A solution is the exact rational payoff vector, or the correct yes/no answer for that decision version.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.3, proved in this paper. It says that SHP is NP-complete. SHP’s instance is a positive integer claim vector \((w_1,\ldots,w_n)\), with every \(w_i\) for \(i<n\) even and \(w_n=1\); its game is \(\BG[\lfloor W/2\rfloor;w_1,\ldots,w_n]\), where \(W=\sum_iw_i\). It asks whether the Shapley value of claimant \(n\) is strictly less than \(1/2\). The paper reduces PARTITION to this decision problem: from values \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\), set \(w_i=2a_i\) for \(i\le m\), \(w_{m+1}=1\), and use estate \(\lfloor W/2\rfloor\). An exact partition makes the special claimant’s marginal contribution zero in both a permutation and its reverse, yielding a Shapley value below \(1/2\); without a partition, the paired marginal contributions always sum to \(1\), yielding value \(1/2\).

The mirror is computationally concrete but should not be sold as preserving that hardness. With rational type masses and claims, Bankruptcy Mass-Shapley is exactly computable by the formula above, in polynomial time in the support size \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\). For the direct normalized encoding of an SHP instance, take \(\mu_i=1/n\), \(a_i=w_i\), \(e=E/n\), and target \(n\). Then \(\psi_n=E/W<1/2\): the strict inequality is automatic, and the PARTITION distinction has vanished. I expect this mirror to be tractable. That is a meaningful continuization result: it identifies the finite subset-sum effect that disappears when the population becomes a mass distribution.

The case for plausibility is that the limit uses the paper’s same bankruptcy characteristic function and the same per-claimant Shapley question. Estate per capita scaling with population is a natural co-modification, and identical claimants are exactly what makes a high-multiplicity regime sensible. In a scenario with millions of claimants but few claim tiers, the output is the per-capita allocation by tier—the quantity a population-level decision maker would often want.

The weak point is real: the hardness reduction has a number of distinct claim amounts that can grow with \(n\), and its strict threshold detects exact finite granularity. A referee could regard the limiting problem as too simplified if they require preservation of SHP’s yes/no behavior rather than a continuous version of its underlying allocation problem. The replication definition is why I still think the mirror is honest: it makes the change explicit and derives the continuous value as a limit of the paper’s finite games.

Further questions follow naturally: how quickly does the finite-\(k\) Shapley value approach \(\psi_t\), especially near the threshold? Can that convergence give additive approximation or preprocessing guarantees when \(\tau\) is small? And which variants preserve indivisibilities or correlated arrival structure strongly enough that subset-sum hardness survives?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only real anchor is Theorem 3.3: SHP is NP-complete. Its witness is an exact half-sum among finitely many claims, detected through the marginal contributions of one claimant whose claim is \(1\). That gives a strong objection to the *specific predicate* in the proposed mirror. Under the replication limit, the cumulative claims before a randomly ordered claimant concentrate around \(UA\), where \(U\) is uniform and \(A\) is the mean claim. The limiting payoff is therefore \(a_t e/A\); for the designated claimant, the test becomes \(e/A<1/2\). The finite subset witness and its exact threshold disappear.

A better version can retain a tagged claimant of zero population mass. But its limiting payoff is still determined by the aggregate claim total, so the tag does not preserve the subset-sum structure. Retaining the finite replication count would preserve such effects, but then the object being studied is the high-multiplicity *finite* game, not the continuous limit. The proposed limit is well-defined; the objection is that it changes what Theorem 3.3’s decision problem asks.

That distinction does not, by itself, defeat the mirror under this programme’s rules: the continuous problem need not preserve the finite answer, and tractability after continuization can be a worthwhile result. Nor do claimant identities or multiplicity defeat it. Claim tiers can be meaningful types, and a large class of otherwise identical claimants is a plausible population regime. The straightforward formula is also not a valid objection just because it gives an easy answer.

So the negative case is weak. I can argue that the proposed mirror does not illuminate the NP-completeness mechanism in SHP, and that connecting its limit back to finite high-multiplicity instances would require separate convergence or approximation results. But those results could themselves be valuable, and nothing in the paper rules out a sensible continuous-population bankruptcy question. I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists.
