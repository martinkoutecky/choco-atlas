The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a valid case with the required anchors: this paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 1 proves an axiomatic impossibility: no individually rational, strategy-proof mechanism size-wise dominates a non-wasteful, truncation-invariant, extension-responding mechanism. Theorem 2 proves a second axiomatic impossibility under efficiency, group strategy-proofness, and a capacity condition. Both proofs are in the paper. Corollaries 1–4 apply those results to named mechanisms and related classes. None says a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity.

There is a tempting population-level question adjacent to Theorem 1: represent agents by preference types and their masses, scale object supplies with population, and ask whether a rule assigning integral objects to individuals can satisfy the theorem’s axioms while weakly dominating a benchmark in the fraction assigned. But that is a continuized axiomatic mechanism-design question, not the computational mirror this programme asks for. In a nonatomic population, an individual’s report may not affect the aggregate, making strategy-proofness vacuous unless the model adds a substantive way to represent individual deviations. And adding such a model would not turn Theorem 1 into a complexity anchor.

So I have no defensible continuous computational problem per anchor, nor a meaningful A/B/C prediction. The paper’s results concern whether particular mechanisms can be dominated, not the computational difficulty of finding or evaluating them.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is at the threshold: the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are axiomatic impossibility results about mechanisms; their corollaries apply those results to named mechanisms. None classifies a computational problem, specifies an algorithmic input and output, or makes a claim about complexity. So there are no valid computational anchors here.

The best population-level version of Theorem 1 would model a large school district by masses of preference types, scale object supplies with the population, and compare the fraction assigned by different mechanisms. That is a sensible aggregate matching question, but the theorem’s individual strategy-proofness condition loses its force in an atomless population: one person’s report does not change the distribution. Making deviations by positive-mass cohorts meaningful requires a new incentive condition. That may be worth studying, but it is an axiomatic reformulation, not a computational mirror of Theorem 1.

Theorem 2 has a stronger possible repair because it already involves group strategy-proofness. One could define deviations by cohorts of positive mass. But its comparison mechanism must also be strategy-proof, and weak population-monotonicity concerns an individual withdrawing. In the continuum, that individual-level condition becomes vacuous unless it too is replaced. Those changes produce a new axiomatic question; they still do not supply a computational result from this paper to mirror. Computing continuous assignment shares for DA, TTC, or another rule might be useful work, but it would be a new algorithmic question in the same setting.

So the negative case is strong for this paper under ChoCo’s scope: none of its named results provides a computational anchor, and the most charitable continuous versions change the incentive concepts that drive those results. The broader claim that no worthwhile computational problem could ever be built from this matching setting would go too far; the case here is that such a project would not be a continuous computational mirror of this paper.
