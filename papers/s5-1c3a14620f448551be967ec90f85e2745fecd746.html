The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Equations (0.1)–(0.3) state the proposed seat-share formula and its implications; the paper tests the formula empirically. Those are not complexity results, so the set of eligible anchors is empty.

The strongest plausible continuous problem is the paper’s own prediction task. Treat each party as a type, with mass equal to its normalized national vote share \(p_i\). Given those rational masses, the assembly size \(s\), and the number of districts \(c\), compute estimated seat shares. With \(m=s/c\) and \(n\) significant parties, the paper’s estimate is
\[ \hat q_i=\left(1+\frac{n}{2m}\right)p_i-\frac{1}{2m}, \qquad \hat s_i=s\hat q_i. \]
A solution is the vector of estimated seat counts. This is plausible for large electorates with many voters and relatively few parties; calculating the estimate is polynomial-time by direct evaluation. The paper says the formula is exact under assumptions A1–A3 and an approximation otherwise.

That formulation is already the paper’s model, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its discrete complexity results. The authors would recognize it, but there is no discrete hardness result here whose fate in the continuum could be studied, and no decision or optimization objective with a complexity classification. So I cannot honestly supply one continuous problem per anchor or assign the requested trichotomy to an anchor. The central weakness is decisive: this paper offers an aggregate prediction formula, not a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition here asserting the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s results are a seat-share formula, conditions under which it is exact, and empirical evidence about its accuracy. So there is no complexity result to mirror.

The proposed prediction task is the closest paper-linked candidate, but it is already the paper’s own continuous calculation. Its inputs \(p_i\) are national vote fractions, and its output \(\hat q_i\) is a fractional seat share. Evaluating
\[ \hat q_i=\left(1+\frac{n}{2m}\right)p_i-\frac{1}{2m} \]
is a direct calculation, not a new computational question for continuous optimization. Calling each party a “type” does not add substance: it repackages the vote-share vector the paper already uses. This model predicts the paper’s estimate; it does not compute the actual district-by-district allocation.

A richer mirror could use masses of voters by district and party choice, then ask how much vote mass must move to give a party a seat majority. That is more faithful to the actual electoral procedure and fits a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But it requires adding a transfer-cost model and an optimization objective that the paper does not study. With those additions, the exact post-transfer D’Hondt allocation is still obtained by sorting quotient values for each district; the resulting optimization problem would be a new electoral-control problem, not a mirror of a computational result in this article. Conversely, retaining only national shares preserves the paper’s convenient formula but leaves out the district variation that its own assumptions identify as a source of error.

The negative case is strongest as a rejection of this article as a continuous-complexity mirror: its most plausible task is already expressed in proportions and solved by a closed-form estimate, while richer computational questions require introducing a different model. I cannot establish the universal claim that no adjacent continuous vote-control problem could be worthwhile; such a problem is coherent. But this paper supplies no computational result that would make it a mirror of the kind ChoCo is meant to classify.
