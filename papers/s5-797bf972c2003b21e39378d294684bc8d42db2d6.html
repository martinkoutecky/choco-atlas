The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. Its only numbered result, Theorem 1, is proved in the paper and gives an axiomatic characterization: when \(q\le n/2\), no voting rule is anonymous, responsive, and \(q\)-neutral; when \(q>n/2\), a rule has all three properties exactly when it chooses one fixed alternative if and only if at least \(q\) voters strictly support it. The theorem does not classify a computational problem by complexity, or give an algorithm, optimization result, or encoding used for computation. So it does not qualify as an anchor under the requested standard.

There is a natural high-multiplicity setting for the paper’s voting rule: a large parliament whose members fall into at most three preference types, \(x\succ y\), \(y\succ x\), and indifference. Their masses are the fractions of legislators in each type. For a fixed reform alternative and a quota fraction \(\alpha>1/2\), the continuous rule selects the reform exactly when its strict-supporter mass is at least \(\alpha\). That is a plausible continuous version of the rule, with many legislators and few types.

But characterizing this continuum rule through anonymity, responsiveness, and a continuum version of \(q\)-neutrality would be an axiomatic question, not a computational one. Treating the trivial task of comparing the reform’s mass with \(\alpha\) as an anchor would invent a computational result the paper does not claim. I therefore can’t state a qualifying continuous problem per anchor or give it a defensible tractability or hardness classification. The weak point is decisive: the paper offers a natural population-continuum interpretation, but no named computational finding for that interpretation to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the gap: Theorem 1 is the paper’s only named result, and it characterizes a voting rule through axioms. A continuum version of that characterization would still be an axiomatic question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The three-type parliament is a credible high-multiplicity setting. Write its masses as \(\mu_{x\succ y}, \mu_{y\succ x}, \mu_I\), and let the qualified quota be a fraction \(\alpha>1/2\). The direct computational question—does the reform pass?—is just whether its strict-supporter mass is at least \(\alpha\). With binary rational inputs, that is one comparison. Anonymity and the rule’s other defining features have already erased everything else that could make the computation harder.

The strongest more ambitious mirror would be to ask for the least-cost mass transfer that gets the reform over quota. But with the paper’s three preference types and type-to-type conversion costs, this is a constant-size linear program. Allowing many cost types still gives a linear program whose size is polynomial in the support; adding campaign constraints or other combinatorial restrictions to make the problem harder would supply the computational substance from outside the paper. That could be a worthwhile new problem, but it would not mirror a result this paper established.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s stated remit: the paper offers a natural continuous population model, but no worthwhile computational finding to carry into it. The limit of that case is equally clear: the parliament setting itself is sensible, so I cannot honestly claim that no continuous mirror could ever be formulated. The case is that any direct mirror is axiomatic or computationally immediate, while a more substantial computational problem would need new structure beyond the paper.
