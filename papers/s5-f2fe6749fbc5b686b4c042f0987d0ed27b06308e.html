Why no mirror

Theorem 3 is an algorithmic result, but it counts rounds in which each named worker supplies a reward observation. In a continuum of repeated types, a full-support fractional matching exposes every supported type pair in parallel, making the type-level reward means exact; the finite-exposure oracle that prevents this is an extra sampling constraint, not a consequence of continuizing the population. The stated mirror therefore does not preserve a sampling problem supplied by the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a type-mass version of the paper’s pure-exploration problem. It keeps the aim of learning the proposer-optimal stable outcome, rather than replacing it with the easier task of computing a stable outcome from known preferences.

Let \(I\) be worker types and \(J\) employer types, with rational masses \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_j\), where \(\sum_i\alpha_i=1\) and \(\sum_j\beta_j\ge 1\). A worker type’s complete description includes its reward distribution \(F_{ij}\) against every employer type \(j\); its unknown preferences rank employer types by the means of those distributions. Each employer type has a known strict ranking over worker types. A feasible type-level matching is a flow \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{ij}=\alpha_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le\beta_j\). Its fractional entries represent shares of a cohort assigned to a type, while each individual still receives one employer.

Call a flow stable when there is no type pair \((i,j)\) for which positive worker mass of type \(i\) is assigned below \(j\) and positive employer capacity of type \(j\) is vacant or assigned below \(i\). The target is the proposer-optimal stable flow: for every worker type \(i\) and every preference prefix, it assigns at least as much of \(i\)’s mass into that prefix as any other stable flow does.

In each exploration round, the learner chooses a feasible fractional matching \(q^t\), without requiring it to be stable. Feedback for pair \((i,j)\) comes from \(F_{ij}\); the model treats queried mass \(q^t_{ij}\) as that much sampling exposure, with confidence improving at the usual inverse-square-root rate. The Type-Mass PCOS problem is to give an adaptive policy that stops within \(B\) rounds and returns the proposer-optimal stable flow with probability at least \(1-\delta\), while minimizing the required \(B\). This mass-normalized feedback convention makes the statistical experiment explicit; it is a co-modification, not something proved by the paper.

A plausible regime is large-scale hiring or job placement: many workers share a small number of complete skill and reward profiles, and many openings share employer-side rankings over those profiles. Here the number of people can be far larger than \(|I|+|J|\), and repeated profiles are a credible form of high multiplicity. The continuous object is the society’s distribution over types; the fractional flow is how the aggregate assignment represents individual integral matches. The authors could reasonably recognize the question: it retains their uncertain preferences, unrestricted exploratory assignments, and exact proposer-optimal stable outcome.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that the Improved Elimination Algorithm is a \(\delta\)-PCOS algorithm with probability at least \(1-\delta\), and bounds its matching samples as follows. For the active pair set \(S\), let \(\mathcal P(S)\) be the subsets \(r\) whose removal reduces the active bipartite graph’s maximum degree by one. The theorem chooses successive such sets \(r_s\) to minimize the largest elimination threshold \(t_{p,a}\) within each set. If \(t_{\max}\) is the largest threshold among pairs ranked at least as high as each player’s true optimal partner, and \(n\) is the phase that reaches \(t_{\max}\), the sample bound is
\[ O\!\left(\sum_{s=1}^{n-1}\max_{(p,a)\in r_s}t_{p,a}+(K-n+1)t_{\max}\right). \]
The thresholds come from the paper’s elimination analysis and depend on the nearest reward gap for each player–arm pair. The result’s key idea is particularly apt for the mirror: the learner need not recover every preference, only the preferences needed to identify the optimal stable outcome.

For the type-mass question, I would expect a sample-efficient algorithm parameterized by the number of supported types, confidence, and reward gaps: confidence intervals need only be maintained per type pair, and exploration can be scheduled over fractional matchings. That is a plausible tractability direction, not a result established by Theorem 3. The follow-up questions are whether the proposer-optimal stable flow exists and is unique under the stated prefix order; whether fractional exploration can achieve a support-size sample bound; and what lower bounds govern the number of samples.

The mirror covers the paper’s improved-elimination result for learning the proposer-optimal stable matching. It does not cover regret minimization, the adaptive algorithm’s open sample-complexity analysis, or uncertainty on both sides.

The weakest point is also decisive: Theorem 3 is a statistical sample-complexity guarantee, not a P/NP-style complexity classification, and the paper leaves lower bounds for future work. More seriously, an actual continuum of noisy agents can erase finite-sample uncertainty: if the learner observes exact aggregate feedback from positive mass, learning may become trivial. The proposed finite-information exposure model preserves a meaningful learning problem, but it is an additional modelling choice. So this is a credible, narrow continuous extension of a named algorithmic result; it should not be presented as an established continuous computational-complexity result of the kind ChoCo chiefly seeks.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the proponent’s strongest anchor. It gives a real guarantee about adaptive stopping and sample complexity, so I would not dismiss it as lacking an algorithmic result. But its resource is the number of matching rounds in a market where each named player contributes one reward per round. That resource behaves very differently once repeated types become mass.

Take a market of \(n\) workers drawn from finitely many types \(i\), and employer capacities drawn from finitely many types \(j\). Suppose members of type \(i\) share the same reward distributions against every \(j\), as the proposed mirror requires. Because exploration can use any feasible matching, a single round can assign positive mass \(q_{ij}\) of every worker type \(i\) to every employer type \(j\). For finite support, such a full-support flow is feasible when each type has positive mass and total capacity suffices. In a finite market it yields \(nq_{ij}\) reward observations for that type pair. For fixed nonzero preference gaps, enough \(n\) makes one round sufficient to distinguish all type-level rankings; in the continuum, ordinary independent idiosyncratic feedback averages out exactly.

That scaling removes the bottleneck behind Theorem 3. Its thresholds \(t_{p,a}\) and staged matching-cover schedule manage the samples available to individual players. With cohorts, observations pool across indistinguishable players, and the whole preference table can be explored in parallel. The theorem’s gap-dependent round bound therefore does not carry over as a nontrivial continuous-population bound: under the natural independent-feedback interpretation, more population means more observations per round, until the learning stage collapses.

The proposed finite-information version can preserve a learning problem by defining mass \(q_{ij}\) as only \(q_{ij}\) units of sampling exposure, with confidence improving according to that exposure. But a fraction of a population is not a sample count: \(q_{ij}\) mass in a market of size \(n\) contains \(nq_{ij}\) agents. Keeping the effective sample count fixed requires another premise, such as a capped query budget or shared noise across a whole type pair. Either may be appropriate in a particular application, but it supplies the statistical bottleneck; the continuous population does not. The resulting problem is a finite-support bandit problem with a chosen feedback oracle, expressible without taking the population to a continuum. If instead types distinguish every agent, the model loses the high-multiplicity regime.

So Theorem 3 does not, on its own, make a persuasive ChoCo mirror: its central sample-complexity phenomenon is either washed out by mass parallelism or retained through an added sampling constraint. This is the strongest negative case, rather than a proof that no continuous version could ever be useful. A convincingly motivated finite sampling bottleneck could still make the proposed type-mass learning problem worthwhile; the paper itself does not supply one.
