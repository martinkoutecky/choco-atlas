The continuous mirror question

Given a uniform prior on \(s\in\{A,B\}\), private-signal accuracy \(p\in(1/2,1)\), and a common public signal of accuracy \(q\in(p,1)\), assign ex ante mass \(\rho_r\) to each deterministic voting plan \(r:\{A,B\}^2\to\{A,B\}\). Each plan group receives private signals with the paper's conditional accuracy \(p\), and all voters observe the same public signal. Which distribution \((\rho_r)\) maximizes the probability that the mass-majority vote matches \(s\), and what accuracy does it attain?

The model it lives in

An atomless common-interest voting population with types \((r,z)\), where \(r\) is a contingent voting plan and \(z\) is a private signal; the decision variable is the mass \(\rho_r\) assigned to each plan, and the objective is the ex ante accuracy of the mass-majority decision.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 7's welfare-maximizing strategy profile. It leaves the equilibrium characterizations, equilibrium selection, laboratory behavior, and treatment comparisons without a transferred strategic interpretation.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is Proposition 7, proved in the paper. It says that when \(m^*<(n+1)/2\), the strategy profile with \(m^*\) voters following the public signal and the rest following their private signals uniquely maximizes expected welfare over all strategy profiles \(\Gamma\). Here \(m^*\) is the integer in
\[ \mathbb N\cap\left(\frac{\ln(q/(1-q))}{\ln(p/(1-p))}-1,\ \frac{\ln(q/(1-q))}{\ln(p/(1-p))}\right]. \]
The proof draws on Nitzan and Paroush’s optimal voting rule. Proposition 6 supplies the related equilibrium characterization: below the majority threshold, \(m^*\) is the unique equilibrium count in the paper’s specified class of asymmetric pure strategies; at or above that threshold, the specified profiles produce the obedient outcome.

The paper has no named hardness result or explicit \(P\), FPT, or similar classification. Proposition 7 is a narrower algorithmic lead: it gives an explicit welfare optimizer, though it does not state a complexity bound. If the anchor requirement demands an explicit complexity-class claim, then no result in this paper qualifies.

A continuous problem I would put forward from Proposition 7 is Atomless Expert-Voting Welfare Optimization. Consider a large deliberative assembly in which everyone has the same common-interest objective, private-signal accuracy \(p\), and access to the same expert signal of accuracy \(q\), with \(1/2<p<q<1\). Its types are \((r,z)\): \(z\in\{A,B\}\) is the private signal, and \(r\) is one of the 16 possible voting plans mapping the pair of signals to a ballot. The society is the mass distribution over these types, conditional on the state; \(r\)-mass \(\rho_r\) receives private-signal type \(z\) with mass \(\rho_r p\) when \(z\) is correct and \(\rho_r(1-p)\) otherwise. The decision variable is \((\rho_r)_r\), with \(\rho_r\ge0\) and \(\sum_r\rho_r=1\). The question is to choose that distribution of plans to maximize the probability that the majority decision matches the state. A solution is an optimal mass assignment and its resulting decision accuracy.

For this model, the all-private-signal plan attains accuracy \(1\): in the continuum, the fraction receiving the correct private signal is exactly \(p>1/2\). So the stated problem is tractable, but its answer is degenerate. In the paper’s experiment, \(p=.65\), \(q=.7\), and \(m^*=1\); the corresponding obedient mass fraction tends to zero as the committee grows. The authors could recognize this as the population limit of their welfare-maximizing voting-rule question, which Proposition 7 explicitly asks. A plausible regime would be a large standardized citizen assembly, with many members but only a small number of signal and strategy types.

The soft spot is that the continuum removes pivotality. In an atomless voting game, an individual’s ballot cannot change the result, so the paper’s equilibrium and equilibrium-selection questions largely disappear; the positive mirror covers Proposition 7’s welfare optimum, not the experimental finding or the paper’s main strategic-equilibrium analysis. It also predicts perfect private-signal aggregation, so the finite committee’s trade-off between expert influence and private information does not survive in its original form.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 7 is its closest anchor: it identifies a welfare-maximizing strategy profile in a finite Bayesian voting game, drawing on the optimal voting rule of Nitzan and Paroush. It gives no algorithmic problem, input family, or complexity question for ChoCo to mirror.

The proposed atomless optimization changes Proposition 7’s object. The proposition optimizes over voters’ strategies in a finite committee, where a voter’s ballot can affect the outcome if they are pivotal. Choosing a distribution \((\rho_r)\) over voting plans instead makes a planner choose the population’s behavior. That may be a new policy question, but it is not the optimization the proposition establishes.

More fundamentally, the continuum removes the finite committee’s source of strategic force. Conditional on the state, a continuum with independent private signals has exactly a fraction \(p\) of voters receiving the correct signal. Since \(p>1/2\), sincere private-signal voting makes the decision correct with probability \(1\). An individual’s ballot has zero effect on the outcome, so every voting plan is a best response. The equilibrium analysis that distinguishes informative from obedient voting therefore loses its explanatory force: the continuum permits both outcomes without explaining which voters would select which plan. In the paper’s fixed-\(p,q\) example, the welfare-maximizing number of expert-followers is \(m^*=1\), whose population fraction vanishes as \(n\) grows.

The strongest rescue is to make the question about selective disclosure: choose what fraction of a large assembly receives the expert signal. But with the paper’s assumptions, once voters are assigned to vote by their available signal, every mixture of private-signal and expert-signal voters yields a correct majority almost surely. Both signals are better than random, and their aggregate mass overwhelms the sampling uncertainty that drives the finite model. If voters instead choose their own plans strategically, their zero influence leaves the planner without a predicted response. Preserving pivotality would require retaining a finite voting committee or adding a new source of aggregate uncertainty or strategic coordination. Those may support worthwhile questions, but they change the problem’s core structure.

So the negative case is strongest on value, not on whether a large-assembly scenario is plausible. The paper’s voters are anonymous and homogeneous enough for a continuous population to be a sensible object. What fails is the proposed anchor’s computational content: its strategic mechanism disappears in the continuum, while the remaining welfare optimization has no meaningful uncertainty to aggregate under the paper’s assumptions.
