The proposed mass assignment model is a plausible extension, but it cannot supply the missing computational character of the paper’s results, so the paper fails bit (a).

What the mirror covers

The proposed model would extend the subject of Theorem 7.1 to type-mass assignments. It does not provide computational mirrors of Theorem 8.1 or Corollary 10.2, and none of the paper’s results is covered as a computational result.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can OSP be defined for mechanisms over a continuum of individually strategic applicants while retaining type-mass assignments as outcomes?
  • Which individual-level information, such as priority or history, must the mechanism retain for OSP implementation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible mirror is one-sided school or housing allocation for a large cohort with few distinct rankings. Let \(A\) be the schools, \(T\subseteq L(A)\) the ranking types, and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of applicants of type \(t\). Let \(q_a\) be school \(a\)’s seats per capita, and \(x_{ta}\) the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(a\), with \(\sum_a x_{ta}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ta}\le q_a\). Each applicant still receives one indivisible seat; \(x_{ta}\) is fractional because it describes a group. A natural research question is to characterize which rules mapping ranking distributions to such assignments are Pareto efficient, non-bossy, and implementable by an obviously strategy-proof mechanism. The answer would be a characterization of the rules and an implementing mechanism.

That is a sensible population-continuous assignment question, but it is not anchored in a qualifying result from this paper. Theorem 7.1, proved here, characterizes the finite-agent rules that are OSP-implementable, Pareto efficient, and non-bossy: exactly the hierarchical exchange rules satisfying dual ownership. Theorem 8.1, also proved here, equates dual ownership with acyclicity. Corollary 10.2 characterizes OSP-implementable FPTTC rules by acyclicity of the priority structure. These are structural characterizations; none asserts a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. So the paper has no named computational-complexity anchor, and I cannot honestly present the candidate as the required anchor-based case.

The mirror’s weakest point is also substantial: OSP is defined by individual decisions in an extensive-form game, while \(\mu\) and \(x\) describe aggregate mass. The paper’s dual-ownership condition concerns at most two named owners; there is no evident reason it survives when those owners are replaced by large groups. Extending the allocation setting is plausible, but making the paper’s OSP characterization continuous would require a new account of individual incentives in the population model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that none of the three named results is a computational-complexity result. Theorem 7.1 characterizes assignment rules satisfying OSP, Pareto efficiency, and non-bossiness. Theorem 8.1 gives an equivalent structural condition, and Corollary 10.2 specializes that condition to FPTTC rules. They do not classify an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. The proposed question—characterizing rules for mass assignments with OSP and efficiency properties—therefore remains an axiomatic characterization. Continuizing the population would not bring these results into ChoCo’s computational scope.

Theorem 8.1 is the most tempting route to a computational mirror: the paper says acyclicity is easier for a designer to check than dual ownership. But it gives no complexity bound for checking acyclicity, and the input would be a collection of inheritance trees over named individuals, not a society described by masses of interchangeable types. One could formulate a new recognition problem for those trees, but that would not make the paper’s structural equivalence a complexity result about a continuous population. Corollary 10.2 has the same limitation: it characterizes priority structures; it does not analyze the complexity of a computational task.

The proposed school or housing setting is plausible as a large-population regime, and the variables \(x_{ta}\) are a sensible way to describe aggregate assignments of indivisible seats. The difficulty is preserving the paper’s central object, OSP. In an atomless model where outcomes depend only on the distribution \(\mu\), one person’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. If the mechanism makes that person’s assignment respond to their report, it must retain individual-level information such as identity, priority, or history. Grouping agents by preference alone then loses information the mechanism uses; grouping by every relevant role may leave no useful compression. Treating an entire type mass as a strategic player would define a different game and a different incentive question.

So the three anchors fail as computational anchors, and the stronger mass-assignment proposal does not preserve the paper’s OSP question without introducing new structure. The honest limit of this negative case is that continuum assignment research could still be worthwhile: it would just be a new computational project on mass allocation, not a mirror of the results in this paper.
