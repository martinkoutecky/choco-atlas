The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a committee size \(k\), disjoint candidate labels with interval and dominance constraints, a reference committee \(X\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete rankings of \(C\), does there exist a size-\(k\) feasible committee \(Y\) whose SNTV score \(\sum_{c\in Y}\sum_{t}\mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[t\text{ ranks }c\text{ first}]\) is at least the score of \(X\)?

The model it lives in

The electorate is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types, with binary-rational masses summing to \(1\). Candidate labels, constraints, the reference committee, and the size-\(k\) committee decision remain as in the paper; only the population is represented continuously. For the anchored SNTV problem, compare committees by their mass-weighted first-place scores.

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover Theorem 3.1's SNTV hardness (and its constant-voter Bloc variant), plus the weakly separable optimization results in Theorems 3.3 and 4.1. They do not specify a population-based ranking function for STV or other non-weakly-separable best-\(k\) rules.

Open questions for a prover

  • How do the complexity and parameterized boundaries in \(m\), \(\tau\), and mass bit length vary with the scoring rule and with restrictions on the dominance graph?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Theorem 3.1, proved here. It says that constrained winner existence is NP-complete for weakly separable rules with disjoint candidate labels, even for SNTV; it also says that for Bloc the problem is NP-complete with a constant number of voters.

A natural population model keeps the candidate set and committee discrete, and makes only the electorate continuous. A type is a complete ranking of the \(m\) candidates. The input lists the \(\tau\) ranking types with positive mass, each mass \(\mu_t\) a binary rational, with \(\sum_t \mu_t=1\). Candidate labels and the interval and dominance constraints are exactly as in the paper. The decision variable is a committee \(Y\subseteq C\) of size \(k\).

Call the resulting problem Continuous Disjoint-Label Constrained Winner Existence. Its input also includes a reference committee \(X\) of size \(k\). For SNTV, define \(\mathrm{sc}_\mu(c)=\sum_t\mu_t\,\mathbf 1[c\text{ is ranked first by }t]\), and \(\mathrm{sc}_\mu(Y)=\sum_{c\in Y}\mathrm{sc}_\mu(c)\). Ask whether there is a committee \(Y\) satisfying all constraints with \(\mathrm{sc}_\mu(Y)\geq \mathrm{sc}_\mu(X)\); a yes-instance has such a \(Y\) as its witness. For Bloc, use the fraction of mass ranking each candidate in the top \(k\).

This is a close mirror of the paper’s question: select an admissible committee that is at least as good as the reference under the same voting rule. The type is a voter’s ranking, since that is the complete voter information the problem uses; labels remain attributes of candidates. The regime could be a large electorate choosing a fixed-size representative body, where many people cast one of a much smaller number of distinct ballots. For every finite \(N\)-voter election, setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) preserves all committee comparisons: \(\mathrm{sc}_\mu(Y)=\mathrm{sc}_E(Y)/N\). Thus the source reduction’s clique choices among vertex and edge candidates, enforced by dominance constraints and a score threshold, carry over directly. The reduction’s combinatorics live in the candidate committee, not in population multiplicity. The continuous problem is therefore expected to be Class B: hardness transfers; it is NP-complete, since a proposed committee can be checked using rational arithmetic in polynomial time. The Bloc clause is especially strong evidence: the paper’s constant-voter construction uses only three voters, so its mirror has at most three positive-mass ranking types.

A second mirror is Continuous Tree-Constrained Committee Optimization, anchored in Theorem 3.3, also proved here. That theorem says the constrained winner election problem is solvable in polynomial time when the committee order is polynomial-time computable and fixed-cardinality responsive, the candidate labels are disjoint, and the dominance graph is tree-like. In the continuous version, the instance includes the same ranked-type distribution and a fixed weakly separable scoring rule; candidate scores are weighted by \(\mu\), and the question is to return a highest-scoring size-\(k\) committee satisfying the interval and dominance constraints, or report infeasibility. The score order meets fixed-cardinality responsiveness, and its comparisons can be computed from the \(\tau\) types and their rational masses. This mirror is expected to be Class A: tractable, with running time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), the mass bit length, and the constraint input size. The theorem is broader than this score-based mirror: it applies to any polynomially comparable order with the stated responsiveness property. It raises a natural follow-up: which population-defined extensions of other best-\(k\) rules satisfy those conditions?

A third, independent mirror is Continuous Few-Label Committee Optimization, anchored in Theorem 4.1, proved here. That theorem says constrained winner election for weakly separable rules is fixed-parameter tractable in the number \(p\) of labels, without requiring disjoint labels or a tree-like dominance graph. In the continuous version, use the same mass-weighted scores and ask for a maximum-score feasible committee under arbitrary interval and dominance constraints. The expected classification is Class A, with running time \(f(p)\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\), where \(L\) includes the encoding lengths of the masses and scoring data. The paper’s Venn-region formulation still applies: masses change the score coefficients, while the number of integer decision variables remains bounded as a function of \(p\). Questions that follow include whether the dependence on \(p\) can be improved, and whether analogous bounds hold beyond weakly separable scores.

These mirrors cover the paper’s constrained winner existence and constrained winner optimization results for weakly separable rules, including the named hardness and tractability results above. They do not continuize committee membership: the committee is still a size-\(k\) set of candidates. Nor do they automatically cover rules such as STV whose population-based ranking function would need to be specified separately.

The main soft spot is also clear: rational population masses can be expanded into a finite electorate using a common denominator. So this mirror does not claim that the continuous version escapes the paper’s hardness; its lead result predicts the opposite. Its value is the high-multiplicity formulation, where the input is the support of the population distribution and complexity is measured in \(m\), \(\tau\), and \(L\), rather than an explicit list of voters. The authors should recognize it as their problem: the constraints, committee, and welfare comparison are unchanged, while repeated ranking types are represented by their population mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this paper is about payoff, not whether a continuous mirror exists. All three anchors have straightforward mirrors, and the programme’s stated criteria leave little room to reject them.

For Theorem 3.1, the best version asks whether a constraint-satisfying committee reaches a rational score threshold under a population distribution. If a finite election has \(N\) voters, setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) gives \(\mathrm{sc}_E(Y)=N\mathrm{sc}_\mu(Y)\), so the paper’s comparisons and reduction carry over exactly. The Bloc construction has at most three ranking types. The adverse point is that the hardness is driven largely by candidate-label constraints; the population can be a small fixed input. That may limit how much this result teaches us about population continuization, but it does not defeat the mirror under the programme’s rules.

Theorem 3.3 also has a sound mass-weighted mirror. For a weakly separable rule, the distribution gives rational candidate scores; the paper’s tree-constraint algorithm can compare them with exact arithmetic. Its running time is polynomial in the relevant input lengths, and after computing the \(m\) scores, the ranking support has no further role. That makes this a low-yield mirror for continuous-optimization technology: the result appears to be a direct extension of the paper’s dynamic program, rather than a new use of column generation or continuous optimization.

Theorem 4.1 has the same limitation. The continuous version can use rational mass-weighted scores as objective coefficients in the Venn-region formulation. The number of integer variables remains bounded by the number of labels, so the FPT result transfers. Again, the distribution matters only through candidate scores. That weakens the claim that this paper would reveal a distinctive continuous algorithmic phenomenon; it does not make the computational question ill-posed.

So the negative case is that these mirrors may be routine extensions, and that their algorithmic content is mostly about candidate constraints rather than population structure. But routine extensions still answer complexity questions for a binary-encoded distribution, and this paper has a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large electorate selecting a fixed-size committee. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. Under the programme’s test, at least the Theorem 3.1 mirror survives; the main weakness in the proponent’s case is overstating the likely novelty of the tractability mirrors.
