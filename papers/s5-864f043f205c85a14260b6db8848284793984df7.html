The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is at the level of what this dissertation actually proves: there is no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary classifying the complexity of one of its computational problems. The proponent identifies that correctly, but the consequence is stronger than “the mirror needs new work”: there is no named computational result here for a continuous-population study to mirror. The statement that the preemptive model is NP-hard in general is unnumbered, and the constraint-generation algorithms establish finite convergence, not a polynomial-time bound. Theorem 5 gives conditions for supermodularity; Theorem 6 proves a coalition-proofness property; Theorem 7 characterizes core-selecting mechanisms through competitive equilibrium; and Theorem 8 characterizes an incentive bound. None settles the complexity of computing those objects.

The best proposed setting is a large fleet of interchangeable distributed energy resources, grouped by complete type: location, cost curve, capacity, and reserve capability. That is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the dissertation’s continuous goods do not prevent asking a new population-continuous question about it. But applying Theorem 6 to coalitions of positive mass, or Theorem 7 to a continuum of supplier types, would require new definitions and results. Those could be worthwhile research questions; they would not mirror a computational classification established by this paper. Treating the original bidders as a population would also change what “coalition” means: the theorems concern subsets of finitely many strategic principals, while a continuum model must handle deviations indexed by masses or measurable groups.

Part II offers a weaker candidate still. Its players are regional operators whose identities and network connections determine which coalitions can exchange reserves. A type description would need to preserve that topology and each area’s market data; a continuum of interchangeable areas would generally change the network and the game. More decisively, the least-core constraint-generation algorithm is not a numbered complexity result, and the dissertation expressly leaves its underlying optimization NP-hard in general.

So the negative case is strong against presenting this dissertation as an anchored continuization case: its computational work supplies possible models and methods, but no named complexity result whose population-continuous counterpart can be charted. It is weak as the universal claim that *no scenario* could yield a worthwhile study. The large-fleet setting is plausible, and a new computational analysis there may deserve attention on its own merits. The paper alone does not establish that it would be worthwhile—or that it would not.
