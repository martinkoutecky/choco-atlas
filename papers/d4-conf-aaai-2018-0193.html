The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over ideal locations on \(\mathbb{R}\), let a randomized mechanism choose a facility location \(Y\). For each \(\gamma\), determine the minimum worst-case ratio \(\mathbb{E}[\max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}\mu}|x-Y|]/(\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{supp}\mu)/2)\), subject to normalized standard deviation at most \(\gamma\) on every positive-diameter society and group strategyproofness against deviations by positive-mass submeasures.

The model it lives in

Types are ideal locations \(x\in\mathbb{R}\), and each society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) with rational masses. A mechanism maps \(\mu\) to a randomized facility location; its objective is maximum distance over the support, while incentive constraints compare expected distances for mass coalitions that may change their reported locations.

What the mirror covers

The mirror concerns the one-facility maximum-cost tradeoff in Theorems 2.3 and 2.4. It leaves aside the one-facility social-cost case and the multiple-facility results, including Theorem 3.1.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does GENERALIZED-LRM\(_\alpha\) remain group-strategyproof against arbitrary positive-mass deviations in this distributional model?
  • Does the lower bound of Theorem 2.4 extend to mechanisms satisfying the mass-based incentive condition?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive candidate is the one-facility maximum-cost tradeoff. Imagine a large municipality where many residents share a relatively small set of ideal points. A type is an ideal location \(x\); \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of residents at that location. A randomized mechanism \(M\) maps the finite-support distribution \(\mu\) to a facility location \(Y\). Its maximum cost is \(C_\mu(Y)=\max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}\mu}|x-Y|\), with optimum \(D_\mu/2\), where \(D_\mu\) is the support’s diameter.

A precise continuous question would be: for a normalized standard-deviation budget \(\gamma\), find the smallest worst-case approximation ratio
\[ A(\gamma)=\inf_M\ \sup_{\mu:D_\mu>0}\frac{\mathbb E[C_\mu(Y)]}{D_\mu/2}, \]
subject to \(\operatorname{std}(C_\mu(Y))/(D_\mu/2)\le\gamma\) for every \(\mu\), and to mass-group strategyproofness. For that condition, allow any submeasure of residents to change their reported locations; no such coalition may make every member’s expected distance weakly smaller and a positive mass of members’ distances strictly smaller. A solution is a mechanism satisfying these conditions and attaining the frontier.

The relevant named result is Theorem 2.4, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(\alpha\in[0,1/2]\), no strategyproof one-facility mechanism with maximum-cost approximation ratio \(3/2+\alpha\) can have standard deviation below \((1/2-\alpha)\operatorname{opt}(x)\) on every location vector \(x\). Theorem 2.3, also proved here, gives the matching mechanism: for every such \(\alpha\), GENERALIZED-LRM\(_\alpha\) is group-strategyproof, has expected maximum cost \((3/2+\alpha)\operatorname{opt}(x)\), and standard deviation \((1/2-\alpha)\operatorname{opt}(x)\). Together, they motivate the candidate frontier \(A(\gamma)=2-\gamma\) for \(0\le\gamma\le1/2\). The expected direction is a closed-form frontier, if the finite-agent incentive constraints carry over to mass coalitions.

The regime is plausible: residents at shared ideal points can be a high-multiplicity population, with many agents and relatively few types. But the transfer is not established. The paper’s strategyproofness constraints concern individual agents and finite coalitions; the proposed continuum condition allows positive-mass deviations and drops zero-mass ones. Also, maximum cost depends on the support’s extremes, not the masses, and the paper’s lower-bound proof reduces to two-player inputs. The population continuum may therefore add little to this result.

There is a more fundamental limitation for this programme: Theorem 2.4 is not a computational-complexity result. It proves a mechanism-design lower bound, rather than classifying an algorithmic problem as tractable or hard. Theorem 2.3 gives a mechanism and its guarantee; Theorem 3.1 proves an impossibility for a smooth tradeoff. None asserts a runtime or a complexity classification. So the population-level frontier is the best continuous analogue I can defend, but this paper has no eligible named computational anchor under the stated rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against starts with a scope problem: none of this paper’s named theorems classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Theorems 2.3 and 2.4 characterize a strategyproof mechanism’s approximation and variance; Theorem 3.1 rules out a certain kind of mechanism family. Those are mechanism-design results, not runtime, hardness, or algorithmic results of the kind ChoCo is meant to continuize. So the strongest proposed anchor is not eligible for this programme.

The one-facility mirror is still sensible as a mechanism-design question. A large population sharing ideal locations is plausible, and a distribution over those locations is a natural model. But for maximum cost, the objective depends only on the support’s two extremes, not on the masses. The proposed frontier \(A(\gamma)=2-\gamma\) therefore carries over the paper’s two-extreme calculation; it does not expose a population-scale optimization problem. And the transfer is not automatic: the paper’s group-strategyproofness proof can identify a particular finite player who does not gain, while a mass-coalition condition needs a positive measure of non-gainers. An atomless population may have no mass at its extreme locations. Proving the corresponding mass-incentive theorem could be worthwhile, but it would be a new strategic characterization, not a computational mirror of Theorem 2.4.

The strongest alternative is Theorem 3.1: put a distribution over locations, define social cost by integrating distance to the nearest facility, and ask whether a smooth approximation-variance tradeoff exists under mass-based incentives. Here the masses matter, so this is a more substantial continuous model. But it remains an existence question about strategyproof mechanisms, not a computational one. Also, the finite-agent theorem does not transfer just by replacing counts with masses: individual deviations have zero effect in an atomless society, while positive-mass deviations impose different incentive constraints. Establishing a meaningful analogue would require new work on that incentive model, without yielding the complexity landscape this programme seeks.

So the paper supports a plausible continuous-population *mechanism-design* agenda, especially around the two-facility social-cost impossibility. I cannot honestly claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists. The negative case is narrower: none of the anchors raised, including their strongest natural mass-based versions, gives this programme a computational mirror.
