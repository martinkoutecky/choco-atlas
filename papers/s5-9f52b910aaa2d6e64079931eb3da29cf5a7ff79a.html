The anchor — algorithmic

Theorem 1

  • Theorem 1 — survived: The mass-weighted calibration is a plausible population extension of the theorem, and the opponent’s coupling objection does not rule out calibrating over potential type pairs before assignment.

The continuous mirror question

Given supported vacancy types \(U\) and applicant types \(V\), with rational masses \(\alpha_u\) and \(\beta_v\) summing to \(1\) on each side, and each type’s incomplete weak ranking of the other side’s types, compute the aspiration intensities \(a_{uv}\) for mutually ranked pairs \(E\) that maximize \(\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\lambda_{uv}h_{uv}a_{uv}\), subject to \(\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\lambda_{uv}a_{uv}^{2}=1\) and \(0<a_{uv}\leq1\). Here \(\lambda_{uv}=\alpha_u\beta_v/\sum_{(u',v')\in E}\alpha_{u'}\beta_{v'}\), the normalized Borda score of a type is the mass it ranks below plus half the tied mass divided by the mass it ranks, and \(h_{uv}\) is the paper’s regularized reciprocal-difference coefficient for the two sides’ scores.

The model it lives in

A large two-sided clearinghouse represented by distributions over complete preference types: each vacancy and applicant type has a mass, and each type’s aspiration intensity applies to its potential pair with the other type. Potential pairs are weighted by the product distribution, conditioned on both sides ranking the pair. Theorem 1’s quadratic normalization and reciprocal-difference objective then have a mass-weighted analogue.

The objection that survived

The paper does not prescribe product-measure weighting for aspirations, so that aggregation remains a modeling choice rather than a forced identity with its finite model.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s aspiration-calibration subproblem. It leaves the TOPSIS pipeline, the final \(0\)-\(1\) assignment model, and stability questions aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the product-measure weighting of potential pairs be justified as the intended aspiration aggregate for a particular large clearinghouse?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a large hiring clearinghouse: many vacancies and applicants, with each side containing large cohorts who share the same complete matching-relevant description. A vacancy type has one incomplete, possibly indifferent ranking over applicant types; an applicant type has one over vacancy types. For example, a type could be a job class or a credential-and-location group. The market may have hundreds of thousands of agents but only tens or hundreds of supported types. The paper’s five-position, seven-applicant example is not itself such a regime.

The named anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It gives the optimum for model \(M\text{-}1\): choose positive matching aspirations \(a_{ij}\) to maximize the sum of each aspiration times the reciprocal of \(b\) times the absolute difference between the two sides’ normalized Borda scores, subject to \(\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}^{2}=1\). The theorem gives the closed-form optimum: each aspiration is proportional to that reciprocal difference, with the vector normalized to have unit Euclidean norm. The paper handles zero score differences separately with a positive-denominator convention. This is an algorithmic anchor for aspiration calibration, not a hardness result or a complexity classification for the paper’s final assignment model.

Here is its continuous-population counterpart. Let \(U\) and \(V\) be the supported vacancy and applicant types, with rational masses \(\alpha_u>0\) and \(\beta_v>0\), each side’s masses summing to \(1\). Each type supplies its incomplete weak ranking of the other side’s types. Define its continuous Borda score as the mass it ranks strictly below a type plus half the mass tied with it, divided by the total mass it ranks. This is the population limit of the paper’s normalized Borda scores when copies of each type are tied. Let \(E\subseteq U\times V\) contain the pairs ranked by both sides, let \(\Delta_{uv}\) be their absolute score difference, and let \(h_{uv}\) be the paper’s reciprocal-difference coefficient, using its positive-denominator convention when \(\Delta_{uv}=0\).

Call the problem Continuous Aspiration Calibration. Choose an aspiration intensity \(a_{uv}\) for each \((u,v)\in E\) to solve

\( \displaystyle \max_{a\ge 0}\ \sum_{(u,v)\in E}\alpha_u\beta_v h_{uv}a_{uv} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{(u,v)\in E}\alpha_u\beta_v a_{uv}^{2}=1. \)

Return the optimum aspirations, optionally normalized so that \(\sum_E\alpha_u\beta_v a_{uv}=1\), as in the paper’s subsequent normalization. The weights \(\alpha_u\beta_v\) are the mass of each type pair in the product population. This is the mass-weighted lift of Theorem 1’s pairwise calculation; it makes the aspiration per pair type constant across indistinguishable agents.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. Its optimum is \(a^*_{uv}=h_{uv}/\sqrt{\sum_E\alpha_u\beta_v h_{uv}^{2}}\), so a prover can compute it from the supported types in time polynomial in their number and the input bit length. The exact output can be represented using a square root of a rational. This is a modest, direct continuation of the theorem’s calculation, not a claim that continuous matching dissolves hardness: the paper establishes no named hardness result to dissolve.

The scope is deliberately narrow. This mirrors Theorem 1’s aspiration-calibration subproblem, not the paper’s final \(0\)-\(1\) assignment, its TOPSIS pipeline, or stability. A natural follow-up is whether the calibrated scores feed into a mass-conserving matching model over type pairs, and how that model compares with the paper’s individual assignment problem.

The weak point is that Theorem 1 calibrates weights; it does not decide the matching, and the paper’s named result gives no computational boundary for that central task. Also, the mass-based Borda scores are a principled population-limit extension of the paper’s scores, but the authors could regard that score definition as a substantive modeling choice. The case survives as a mirror of a precise, computed subproblem; it should not be sold as a continuization of the paper’s whole contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchor is that Theorem 1 calibrates weights over *potential named pairs*; it does not use a population distribution. Replacing named agents by types therefore leaves a choice the paper never makes: how much weight should each type pair receive? The proposed \(\alpha_u\beta_v\) weighting treats pairs as if a vacancy and an applicant were independently sampled before matching. But a matching itself induces a coupling \(x_{uv}\), constrained by the two sides’ masses. Weighting by that coupling would make aspiration calibration depend on the assignment; weighting each type pair once would make the population masses disappear. Neither alternative follows from Theorem 1.

One can make that objection stronger by noting that the paper gives no reason to regard independent pair exposure as its intended interpretation. Its model chooses an assignment; it does not sample potential pairs. So the proponent has supplied a plausible market model, but also a substantive assumption about how pairwise aspirations are aggregated. A mass-flow version of the paper’s final assignment model could be a better market-level question, but it would be a new formulation of \(M\text{-}3\), not a continuization of Theorem 1’s calibration result.

That is the best negative case, but it does not defeat the anchor. Theorem 1 is a named result for an optimization problem, even though it makes no complexity claim, and the product measure has a reasonable interpretation as the distribution of potential vacancy–applicant encounters. In a large clearinghouse with repeated preference types, that is a coherent setting the authors could recognize. The paper’s small example does not rule it out.

So I would argue that this is a weak ChoCo priority: the proposed mirror depends on an extra modeling choice, and the paper establishes no complexity boundary for the resulting problem. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The proposed calibration mirror survives that stronger test.
