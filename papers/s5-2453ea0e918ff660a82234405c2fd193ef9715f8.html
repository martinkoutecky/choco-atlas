The strongest rescue would be to give a rational finite-support distribution over voter types and ask an algorithm to find an equilibrium. That could be a useful computational game theory problem, but it would add an algorithmic question the paper does not study. Restricting the input so an algorithm can operate—say, by encoding the preference distribution with finite rational data—would make the new problem well-posed; it would not make it a mirror of Theorem 1.

Theorem 1 also resists a more direct mass interpretation. In an atomless population, one voter’s change of action has zero effect on aggregate support, election results, and prize allocation. With voting cost \(c>0\), that removes the individual pivots that drive the paper’s incentives. To retain those incentives, the model needs finite individual votes or stochastic count fluctuations. The former keeps voters atomic; the latter returns to the Poisson game the paper already analyzes.

Propositions 7 and 8 depend on those same pivots. Their conclusions about multiple prize-competing groups and penalty competition across groups are driven by probabilities of ties and changes in integer vote counts. In a deterministic mass model, an individual deviation changes no group’s mass. In a large-population limit that retains fluctuations, the pivotal probabilities still depend on the fluctuation scale; they are not determined by population proportions alone. Either the core incentive vanishes, or the proposed mirror must preserve the paper’s stochastic microstructure.

This does not rule out a worthwhile computational follow-on, such as studying algorithms for equilibrium computation under a carefully encoded type distribution. But that would be a new research question, not a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s computational results. The negative case is strongest on that scope point and on the loss of pivotality under an atomless mass model. It cannot establish that no independently valuable computational question could be built from the paper.
