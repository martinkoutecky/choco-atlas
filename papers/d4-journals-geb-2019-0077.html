The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) items, value bounds \(v_{\min},v_{\max}\), and at most \(\tau\) complete buyer types, an instance is an ordered online stream of cohorts with rational masses and type valuations \(v_t:2^{[m]}\to\{0\}\cup[v_{\min},v_{\max}]\). Supply is normalized to one unit per item. Each infinitesimal mass unit receives an integral bundle, so the online policy chooses a distribution over bundles for each arriving mass and must keep aggregate use of each item at most one. What is the best competitive ratio \(\alpha^*(m,\rho,\tau)\) achievable by a causal policy against the offline optimum, where \(\rho=v_{\max}/v_{\min}\)?

The model it lives in

An online combinatorial auction with a finite-support distribution of buyer types arriving in ordered cohorts, unit supply per item, and integral bundles for each mass unit; the policy distributes arriving mass across bundles and maximizes welfare against the offline optimum.

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make the case around Theorem 17, proved in this paper (with the appendix giving a proof sketch). In the limited-supply online combinatorial auction setting, with \(m\) items and buyers’ positive bundle values between \(v_{\min}\) and \(v_{\max}\), it says that no online algorithm can achieve a competitive ratio \(o\!\left(\frac{\log m}{\log\log m}+\log\rho\right)\), where \(\rho=v_{\max}/v_{\min}\). The surrounding text says the lower bound applies to randomized algorithms too. This is an algorithmic lower bound on online performance, not an NP-hardness result.

A concrete mirror is Finite-Support Mass Online Combinatorial Auction. There are \(m\) items and at most \(\tau\) buyer types. A type is a complete valuation function \(v:2^{[m]}\to\{0\}\cup[v_{\min},v_{\max}]\), together with its arrival cohort. The input is an ordered stream of cohorts, each with a rational mass; the algorithm learns the current type as it arrives but does not know the future stream or when a cohort will end. Normalize supply to one unit per item, so a unit of mass represents \(k\) identical buyers in the corresponding discrete auction with \(k\) copies of each item.

The action at each point in the stream is a distribution over integral bundles. Write \(x_S(z)\) for the fraction of the infinitesimal arriving mass assigned bundle \(S\) at stream position \(z\). Thus \(\sum_S x_S(z)\leq 1\), and total use of every item \(j\) must satisfy \(\int\sum_{S\ni j}x_S(z)\,dz\leq 1\). The welfare is \(\int\sum_S v(z)(S)x_S(z)\,dz\). Each unit of mass receives an integral bundle; only the type-level average is fractional. The problem is to determine the best competitive ratio \(\alpha^*(m,\rho,\tau)\) achievable by a causal online policy against the offline optimum, including how it depends on the number of distinct types.

This has a credible high-multiplicity regime: standardized procurement or enterprise buyers arrive in repeated cohorts with the same bundle valuations, while the seller has many copies of each product. The paper’s \(m\)-dependent lower-bound construction already has this shape. For \(r=\log m\), its stage-\(j\) buyers share the valuation “value \(1\) for bundles of size at least \(m/r^j\), and \(0\) otherwise.” Dividing the discrete counts and supply by \(k\) gives cohort masses \(r^j\), unit supply per item, and only \(\Theta(\log m/\log\log m)\) types even as the number of actual buyers grows with \(k\). Geometric value tiers similarly give a finite-support version of the one-item \(\log\rho\) lower-bound argument. The mass policy can split a cohort across bundles, but the theorem’s capacity accounting is in aggregate item use, so that extra divisibility need not erase the lower bound.

I would expect the transferred question to yield a lower bound of \(\Omega\!\left(\frac{\log m}{\log\log m}+\log\rho\right)\) when \(\tau\) is large enough to represent those staged families. That is a transfer of an online competitive lower bound, not a claim of computational hardness in the P-versus-NP sense. It also leaves useful questions: how much can a small \(\tau\) improve the ratio, and can online pricing match the best policy in the mass model?

The weakest point is that the paper already studies infinitely many infinitesimal buyers in Section 3 and develops fractional limited-supply intuition in Section 5. So the broad idea of a continuous buyer population is not new here. The defensible distinction is that this mirror keeps each mass unit’s bundle integral and asks how Theorem 17’s lower bound behaves with finitely many repeated valuation types; it should not be presented as an entirely new continuous-auction model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

My strongest case against the Theorem 17 mirror is that its central model is already continuous in this paper. Section 3 explicitly studies infinitely many infinitesimal buyers. Section 5 then derives a fractional limited-supply pricing rule, with a competitive ratio on the order of \(\log(m\rho)\). So the proposed mass auction is not a new way to make the population continuous; it is a finite-support restriction of the paper’s own fractional online-auction model.

The staged construction also fits that existing model directly. At stage \(j\), there are \(k r^j\) buyers of the same valuation type and supply \(k\) per item. Scaling both by \(k\) gives a cohort of mass \(r^j\) and supply one. The lower-bound construction uses only \(\Theta(\log m/\log\log m)\) such types. Its aggregate capacity accounting is naturally expressed in mass terms. Thus the proposed mirror largely repackages the paper’s existing online analysis and lower-bound instance as a finite-support population.

The remaining question, how the best ratio depends on the support size \(\tau\), is not answered exactly by the paper. The fractional limited-supply discussion gives a pricing upper bound, not a full \(\tau\)-sensitive characterization matching Theorem 17. But that looks like a refinement of an already continuous competitive-analysis problem, rather than a new continuization or a new computational-complexity question. The paper gives no finite valuation encoding or runtime model; its lower bound applies to online algorithms regardless of their computational cost. Solving for \(\alpha^*(m,\rho,\tau)\) could be useful online-algorithm analysis, but it would not, by itself, chart the kind of complexity landscape the programme targets.

This is not an airtight negative case. Repeated procurement cohorts are plausible, and the \(\tau\)-dependent refinement could be worthwhile. The honest objection is narrower: the population-continuous version is already present in the paper, so the proposed mirror’s remaining contribution is a support-size parameterization, not a fresh continuous mirror of Theorem 17.
