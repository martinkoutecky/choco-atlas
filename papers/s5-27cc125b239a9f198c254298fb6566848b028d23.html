The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) good categories, \(\tau\) complete valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(b_j\), and normalized monotone \(a\)-submodular valuation oracles \(v_t\), define feasible allocations by nonnegative \(y_{t,D}\) with \(\sum_D y_{t,D}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,D:j\in D}y_{t,D}\le b_j\), where every agent receiving bundle \(D\) gets an integral bundle. Let \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}\) be the maximum welfare over these allocations. Can an algorithm, in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) and with polynomially many value queries, output a polynomial-support allocation \(y\) and common nonnegative prices \(p\) such that unused supply has price zero, every positive-mass state \((t,D)\) satisfies \(v_t(D)\ge\sum_{j\in D}p_j\), every \(A\cap D=\varnothing\) satisfies \(\frac{1}{a}v_t(A\mid D)\le\sum_{j\in A}p_j\), and the welfare of \(y\) is at least \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}/(1+a)\)?

The model it lives in

A repeated exchange market has \(\tau\) complete valuation types carrying masses \(\mu_t\), integral bundles from \(m\) good categories, and per-capita supplies \(b_j\). The decision variables \(y_{t,D}\) distribute each type’s mass among bundles, while common item prices certify individual rationality and outward stability.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s greedy equilibrium and \(1+a\) welfare guarantee, and also proposes a mass version of Theorem 3’s local-optimum price support and \(1+a^2\) bound. It leaves the other equilibrium results, examples, and market dynamics outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the greedy construction be implemented with polynomially many value queries and polynomial support for \(y\) in \(\mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L)\) time?
  • Is the \(1+a\) welfare factor attainable or improvable in this oracle model, and what value-query lower bounds apply?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is around the paper’s approximation results for allocations in \(a\)-submodular economies. The paper has no numbered NP-hardness, FPT, or formal \(P\)-membership result, so the anchors are approximation and construction theorems. Theorem 4, proved here, is my lead: in any \(a\)-submodular exchange economy, every greedy allocation, with the prices set during the greedy process, is a \((1,1/a)\)-local equilibrium, and its welfare is within a factor \(1+a\) of every fractional allocation. Theorem 3, also proved here, is a second anchor: every local optimum in an \(a\)-submodular economy, with any supporting price vector, is a strong \(1/a\)-local equilibrium, and every fractional allocation has welfare at most \((1+a^2)\) times its welfare. Theorem 4 is the stronger lead because it gives a constructive allocation and the better approximation guarantee.

A natural regime is a large, repeated market for a finite catalogue of indivisible goods: for example, many households with recurring valuation profiles competing for stocks of benefit or service categories. Let \(J\) be the \(m\) good categories and \(T\) the \(\tau\) complete valuation types. The type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and valuation \(v_t:2^J\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\). Assume each valuation is normalized, monotone, and \(a\)-submodular as in the paper. Category \(j\) has \(b_j\in(0,1]\) units per agent: in a market of \(n\) agents, its stock is approximately \(nb_j\). Agents still receive integral bundles \(D\subseteq J\); only the mass assigned to each bundle can be fractional. Thus millions of agents can be represented by a small support of complete types, while the per-capita stocks keep the economy nondegenerate.

My lead mirror is Mass-Greedy Local-Equilibrium Approximation. Its input is \(J,T,\mu,(b_j)\), the valuations \(v_t\), and \(a\). A feasible mass allocation is a finite list of nonnegative \(y_{t,D}\) satisfying \(\sum_D y_{t,D}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,D:j\in D}y_{t,D}\le b_j\). Each unit of mass \(y_{t,D}\) consists of agents of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(D\). Let \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}\) be the maximum of \(\sum_{t,D}y_{t,D}v_t(D)\) over feasible mass allocations. The problem is to output such a \(y\) and common nonnegative item prices \(p\), with unused stock priced at zero, so that every positive-mass bundle satisfies \(v_t(D)\ge\sum_{j\in D}p_j\) and, for every \(A\cap D=\varnothing\), \(\frac{1}{a}v_t(A\mid D)\le\sum_{j\in A}p_j\); also require welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{mass}}/(1+a)\). A natural complexity target is polynomial time in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the rational input bit length, using value oracles for the valuations and a polynomial-size allocation output.

I expect the guarantee to have a tractable, Class A analogue: the greedy construction and its price argument are closely tied to the paper’s result. The algorithmic question is genuinely open, though. Replacing many named agents with a few types can make each type split across many different bundles as goods are assigned. The paper’s finite-agent greedy process does not by itself show how to represent or process those masses in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). That is the main reason this is a research question rather than a claim that Theorem 4 already supplies a high-multiplicity algorithm. It also generates questions about oracle complexity, compact output representations, and whether the \(1+a\) factor can be improved.

A second, narrower mirror of Theorem 3 is Mass Local-Optimum Price Support. Use the same instance and feasible mass allocation. Suppose an allocation \(y\) is locally optimal in this sense: for every two positive-mass bundle states \((t,D)\), \((u,E)\), and every \(j\in D\setminus E\), moving an infinitesimal amount of \(j\) from the first state to the second cannot increase welfare:
\[ v_t(D\setminus\{j\})+v_u(E\cup\{j\})\le v_t(D)+v_u(E). \]
The problem is to output supporting prices \(p_j\) such that, for every such seller and recipient state,
\[ v_u(j\mid E)\le p_j\le v_t(j\mid D\setminus\{j\}). \]
The mirrored conclusion is that \(y,p\) form a strong \(1/a\)-local equilibrium and that every feasible fractional benchmark has welfare at most \((1+a^2)\) times the welfare of \(y\). I expect the price-certificate and welfare-guarantee part to be tractable: the local inequalities give the supporting-price intervals, and the paper’s structural argument then applies. Finding a local optimum efficiently is a separate question; the paper notes that single-item improvement sequences can be exponentially long even in submodular economies. This mirror therefore covers the price-support and approximation content of Theorem 3, not a fast local-search algorithm.

The authors have a plausible reason to recognize these as their problems: agents retain their complete valuations, goods remain indivisible at the agent level, prices remain common item prices, and the questions still concern bilateral transfers, local equilibrium, and welfare against a fractional benchmark. Scaling inventories with population is a co-modification needed to make a large-agent regime meaningful. It preserves the paper’s economic question rather than replacing it with continuity of outcomes, prices, or time. The paper’s discussion of Aumann’s continuum of traders is relevant background, but it does not already solve these computational mass-allocation questions.

The weak point is that the paper’s results are stated for finite economies and do not establish the proposed \( \mathrm{poly}(m,\tau,L) \) bounds. In particular, atomless type mass may spread across many bundle states, so the finite-agent greedy proof may have no compact implementation. I would still defend the mirror: it asks the same authors’ welfare and equilibrium questions in a credible high-multiplicity regime, and it gives the prover precise approximation and price-support claims to test. The scope is the paper’s \(a\)-submodular approximation results, not its entire account of exchange economies or the dynamics that might produce an equilibrium.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s goods do not scale with its population. With \(m\) indivisible objects and \(n\) agents, at most \(m\) agents can receive a nonempty bundle. If \(m\) stays fixed as \(n\) grows, the fraction of agents served is at most \(m/n\). Theorem 4’s finite greedy allocation therefore has no nondegenerate population limit under that reading.

The proposed per-capita capacities \(b_j\) repair this by scaling supply with population. That is a plausible model, but it changes the economy into one with many copies of each good category. In that model, a mass allocation over integral bundles is a configuration LP: \(y_{t,D}\) records how much of type \(t\) receives bundle \(D\). Aggregating the paper’s fractional-allocation variables across agents of the same type gives the same constraints. This makes the welfare guarantee a close lift of the paper’s existing fractional benchmark, rather than a new consequence of population continuity. It also leaves the proposed complexity target underspecified: explicit valuation tables, value oracles, and compact representations of \(y\) lead to different algorithmic questions. A better formulation could specify those choices and study pricing or compressed allocation; that would be a real high-multiplicity question, but it is not yet the proponent’s claimed polynomial-time mirror.

Theorem 3’s proposed mirror is weaker as a computational anchor. Given a locally optimal mass allocation with finite support, the price-support condition reduces, for each item \(j\), to choosing a price between the largest recipient marginal value and the smallest seller marginal value. Local optimality ensures that interval is nonempty. The welfare bound then follows by carrying the paper’s proof over to mass sums. So the proposed task of outputting prices and verifying the guarantee is essentially a certificate calculation. A more substantive question would be how to find a local optimum, but the paper already notes that finite single-item improvement sequences can be exponential. The mass version has no evident simplification; with scaled inventories, it is also a new multi-unit search problem.

These points weaken both proposed anchors, but they do not defeat the best mirrors. Repeated consumer or procurement types and per-capita supplies can describe a sensible large market; type-level allocation across integral bundles respects the population scope. Nor can the negative case treat existing high-multiplicity work as a collision. The honest limit is that the paper does not yet specify the valuation encoding or output representation needed for a complexity classification. That makes the proponent’s particular polynomial-time targets premature. It does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: a carefully specified configuration-LP or pricing problem could be one. The case against a universal “no scenario” verdict is therefore weak.
