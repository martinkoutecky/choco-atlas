The continuous mirror question

Given finite encoded support points \(p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\in\mathbb{R}^2\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), a finite encoded candidate-site set \(M\subset\mathbb{R}^2\), and \(k\le |M|\), does there exist \(X\subseteq M\) with \(|X|=k\) such that for every \(y\in M\), \(\sum_{i:\,\|p_i-y\|_2<d(p_i,X)}\mu_i<1/k\), where \(d(p_i,X)=\min_{x\in X}\|p_i-x\|_2\)?

The model it lives in

Treat residents at the same location as one type with mass \(\mu_i\); their cost is Euclidean distance to the nearest facility. Choose \(k\) facilities from \(M\), and decide whether any location in \(M\) can attract a strictly benefiting mass of at least \(1/k\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the existence question in Theorem 10. It leaves the approximation and greedy-capture results, graph results, and sample-generalization result outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does hardness persist when the support size \(\tau\) is bounded, or is the problem tractable or fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\)?
  • What is the complexity as a function of \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and \(|M|\)?
  • Does the same existence question remain hard when every point of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is an available facility site?

The case FOR (proponent)

The cleanest case is the paper’s exact fairness-existence result. The scenario is facility planning for a large population recorded at a manageable number of standardized locations: for example, residents grouped by geocoded service site, with all residents at a site facing the same Euclidean distance to every candidate park location. A type is a location \(p_i\in\mathbb R^2\); its mass \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of residents at that location. Thus the population can be enormous while the support size \(\tau\), the number of distinct location types, is much smaller. This is close to the paper’s own parks example, and it makes “at least \(n/k\) people deserve a facility” mean “at least \(1/k\) of the population is entitled to one.”

My lead mirror is Mass-Proportionally-Fair Clustering Existence. An instance is a finite support \(p_1,\ldots,p_\tau\in\mathbb R^2\), rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), a finite set \(M\subset\mathbb R^2\) of permitted facility sites, and an integer \(k\le |M|\). The decision variable is a set \(X\subseteq M\) of \(k\) centers; there is no cost-minimization objective. Write \(d(p,X)=\min_{x\in X}\|p-x\|_2\). A center set is fair exactly when, for every \(y\in M\),
\[ \sum_{i:\,\|p_i-y\|_2<d(p_i,X)}\mu_i < \frac1k. \]
The sum is the mass that could strictly benefit from deviating to \(y\). A blocking coalition may take part of a type’s mass, as a continuum population permits. The question is whether a fair \(X\) exists, with \(X\) as the witness when the answer is yes.

The anchor is Theorem 10, proved in this paper (the proceedings text gives a proof sketch and points to the full proof). It states that, for \(X=\mathbb R^2\), finite \(N\subset X\), finite \(M\subset X\), integer \(k\), and Euclidean distance, deciding whether a proportionally fair \(k\)-clustering exists is NP-hard. The reduction is from planar monotone rectilinear 3-SAT; its initial construction has \(n/k=2\), and the proof explains how to extend it to any even value. This is the paper’s existence problem itself, not a continuous encoding or surrogate.

The bridge is exact on the hard instances: give each of the paper’s \(n\) points mass \(1/n\). For any proposed clustering and deviation site, beneficial mass is at least \(1/k\) exactly when the number of strictly benefiting points is at least \(\lceil n/k\rceil\), the paper’s blocking-coalition threshold. So the paper’s reduction establishes NP-hardness for the mass version too. For the unrestricted support family, I expect hardness to transfer (Class B). This is a genuine population mirror: the paper’s result supplies a hardness answer on the equal-mass subfamily, while the weighted continuous model remains a distinct question.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: agents still care about distance to the nearest facility, the number of facilities is still \(k\), and proportional entitlement is the same population share expressed as mass. The paper already treats agents as data points and defines fairness through groups of them; replacing counts by mass is the direct high-multiplicity analogue. The scope here is deliberately limited to Theorem 10’s finite candidate-site setting. It does not claim a mirror of the paper’s approximation, graph, or learning results, and it does not turn the candidate-site set into a continuum.

The soft spot is that Theorem 10’s reduction has one unit-mass type per point. It proves hardness for the general mass-input problem, but it does not show what happens in the regime that most motivates high multiplicity, where \(n\gg\tau\). The next questions are whether the problem is tractable or fixed-parameter tractable in \(\tau\), whether hardness persists with large multiplicities over few types, and whether the same existence question remains hard when every point of \(\mathbb R^2\) is an available center.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make a strong case against this anchor. Theorem 10 is a named computational result, and its mirror keeps the population as the continuous object. A facility-planning instance with residents grouped at modeled locations and a finite set of permitted sites gives a coherent type: residents at one location have identical distances to every site. A blocking coalition then has mass at least \(1/k\), exactly matching proportional entitlement.

The strongest objection is narrower: the reduction’s points can all be distinct, so it establishes hardness on instances with \(\tau=n\), not in the high-multiplicity regime \(\tau\ll n\). That leaves the central algorithmic question open. But it does not make the question unworthy: understanding whether the mass problem becomes tractable, or how its complexity depends on \(\tau\) and \(L\), is precisely useful work for this programme. Nor does the finite candidate-site set undermine the mirror; the population is what is continuized.

Nearby work studies approximation guarantees with finite agents, including settings with an infinite candidate space; that appears distinct from computational existence over a mass input. [Proportional Fairness in Clustering: A Social Choice Perspective](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18162)

So the negative case is weak. The reduction’s limited evidence about high multiplicity is a real gap in the proponent’s case, but I can’t turn it into a sound reason to reject the mirror. Theorem 10’s anchor survives.
