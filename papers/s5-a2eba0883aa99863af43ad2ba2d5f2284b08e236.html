The electorate is a convincing high-multiplicity object: Section 2 already represents it by masses on four \((w,e)\) types. But that makes the proposed population mirror an identity. The best computational candidate—calculate the Condorcet winner tax rate from the model parameters—also has little substance here. The paper gives explicit preferred-rate formulas, and the winning type is determined by mass thresholds. With rational inputs, evaluating the formulas and comparisons takes polynomial bit time; the model has four types and one policy variable.

A larger finite support of skill and work-preference types might motivate an algorithmic follow-up. Keeping the paper’s quadratic utilities and scalar tax choice, though, offers no apparent hard computational bottleneck: utilities aggregate across types, and the equilibrium is still a one-dimensional median-voter calculation. A nontrivial problem would likely require changing the model—for example, adding nonlinear tax schedules or heterogeneous ethical concerns. Those may be worthwhile research directions, but they are new questions rather than computational mirrors of this paper’s results. The empirical logit analysis offers no stronger anchor; estimating its coefficients is a statistical task, not a computational social-choice problem.

So the negative case is strongest on the paper’s actual scope: there is no named computational result to mirror, and the natural calculation is already explicit and small. It is weaker as a universal claim: a substantially expanded model could yield worthwhile computational work, but the paper gives no basis for treating that work as a mirror of its results.
