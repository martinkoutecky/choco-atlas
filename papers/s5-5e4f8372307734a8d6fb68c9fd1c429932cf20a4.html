What the mirror covers

No result is covered by a surviving mirror. Theorem 2, supported by Lemmas 5 and 6, gives the robustness separation; Lemma 3 is a concentration statement and Theorem 4 is a cited Chernoff bound.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational complexity result, so I can’t make an anchor-compliant case for a continuous mirror.

The nearest candidate is Theorem 2, proved here from Lemmas 5 and 6. It says that for every \(0<\mu<1/2\) and \(1/6<\delta<1/2\), there are positive \(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,d\) such that the constructed random graph is, with high probability, robust against the iterative strong adversary but not the non-iterative strong adversary. That is a structural existence result about opinion propagation, not a classification of a computational problem as tractable or hard. Lemma 3 supplies a random-graph concentration bound; Theorem 4 is a cited Chernoff bound. Neither supplies a qualifying computational anchor.

A continuous strong-adversary optimization problem on a population of network types could be a sensible follow-on. But presenting it as a mirror of a named complexity result in this paper would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only plausible anchor is Theorem 2; Lemmas 5 and 6 are its proof, not separate computational results. The theorem establishes that certain random finite graphs are robust against one adversary and not another with high probability. It asks for no algorithm, and gives no complexity classification. Turning it into “choose expert mass on a continuous network to minimize the final fraction believing the truth” would create a computational problem the paper never formulates. To make that a complexity question, one must choose an input representation for the network, define the iterative process on it, and specify what counts as success. That may be a worthwhile project, but it is not a computational result of this paper waiting to be continuized.

There is a sharper obstacle to the most natural mass-based version. The counterexample depends on \(D\), which has positive population mass but consists of degree-one vertices, each attached to a distinct vertex of \(J\). Those \(dn\) edges have vanishing density as \(n\) grows. In a dense continuum interaction model, they disappear: \(D\) has zero mass of expert neighbours, so it cannot inherit \(J\)’s label in a later round. Giving \(D\) positive-mass interactions instead changes the key mechanism, since those vertices would then have neighbours during the initial round. Preserving the original effect requires retaining each leaf’s specific link to a particular \(J\)-vertex—or keeping the finite sparse graph. In the former case, those links distinguish agents individually; in the latter, the society has not been replaced by a continuous population in the programme’s sense.

One could use a richer sparse-limit model or a continuum of matched pairs and ask an adversarial optimization question there. But that would be a new model whose encoding and computational problem need independent motivation. It might be useful; the paper itself does not establish that it is the right next problem. So the proponent is right that there is no anchor-compliant case for a mirror. The negative case is strong on that point, but the broader claim that no richer continuous network model could ever be worthwhile is not airtight.
