The continuous mirror question

Given rational cohort shares \(\rho\in(0,1)\), per-capita supply \(s\), and independent finite-support demand laws \(D_1,D_2\) with positive rational support, choose a nonanticipative policy \(a(d_1), b(d_1,d_2)\) for two sequential cohorts. Maximize \(\mathbb{E}[\min\{a(D_1)/(\rho D_1),\ b(D_1,D_2)/((1-\rho)D_2)\}]\), subject to feasible supply and demand allocations; return an optimal policy and its value, and characterize whether the first-cohort policy has a mass-adjusted demand threshold and concavity property.

The model it lives in

A continuum of agencies divided between two sequential cohorts of masses \(\rho\) and \(1-\rho\). Per-capita supply scales with cohort mass, demand varies with cohort-level shocks, and total cohort allocations \(a\) and \(b\) are distributed proportionally so each agency in a cohort receives the same fill rate.

The objection that survived

The stochastic threshold is preserved by common cohort shocks; under independent agency-level demand, aggregate demand could concentrate as the population grows and Theorem 1’s behavior might not persist.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the two-node allocation structure in Theorem 1 and, under its demand premises, the ordering result in Theorem 3. It leaves the multistage dynamic program, heuristics, information-value analysis, and waste analysis outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • For unequal cohort shares, what threshold and concavity properties hold for the optimal first-cohort allocation \(a(d_1)\)?
  • Under independent agency-level demands, does the aggregate population limit retain a nontrivial sequential allocation problem, or does demand concentration change the policy structure?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a defensible mirror, though it covers a narrow slice of the paper. The article has no named theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. Its best anchors are proved results about optimal allocation and sequencing; I would use those without presenting them as complexity results. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, is the lead: for a two-node SRA-e problem and fixed supply \(s_1\), there is a demand threshold \(T_d(s_1)\). If the first node’s demand \(d_1\) is at most that threshold, the optimal allocation is all of \(d_1\). Above the threshold, the optimal allocation is \(H_1(s_1,d_1)<d_1\). The threshold is nondecreasing in supply, and \(H_1\) is strictly increasing and concave in demand.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large relief network that serves many recipient agencies in two scheduled waves. Agencies in a wave share its route position, service rules, and demand profile; their individual requests are revealed when that wave arrives. The wave’s total demand can vary with an area-wide shock. Let \(\rho\) and \(1-\rho\) be the two waves’ population shares, with \(\rho\in(0,1)\). As the number of agencies grows, those shares stay fixed while the number of distinct types remains two. If the wave’s stock is allocated in proportion to observed requests, every agency in it gets the same fill rate. That preserves the paper’s equity objective at the individual level.

Call the lead problem Two-Wave SRA-e\(_\infty\). Its instance consists of rational \(\rho\), per-capita supply \(s\), and independent, finite-support demand distributions \(D_1,D_2\) with rational probabilities. Demand \(d_i\) is average demand per agency in wave \(i\), so its total demand is \(\rho d_1\) or \((1-\rho)d_2\). After observing \(d_1\), choose a total allocation \(a(d_1)\) to wave one. After observing \(d_2\), allocate \(b(d_1,d_2)\) from the remaining stock. The question is to return an optimal nonanticipative policy and its value, maximizing

\[ \mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{ \frac{a(D_1)}{\rho D_1}, \frac{b(D_1,D_2)}{(1-\rho)D_2} \right\}\right], \]

subject to \(a\le s\), \(a\le \rho D_1\), \(b\le s-a\), and \(b\le(1-\rho)D_2\). This is a finite rational LP: use a first-wave allocation variable for each \(d_1\), and a scenario fill-rate variable for each pair \((d_1,d_2)\). Its size is polynomial in the two support sizes and input bit length, so I expect this finite-support version to be Class A. A further structural question is whether the optimal first-wave policy has a mass-adjusted version of Theorem 1’s threshold and concavity properties.

A second, narrower anchor is Theorem 3, also proved here. In a two-node SRA-e problem where demands come from the same distribution family, have different means, and have identical standard deviations, the theorem says that visiting the nodes in increasing order of mean gives a higher expected minimum fill rate. Its mirror, Two-Wave SRA-e Ordering, uses two equal-mass waves with demand laws satisfying those premises. Given supply and both laws, return the better order and an optimal allocation policy for that order. With finite rational supports, evaluate the two orders using the LP above; this is again Class A, and the theorem predicts that the lower-mean wave goes first. Natural follow-ups are whether that ordering rule survives unequal masses, more than two waves, or route costs.

The mass interpretation is doing real work here: \(a\) and \(b\) are aggregate quantities, supply scales with population, and proportional allocation makes each wave’s fill rate meaningful for every member. Those are co-modifications needed to make the population continuous, not a change to the objective. The mirror covers the paper’s fixed-route allocation and two-node sequencing results, not its full \(N\)-node dynamic program, heuristics, information-value study, or waste analysis.

My weakest point is the batching assumption. The paper visits agencies one at a time and observes their demands individually; this mirror observes and serves a whole wave together. That is plausible for coordinated relief cohorts under shared regional conditions, and it keeps the sequential information and equity questions intact, but an author could reasonably say it is a specialized variant of their model. I would not claim that the paper’s actual food-bank routes are high-multiplicity. The case is that its underlying SRA-e objective has a credible continuous-population regime, with a precise, tractable two-wave question that can test the mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not use “there is no named result” as the decisive objection: Theorems 1 and 3 are proved results about an optimization policy and a visitation order. The stronger negative is that both concern route positions, while the proposed mirror puts mass on groups of agencies. That is a plausible new SRA model, but it does not make population multiplicity do the work.

For Theorem 1, the two-wave proposal replaces each agency’s demand by a random per-capita demand shared by an entire wave. Its LP is a finite-support stochastic program over those wave-level demand scenarios; the agency masses enter as weights and capacity coefficients. If instead many interchangeable agencies have independent demands, their aggregate per-capita demand concentrates as the cohort grows, and the stochastic threshold phenomenon the proposal aims to mirror largely disappears. Keeping that uncertainty through a shared regional shock is plausible in relief operations, but it is uncertainty in the environment, not uncertainty generated by a continuum of agencies. The resulting problem may be worth studying as a stochastic allocation model; it is a weak fit for a programme about the computational consequences of making the population continuous.

Theorem 3 has the same problem. With two equal-mass waves, the low-mean-first conclusion simply carries over to the two-wave ordering; population mass adds no ordering decision. With unequal masses, the question changes, but there are still only two orders to evaluate. Extending to many waves creates a more substantial sequencing problem, yet its scale comes from the number of distinct route stages. If many agencies share a stage and are served together, that is batching and the sequential process changes. If they are served one by one, their route positions and observed demand histories matter; treating them as one type erases information the policy uses. A continuous route-position model might be possible, but it would be a new routing and control problem, not a mass version of Theorem 3.

The best case for the proponent is a large relief network with cohorts exposed to common demand shocks and served in scheduled batches. That can be a sensible application, and proportional allocation can preserve the minimum-fill objective within each cohort. I cannot honestly rule it out as a worthwhile model. My negative case is narrower: the proposed two-wave LP does not show that the paper’s population axis has a useful continuous mirror. It either collapses under independent individual demand or retains stochasticity by adding a common-shock structure, while the ordering result remains about a small set of stages. The universal “no scenario” claim is therefore weak; the strongest objection is that the plausible surviving scenario is a new stochastic SRA variant rather than a continuization that exposes a distinct computational landscape.
