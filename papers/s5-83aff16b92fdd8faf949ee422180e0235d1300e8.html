The case FOR (proponent)

There is a natural, authors-recognizable continuous mirror here, but I can’t make an anchor-compliant case from this paper: it has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Tables 3 and 4 report benchmark runtimes. The introduction does say that Dodgson-winner determination is complete for parallel access to NP and W[1]-hard parameterized by the number of voters, but attributes those results to references [4] and [5]. They are background claims, not numbered results of this paper. Under your anchor rule, there are no qualifying anchors.

The best candidate mirror is the exact Dodgson score. Let a type be a complete ranking \(t\in S_m\), and let \(\mu_t\) be its share of the electorate, with rational masses on a finite support. For a target candidate \(c\), choose mass transfers \(x_{t,\pi}\) from each supported ranking \(t\) to output rankings \(\pi\in S_m\), preserving each type’s mass: \(\sum_\pi x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\). The resulting mass on \(\pi\) is \(\mu'_\pi=\sum_t x_{t,\pi}\). Define

\[ D_\infty(\mu,c)= \inf_x \sum_{t,\pi} d_K(t,\pi)x_{t,\pi} \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{\pi:\,c\succ_\pi d}\mu'_\pi>\tfrac12 \ \text{for every }d\ne c, \]

where \(d_K\) is Kendall distance, the number of adjacent swaps needed to turn one ranking into another. The problem is: given \(C,\mu,c\), compute \(D_\infty(\mu,c)\). A precise certificate can give the exact value and an optimal assignment for the corresponding closed constraints \(\sum_{\pi:c\succ_\pi d}\mu'_\pi\ge\tfrac12\); mixing that assignment with a positive amount of mass assigned to rankings putting \(c\) first gives strict Condorcet victories at costs arbitrarily close to the infimum.

A plausible regime is a large ranked-ballot election with a small fixed slate: for five candidates, for example, there are at most \(120\) ranking types, while the electorate could have millions of voters and much smaller observed support. Each type has the same preferences and swap costs, and mass means a fraction of the electorate. This preserves the paper’s question—how many adjacent swaps are needed to make a candidate a Condorcet winner—while relaxing voter-by-voter granularity. It mirrors the score calculation, and therefore the winner obtained by minimizing those scores; it does not mirror the paper’s empirical runtime claims.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A). Its direct formulation has exponentially many output-ranking variables, but the pricing question for a source ranking is how to place \(c\) relative to its opponents at minimum Kendall cost, given linear weights for the pairwise victories. The choice of which opponents to put above \(c\) appears reducible to a directed minimum-cut problem on their order in the source ranking. That is a promising route to a polynomial pricing oracle, not a result proved in this paper. It raises follow-up questions about exact bit complexity, the gap between continuous scores and scores in large replicated electorates, and how ties at exactly half the mass should be handled.

The main weakness is that this formal mirror is a proposal, not one of the paper’s stated results, and the paper’s experiments use small voter profiles rather than a high-multiplicity regime. The strict-majority condition also means the continuous optimum may be an infimum approached from above rather than an attained strict winner. That is manageable in the definition, but it underscores why the mirror cannot be presented as something the paper itself establishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper does not establish a new computational result to mirror. Its complexity claims are background claims attributed to earlier work. Its own results are benchmark measurements: runtimes, search-space behavior, and maximum solvable instance sizes for particular implementations. Those measurements depend on finite voter counts, integer swap layers, the placement of the first solution, and implementation details. They do not yield a worst-case complexity claim about societies represented by distributions.

The proponent’s exact-score formulation is the best possible counterexample. A distribution over rankings, with mass moved at Kendall cost until a candidate has strict pairwise majorities, is a coherent computational problem. For a large election with a small ballot support, mass is meaningful, and the score can be normalized per voter. Nothing about the paper’s subject makes that model unreasonable. But it asks for a new continuous algorithm and its complexity; it does not continue the paper’s benchmark results. The paper’s DFS, uniform-cost, caching, and iterative-cost algorithms operate on discrete profiles and integer swap layers. Their reported runtimes have no direct continuous counterpart. The proposed pricing oracle is a promising route into a different optimization problem, not a result of this paper.

So the negative case holds if “mirror” means a mirror of the paper’s own results: there are no qualifying named complexity results, and the empirical findings do not transfer. It does not support the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous question exists here. The exact Dodgson-score problem is a strong, natural question in its own right. If the reader accepts a mirror of the paper’s underlying computational task rather than one of its results, that anchor survives, and the universal negative case is weak.
