The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a fully compliant positive case under the anchor rule: the paper has no numbered result asserting that a problem is NP-hard, in P, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity. Its numbered results concern payment properties and auction structure. The closest anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper: for a single-minded combinatorial auction with a single effective core constraint, the VCG-nearest payment rule is non-decreasing. Here “single effective” means that one core constraint, together with voluntary participation, defines the core for the fixed interest profile and winner allocation.

The strongest positive candidate is a tagged-bidder, fluid-background VN monotonicity question. Consider a large procurement or capacity auction with finitely many bidder types: each type has a desired bundle and a common value class, and type \(t\) occupies rational mass \(\mu_t\). Let the supply of item \(j\) be \(q_j\) per unit of population. In an \(N\)-replica, there are \(N\mu_t\) identical bidders of each type and \(Nq_j\) units of each item. Retain one tagged bidder whose payment is being studied. Compute the ordinary VN payment in each finite replica, and take its limit as \(N\) grows, when that limit exists.

The problem is: given the finite type support, masses, bundles, per-capita supplies, background bids, and two bids \(b_i<b_i'\) for the tagged bidder, decide whether the tagged bidder’s limiting VN payment at \(b_i'\) is at least its limiting payment at \(b_i\). Restrict to intervals where the efficient allocation is unchanged and each replica’s core has a single effective constraint. A solution is a proof of monotonicity for all such inputs, or a rational counterexample giving the instance and the two bids. This is a fluid-market analogue of Theorem 4.2: the large background population is continuous, while the tagged bidder preserves the paper’s unilateral bid change.

For this restricted problem I expect the positive answer to survive, in the tractable, structural sense: apply Theorem 4.2 in each finite replica, then pass the payment inequality to the limit. The weighted squared-distance objective in the limit reflects the replica objective, since the contribution of a type is proportional to its mass. Follow-up questions include proving convergence of the VN and VCG payments, recognizing the single-constraint condition from the type structure, and finding what happens outside that condition.

The regime would be many buyers in a few bundle and value classes, with supplies scaling alongside the population: \(N\) is large while the number of types \(\tau\) stays small. Mass represents population share; fractional type allocations and per-capita supplies are the co-modifications that make this a fluid version of the auction. The paper’s subject remains the core and the payment rule, rather than fractional outcomes for their own sake.

The weak point is substantial. The paper’s result concerns one bidder in a finite auction; the proposed mirror adds a fluid background, scaled supplies, and a limiting payment rule. Preserving a tagged bidder also means the entire society is not atomless. Most of all, Theorem 4.2 is not a computational-complexity result, so this proposal cannot anchor the complexity-landscape claim the programme asks for. It is a plausible continuous extension of a payment theorem, but under the stated anchor requirement it is not a qualifying positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that the paper proves no computational result. Theorem 4.2 establishes a payment property; the other results characterize when that property holds and what it implies for bidders. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem or gives an algorithmic bound. The core may raise interesting computation questions, but those would be follow-on questions, not continuous mirrors of a complexity result in this paper.

The tagged-bidder proposal is mathematically coherent, but under its stated restriction it adds no computational question: Theorem 4.2 gives monotonicity in every finite replica, so whenever the two payment limits exist, the inequality follows by taking limits. Deciding whether monotonicity holds is therefore “yes” throughout the proposed domain. Studying whether those limits exist or how to compute them could be worthwhile, but that is a new convergence or algorithmic problem, not the paper’s theorem. And the tagged bidder retains an individual whose payment survives even as their population share goes to zero; the type distribution alone does not encode that payment.

A stronger population-level formulation could let a whole bidder type change its bid and ask how the type’s average or total payment responds. That is a genuine mass-based question, but it changes the comparison: the paper raises one bidder’s bid while holding everyone else’s fixed; the new model changes the bids of an entire cohort. Theorem 4.2 does not settle it. Such a problem could merit study, but it would be an unanchored extension rather than evidence that this paper supplies a ChoCo complexity mirror.

I would reject the proposed anchor as a qualifying case for the programme. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous extension: repeated bidder types in a large procurement market are plausible, and type-level core-payment computation might be interesting. The negative case is strongest as a judgment about this paper’s fit with ChoCo’s computational-complexity aim, not as proof that every continuous version would be unworthy.
