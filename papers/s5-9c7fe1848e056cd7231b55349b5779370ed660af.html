What the mirror covers

The proposed question draws on Theorem 2’s efficient-equilibrium cutoff and Proposition 3’s refinement for two efficiency-concerned candidates; it does not cover the paper’s full equilibrium characterizations or its extensions.

The case FOR (proponent)

Under the anchor rule, this paper has no named computational-complexity result. The closest result is Theorem 2, proved here: for \(\beta\in(2/3,1)\), an equilibrium in which both candidates propose zero pork exists when \(\varepsilon\leq\bar\varepsilon\), and all equilibria are inefficient when \(\varepsilon>\bar\varepsilon\). The cutoff is \(1/2\) if at least one candidate is office-motivated, and \((3-2\beta)/2\) if both are efficiency-concerned. Proposition 3, also proved here, further characterizes the both-efficiency-concerned case: below \((2-\beta)/(6-4\beta)\), multiple pure equilibria exist, including the efficient one; between that cutoff and \((3-2\beta)/2\), the efficient pure equilibrium is unique; above it, there is no pure equilibrium, and a mixed equilibrium has expected pork of at least \(9/7\) projects. These are equilibrium-characterization results, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified by computational complexity.

The strongest derived mirror I can make is an efficient-equilibrium existence problem for a mass electorate. Take three equally large constituencies, with voter types \(a,b,c\) of mass \(\mu_i=1/3\). A type includes its district, preferences, and access to proposal information; members of a type share those properties. Candidates choose \(x^J\in\{0,1\}^3\), where \(x^J_i\) says whether candidate \(J\) promises pork to district \(i\). Voters’ information follows the paper’s \(\varepsilon\)-probability public-revelation process, and the district masses determine the vote. Scale each project’s total cost to \(1/3\), so each voter still bears \(1/3\) of the cost per project. Given rational \(\beta,\varepsilon\) and candidate motivation types, ask whether a perfect Bayesian equilibrium exists in which both candidates choose \(x^J=(0,0,0)\); a solution is a strategy and belief profile witnessing such an equilibrium, or a determination that none exists.

Theorem 2 supplies an exact threshold answer for this proposed decision problem, so it yields a simple polynomial-time test by rational arithmetic. That is the positive case: the voter population has high multiplicity, the types retain the paper’s preferences and information structure, and the question concerns its campaign competition. But the complexity classification is a consequence of my recasting, not a complexity claim made by the paper. The support has only three types, so the result does not open a broader algorithmic landscape.

The weakest point is that the paper’s strategic electorate consists of three representative voters. Treating each type’s mass as casting a bloc vote preserves their pivotal role, but individual voting in a genuinely nonatomic electorate would not: any one voter would have zero influence on the winner. The mirror therefore needs a constituency-representation or bloc-voting scenario, and that assumption narrows its reach. The natural follow-up would be to let the number of districts or voter types grow and ask whether the equilibrium-existence and pork-outcome questions remain tractable. That is a plausible continuous research question, but it is not anchored by a named complexity result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither anchor is a computational result in the paper. Theorem 2 and Proposition 3 characterize perfect Bayesian equilibria and how their existence changes with information and candidate motivation. The proposed “efficient-equilibrium existence” problem makes those results look algorithmic by turning them into a decision query, but the paper itself does not ask for an algorithm or classify the complexity of a computational problem. Its model has two candidates, eight proposals per candidate, and three representative voters. Giving those three voter types masses \(1/3\) each does not make population mass a substantive input to the equilibrium question.

There is also a problem with preserving the voting mechanism. If the electorate is genuinely nonatomic, an individual voter has zero influence on the winner. The paper’s sequential-rationality and weak-dominance assumptions then no longer select the same voting behavior. One can restore the proposed mirror by making each constituency vote as a bloc, or by stipulating that everyone of a type votes sincerely and counting their votes by mass. Both are coherent modeling choices, but the first retains three strategic voters rather than a nonatomic electorate; the second adds an aggregate voting rule that does the work the paper’s pivotal voters did. With three fixed blocs, the population is replicated, while the strategic game remains the paper’s finite one.

That also defeats Proposition 3 as a distinct anchor. Its mixed-equilibrium characterization concerns candidates mixing over the same finite set of policy vectors. A mass electorate within the three constituencies adds no new population-level decision to that analysis. A stronger proposal would let the number of constituencies and their masses vary, then study equilibrium existence or welfare for the resulting weighted electorate. That is a plausible new game-theoretic project, but it needs a new voting and equilibrium analysis; it is not supplied by Proposition 3, and the paper states no computational result for it.

So the negative case is that these anchors support, at most, a mass-based restatement or an independently developed equilibrium-computation problem—not a computational mirror of a result in this paper. This case is not airtight: constituencies as large groups with shared preferences and information make a continuous population interpretation plausible, and the authors might recognize a weighted-mass extension as their problem. What remains weak is the step from that plausible interpretation to a worthwhile *computational* continuation.
