The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible population mirror would take types to be complete preorders \(R_t\) on the finite meet-semilattice \(A\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of society of each type. Let \(q_{t,a}\) be the mass of type \(t\) choosing action \(a\), where \(\sum_a q_{t,a}=\mu_t\); the outcome is the meet of the actions chosen by positive mass. Ask for the least number \(r\) of preference indifference classes for which some such society has no action allocation immune to deviation by any permitted positive-mass coalition, with every member of the deviating coalition strictly preferring the resulting meet. This is a recognizable extension of the paper’s equilibrium and exact-core question. Its weakest point is that the paper’s active coalition structure \(M\) is a family of finite player subsets; deciding which measurable coalitions are active is a substantive co-modification.

The closest named foothold is Theorem 4.5, proved in this paper: when \(N\in M\), the stability index of the meet game form for the \(M\)-exact core, and therefore for \(M\)-equilibrium, is \(\nu_M+\gamma_A(\nu_M)+1\). Theorem 4.4, also proved here, says the meet game form is always \(M\)-\(\beta\)-stable, and is \(M\)-exactly stable—and hence \(M\)-solvable—if and only if \(N\notin M\) or \(\delta_A<\nu_M\). These results give a strong structural basis for exploring a population version.

But neither is a qualifying computational anchor under your criteria. Here “\(M\)-solvable” means that an equilibrium exists for every preference profile; it does not assert a polynomial-time algorithm. The paper states no result about computational complexity, such as membership in P or NP-hardness. So the proposed population stability-index problem is exploratory: I cannot honestly present it as a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. The paper’s Theorems 4.4 and 4.5 characterize stability and give a stability index in terms of \(\nu_M\), \(\delta_A\), and \(\gamma_A\). They do not analyze the complexity of deciding stability, finding an equilibrium, or computing that index. The fact that the subject is a game form, or that the results have theorem numbers, does not make them computational results.

That also defeats the proposed stronger mirror. Even granting a natural continuum model—with preference types, mass allocations over actions, and deviations by positive-mass coalitions—the question “for which \(r\) can stability fail?” remains a population version of the paper’s stability-index question. It asks for a stability characterization, not an algorithm or complexity classification. The same applies to the \(\beta\)-core and exact-core claims in Theorem 4.4 and the equilibrium/exact-core index in Theorem 4.5. Recasting one as a search or decision problem could be worthwhile, but it would introduce a new computational question; it would not continue a computational result this paper established.

So the case against this paper as a ChoCo mirror is strong: its results sit outside the programme’s computational scope. I would not claim that no computational project inspired by the meet game could ever be worthwhile. That broader claim exceeds what the paper lets us establish.
