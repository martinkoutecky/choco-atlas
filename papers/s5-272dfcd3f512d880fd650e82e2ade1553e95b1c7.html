What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is the paper’s social media setting: it reports \(3{,}595{,}205\) users and models two belief types, informed and misinformed, with different prior distributions and message precisions. A continuum mirror could represent those users by type masses and their communication by a type-to-type mixing matrix. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: many users, few modeled types, and polarization remains the population-level outcome the authors care about. The paper’s model claims that, under sufficient prior uncertainty and random communication, polarization does not arise after updating; it also links the effect of increased homophily to whether fake signals have zero precision. ([Article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-019-0344-4))

But those are unnumbered analytical claims, not computational results. Table 1 reports Granger-causality tests; the model studies comparative statics. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result such as NP-hardness, polynomial time, or fixed-parameter tractability. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s strongest possible anchor is its two-type belief-updating model. A population with millions of users and a small number of belief types is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and masses plus a type-to-type mixing matrix are a sensible representation under the model’s assumptions. That part of the case for a mirror survives: it would be wrong to reject it merely because the paper starts from Twitter users or because types aggregate people.

The problem is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its empirical finding is a time-series claim about homophily and polarization, and its theoretical claims are analytical comparative statics. The theoretical model already describes aggregate beliefs as a density-weighted mixture of distributions. Recasting its two classes as masses is natural, but the resulting question—whether an update produces a bimodal distribution, or how homophily affects polarization—is still the paper’s analytical question. No theorem asks for an algorithm or classifies its complexity.

The best computational extension would make that model an explicit finite-input problem: given rational class masses, priors, signal precisions, and a mixing matrix, determine whether the updated belief distribution is polarized. But the paper’s reported model has two classes and studies precisely the relevant qualitative conditions. Extending the input to arbitrarily many types, or asking for the mixing matrix that maximizes polarization, could create a computational problem; it would also add a general model or an intervention objective that this paper does not study. That is a possible new project, not a computational mirror anchored in one of its results.

The empirical route has a different obstacle. Its measures use user classifications, mention relationships, and monthly changes over eleven years. Aggregating to belief masses can preserve some group-level quantities, but it discards network structure and interaction histories relevant to the empirical claim. Keeping those details as part of each type could make the support grow with the number of users, removing the high-multiplicity advantage. And the Granger-causality analysis remains a time-series inference task; a continuum of users does not turn it into a complexity question about society.

So the negative case is strong on the programme’s eligibility test, but not airtight as a claim that no useful follow-on could ever be built. The large-population setting and the model’s aggregate representation are genuine strengths. What is missing is a computational question from this paper that continuization would let ChoCo classify; making one requires adding a task the paper does not pose.
