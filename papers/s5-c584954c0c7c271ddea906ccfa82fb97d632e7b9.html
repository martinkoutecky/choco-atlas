What the mirror covers

No continuous computational mirror is anchored: Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 characterize mechanisms, and Proposition 1 compares truthful rules on the boundary and the full body.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population scenario is a large city choosing a shared facility on a strictly convex feasible frontier \(K\). Residents have support-linear preferences, with only \(\tau\) distinct directions among \(N\) people, where \(N\gg\tau\). A society would be a distribution \(\mu\) over directions, with \(\mu_p\) the fraction of residents reporting \(p\), and the outcome a point on \(\partial K\).

The nearest named result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for every finite \(n\), every dimension \(d\ge2\), and every compact, full-dimensional, strictly convex \(K\), a total rule \(F:(S^{d-1})^n\to\partial K\) is individually strategy-proof and diagonally unanimous if and only if there is one fixed participant \(j\) such that \(F(p_1,\ldots,p_n)=x_K(p_j)\) at every profile. This is a strong characterization, but it is not a computational result of the kind requested: the paper establishes no complexity class or algorithm for a computational problem. Proposition 1, which shows that weighted averages give truthful non-dictatorial rules when interior outcomes are allowed, is also a mechanism characterization, not a computational anchor.

A distribution-based mirror exposes the main difficulty. If a rule sees only \(\mu\), an individual in an atomless population has zero mass, so changing that person’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged. Individual strategy-proofness then imposes no restriction. For example, a rule could choose \(x_K(\bar p/\|\bar p\|)\) when \(\bar p=\int p\,d\mu\ne0\), and a fixed boundary point otherwise; it satisfies unanimity at every \(\delta_p\), while aggregating reports rather than selecting a named resident. Requiring a positive mass to misreport would avoid that vacuity, but would impose a cohort or coalition incentive condition absent from the paper.

So the setting is plausible, but the theorem’s fixed dictator is precisely the information a type distribution discards. The paper already has a continuous outcome space; its society is finite and labeled. Its weakest point as a positive ChoCo case is that a faithful population mirror changes the meaning of individual strategy-proofness, while preserving the theorem’s strategic force requires adding a different incentive model. I would not offer a continuous problem as an anchor here: the paper has no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor is Theorem 1, and it is not a computational result. It characterizes every strategy-proof, diagonally unanimous rule as a fixed dictatorship, but the paper defines no decision or optimization problem, finite encoding, or running-time measure. Its object is an arbitrary rule on a continuous domain, and \(K\) may be any compact strictly convex body. There is therefore no stated complexity result to carry into a high-multiplicity version. The same applies to Corollary 1 and Proposition 1: they characterize mechanisms, rather than algorithms.

The proposed city scenario is a sensible large-population story, but its distribution over preference directions loses the theorem’s key object: the identity of the fixed controlling participant. If the rule depends only on an atomless distribution \(\mu\), one person’s report change leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, so individual strategy-proofness imposes no constraint. Diagonal unanimity fixes the outcome at distributions concentrated on a single direction, but leaves mixed distributions largely unrestricted. A rule based on the normalized mean direction, with any fixed boundary outcome when the mean is zero, illustrates how little the theorem’s incentive condition says about such a distribution rule.

A stronger proposed mirror could let an entire positive-mass type cohort deviate. That avoids the zero-mass problem, but it replaces unilateral individual strategy-proofness with a cohort or group incentive condition the paper does not study. Alternatively, one could preserve individual identities or a distinguished dictator, but then the aggregate distribution no longer contains the information the theorem’s conclusion requires. Either route could motivate new research; neither supplies a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

So the case against this paper as a ChoCo anchor is strong and direct: it has no named computational result to mirror, and the most plausible population formulation either erases the theorem’s strategic content or changes its incentive model. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous computational project could grow out of the paper. The narrower, better-supported conclusion is that such a project would be a new question, not a mirror of this paper’s results.
