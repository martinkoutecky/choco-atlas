The continuous mirror question

Given applicant types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), placement categories \(J\) with rational supplies \(q_j\) summing to \(1\), eligibility edges \(E\subseteq T\times J\), rational utilities \(u_{tj}\), and a positive nonincreasing rational step function \(\omega\), find a feasible mass flow \(x_{tj}\ge 0\) maximizing \(G_\omega(x)=\int_0^1\omega(s)Q_{\nu_x}(s)\,ds\), where \(\nu_x=\sum_{(t,j)\in E}x_{tj}\delta_{u_{tj}}\), \(\sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_tx_{tj}=q_j\), and \(Q_{\nu_x}\) is the increasing quantile function. Determine its complexity and whether an exact optimum can be accompanied by a polynomial-time verifiable certificate analogous to Theorem 1’s rank-alignment condition.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is the paper’s fair assignment problem in a large placement market: many applicants compete for standardized jobs or training placements, with each person receiving one whole placement. A type records everything relevant to the assignment—preferences, eligibility, and utility for each placement category. In this regime the number of people \(N\) is very large while the number of types \(\tau\) and placement categories stays modest. Slot supplies scale with the population, so fractions describe the aggregate matching.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. In the authors’ notation, take an extreme solution \((v,t,y^*)\) of the dual LP (10), and let \(z^*\) optimize the modified 0,1-LP \(P_{y^*}\), whose utility for agent \(i\) is weighted by \(\sum_k y^*_{ik}\). Sort the resulting utilities so \(T_1^*\ge\cdots\ge T_n^*\). The theorem says that if, for every \(k\), \(y^*_{ik}=w'_k\) for all \(i\ge n+1-k\), and \(y^*_{ik}=0\) for all \(i\in[n-k]\), then \(z^*\) is optimal for the original fair 0,1-LP (9). In effect, the condition assigns the relevant GGI marginal weights to the lowest-utility ranks, and certifies fairness optimality through a modified linear objective.

The continuous question — Continuous GGI Assignment. An instance gives applicant types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\); placement categories \(J\) with rational supplies \(q_j\) summing to \(1\); an eligibility relation \(E\subseteq T\times J\); and rational utilities \(u_{tj}\ge0\). It also gives a fixed positive, nonincreasing rational step function \(\omega\) on \([0,1]\), with \(r\) steps. A feasible solution is a mass flow \(x_{tj}\ge0\), zero outside \(E\), with \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{tj}=q_j\). Each person receives an integral placement; \(x_{tj}\) says what fraction of type \(t\) receives category \(j\). Under a symmetric lottery across each type, its members have the same expected utility
\[ v_t=\frac{1}{\mu_t}\sum_j u_{tj}x_{tj}. \]
The question is to return an optimal feasible flow and an exact optimality certificate for
\[ G_\omega(x)=\int_0^1 \omega(s)Q_x(s)\,ds, \]
where \(Q_x\) is the increasing quantile function of the population distribution \(\sum_t\mu_t\delta_{v_t}\).

For this defined model I expect Class A, tractable. The step profile lets the rank-weighted objective be represented by a polynomial-size LP. If its steps have lengths \(\beta_\ell\) and values \(\omega_\ell\), one form maximizes \(\sum_t\mu_t a_t+\sum_\ell\beta_\ell b_\ell\), subject to the flow constraints, \(\mu_t v_t=\sum_j u_{tj}x_{tj}\), and \(a_t+b_\ell\le\omega_\ell v_t\) for every \(t,\ell\). This is the dual representation of assigning rank weights across type masses. The resulting LP is polynomial in \(\tau, |J|, |E|, r\), and the input bit length. Its rank-weight certificate plays a role analogous to Theorem 1’s certificate; it is a continuous counterpart, rather than a claim that the theorem’s exact finite-index condition transfers unchanged.

This is deliberately a mirror of the paper’s assignment result, not of every problem in its general 0,1-LP framework. Further questions include whether Theorem 2’s approximation guarantee for the maximum-weight solution has a useful continuous limit, when the ex-ante objective agrees with discrete GGI on individual utilities, and whether larger combinatorial menus admit efficient pricing.

The soft spot is the fairness measure. The paper applies GGI to the realized vector of individual utilities; this mirror applies it to type-level expected utilities. A type can therefore contain people who receive different placements while the objective sees their common expected utility. That is a coherent objective for a symmetric lottery evaluated before placements are assigned, and the brief permits fractional type-level averages, but the authors could reasonably regard it as a change to the fairness question. The case covers this explicit ex-ante assignment regime; it does not establish a mirror for realized within-type inequality.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proponent’s LP changes what is being called fair. The paper applies GGI to the utilities people receive from their assignments. The proposed model applies it to each type’s *expected* utility. If a type is split between placements worth \(0\) and \(10\), the paper’s objective sees a population distributed across those utility levels; the proposed objective sees everyone at the type’s average. Those can have different GGI values. The latter is a coherent ex-ante fairness question, but it is not an exact continuation of the paper’s objective.

For an exact realized-utility mirror, let \(x_{tj}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to placement \(j\). The utility distribution is then \(\nu_x=\sum_{t,j}x_{tj}\delta_{u_{tj}}\), and the objective is \(\int_0^1\omega(s)Q_{\nu_x}(s)\,ds\). The proposed LP’s type-level utility variable no longer represents this objective: now the masses at each utility level depend on the assignment. The rank-coupling formulation has assignment masses and ranking variables interacting, so the paper’s linearization does not immediately give the claimed polynomial-size LP. The two-outcome case already shows the distinction: with \(\omega=2\) on the first half of ranks and \(\omega=1\) on the second, if mass \(x\) has utility \(0\) and the rest utility \(10\), the objective is \(15-20x\) for \(x\le \tfrac12\) and \(10(1-x)\) for \(x\ge \tfrac12\). It is a function of the *distribution of realized outcomes*, not of a common expected utility for the type.

There is also a narrower mismatch with the chosen anchor. Theorem 1 gives a sufficient certificate for optimality when rank-indexed dual weights line up with the sorted utilities. It does not establish a complexity bound or give a polynomial algorithm for fair assignment. A continuum version would need to replace that finite certificate and show how it interacts with assignments that split each type’s mass across several realized utility levels. The proposed expected-utility LP does not do that.

These points defeat the proponent’s tractability claim as stated, but they do not defeat the better mirror. A large placement market with a fixed set of applicant types and placement categories is a sensible high-multiplicity regime; the exact realized-utility objective is well-defined there. Whether its optimization has a useful algorithm is precisely the computational question, and potential difficulty is not a reason to reject it. The closest cited randomized fair-optimization work studies lotteries over feasible solutions, not a continuous population, so it does not settle this mirror either. [Gilbert and Spanjaard (2017)](https://agt2017.net.technion.ac.il/files/2017/07/AGTIJCAI2017_paper_4_GilbertS.pdf)

So I cannot honestly make the universal negative case. The ex-ante LP is not a faithful mirror of the paper’s realized-utility fairness objective, and Theorem 1 does not itself supply the computational result the proponent’s framing suggests. But the corrected ex-post assignment question survives those objections and appears worth asking.
