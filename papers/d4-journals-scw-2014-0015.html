What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation follows Theorem 13's existence condition for anonymous, neutral majority rules; it leaves the other existence results, rule counts, and orbit-structure results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I can defend is a continuous version of the paper’s rule-existence problem. The paper’s lead anchor is Theorem 13 (stated earlier as Theorem B), proved in the paper: for \(h,n\ge2\) and an integer \(\nu\) with \(h/2<\nu\le h\), an anonymous and neutral \(\nu\)-majority rule exists exactly when \(\gcd(h,n!)=1\) and \(\nu>(n-1)h/n\). The paper has no named result about NP-hardness, tractability, or another complexity class; Theorem 13 is an existence characterization, not a complexity theorem.

A plausible population regime is a large professional association whose members rank a fixed set of policy proposals using standardized ballots. Let each type be one of the \(n!\) strict rankings. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those rankings, with support size \(\tau\) potentially much smaller than the number of members. Here \(\mu_t\) is a fraction of members, and the output is a strict social ranking. This preserves the paper’s objects and makes population size irrelevant once the type masses are given.

My lead continuous problem is: given \(n\) and a rational threshold \(\alpha\in(1/2,1]\), does there exist a deterministic algorithm \(A\) that takes any listed rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings and returns a strict ranking \(F(\mu)\), with running time polynomial in \(n,\tau,L\), such that:

  • if at least an \(\alpha\)-fraction of the society ranks \(x\) above \(y\), then \(F(\mu)\) ranks \(x\) above \(y\); and
  • for every renaming \(\psi\) of the alternatives, \(F(\psi\mu)=\psi F(\mu)\).

The solution sought is such an algorithm; this is a feasibility question, with no optimization objective. The authors would recognize it as a direct population-mass counterpart of their rule: the type space, strict-ranking outcome, neutrality, and majority requirement all remain intact, while individual counts become fractions.

On the full space of rational distributions, though, the answer is no for every \(n\ge2\) and every \(\alpha\). Take \(\mu\) uniform over all \(n!\) rankings. It is unchanged by every alternative renaming, so neutrality would require \(F(\mu)=\psi F(\mu)\) for every \(\psi\). No strict linear order is fixed by a nonidentity renaming. Thus no such rule exists, even before considering the majority condition. This is a continuum-domain symmetry obstruction, not a hardness result. It also explains a difference from the finite theorem: a uniform distribution is available in the continuous domain regardless of \(h\), whereas the finite theorem’s coprimality regime rules out the corresponding profile.

That is my weakest point, and likely decisive for ChoCo: the paper studies axiomatic existence and counting, rather than computational complexity, and the natural full-domain mirror is immediately impossible. The further question is whether an equivariant majority selector can be computed on societies with no nontrivial alternative symmetry. Allowing lotteries over social rankings is another possible escape, but it changes the outcome space rather than continuing the population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 13 characterizes when a *global function* satisfying anonymity, neutrality, and a majority axiom exists. Its companion results count such functions. Neither asks for an algorithm on an input society, and the paper’s group theory serves the existence and counting arguments. The proposed mirror must add a computational task before ChoCo’s usual complexity questions can begin.

The professional-association scenario is sensible: rankings are finite types, and large populations can be represented by rational masses. That makes the model a reasonable high-multiplicity object. It does not supply the missing computational problem. Given one society, form the directed graph of pairwise comparisons supported by at least an \(\alpha\)-fraction of the mass. A compatible strict ranking exists exactly when this graph is acyclic, and a ranking can then be found by topological sorting. With the society listed by its \(\tau\) supported types, this is straightforward to compute. That local task does not capture Theorem 13’s global requirement that outputs across *all* societies fit together into one neutral rule.

Imposing that global requirement exposes a deeper mismatch. The full distribution domain includes the uniform distribution over rankings, which is unchanged by every alternative renaming. Neutrality would require its output ranking to be unchanged by every renaming too; no strict ranking has that property. So the proposed full-domain deterministic mirror is impossible for a symmetry reason, regardless of its majority threshold. Restricting the domain to distributions with no alternative symmetries avoids this obstruction, but then the computational question becomes how to choose outputs equivariantly across renamed inputs. That is a canonical-selection problem added to the paper’s model. The majority condition itself still reduces to the graph calculation.

One could instead ask for a continuous axiomatic existence theorem, or change the outcome to a set of rankings or a lottery to handle symmetric societies. Those may be coherent questions, but the first lies outside ChoCo’s computational scope, while the second changes the outcome model. So the paper offers a plausible population interpretation but no strong computational mirror anchored in its results. This negative case is strongest on the category mismatch; it is weaker as a universal claim. An efficient equivariant selector on a carefully chosen domain could become an interesting new problem, though its motivation would need to come from beyond this paper.
