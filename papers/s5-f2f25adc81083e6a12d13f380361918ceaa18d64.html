What the mirror covers

No computational result is mirrored; the paper's named theorems concern preference changes and stability after pair deletion. The proposed VM rematching objective is a speculative extension rather than a result established by the paper.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited: this paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Theorem 1, proved here, gives a condition under which a one-sided preference change is “minor”: no agent ranked below the old partner rises above that partner. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that deleting any matched pair from a stable matching and instance leaves a stable matching for the reduced instance. Both are structural claims; neither classifies a problem as tractable or hard. The paper describes Algorithms 1 and 2, but gives no complexity bound, and says computational costs are not its focus.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting in the paper’s VM/server motivation: many VMs and servers sharing a small number of complete preference and resource types, with the mass of each type representing its share of the deployment. One could ask for a stable new mass matching that minimizes the mass of agents whose assignments change after preferences update. That would be a natural research question, but it is not anchored by a qualifying named result here, and the paper does not formalize revision cost as that objective. I therefore can’t present it as the required positive mirror without overstating what the authors established.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has no qualifying anchor. Theorem 1 characterizes a “minor” preference change, and Theorem 2 says that deleting a matched pair preserves stability in the reduced instance. Neither is a complexity result. Algorithms 1 and 2 have no runtime bound, and the paper explicitly says computational costs are not its focus. That is the strongest case against a *mirror of this paper’s results*.

The two theorems also resist direct lifting to population masses. Theorem 1 asks whether a particular agent still wants their particular old partner; a type-level allocation can split a cohort across several partner types, so it has no single old partner unless the model is enriched. Theorem 2 removes one pair, which has zero mass in a continuum. Removing a positive-mass cohort instead would be a new claim, not the limit of deleting one pair. The update procedure likewise works with named agents entering a queue one at a time; its “room” and queue are not themselves a mass-transfer algorithm.

But the better VM mirror is hard to dismiss. Give cohorts complete VM and server types, record the mass transitioning from each old preference type to each new one, and ask for a stable rematching that minimizes the mass whose assignment changes. That formalizes the paper’s stated concern about how many VMs must stop during rematching. Continuum matching models already show that aggregate stable matching is a coherent object, though they do not answer this update-and-recourse question ([Azevedo and Leshno](https://doi.org/10.1086/687476)). Under ChoCo’s scope, this is a plausible new computational problem, and the authors’ VM motivation makes it plausible they would recognize it.

So I can defend a narrow negative: the paper supplies no named computational result for the other analyst to mirror, and the proposed VM optimization is not anchored by either theorem. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The strongest proposed version survives that broader test; the negative case is weak exactly there.
