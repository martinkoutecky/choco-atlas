Theorem 3.1

Every anchor argued

  • Theorem 3.1 — survived: The continuous version retains indivisible household bundles and prefix TEFX; scaling rational masses and supplies gives a well-defined population allocation problem.

The continuous mirror question

Given binary valuation types \(\Theta\) with rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), a horizon \(T\), item kinds \(G\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_{r,g}\), compute a finite-support rational assignment \(\lambda_{\theta,h}\). Each history \(h=(B_1,\ldots,B_T)\) gives one household integral bundles of goods in each round; the assignments must sum to each type's mass, use exactly each round's supplies, and satisfy TEFX at every prefix between every pair of positive-mass type-history assignments. Is there an algorithm with polynomial running time and polynomial output support in \(|G|, T, |\Theta|\), and the input bit length?

The model it lives in

A continuum of households has types \(\theta\in\Theta\), binary valuations \(v_\theta(g)\), and masses \(\mu_\theta\). Rational per-capita supplies \(q_{r,g}\) scale with population size, while each household receives whole goods. The decision variables are rational masses \(\lambda_{\theta,h}\) over integral allocation histories; there is no welfare or cost objective. Every pair of histories with positive assigned mass must meet the paper's TEFX condition at each prefix.

The objection that survived

The finite-agent termination result gives no polynomial bound for the distributional input, and TEFX constraints couple every pair of supported histories, so a compact algorithm remains unestablished.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the strong-binary TEFX construction in Theorem 3.1. It leaves the paper's impossibility, approximation, and TMMS results outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a TEFX assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(|G|, T, |\Theta|\), and the input bit length, with polynomial output support?
  • What upper or lower bounds can be established for the number of distinct histories needed in a feasible assignment?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper: under strong binary valuations, a TEFX allocation can be computed in finite time. Its algorithm uses historical backtracking to preserve EFX at every round. The theorem does not claim polynomial time, and the paper gives no NP-hardness result; its negative results are impossibility results about whether a fair allocation exists. ([Theorem 3.1 in the paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.17224))

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large food-bank programme with many households but relatively few stable need profiles. A type is a household’s complete binary valuation vector over the item kinds: it values an item at \(1\) if it needs it and \(0\) otherwise. Mass \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of households with type \(\theta\). Each distribution round has a rational per-household stock \(q_{r,g}\) of each item kind \(g\), with every stocked kind valued by at least one positive-mass type. The regime might have hundreds of thousands of households and dozens of types. Whole item bundles still go to individual households; only the shares of households assigned to different bundle patterns are fractional. Scaling stock per household is a natural part of this mirror.

I would call the resulting search problem High-Multiplicity Strong-Binary TEFX. Its input is the finite type set \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\), the item kinds \(G\), a horizon \(T\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_{r,g}\). A history \(h=(B_1,\ldots,B_T)\) specifies an integral multiset \(B_r\) of items for one household in each round. The solution is a finite-support set of nonnegative rational weights \(\lambda_{\theta,h}\): \(\lambda_{\theta,h}\) is the mass of type-\(\theta\) households assigned history \(h\). It must satisfy \(\sum_h\lambda_{\theta,h}=\mu_\theta\) and, for every round \(r\) and item kind \(g\), \(\sum_{\theta,h}\lambda_{\theta,h} b_{r,g}(h)=q_{r,g}\), where \(b_{r,g}(h)\) counts copies of \(g\) in \(B_r\).

The allocation must also be TEFX at every prefix. Writing \(B^{\le s}(h)\) for the cumulative bundle through round \(s\), for every pair of positive-mass type-history assignments \((\theta,h)\), \((\theta',h')\), every \(s\), and every item \(g\) in \(B^{\le s}(h')\), require \(v_\theta(B^{\le s}(h))\ge v_\theta(B^{\le s}(h')\setminus\{g\})\). Considering all ordered pairs enforces the condition in both directions. The task is to find such weights and histories; it adds no welfare or cost objective.

This is recognizably the authors’ problem: it keeps binary need valuations, allocation of indivisible goods, and cumulative EFX. It also matches their informed temporal setting, since the output is a complete allocation history that is fair at every prefix. For rational inputs, a common-denominator expansion turns the masses and supplies into a finite instance, so Theorem 3.1 gives a finite-support solution. But the expanded population can be exponential in the bit length of the input. The continuous mirror’s computational question is whether one can construct a compact solution directly from the type masses and per-capita supplies.

My provisional expectation is Class A, at least with a small number of types and item kinds: the theorem already gives finite-instance existence, and type aggregation may let an algorithm track mass by bundle history without tracking named households. The key open issue is whether the pairwise fairness constraints on all supported histories permit that compression. In particular: can a direct algorithm run in time polynomial in \(|G|,T,\tau,L\), where \(\tau=|\Theta|\) and \(L\) is the input bit length? How small can a sufficient set of supported histories be?

The weak point is that Theorem 3.1 proves only finite-time computation, not an efficient algorithm, and the rational finite-instance expansion already settles existence. So this is a strong mirror of the paper’s constructive question, but its new content would be the compressed high-multiplicity complexity, not a new existence theorem. This case covers the strong-binary TEFX result only; it makes no claim about the paper’s identical-days impossibilities, approximation ratios, or TMMS results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 3.1 gives a finite-time construction, with no polynomial running-time bound or complexity classification. For rational type masses and per-capita supplies, let \(D\) be a common denominator. Expanding to \(D\) households and the corresponding integral stock turns the proposed instance into a finite one covered by the theorem, provided each stocked item is liked by some positive-mass type. Thus feasibility is already settled: every valid instance has a solution.

The only nontrivial computational question is whether one can find a *compact* solution in time polynomial in the type count, horizon, and input bit length. The proponent’s output format does not bound its support. A solution may need many distinct integral bundle histories, and TEFX couples every pair of histories in the support at every prefix. Those support-dependent constraints do not form an ordinary LP over the masses. The paper’s backtracking algorithm also relies on named agents and historical reallocations, so aggregating those operations by type is not automatic. This makes the proponent’s provisional Class A forecast unsupported.

That objection does not defeat the best mirror. A large food-bank programme with many households and a small set of binary need profiles is a credible high-multiplicity regime. Each household still receives whole goods; masses describe how households are distributed across integral histories. TEFX remains meaningful between every pair of positive-mass histories. A direct compact algorithm, or a proof that compact solutions cannot always exist, would be a real computational result.

So the negative case can narrow the anchor: the paper proves finite computability, while the interesting continuous question is a further compactness and complexity question. I cannot honestly argue that this makes the mirror unrecognizable or unworthy. The population model is sensible, and the unresolved support question is exactly where a worthwhile computational mirror could lie.
