The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of types \(t=(q_t,y_t)\), rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), and \(q_t\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(1/2,1)\), where \(b_t=q_t\) for \(y_t=A\) and \(b_t=1-q_t\) for \(y_t=B\), compute a Kelly competitive-equilibrium price \(p\) and each type’s per-capita investments \(x_t^A,x_t^B\). Require type-wise best responses under unit per-capita endowment and market clearing, \(\frac{1}{p}\sum_t\mu_t x_t^A=\frac{1}{1-p}\sum_t\mu_t x_t^B\); determine whether binarizing \(p\) agrees with majority using weights \(2q_t-1\) and masses \(\mu_t\).

The model it lives in

Types are competence-and-signal pairs \(t=(q_t,y_t)\) with mass \(\mu_t\); decisions are per-capita investments \(x_t^A,x_t^B\) and the equilibrium price \(p\). Each type maximizes expected log payout, and aggregate investment satisfies market clearing. The price is the mass-weighted average belief, \(p=\sum_t\mu_t b_t\), so the finite-support computation uses a scan and rational weighted sums.

The objection that survived

If signals are independently generated from a fixed competence distribution, continuum masses erase finite-sample fluctuations and make the truth-tracking decision correct almost surely; the stated mirror fixes the realized \((q,y)\)-mass distribution and does not carry over that error-probability question.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named hardness or complexity class result in this paper. My best positive case is Theorem 4, proved here: in Kelly markets, for every competence profile \(q\) and induced belief profile \(b\), weighted majority with weights \(w_i\propto 2q_i-1\) chooses the same alternative, including ties, as the market decision obtained by binarizing its equilibrium price \(p_{KC}(b)\). The supporting Lemma 5, attributed to [5] and reproved in the appendix, gives the price explicitly: \(p_{KC}(b)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i b_i\). These results establish an efficiently computable aggregation rule, though they do not themselves present a complexity classification.

The mirror I would put to a prover is Continuous Kelly Condorcet Aggregation. An instance is a finite support of types \(t=(q_t,y_t)\), where \(q_t\in\mathbb Q\cap(1/2,1)\) is the type’s competence, \(y_t\in\{A,B\}\) its observed signal, and \(\mu_t\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) its population mass, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Its posterior belief is \(b_t=q_t\) for \(y_t=A\), and \(b_t=1-q_t\) for \(y_t=B\). Each unit of mass represents traders of that type with the paper’s unit endowment and Kelly utility. Find the competitive equilibrium price \(p\) and each type’s per-capita investment in the two securities. A solution must give best responses at \(p\) and satisfy market clearing:
\[ \frac{1}{p}\sum_t\mu_t x_t^A = \frac{1}{1-p}\sum_t\mu_t x_t^B. \]
Then report the price-induced decision: \(A\) if \(p>1/2\), \(B\) if \(p<1/2\), and a tie if \(p=1/2\). This is an equilibrium computation, not a social optimization problem: each type maximizes its expected log payout.

The paper’s Lemma 5 gives the solution directly:
\[ p=\sum_t\mu_t b_t. \]
At that price, type \(t\) invests \(\max\{(b_t-p)/(1-p),0\}\) in \(A\) and \(\max\{(p-b_t)/p,0\}\) in \(B\). Theorem 4’s weighted-majority equivalence extends from sums to these mass-weighted sums. Thus the continuous problem is tractable by a scan over the \(\tau\) supported types, with polynomial bit complexity in \(\tau\) and the rational input length. Its expected direction is tractable.

The regime is a large prediction market or binary-decision crowd with a small number of calibrated competence classes: many traders share a competence class and signal outcome, so \(n\) can be very large while \(\tau\) stays small. Mass means the fraction of participants of each type; aggregate endowment scales with that mass, and the market-clearing price is unchanged by this normalization. This preserves the paper’s market and its price-taking equilibrium. It also matches the authors’ own motivation for competitive equilibrium: participants act as price takers in a large group. The paper notes a continuum-player analogue for Naive-market quantiles in its discussion, which supports the plausibility of population continuity but supplies no complexity result.

This mirror covers Theorem 4’s Kelly-market and competence-weighted-majority correspondence. It does not continuize the outcome space or the uncertainty model: the population is the continuous object. Further questions include whether the same formulation handles continuous competence distributions, whether heterogenous utilities preserve tractability, and whether the taxed-market limit in Theorem 5 extends to type masses.

The weak point is that this gives the programme a clean, author-recognizable continuous computation, but not a hard computational question: the price is an average, and the paper proves no discrete hardness that might dissolve in the continuum. Also, the small-\(\tau\) scenario depends on competence classes being a convincing grouping; if most participants have distinct competences, \(\tau\) may be nearly \(n\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to the Kelly mirror is about which object is being continued. The paper’s central concern is truth-tracking under independent, finite signal draws. If competence classes become a continuum population, those signal shares settle to their expected values. For competence distribution \(\nu\), conditional on \(A\), the average posterior is
\[ \int \bigl(q^2+(1-q)^2\bigr)\,d\nu(q) =\frac12+2\int (q-\tfrac12)^2\,d\nu(q)>\frac12. \]
Conditional on \(B\), it is below \(\frac12\). The market decision is therefore correct with probability one. That continuum retains the market calculation but washes out the finite-sample uncertainty whose quality the paper set out to compare. Keeping nonzero error probabilities requires retaining finite signal counts and their fluctuations.

The proposed price-computation problem avoids that collapse by taking an arbitrary realized distribution over \((q,y)\) as input. That is a coherent model: competence-and-signal classes are complete types, large crowds with repeated classes are plausible, and market clearing scales naturally by mass. For that reason, I cannot defeat it on type modeling or on the ground that it continues the outcome space instead of the population. Theorem 4 and Lemma 5 do yield a continuous price \(p=\sum_t\mu_t b_t\), with type-level best responses. That is the negative case’s main weakness.

The remaining case is that this is not the kind of computational result ChoCo is meant to mirror. Theorem 4 establishes an identity between two aggregation rules, and Lemma 5 characterizes the equilibrium price. Neither poses or classifies a computational problem such as an intervention, optimization, or robustness task; the paper gives no complexity result. Turning the identity into “given \(\mu\), compute the price and investments” is legitimate, but it promotes the paper’s closed-form characterization into a new computational query. A tractable answer alone is not an objection; the concern is that there is no computational-complexity gap here for continuization to address.

A more faithful continuation of the paper’s truth-tracking question thus loses its finite-population uncertainty, while the proposed equilibrium-computation continuation preserves the price calculation but sets that question aside. This is a case against the mirror’s value to ChoCo, not a demonstration that the continuous market model is incoherent. If the programme counts any author-recognizable equilibrium computation as a worthwhile mirror, Theorem 4 survives the objection.
