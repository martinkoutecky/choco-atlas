What the mirror covers

No mirror covers Theorem 1.1's mechanism-distortion optimality or Theorem 1.2's rigidity characterization; the remaining results are structural mechanism-design results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population setting is large-scale facility location: residents have ideal points in \(\mathbb R^d\), many share one of \(\tau\) locations, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction at location \(t\). A facility location \(y\) has population cost \(\sum_t \mu_t\lVert t-y\rVert\). That is a sensible high-multiplicity description of a society.

But it does not give this paper a qualifying anchor. The paper contains no named computational-complexity result of the kind this task requires. Theorem 1.1 proves that the coordinate-wise median achieves the best worst-case approximation ratio among a class of mechanisms. Theorem 1.2 characterizes mechanisms under bounded influence. Neither asserts that an input problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified; the paper studies mechanisms as functions, not algorithms on encoded instances.

There is also a strategic mismatch in the tempting distributional mirror. If residents are infinitesimal, one person’s report does not change \(\mu\), making individual strategyproofness vacuous. Letting an entire positive-mass type deviate instead changes the strategic unit to a coalition, a condition the paper does not establish. Keeping finite agents with repeated locations preserves the original incentives, but then the paper still provides no computational anchor for a continuous mirror.

So I cannot honestly state a prover-ready continuous problem *per anchor*: there are zero qualifying anchors. The continuous facility-location population is a plausible adjacent model, but treating it as a mirror of one of this paper’s named computational results would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that the paper offers no computational complexity result to mirror. Theorem 1.1 proves a worst-case distortion guarantee; Theorem 1.2 characterizes the form of certain mechanisms. Neither classifies an encoded problem by running time or complexity. Here “approximation ratio” measures social cost, not the performance of an algorithm. The input population remains finite, even though reports and outcomes lie in \(\mathbb R^d\); that continuous space is not a continuous society.

The strongest candidate mirror is large-scale facility location with a distribution \(\mu\) over residents’ ideal points and cost \(\int \lVert x-y\rVert\,d\mu(x)\). For an atomless society, one resident’s report does not change \(\mu\), so individual strategyproofness imposes no constraint. The mechanism-design tradeoff in Theorem 1.1 disappears. Treating a positive-mass type as the strategic player preserves influence, but changes the unit of strategy to a bloc and gives a different incentive problem.

There is a more careful repair: retain a population-size parameter \(N\), give each individual mass \(1/N\), and let a report change that mass between types. Fractional type masses could then provide a meaningful relaxation with nonvacuous individual incentives. That may be a worthwhile new distortion or characterization question. It still would not mirror a computational result in this paper, and it would need to define the fractional-mass mechanism class and its strategyproofness conditions from scratch.

So the proponent’s large-population scenario establishes that facility-location societies can have a sensible high-multiplicity regime; it does not supply a qualifying anchor. The negative case is strongest under ChoCo’s stated computational remit. If “worthwhile” includes new, noncomputational mechanism-design theory, the universal claim is weaker: the fractional-mass extension could be interesting, but it would be a new project rather than a continuous mirror of this paper’s contribution.
