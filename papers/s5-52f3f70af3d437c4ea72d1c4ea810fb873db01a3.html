The continuous mirror question

Given finite variables \(V\), domains, a DAG \(G\), rational masses \(\mu\) over finitely many UCP-network types sharing the qualitative skeleton, and a partial assignment \(z\), find a completion \(v^\star\in\operatorname{Comp}(z)\) maximizing per-capita welfare \(\bar u_\mu(v)=\sum_t\mu_tu_t(v)\).

The model it lives in

A finite set of recurring UCP preference types \(t\) carries population masses \(\mu_t\); the shared decision variable is a discrete public configuration \(v\), and the objective is maximizing aggregate per-capita utility \(\bar u_\mu(v)\) under the partial assignment \(z\).

The objection that survived

Under the common qualitative skeleton, every type receives the same forward-sweep completion, so \(\mu\) cannot affect the selected outcome; allowing conflicting local orders removes Proposition 4's premise.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers Proposition 4 and gives aggregate counterparts of Propositions 2 and 3; it leaves the paper's dominance-query calculation, GAI variable-elimination discussion, action optimization under Bayes nets, and elicitation procedure outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can aggregate optimization remain tractable when types have conflicting local qualitative orders?
  • What is the complexity of optimizing the induced GAI utility when averaging destroys the UCP dominance structure?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a population-level welfare version of the paper’s outcome-optimization problem. It is a genuine continuous-population mirror, though not a hardness-dissolution result: this paper contains no named NP-hardness, coNP-hardness, or completeness theorem. Its relevant named results are algorithmic propositions proved in the paper.

Take a large population choosing one common public configuration: for example, a municipality selecting a standard service package, or a public website selecting one common content configuration. The variables \(V=\{X_1,\ldots,X_n\}\), their finite domains, and a DAG \(G\) are shared. A type \(t\) is a complete UCP-network specification: its local factors \(f_X^t(X,U_X)\), tradeoff weights, local value functions, and tie-breaking conventions. Types agree on the qualitative ordering of values in each local parent context, but may differ substantially in quantitative utilities. The society is \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users of type \(t\), with \(\tau=|T|\ll N\), the actual number of users. Thus \(N\) may be millions while the number of recurring preference types is modest.

The lead problem is:

\[ \textbf{Population-UCP-Optimize}_{\infty}. \]

An instance consists of \(V\), \(G\), rational type masses \(\mu\), one UCP utility \(u_t\) for each type, and a partial assignment \(z\). Every member of the population receives the same complete outcome \(v\in\operatorname{Comp}(z)\). Find

\[ v^\star\in\arg\max_{v\in\operatorname{Comp}(z)} \bar u_\mu(v), \qquad \bar u_\mu(v)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t u_t(v). \]

A solution is the discrete outcome \(v^\star\), together with its exact aggregate utility if desired. No outcome is fractional: the continuum is solely the population distribution.

This directly mirrors the paper’s central optimization problem. Proposition 4, proved in this paper, states that the forward-sweep procedure constructs

\[ \arg\max\{u(v):v\in\operatorname{Comp}(z)\}. \]

Specifically, after fixing \(z\), take a topological ordering of the remaining variables and assign each variable its most preferred value given the already assigned parent values. The resulting completion is optimal.

For the population problem, form the averaged local factors

\[ \bar f_X(x,u)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t f_X^t(x,u). \]

Then

\[ \bar u_\mu(v)=\sum_X \bar f_X(v_X,v_{U_X}). \]

Under the stated common qualitative skeleton, the UCP dominance inequalities remain valid after taking these nonnegative weighted sums. Hence the aggregate utility is itself a UCP-network on \(G\), and Proposition 4’s forward sweep solves \(\textbf{Population-UCP-Optimize}_{\infty}\). With explicitly represented local tables, the algorithm is polynomial in \(\tau\), the table sizes, and the bit-length of the rational masses.

I would classify this mirror as Class A. Its finite analogue with \(n_t\) agents is obtained by setting \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), so the continuous formulation is also the high-multiplicity version of the same utilitarian choice problem. The population mass matters: changing \(\mu\) can change the socially optimal configuration, even though each individual outcome remains integral.

A useful supporting mirror is:

\[ \textbf{Population-UCP-Validity}_{\infty}. \]

Given \(G\), rational masses \(\mu\), and type-specific factor tables, form \(\bar f_X\) and ask whether \(G\) is a valid UCP-network for \(\bar u_\mu\). A solution is either a valid certificate or a violating local configuration.

This mirrors Proposition 2, proved here. For a variable \(X\) with parents \(U\), children \(Y_1,\ldots,Y_k\), and the paper’s associated \(Z_i,U_i\), \(X\) dominates its children when, for every relevant \(u,x_1,x_2,z,y_1,\ldots,y_k\),

\[ f_X(x_1,u)-f_X(x_2,u) \ge \sum_i \left( f_{Y_i}(y_i,x_2,u_i,z_i) - f_{Y_i}(y_i,x_1,u_i,z_i) \right) \]

whenever \(f_X(x_1,u)\ge f_X(x_2,u)\). Proposition 2 says that \(G\) is a UCP-network if and only if every variable dominates its children. The population version asks exactly the same structural question for the aggregate society utility. With explicitly tabulated factors and rational masses, it can be checked by local rational inequalities; with compact implicit tables, its complexity depends on the table representation.

A second supporting problem is:

\[ \textbf{Population-UCP-Minspan-Certificate}_{\infty}. \]

Given the same population instance, compute for every \(X\)

\[ \operatorname{Minspan}(x_1,x_2) = \min_{u\in\operatorname{Dom}(U)} \left| \bar f_X(x_1,u)-\bar f_X(x_2,u) \right|, \]

take the minimum over \(x_1,x_2\) to obtain \(\operatorname{Minspan}(X)\), and define \(\operatorname{Maxspan}(X)\) analogously with a maximum. Output a certificate whenever

\[ \operatorname{Minspan}(X) \ge \sum_{Y_i\text{ child of }X}\operatorname{Maxspan}(Y_i) \]

holds for every \(X\).

This is Proposition 3, also proved here. The paper states that the relevant span values can be computed in \(O(|\operatorname{Dom}(X)|\,|f_X|)\) time and that the sufficient condition is polynomial in the explicitly represented network parameters. In the population version, averaging the factors adds a polynomial factor in \(\tau\). This certificate problem is weaker than exact validity, but it is a natural continuous diagnostic for when the population aggregate retains the authors’ tractable structure.

The authors should recognise the lead problem as their problem’s population analogue: their object is still a UCP utility over discrete outcomes, and the task is still optimization under a partial assignment. The only change is that the utility being optimized is the per-capita welfare of a high-multiplicity society. The paper’s additive-factor semantics makes this extension especially natural.

The weakest point is that the paper studies a single decision maker, not collective choice. The utilitarian aggregation layer is therefore a genuine extension, rather than a literal restatement. Also, \(\mu\) is an input distribution rather than an optimized mass-transfer variable, so this is a cleaner high-multiplicity mirror than a bribery-style continuum problem. The case nevertheless survives because the population is the continuous object, the outcome remains discrete, the regime has a credible many-agents/few-types interpretation, and Proposition 4 supplies an exact algorithm for the resulting aggregate problem.

The main follow-up question is what happens when types disagree on the qualitative UCP skeleton. Then averaging can destroy the UCP property, making \(\textbf{Population-UCP-Validity}_{\infty}\) the relevant boundary problem. That is precisely where a broader continuum-specific complexity question could begin.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the proposed lead mirror is degenerate under exactly the condition used to justify it.

For \(\textbf{Population-UCP-Optimize}_{\infty}\), all types share the same qualitative local ordering and DAG. Proposition 4’s forward sweep therefore selects the same value at every variable for every type. Consequently,

\[ \arg\max_v u_t(v) \]

is identical for all \(t\), and hence also for

\[ \bar u_\mu(v)=\sum_t\mu_tu_t(v). \]

The masses may change the numerical welfare of the chosen configuration, but they cannot change the configuration itself. This is not a meaningful collective-choice problem: the population is unanimous in the only sense that matters to optimization. The continuous society has become a weighted record of cardinal intensities, not a computational object whose composition affects the decision.

The obvious repair is to allow types to disagree about local orderings. That makes \(\mu\) consequential, but it removes the premise carrying Proposition 4. The aggregate utility need not be a UCP-network, so the forward-sweep theorem no longer applies. If the aggregate happens still to satisfy the UCP conditions, the problem reduces to first computing one synthetic utility \(\bar u_\mu\) and then applying the original single-agent theorem. Thus the proposed anchor has a dichotomy: either the common skeleton is retained and the population is decision-theoretically inert, or heterogeneity is admitted and the named result no longer supplies the algorithm.

The same objection defeats \(\textbf{Population-UCP-Validity}_{\infty}\). Proposition 2 is a test on one utility function and one graph. Computing \(\bar f_X\) and applying the proposition merely replaces a list of tables by their weighted sum. With a common qualitative skeleton, validity is inherited and the test is vacuous; with conflicting types, it is a representation test for the synthetic utility, not a computational question about a continuous society.

\(\textbf{Population-UCP-Minspan-Certificate}_{\infty}\) is weaker still. Its span inequalities are simply Proposition 3 applied after averaging. They may vary with \(\mu\), but they certify a property of \(\bar u_\mu\), not a population-level intervention, robustness question, or optimization over mass. The continuum contributes only coefficients to a finite table calculation.

This does not establish the universal negative claim. A stronger mirror would let recurring population types have genuinely different local rankings, define a socially calibrated aggregate objective, and study when mixtures preserve UCP structure or instead induce a higher-treewidth GAI problem. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity question, and the paper’s discussion of GAI optimization gives it a plausible connection to the authors’ work. The honest conclusion is therefore that the negative case defeats the proponent’s specific anchors, but cannot credibly show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
