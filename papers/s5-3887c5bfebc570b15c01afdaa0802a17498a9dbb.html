The continuous mirror question

Given finite client and provider type sets with rational masses, per-agent loads and capacities, eligibility, client rankings of provider types, and a common provider priority order, find a rational aggregate assignment satisfying capacities and having no blocking type pair. Is this search problem solvable in time polynomial in the type counts, eligibility size, and input bit length?

The model it lives in

A society is represented by client and provider type distributions μ and ν. Each type includes its relevant preferences and per-agent load or capacity; variables x_{ij} give the total load assigned between eligible types, with stability defined by whether each type pair has remaining mutually acceptable load or capacity.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 7’s fractional stable-allocation objective under common provider priorities. It leaves aside the distributed round bound, individual strategyproofness, and the fair tie-breaking results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a stable aggregate assignment always be found in time polynomial in the support size, eligibility size, and input bit length?
  • Can a tagged-agent or finite-population lift preserve the paper’s individual incentive guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a type-continuum version of the paper’s fractional capacitated stable matching problem. The paper already moves from one-to-one matching to divisible client loads and provider capacities; replacing repeated, interchangeable clients and providers with masses is a natural next step. The continuity here is in the population as well as the assignment: fractional loads are already part of the paper, but they do not by themselves make the population continuous.

The anchor is Theorem 7, proved in this paper. It states that when providers have common preferences, there is a deterministic CONGEST distributed mechanism for fractional capacitated stable matching that is incentive-compatible for clients and runs in \(O(S\Delta_H+\log^* n)\) rounds. Here \(S\) is the number of provider preference classes and \(\Delta_H\) is the maximum degree of the clients’ conflict graph. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result; the paper gives no comparable named NP-hardness result for this problem.

Lead mirror: Type-Continuum Fractional Stable Matching. Consider finite client and provider type sets \(I\) and \(J\), with rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\). A client type includes its per-client load \(\ell_i\), ranking \(\succ_i\) of eligible provider types, and other relevant attributes; a provider type includes its per-provider capacity \(q_j\). Eligibility is given by \(E\subseteq I\times J\). Providers use a common priority order \(\succ_P\) over client types, restricted to the types each can serve. The regime is, for example, a large service network handling millions of small, divisible jobs but only hundreds of recurring job and provider profiles. Two jobs belong to the same type only when the model treats them as identical in all relevant respects.

For each eligible pair, the decision variable \(x_{ij}\geq 0\) is the total load assigned from client type \(i\) to provider type \(j\). Write \(d_i=\mu_i\ell_i\) for total client load and \(u_j=\nu_jq_j\) for total provider capacity. A feasible output satisfies
\[ \sum_{j:(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\leq d_i,\qquad \sum_{i:(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}\leq u_j. \]
The task is to return a rational feasible flow with no blocking type pair. Precisely, for eligible \((i,j)\), let
\[ A_{ij}(x)=d_i-\sum_qx_{iq}+\sum_{q:\,j\succ_i q}x_{iq} \]
be the load of type \(i\) that is unassigned or currently sent to a less-preferred provider, and let
\[ B_{ij}(x)=u_j-\sum_kx_{kj}+\sum_{k:\,i\succ_P k}x_{kj} \]
be provider type \(j\)’s unused capacity or capacity used by lower-priority client types. Stability requires \(A_{ij}(x)=0\) or \(B_{ij}(x)=0\) for every eligible pair. The computational question is whether such an \(x\) can be found in time polynomial in the type counts, \(|E|\), and the input bit length. This is a search problem: its objective is to produce a stable allocation, rather than optimize an artificial cardinal utility.

I expect this mirror to be tractable in the finite-support regime, plausibly by a type-level version of the paper’s greedy batch-proposal algorithm. Theorem 7 does not establish that support-size bound: its running time is in distributed rounds on an agent graph. The mirror asks the new question of whether those repeated agents can be compressed to their masses and types.

The scope is specifically Theorem 7’s fractional capacitated matching result. The paper’s Theorem 5, citing Floréen et al. [16], says that computing stable matching in the LOCAL model requires \(\Omega(n)\) rounds even when clients and providers have maximum degree 2. I would not make that a second mirror: the lower bound depends on long-range graph structure, which a distribution over types and a type-compatibility relation do not retain. The paper’s discussion of master preference lists [20] supports the plausibility of the preference regime, but it is not a collision; the population can still be the new continuous object.

The weakest point is that this mirror compresses away two features central to Theorem 7: network locality and individual truthfulness. In a mass-flow input, a single agent has zero mass, so a direct individual incentive claim needs a careful tagged-agent or finite-population lift; claiming truthfulness for an entire type cohort would change the strategic unit. I would keep the main question focused on stable-flow computation and treat a strategyproof lift as a follow-on. That limitation weakens the mirror of the *mechanism*, but the underlying stable-allocation question remains close to the paper’s own divisible-workload model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 7 is the proponent’s strongest anchor: it is a genuine algorithmic result, and the paper itself motivates fractional loads as divisible work. Neither the presence of fractional assignments nor the existence of high-multiplicity matching work is an objection. The problem is that the theorem’s computational content is specifically a distributed mechanism: clients and providers communicate over an individual graph, and the running time counts synchronous rounds. A type-level flow with masses \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) and compatibility relation \(E\) has no such communication process. It asks for a centralized stable allocation.

The better version of the mirror would preserve the graph. But a client’s full type would then have to record its feasible provider neighborhood, as well as its preferences and load. For arbitrary sparse graphs, that can leave as many types as clients; the mass description has not compressed the instance. If types instead describe recurring job and provider profiles, with every compatible pair connected, the proposed flow is coherent and may fit large service markets. It also replaces the paper’s graph with a complete type-level compatibility model. The paper’s round bound and its coloring step do not transfer to that model. A continuum graph or edge-density model might retain more structure, but it would need to encode adjacency patterns and local bottlenecks—the features that drive the distributed computation—rather than just population masses.

There is a related loss in the mechanism guarantee. In a nonatomic mass model, one client’s report changes zero mass, so individual truthfulness is vacuous if the output is only an aggregate flow. Treating an entire type cohort as the strategic player changes the theorem’s incentive claim. A tagged-client construction could restore an individual outcome and deviation, but it adds an atom to the continuum model; it still does not supply the local communication problem from Theorem 7.

So the negative case is that the proposed mirror either drops the theorem’s defining distributed and strategic content, or retains that content only by carrying graph-level detail that may erase the finite-support advantage. That is a serious mismatch, but not a decisive case against every mirror. A type-level stable-allocation question for repeated, divisible workloads is plausible and close to the paper’s subject; the authors may well recognize it as a worthwhile extension. My case against is therefore weaker than the proponent’s case for.
