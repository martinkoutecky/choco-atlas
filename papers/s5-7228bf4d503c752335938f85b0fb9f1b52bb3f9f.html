The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying positive case under the required anchor rule. The paper has no named computational result: it gives no complexity classification, algorithmic result, or computational formulation that it uses to solve an underlying problem.

Its named results are Proposition 1, proved here, which says that under fixed order every subgame-perfect equilibrium reaches agent \(a\)’s preferred alternative \(\alpha\) at stage one, regardless of patience; and Proposition 2, also proved here, which says that under switching order the most patient agent’s preferred alternative is reached at stage one. These are equilibrium-characterization results, not computational anchors.

The weakest possible positive analogy would be a distribution over many independent bilateral conclaves, with types describing the participants’ preferences, deadlines, and voting order, and mass representing the share of conclaves of each type. One could ask for the aggregate shares receiving each first-stage outcome. But that continuizes a population of *conclaves*, not the population of voters in the paper; the propositions would yield those shares by direct aggregation. A continuum of voters in a single sequential-unanimity game would change the strategic structure the propositions analyze.

So I cannot give a prover-ready continuous computational problem tied to a qualifying anchor without overstating the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that neither Proposition 1 nor Proposition 2 is a computational result. Proposition 1 characterizes the subgame-perfect equilibrium outcome under fixed order; Proposition 2 does the same under switching order. Both are statements about a game with two named, strategically distinct voters and infinitely many possible stages. The paper gives no complexity question, algorithm, or computational formulation to continuize. The unbounded dimension is time, which is outside ChoCo’s population scope.

The best aggregate interpretation is a large collection of independent two-voter conclaves. A type would need to describe a whole conclave: its participants’ preferences and deadlines, plus its order scheme. A distribution over those types could represent the share of conclaves with each first-stage outcome. That is coherent, but it continuizes a population of games, not the population voting within one society. The propositions characterize each game; asking for aggregate outcome shares is a new question layered on top of them. For Proposition 2 in particular, voter marginals alone would not suffice: which deadline belongs to which preferred alternative within each pair matters, so the type must retain the pairing.

The more ambitious version would add interventions: for example, find the least-cost mass of conclaves whose preferences, deadlines, or order can be changed to shift the aggregate outcome. That could produce a continuous optimization problem. But its costs, permitted interventions, and aggregate target would all be new ingredients. The paper itself names bribing as a possible extension; it does not establish a computational result about bribery that this mirror could carry over.

Putting a continuum of voters inside one conclave is a poorer fit. The propositions depend on who moves first and on comparing the patience of two particular voters. With a continuum, an ordering with a first mover needs to be specified, and replacing individuals with mass changes the strategic unit and unanimity condition. One could define a game among blocs, but that would be a new game rather than a population limit of the two-agent result.

So the negative case is strong for this paper as a source of computational anchors, but I cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous research could grow from it. A study of interventions across many independent councils might be worthwhile. The case against is that such a study would supply the computational problem and the population model itself; neither is a continuous mirror of the results this paper proves.
