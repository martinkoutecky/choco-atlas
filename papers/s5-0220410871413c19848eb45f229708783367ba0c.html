The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of student types \(T\), projects \(P\), lecturer groups \(\Lambda\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\), weak preference orders, and rational project and lecturer capacity shares, decide whether there is a mass assignment \(x_{t,p}\geq 0\) and unmatched mass \(u_t\geq 0\) with \(\sum_p x_{t,p}+u_t=\mu_t\) that has no positive-mass blocking exchange under the mass analogue of Definition 3, including its project and lecturer capacity cases and same-lecturer exception. If one exists, construct it; determine the complexity in the support size and input bit length without expanding the masses into individual students.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic student cohort represented by finitely many complete preference and priority types, with \(x_{t,p}\) denoting the mass of type \(t\) assigned to project \(p\). Individuals still receive at most one whole project; project and lecturer capacities are rational cohort shares, and stability forbids the paper's strong blocking exchanges by positive mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's existence decision and construction of a strongly stable solution; it leaves the theorem's student-optimal guarantee for split type mass to be defined and proved, and does not address the paper's other stability variants.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can existence of a strongly stable mass assignment be decided in time polynomial in the type support, project and lecturer counts, and rational input bit length?
  • Whenever stable mass assignments exist, is there one whose assignment distribution for each type weakly dominates every other stable distribution under that type's preference order?
  • When masses and capacities come from a finite high-multiplicity instance, when does a stable mass assignment correspond to a discrete strongly stable matching?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Mass-SPA-ST-Strong, the continuous-population version of the paper’s problem of finding a strongly stable matching in SPA-ST.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for an SPA-ST instance \(I\), Algorithm SPA-ST-strong determines in \(O(m^2)\) time whether a strongly stable matching exists, where \(m\) is the total length of the students’ preference lists. If one exists, every execution finds a student-optimal strongly stable matching: each assigned student gets a project at least as good as the one she could receive in any strongly stable matching, and each unassigned student is unassigned in every strongly stable matching. The paper mentions NP-hardness of finding a maximum-size weakly stable matching in its discussion of prior work, but that is not a numbered result proved here, and it concerns a different objective and stability notion. Theorem 1 is therefore my only anchor.

Here is the mirror. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete student types, each with a weak order over acceptable projects; \(P\) a finite set of projects; and \(\Lambda\) a finite set of lecturer archetypes, each offering a subset \(P_\ell\) and having a weak order over student types. Group students together only if their project preferences and their rank under every lecturer archetype agree. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Project and lecturer capacities \(c_p\) and \(d_\ell\) are rational shares of the cohort.

A proposed solution is a mass assignment \(x_{t,p}\geq 0\) and unmatched mass \(u_t\geq 0\), satisfying
\[ \sum_{p\in A_t}x_{t,p}+u_t=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{t,p}\leq c_p,\qquad \sum_{p\in P_\ell}\sum_{t\in T}x_{t,p}\leq d_\ell. \]
Each individual still receives at most one project. A type’s mass may be split across projects because different members of that type can receive different projects; no individual receives a fractional project.

Call such an assignment strongly stable if it has no positive-mass blocking exchange following Definition 3 of the paper. In detail, a block chooses some \((t,p)\) and a positive amount \(\epsilon\) of type-\(t\) mass that is unassigned or strictly prefers \(p\) to its current project, or is indifferent to its current project in the second part of the strong-stability definition. That mass enters \(p\). If \(p\) or its lecturer is full, the exchange displaces the corresponding \(\epsilon\)-mass from the least-preferred occupied tier at that project or lecturer, exactly as in the paper’s three capacity cases. For a strictly improving student move, the lecturer must accept without being worse off; for a student-indifferent move, the lecturer must strictly improve. If the entrant is already assigned to that lecturer, use the paper’s same-lecturer exception. Thus fullness means equality with the mass capacity, and “worst students” becomes the least-preferred occupied type tier. The problem asks: given \((T,\mu,P,\Lambda)\), preferences, and capacities, determine whether a strongly stable mass assignment exists; if it does, construct one that is student-optimal in the typewise sense that its assignment distribution weakly dominates every other strongly stable distribution for each type’s preference order.

A plausible regime is a large, centralized practicum or project-placement service across campuses. Many students choose among a fixed catalogue of projects, and standardized academic profiles and preference lists produce relatively few complete types compared with the cohort size—for example, tens or hundreds of types among many thousands of students. Lecturer archetypes represent repeated supervisors or departments with the same project menus, capacity shares, and priority tiers over those profiles. The scenario is conditional on that repetition: if every student has a distinct preference or priority profile, the support grows with the population and the high-multiplicity case is no longer the relevant regime.

I expect Mass-SPA-ST-Strong to be Class A, likely polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|P|\), \(|\Lambda|\), and the bit length \(L\) of the rational input. The anchor makes the underlying stability question central and computational, and the mirror preserves its objects and blocking logic. The continuous work would be to replace individual counts by mass while retaining the lecturer and project capacity interactions—potentially through a fractional matching or optimization formulation. This is a prediction, not a consequence of Theorem 1: its \(O(m^2)\) algorithm operates on an explicit finite instance, and expanding binary-encoded mass denominators into individual students could take time depending on the population size.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s existence test and student-optimal output for strong stability; it does not try to cover every variant or result mentioned in the paper. It also raises useful follow-up questions: does a typewise student-optimal continuous assignment always exist when any strongly stable mass assignment exists? Can one find it in time polynomial in the support size and bit length, without expanding the cohort? And when masses and capacities come from a finite high-multiplicity instance, when does a continuous solution correspond to a discrete strongly stable matching?

The weakest point is the passage from an algorithm on individual students to an algorithm on rational masses. Strong stability depends on ties and on which preference tier occupies the capacity boundary, so fractional assignments could create behavior that the finite algorithm does not capture. That gap needs a real theorem, especially for the student-optimality claim. The case still seems worth making because the gap is precisely about compressing repeated agents and their capacities; it does not arise from replacing the paper’s matching question with fractional outcomes or a different stability notion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only strong anchor is Theorem 1: deciding whether a strongly stable SPA-ST matching exists, and finding a student-optimal one, in \(O(m^2)\) time. The best objection is that the proposed compression works only when complete student types repeat—including each student’s project ranking and priority tier under every lecturer. In many actual placements, individualized lecturer rankings or tie-breaks make nearly every student a different type, so \(\tau\) grows with the cohort. Keeping \(\tau\) small may require replacing named lecturers and projects with archetypes or categories, which risks changing the project-specific capacity and blocking structure the theorem is about.

That is a real limitation on the proposed practicum scenario, but it does not defeat its better version. A large placement system with standardized project lists, coarse priority tiers, and capacities modeled as cohort shares is plausible. The programme explicitly permits that capacity co-scaling, and the resulting type-mass assignment preserves a computational question: can one decide existence and construct a strongly stable assignment from rational masses without expanding them into individuals? The paper’s theorem does not answer it.

Fractional strong stability and strong ex ante stability in matching are established ideas: for example, Neme and Oviedo characterize strongly stable fractional matchings in a many-to-one setting, and Kesten and Ünver study strongly ex ante stable school-choice lotteries ([Neme and Oviedo](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12500), [Kesten and Ünver](https://doi.org/10.3982/TE1558)). That weakens any claim that the stability concept itself is new, but those treatments do not settle the type-support complexity question for this multi-project SPA model.

So the honest negative case is narrow: the mirror depends on a restrictive, explicitly modeled regime, and the student-optimal claim needs a careful typewise definition when mass is split. Neither point rules out a worthwhile mirror of the existence problem. I cannot make a convincing case that no scenario or better formulation survives.
