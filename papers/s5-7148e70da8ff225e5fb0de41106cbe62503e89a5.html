What the mirror covers

Theorem 8.1 gives probability identities for several voting-power indices; the paper does not analyze the complexity of evaluating those indices or give an algorithmic result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for power of constituencies or shareholder blocs in a large representative institution. Many citizens can be grouped into a smaller set of cohorts that share the voting attributes relevant to the rule. A type records a cohort’s population share, voting weight, available actions, and action distribution; its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the represented population in that cohort. This fits the paper’s examples of representatives whose voting weights reflect unequal populations.

A precise candidate mirror is Type-Mass Banzhaf Evaluation. An instance gives a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita weights \(a_t\), a quota \(q\), and independent finite-support rational action distributions \(\nu_t\) over each bloc’s vote \(x_t\). The outcome is pass exactly when
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t a_t x_t\ge q. \]
For a distinguished type \(t\), compute exactly
\[ \Delta_t=\Pr(\mathrm{pass}\mid x_t=1)-\Pr(\mathrm{pass}\mid x_t=0), \]
where the other blocs’ actions follow their stated distributions. The answer is the exact rational value of \(\Delta_t\). This asks about the influence of a voting bloc whose represented population is a mass, while preserving the paper’s central idea of measuring power through changes in outcome probability.

The closest named result is Theorem 8.1, proved in this paper. For an outcome \(O\) and player \(i\), it expresses Shapley–Shubik as \(\Pr(x_i^{O\min})[\Pr(O\mid x_i^{O\max})-\Pr(O\mid x_i^{O\min})]\); Banzhaf and Straffin as \(\Pr(O\mid x_i^{O\max})-\Pr(O\mid x_i^{O\min})\); Johnston as \(\Pr(O)-\Pr(O\mid x_i^{O\min})\); Coleman Initiate Action as \([\Pr(O\mid x_i^{O\max})-\Pr(O)]/[1-\Pr(O)]\); and Coleman Prevent Action as \([\Pr(O)-\Pr(O\mid x_i^{O\min})]/\Pr(O)\). The proposed \(\Delta_t\) mirrors its Banzhaf identity.

There is an important limit to the positive case: Theorem 8.1 is not an eligible computational anchor under the stated rule. It gives probability identities, not a result that an underlying problem is in P, hard, FPT, or another complexity class; the paper gives no such named computational result. So this is a plausible continuous question suggested by the paper, not a continuous problem anchored to one of its qualifying results.

For the candidate question, I would expect discrete hardness to transfer whenever the chosen discrete power-evaluation problem embeds: normalized masses and rescaled weights can reproduce a finite weighted voting game. That expectation would need its own discrete hardness result; this paper supplies none. The mirror’s weakest point is the change from an individual voter’s unilateral influence to a bloc’s influence. In a genuinely atomless population, one person has zero mass and usually cannot change the outcome. The constituency scenario makes the bloc a meaningful decision-maker, but the authors might still regard that as a group-power problem rather than the power of their individual player.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 8.1 is the proposed anchor, but it gives identities between voting-power indices and probabilities. It states no complexity bound, hardness result, or algorithmic problem. The paper also gives no finite input representation for its general voting games, so there is no defined complexity landscape to carry over to a continuous population.

The proposed Type-Mass Banzhaf question doesn’t fix that gap. As stated, it gives each type one vote \(x_t\), with its mass scaling that vote’s weight. The game still has one player per type; the represented citizens are not repeated agents with independently varying actions. Treating those citizens as the players would change the query: Theorem 8.1 measures one player’s unilateral influence, while a population mass represents a bloc acting together. A bloc-power question may be useful, but it is a new computational problem, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

A broader version could ask how changing a mass of citizens by type affects a collective outcome. That may be a worthwhile research question in its own right. But the paper neither formulates nor analyzes it, and adopting the mass model would supply the missing computational problem rather than mirror one. The paper’s allowance for a continuous action space also doesn’t close the gap: its player set remains finite, so that is continuity of actions, not of the population.

This is a strong negative case for a *paper-anchored* mirror. It cannot establish that no related continuous computational question would be valuable; it shows that this paper provides no computational result to anchor one to.
