The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a mirror on. Propositions 1–5 are proved here, but they concern equilibrium existence and stability, and how equilibrium infection levels change with mixing; none classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The finite-population simulations in Section 6 do not supply such an anchor either.

The closest result is Proposition 4, proved in Appendix A9. It says that, over the range where a positive stable equilibrium exists, average diffusion decreases with mixing when \(\lambda_1\lambda_2\le 1\), tending to zero at the extinction threshold. It increases with mixing if \(\lambda_1^{3/4}(\lambda_2+1)\ge\lambda_2^{3/4}(\lambda_1+1)\), a condition requiring \(\lambda_1\lambda_2>1\) and \(\lambda_2>3\). In the remaining cases it first increases and then decreases, with an interior maximum; its minimum is at \(m=1\) if \(\lambda_1<\sqrt{\lambda_2}/(\lambda_2-\sqrt{\lambda_2}+1)\), and at \(m=0\) otherwise. This is a strong motivation for an algorithmic question, but it is not a qualifying computational anchor.

The most plausible question is Continuous Equilibrium Mixing Design. Consider a large institution with two equally sized groups, each containing many agents who share the same contagion or adoption rate. The two complete types are their respective rate parameters \(0<\lambda_1<\lambda_2\), with \(\lambda_i=p\nu_i/\delta_i\). Their population masses are \(\mu_1=\mu_2=1/2\). Given rational \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\), a planner chooses a between-group interaction rate \(m\in[0,1]\). For each \(m\), let \((\rho_1^*(m),\rho_2^*(m))\) be the stable equilibrium on the positive branch when one exists, and \((0,0)\) otherwise, using the limiting equilibrium as \(m\to0^+\) at the endpoint. The question is to find a global maximizer of average equilibrium adoption, \(\frac12(\rho_1^*(m)+\rho_2^*(m))\), and its equilibrium values. The equilibrium must satisfy
\[ \rho_i=\lambda_i(1-\rho_i)\bigl((1-m)\rho_i+m\rho_j\bigr),\qquad i\ne j. \]
A solution could give the optimizer and equilibrium as exact algebraic numbers, or provide a certified approximation to a requested precision.

This scenario has a credible high-multiplicity interpretation: two large cohorts of agents with shared adoption and recovery parameters, rather than a small committee whose members must be tracked individually. Its type count is two, regardless of population size. The authors would likely recognize the objective and decision variable: Section 5 explicitly discusses choosing mixing to maximize average diffusion. The likely direction is tractability for this two-type problem; Proposition 4 and the appendix’s optimizer calculation already give the case split and an algebraic route. Further questions include the bit complexity of exact optimization, and what changes with many types or constrained mixing patterns.

The weakness is substantial: the paper already models group infection fractions with continuous mean dynamics and analytically studies the planner’s mixing objective. So this candidate is close to an algorithmic reformulation of work the paper has already done, rather than a new discrete problem whose population needs continuizing. And because the paper has no qualifying computational anchor, this cannot meet the prompt’s anchor requirement without relabelling an analytic proposition as a complexity result. The positive case is therefore real but limited: the population regime and computational question are plausible, while the paper itself offers no named complexity result to support the requested mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central problem is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–5 concern equilibrium existence, stability and comparative statics; Section 6 reports simulations. None studies worst-case complexity or an algorithmic problem. That leaves the proposed anchor without the kind of result this programme is meant to continuize.

Proposition 4 is the closest candidate, but it studies how equilibrium diffusion varies with the mixing level. The proposed “Continuous Equilibrium Mixing Design” is already the paper’s own planner question: Section 5 discusses choosing mixing to optimize average diffusion, and Appendix A9 derives the optimizer. The proposed task would package that existing analysis as an algorithm with rational inputs and certified output. The groups’ infection fractions and the mixing parameter are already continuous, so this adds computational framing without making the population continuous as a new modeling move.

A richer version—such as choosing a constrained interaction matrix for many groups—could pose a new computational problem. But its combinatorics would come from the added policy-design constraints and network structure, not from continuizing this paper’s population. Likewise, exact computation for the finite stochastic SIS process would need a new target such as a finite-horizon metric or quasi-stationary behavior; the paper’s positive endemic equilibrium is a mean-dynamic object, while the finite SIS process eventually reaches its infection-free absorbing state.

The negative case is strongest against a *mirror of this paper*, not against every possible research project inspired by it. Two large homogeneous cohorts make high multiplicity sensible, so that is not a sound objection. But the paper already uses a continuous population description, and its central planner objective is analytically treated. Turning it into a complexity question would either restate an existing result or require adding a different computational problem.
