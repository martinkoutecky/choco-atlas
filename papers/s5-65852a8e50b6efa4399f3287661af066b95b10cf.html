What the mirror covers

The candidate question covers the paper’s agenda-sensitivity analysis. The Copeland comparisons, manipulation examples, and discussion of the \(\beta\) coefficient have no separately anchored computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

A plausible population mirror is a group decision over watershed policies, with many affected residents or stakeholders rather than five committee representatives. A type is a complete ranking of the available policies, and \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with ranking \(t\). Given the supported rankings and their rational masses, compute the Veto scores by weighting each type’s contribution to each alternative’s force and weakness by \(\mu_t\). A concrete computational question is: given \(\mu\), two alternatives \(a,b\), and an alternative \(z\), does deleting \(z\) reverse the Veto ordering of \(a\) and \(b\)? The input is the supported rankings and masses; the answer is yes or no. Directly recomputing the scores before and after deletion appears tractable in \(m,\tau,L\). The paper’s worked examples make this question recognizable as an analysis of the Veto method, and a large population with a modest number of shared ranking types is plausible.

But that is only a candidate model, not an anchored case under your criterion. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result—no result about complexity, tractability, or algorithms to mirror. Its findings about sensitivity to irrelevant alternatives come from worked examples and comparisons of rankings, including Tables 9–15 and Example 1. Those support the proposed question informally, but they are not eligible anchors. So I cannot honestly present a named-result-based positive case here.

The weak point in the candidate mirror is substantial: the paper studies a small committee of representatives, while the population scenario treats individuals as equally weighted decision makers. Also, evaluating Veto on a supplied distribution looks like straightforward aggregation; the paper does not establish that making the population continuous creates a new computational challenge.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is decisive under the paper’s stated anchor test: it gives no numbered computational result to mirror. Its findings are comparisons of rankings, examples of agenda sensitivity, and conclusions about the Veto method’s properties. There is no theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about the complexity or algorithms for a computational problem. The proposed deletion test therefore has no eligible result behind it. That is not a claim that the question is uninteresting; it means it is a new computational question prompted by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its results.

The natural large-population version is coherent. Give each voter a ranking type \(t\), with mass \(\mu_t\), and let \(g_i(t;A)\) be that type’s contribution to alternative \(i\)’s Veto score on agenda \(A\). Then the score is \(\alpha_i(\mu;A)=\sum_t \mu_t g_i(t;A)\). For a finite electorate with \(n_t\) voters of each type and \(N\) voters total, the original score is \(N\alpha_i(\mu;A)\), where \(\mu_t=n_t/N\). So the population distribution simply supplies weights to the same calculation. The proposed question—whether deleting \(z\) reverses the ordering of \(a\) and \(b\)—changes the agenda while holding that society fixed. It is an agenda-sensitivity question, not a computational question created by continuizing the population. Recomputing the scores over the supported types may be easy, but ease is not the objection; the missing anchor and the unchanged object of study are.

A stronger candidate would ask for the minimum mass of voters whose rankings must change to make a target alternative win. That puts mass directly into a computational problem, and may be worth studying. But it adds a voter intervention, a cost model, and a target-winner objective that this paper does not define. The paper’s manipulation concern is that whoever selects the alternatives can alter the outcome. Changing voters’ reports is a different intervention. Its continuous version could be a ChoCo project in its own right, but it is not a mirror of the paper’s reported result.

This supports a strong negative case for an anchored mirror of this paper. It does not prove the broader universal claim that no worthwhile continuous problem could be developed from the Veto method: a new mass-manipulation or robustness problem might be valuable. The honest limit of the case is that the paper itself supplies no computational result for such a mirror, and its central agenda-sensitivity findings do not become population-computational merely by weighting voter rankings.
