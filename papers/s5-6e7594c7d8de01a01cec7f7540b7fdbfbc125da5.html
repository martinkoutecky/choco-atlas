The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest honest case is an analytic bridge to a continuous population model, but it does not meet the required anchor test: the paper contains no numbered result classifying a computational problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or similar. The introduction says computational aspects are outside its scope. Its references to MAX-CUT hardness are background and motivation, not complexity results proved here.

The closest mirror I can make precise is this. Fix \(k\) candidates and let the types be the \(k!\) complete rankings. In a large, exchangeable electorate—say, millions of voters with a fixed number of possible rankings—the type counts have high multiplicity. Under the paper’s impartial-culture assumption, the type proportions fluctuate around \(\mu_t=1/k!\). Center and normalize those proportions; in the large-population limit they give a Gaussian vector \(X\) on the sum-zero subspace of type frequencies. Independent vote corruption gives a correlated vector \(Y\), with correlation \(\rho=1-\varepsilon\).

A continuous decision rule is a measurable partition \((\Omega_1,\ldots,\Omega_k)\) of that Gaussian space: choose winner \(i\) when the fluctuation vector lies in \(\Omega_i\). Require each cell to have Gaussian measure \(1/k\), and impose CLC by forbidding a cell for candidate \(j\) from containing profiles where every other candidate defeats \(j\ head-to-head. The objective is to maximize \[ \sum_{i=1}^k \Pr(X\in\Omega_i,\;Y\in\Omega_i), \] the probability that corruption leaves the winner unchanged. This is a plausible population-limit formulation of the paper’s ranked-choice stability question: types are rankings, mass is the fraction of voters of each ranking, and the decision variable is the winner rule. The nearest numbered results are not eligible anchors. **Theorem 4.6, “Dimension Reduction” (proved here),** says that any CLC-respecting partition maximizing Problem 4.4 can, after rotation, be represented using only k!+k-1\) dimensions. Theorem 1.5 (proved here) says that if the Plurality-is-Stablest Conjecture holds at a given corruption probability, then the Borda-is-Stablest Conjecture holds at that probability. Both concern analytic structure or stability, not computational complexity. Theorem 1.6’s low-dimensional statement and the cited results in Section 3 do not supply a complexity classification either.

So I would hand the Gaussian-partition problem to an analytic prover, but I cannot honestly assign it ChoCo’s tractable / hardness-transfers / continuum-specifically-hard classification. As stated, it has no finite encoded input or algorithmic task, and the paper’s results do not give one. Turning it into “approximate the optimum to within \(\delta\)” would require choosing an input representation and approximation model that the paper does not study.

The weakness is substantial: the model captures the continuous fluctuations of type masses near the uniform society, but its main question is noise stability, and the paper already formulates that question as a Gaussian partition problem. It does not study computation over a supplied society distribution \(\mu\). Thus it is a credible population-limit connection, but not a positive computational mirror anchored by this paper’s named results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchor test: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. It explicitly says computational aspects are outside its scope. Theorem 1.5 transfers one conjectured noise-stability bound to another; Theorems 1.6 and 4.6 give low-dimensional structure. None classifies an algorithmic problem by tractability, hardness, or approximation. Under ChoCo’s stated standard, that is decisive: there is no computational result here for a continuous mirror to re-express.

The high-multiplicity setting itself is quite plausible. With \(k\) candidates, voters have one of \(k!\) ranking types, and impartial culture gives each type mass \(1/k!\). But the nontrivial continuous limit in the paper is not a supplied society distribution. If \(\hat\mu_n\) is the vector of empirical ranking proportions, then \(\hat\mu_n\) converges to the uniform vector \(u\). The Gaussian object comes from the rescaled fluctuations \(\sqrt n(\hat\mu_n-u)\). At the population-mass scale, independent corruption sends \(\mu\) to expected mass \((1-\varepsilon)\mu+\varepsilon u\); the random fluctuations that make noise stability interesting disappear in the limit. The Gaussian partitions in Problem 4.4 and Theorem 4.6 capture those fluctuations, not a continuum of input societies, and the paper studies their analytic structure rather than their computational complexity.

That also defeats the proponent’s two nearest anchors. Theorem 1.5 is a conditional implication between stability conjectures, not an algorithmic claim. Theorem 4.6 can be restated as a dimension bound for an optimizing Gaussian partition, but it gives no encoded input, computational task, or algorithm. A stronger proposed mirror might optimize noise stability over CLC-respecting partitions for a supplied ranking distribution \(\mu\), perhaps asking for a \(\delta\)-approximation. That could be a new research problem, but it adds a computational objective and representation choices the paper does not make; it is not a mirror of a computational result in the paper.

So the negative case is strong for the programme’s actual gate: no worthwhile *computational mirror of this paper’s results* is established. Its weakness is the universal phrase “in any scenario.” One could invent a computational problem around Gaussian partitions or noise-stable rules that the authors might find interesting. The paper gives no basis for ruling out every such project. But that possibility supports a new computational agenda, not a continuous mirror anchored by this paper.
