What the mirror covers

The proposed extension covers only an anonymous high-multiplicity version of the PFBR and strategy-proofness portion of Theorem 2; it leaves arbitrary coalition-indexed ballots, voter-specific coefficients, and Theorem 1's domain characterization untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the anonymous positive-mass-strategy-proof extension admit a finite characterization and an efficient recognition algorithm?
  • Can identity-sensitive coalition-indexed ballots be represented with finitely many role types without destroying the high-multiplicity regime?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: this paper admits a credible continuous-population mirror, but it has no qualifying computational anchor. It contains no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), parameterized hardness, or an algorithmic complexity bound. It is an axiomatic characterization paper. I would therefore record “no computational anchor,” rather than mislabel its structural theorems as complexity results.

The closest substantive anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that if \(\mathbb D\) satisfies minimal richness, diversity, and no-restoration, then \(\mathbb D\) is a \((\underline{k},\overline{k})\)-hybrid domain; moreover, an RSCF \(\varphi:\mathbb D^n\to\Delta(A)\) is unanimous and strategy-proof exactly when it is a probabilistic fixed ballot rule, with the additional constrained-random-dictatorship condition when \(\overline{k}-\underline{k}>1\). In particular, the rule is determined by monotone probabilistic ballots indexed by the coalitions \(S(k,P)\) of voters whose peaks lie at or to the right of \(a_k\). The theorem is proved here, although its proof invokes earlier cited characterization results.

My lead mirror of Theorem 2 would be a continuous hybrid-PFBR problem. Take a fixed ordered alternative set \(a_1\prec\cdots\prec a_m\), a finite set \(\mathcal T\) of complete hybrid preference types, and a society distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal T)\). Thus \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with ranking \(t\). For each \(k\), define the cumulative peak mass

\[ q_k(\mu)=\sum_{t:\,r_1(t)\in\{a_k,\ldots,a_m\}}\mu_t. \]

Replace the paper’s coalition-indexed ballots \(\beta_S\) by an anonymous ballot curve \(\beta:[0,1]\to\Delta(A)\), where \(\beta(q)\) is the ballot associated with a coalition of mass \(q\). Writing

\[ B_k(q)=\beta(q)([a_k,a_m]), \]

require every \(B_k\) to be nondecreasing, with \(\beta(0)=\bm e_{a_1}\) and \(\beta(1)=\bm e_{a_m}\). The induced continuous rule is

\[ \Phi_\beta(\mu)(a_k) = B_k(q_k(\mu))-B_{k+1}(q_{k+1}(\mu)), \]

with \(q_{m+1}=0\). If \(\overline{k}-\underline{k}>1\), impose the continuous version of the paper’s constrained random dictatorship:

\[ B_{\overline{k}}(q)=q,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{\underline{k}}\beta(q)(a_j)=1-q, \]

and \(\beta(q)(a_j)=0\) for \(\underline{k}<j<\overline{k}\).

The precise research problem is: given an explicit finite hybrid type set \(\mathcal T\), rational piecewise-linear candidate rule \(F:\Delta(\mathcal T)\to\Delta(A)\), and the thresholds, decide whether \(F\) is unanimous and mass-strategy-proof and whether it admits the above continuous PFBR representation; if so, output the ballot curve \(\beta\). A mass deviation replaces \(\delta\) of type \(t\) by type \(t'\), producing \(\mu-\delta e_t+\delta e_{t'}\), and truthfulness requires the original lottery to stochastically dominate the deviating lottery according to \(t\). For a fixed \(\mu\) and \(\beta\), evaluating \(\Phi_\beta(\mu)\) is plainly tractable by finite summation; the theorem-level recognition and characterization problem is the genuinely interesting question.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime in the paper’s own public-facility setting: a large city has many residents, but only a modest number of feasible facility locations and finitely many location-induced preference rankings. The continuous object is specifically the population distribution \(\mu\); the lottery over alternatives remains finite. The natural-order, threshold, middle-interval, tops-only, and ballot structure all survive, so the authors could recognize the question as theirs rather than as an unrelated fractional voting problem. Typically one would have \(n\) very large and \(\tau=|\mathcal T|\) comparatively small.

I would expect the fixed-rule evaluation problem to be Class A. A full continuous characterization might also be tractable through monotone-function and linear-inequality methods, but that is a conjecture, not a result of this paper. No hardness-transfer claim is available because the paper proves no discrete computational hardness.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper’s ballots \(\beta_S\) may depend on the identities of voters, whereas the continuous mirror collapses coalitions to their mass \(q\). That is an anonymity restriction. Also, unilateral strategy-proofness becomes vacuous for a nonatomic individual unless it is replaced by a positive-mass deviation notion. Thus this is a convincing mirror of the anonymous, high-multiplicity subcase of Theorem 2, not an exact continuization of every RSCF covered by the theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive negative point is that this paper supplies no computational anchor. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are structural and axiomatic characterizations. There is no named decision, optimization, complexity, approximation, or algorithmic result, nor even an input encoding or input-size parameter. The paper asks which domains and RSCFs satisfy axioms; it does not ask how to compute anything. Under ChoCo’s gate, that is already decisive.

Theorem 1 cannot be rescued by assigning masses to preferences. Minimal richness, diversity, and no-restoration are properties of the support \(\mathbb D\), and the strong-connectivity graph is unchanged by the frequencies with which its preferences occur. A distribution over \(\mathbb D\) therefore adds a population vector that the theorem does not use. This would be a continuous wrapper around an axiomatic domain theorem, not a continuous computational mirror.

Theorem 2 is the proponent’s strongest possible anchor, but the proposed mirror changes its central object. The paper’s ballots are indexed by arbitrary coalitions \(S\subseteq N\), not merely by their cardinality. The constrained-random-dictatorship condition even permits voter-specific coefficients \(\varepsilon_i\). Thus two voters with identical preferences may remain computationally distinct because the rule treats their identities differently.

There are only three ways to proceed, and none gives the required mirror. If identity is retained, it must be part of the voter type. Then the supposed type classes are really named roles, and arbitrary \(\beta_S\) requires essentially individual-level information; the high-multiplicity gain disappears. If one replaces \(\beta_S\) by a curve \(\beta(q)\), one has imposed anonymity and discarded a substantial part of Theorem 2. That may define a sensible new anonymous theorem, but it is not the theorem proved in the paper. If one retains only a finite number of role types, one has again redefined the rule from coalition-indexing over voters to mass-indexing over roles.

The strategy-proofness condition has a second, independent failure. In an atomless society, changing one voter’s report leaves \(\mu\) unchanged, so unilateral strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. The proposed replacement by a positive-mass deviation,

\[ \mu\longmapsto \mu-\delta e_t+\delta e_{t'}, \]

is not the paper’s condition: it is a coordinated block deviation by mass \(\delta\). Requiring stochastic dominance for every such deviation is a new coalitional incentive axiom. Keeping deviations infinitesimal makes them ineffectual; keeping them macroscopic changes the problem.

The proposed recognition task—given a piecewise-linear \(F:\Delta(\mathcal T)\to\Delta(A)\), decide whether it has a continuous PFBR representation—is therefore invented around the paper rather than extracted from it. For fixed \(\beta\), evaluation is just the finite calculation

\[ \Phi_\beta(\mu)(a_k) = B_k(q_k(\mu))-B_{k+1}(q_{k+1}(\mu)). \]

The paper contains no computational reason to study recognition of such an \(F\), no optimization problem, and no discrete computational theorem whose high-multiplicity behaviour is being tested. A hard or interesting recognition problem could certainly be designed, but then its interest would come from the new problem, not from continuizing this paper.

This is not an objection that the continuous answer might be easy, nor a claim that continuum alternatives in the cited literature count as prior art. It is more basic: the paper’s population is only the finite index set on which its axioms and coalitions are defined. Replacing that index set by mass either erases identity-sensitive content, makes incentives vacuous, or introduces a different coalitional model.

I would therefore reject a continuous mirror for this paper. The honest concession is that an anonymous, mass-strategy-proof extension could become a worthwhile new social-choice project. It is not, however, a continuous computational mirror of any named result in this paper.
