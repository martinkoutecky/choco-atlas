The continuous mirror question

Given finite firm and worker type sets with rational masses, complete type-level preferences over a finite catalog of packages, and rational resource coefficients with capacities scaled by type mass, compute a feasible mass schedule \(x_a \ge 0\) with no blocking package. A package blocks if positive mass can be shifted from its participants’ worst currently used situations into that package while preserving capacities, and the firm type and every participating worker type strictly prefer it under the paper’s externality-adjusted worker order.

The model it lives in

A type-mass hiring market: each package pairs a firm type with a bundle of worker types, and using it consumes resources according to \(\pi_a(i)\) against capacities \(b_i\) proportional to type masses \(\mu_i\). Types carry complete preferences, including package-level complementarities; stability applies the paper’s worst-situation blocking condition to positive mass.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s stable π-schedule computation for a finite type-based package catalog. It leaves Theorem 1’s conversion to stable full-time matchings under π-concavity and Theorem 2’s specialized leader-follower class outside its claim.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a type-level version of Scarf’s construction find a stable schedule in time polynomial in the number of types, listed packages, and input bit length?
  • Which type-level π-concavity conditions convert these schedules into stable full-time allocations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification result. Its strongest computational anchor is Theorem 3, proved here: for a finite many-to-one market and a given \(\pi\)-scheme, Scarf’s algorithm terminates with a feasible stable \(\pi\)-schedule matching. Stability uses the paper’s worker preferences with the artificial externality that a worker is slightly better off when her employer is better off. The theorem gives a constructive algorithm, but no polynomial running-time bound. It produces a stable schedule; a stable full-time matching follows only with the additional \(\pi\)-concavity condition of Theorem 1.

A plausible mirror is Type-Compressed Stable \(\pi\)-Schedule Matching. Consider a large hiring market with rational masses of firm types and worker types. A worker type records the complete preference over contracts and employers, as well as relevant capacities and other parameters. A firm type records its complete ranking over a finite list of acceptable team or contract packages, including complementarities among workers. For each package \(a\), the input gives its resource use \(\pi_a(i)\) for every type \(i\); the available capacity \(b_i\) scales with that type’s mass. A solution is a rational schedule \(x_a\geq 0\) satisfying \(\sum_a \pi_a(i)x_a\leq b_i\) for every type. It must also be stable: no package can be introduced for positive mass by drawing firms and workers from situations they rank worse than that package, while respecting capacities. For workers, use the paper’s externality-adjusted order when comparing situations.

The regime could be a large regional or national hiring round: many applicants and repeated employer positions, but relatively few complete preference, credential, and contract types. A type’s mass is the fraction of the relevant population sharing that full description; the intended setting has far fewer types than people. Theorem 3’s package-level complementarities remain in the model. The population becomes continuous through the masses and scaled capacities, not merely through the schedule variables. This is a credible high-multiplicity setting when the market really does reuse a small catalogue of complete types.

I would expect the explicit finite-package version to be tractable (Class A), though cautiously. Aggregating identical agents makes the resource system depend on the number of types and listed packages rather than the headcount, and Theorem 3 already constructs a stable schedule from a finite resource system. The open algorithmic question is whether one can find it in time polynomial in the number of types, the number of packages, and the input bit length. The paper’s termination proof does not supply that bound. This mirror covers Theorem 3’s stable-schedule computation; it does not claim that the output is a stable full-time matching.

The paper itself cites Che, Kim, and Kojima’s work on stable matching in large economies. That is supporting evidence that a large-market regime makes sense here, not a collision: it does not answer the type-compressed computational question. Natural follow-ups are whether the type-level problem admits a polynomial algorithm, whether a type-level \(\pi\)-concavity condition converts its schedules into stable full-time allocations, and whether such algorithms give exact or additive-approximation methods for large finite markets.

The weakest point is that the paper’s inputs are rankings over assignments of named agents. My mirror replaces those assignments with type-level package templates and asks the authors to accept that extension. Also, Scarf’s algorithm is finite but has no stated complexity bound. Those are real gaps, but they leave a well-posed question about the computational value of representing a repeated workforce by its types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 3’s object is still a finite market of individually named agents. Its firms rank assignments of particular workers, and a worker’s situation includes the particular firm and colleagues involved. To group agents faithfully, a type must preserve all those distinctions. In a market with idiosyncratic rankings over assignments, the number of types can therefore grow with the population, leaving no high-multiplicity compression.

The proponent’s package model gets around this by making preferences anonymous: agents’ rankings and resource use depend on types and package templates, not on which particular people participate. That is a coherent model, but it changes a central part of the paper’s market. If a package is instead allowed to name its participants, the catalog can grow with the population and the compression disappears. This makes the proposed mirror a restricted extension, rather than an automatic continuization of the general result.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. A hiring market with repeated employer and applicant types, and preferences over a finite catalog of type-based packages, is plausible. The paper’s model accommodates complementarities, so preferences over groups do not by themselves rule it out. Scarf’s construction is an algorithmic result, even though Theorem 3 gives no polynomial running-time bound; asking whether a type-level version admits an efficient algorithm is a legitimate computational question. The paper’s citation of Che, Kim, and Kojima supports the large-market setting, but their existence result does not settle that complexity question.

So the case against is narrower than the requested universal claim: the paper does not supply a ready-made compression, and the mirror needs an explicit anonymity and input-representation choice. I cannot honestly say that this rules out every worthwhile scenario. The proponent’s finite-catalog hiring model survives those objections, so I would call the negative case weak rather than claim the anchor is defeated.
