The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a fixed tie-breaking rule, and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, let \(w\) be the IRV winner at \(\mu\). Is there a candidate \(d\ne w\) and a nonnegative mass transfer \(x_{r,s}\) from sincere ranking type \(r\) to reported ranking \(s\), with \(\sum_s x_{r,s}\le\mu_r\) and \(x_{r,s}=0\) unless \(r\) ranks \(d\) above \(w\), such that \(d\) wins IRV at the resulting distribution \(\mu'_q=\mu_q-\sum_s x_{q,s}+\sum_r x_{r,q}\)?

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings \(r\), with mass \(\mu_r\). Decision variables \(x_{r,s}\) transfer eligible mass from sincere to reported rankings; the question is whether such transfers can make a preferred candidate the IRV winner under the fixed tie-breaking rule.

The objection that survived

Theorem 2’s equivalence depends on fluctuations in random finite profiles near the uniform distribution, so it does not establish the same equivalence for arbitrary deterministic societies of ranking mass.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the IRV coalitional-manipulation predicate underlying Theorem 2. It leaves aside the theorem’s Impartial Culture probability limit and Gaussian formulas, as well as Theorem 1’s Plurality with Runoff result.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of this decision problem in the number of candidates, support size, and rational input bit length?
  • Does the discrete NP-completeness cited in the paper transfer to fractional mass transfers, or does the continuous relaxation change the complexity?
  • Can the high-probability SCW characterization be extended to any meaningful class of deterministic continuous societies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a continuous version of IRV coalitional manipulation. There is an important limit on the evidence, though: this paper has no named computational-complexity result that qualifies as an anchor under your rule. Its NP-completeness statement for IRV manipulation is attributed to Bartholdi et al. The paper’s own Theorems 1–4 and Corollary 1 concern limiting probabilities and asymptotic behavior, rather than membership or hardness in a complexity class.

The closest named result is Theorem 2, proved here: under Impartial Culture, as the number of voters tends to infinity, with high probability IRV is immune to coalitional manipulation if and only if a Super Condorcet Winner exists. It is a result about the manipulability property, but not a computational-complexity result, so it cannot serve as a qualifying anchor.

A concrete continuous problem suggested by that result is Continuous IRV Coalitional Manipulation. An instance is a finite set of \(m\) candidates, a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings, and a fixed tie-breaking rule. Each ranking \(r\) with \(\mu_r>0\) is a type: its members share the same sincere preferences. Let \(w\) be the IRV winner at \(\mu\). Decide whether there are a candidate \(d\ne w\) and nonnegative transfers \(x_{r,s}\) from sincere type \(r\) to reported ranking \(s\), satisfying
\[ x_{r,s}=0\ \text{unless }d\succ_r w, \qquad \sum_s x_{r,s}\le \mu_r. \]
The resulting society has
\[ \mu'_q=\mu_q-\sum_s x_{q,s}+\sum_r x_{r,q}. \]
A solution is such a \(d\) and transfer plan with \(d\) the IRV winner at \(\mu'\). This preserves the paper’s coalition condition: every changed voter’s true ranking strictly prefers the new winner. The decision variable is a mass transfer, and the objective is existence, rather than minimum cost.

A plausible regime is a large electorate where agents submit rankings from a relatively small set of shared ballot types: for fixed \(m\), there are at most \(m!\) types, while the electorate can grow without bound. That is a natural high-multiplicity regime, and the paper itself studies fixed \(m\) as \(n\) grows. Authors could recognize this as a continuous form of the manipulation predicate underlying their analysis. For variable \(m\), a direct approach can enumerate elimination orders and solve linear feasibility problems over transfers; its size is polynomial in \(m!\), which is exponential in \(m\). I would leave its ChoCo classification open, with a tentative lean toward hardness driven by the candidate agenda. The paper gives no result showing that discrete hardness survives fractional transfers.

The weakest point is the gap between that decision problem and the paper’s main theorem. Under Impartial Culture, normalized profiles concentrate at the uniform distribution, while Theorem 2’s limiting conclusion depends on fluctuations around it at scale \(\sqrt n\). A deterministic continuous society at the uniform distribution loses those fluctuations. Modeling them directly would make randomness over profiles central, which is outside this programme’s population-continuization goal. So the paper offers a credible high-multiplicity setting and a concrete continuous manipulation question, but no qualifying named computational anchor—and its principal asymptotic result does not itself establish the continuous problem’s complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that it does not match the paper’s contribution. None of its named theorems or corollaries establishes a computational complexity result. Theorem 2 proves a high probability equivalence under Impartial Culture; Theorems 3 and 4 give Gaussian probability formulas; the corollary gives a limiting CM rate. The NP completeness result mentioned in the introduction is prior work by Bartholdi et al., not a result of this paper.

That mismatch matters for the proposed mirror. Under Impartial Culture, the normalized society converges to the uniform distribution. Theorem 2’s limiting statement still depends on fluctuations around that point. A deterministic continuous society at the uniform distribution cannot represent the probability of those fluctuations. Recovering the theorem’s limiting rate requires a distribution over profile fluctuations—the noise angle the programme puts out of scope, and one the paper already analyzes with its Gaussian formulas.

The proposed mass transfer problem is still a natural high multiplicity version of IRV coalitional manipulation. Rankings are complete types, their mass has a clear electoral meaning, and the manipulation condition depends on voters’ preferences rather than their identities. Allowing a coalition to move fractional mass is a sensible model. Those facts defeat the obvious objections about multiplicity and atomlessness.

So the negative case can disqualify the proposal as a direct mirror of Theorem 2: it asks for the computational complexity of manipulation on arbitrary societies, while the theorem studies limiting probabilities under a particular random model. But I cannot honestly argue that this stronger problem is not worthwhile for ChoCo. It is a plausible computational question about the paper’s central predicate, even though it is not the complexity result this paper proves. The case against is therefore strong on anchoring and weak on the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
