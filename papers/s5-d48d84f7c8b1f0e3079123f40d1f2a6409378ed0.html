What the mirror covers

No named computational result is covered. Proposition 3.1 is statistical, while the allocation and learning gains are empirical evaluations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is for the *setting*, not for a named complexity result in this paper. Its nearest formal anchor is Proposition 3.1, proved in the appendix using a result cited from Ghosh et al.: if an LLM proxy answers comparison queries with accuracy above \(50\%\), the student’s true valuation function minimizes the training loss on the proxy-generated noisy data. That is a statistical robustness claim, not a result about a problem being in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The paper’s efficiency and learning improvements are experimental findings, not numbered complexity results. So there is no qualifying named computational anchor here.

A plausible continuous follow-up would model a large course-allocation cohort with a small number \(\tau\) of recurring, *exact* preference profiles among \(N\) students. A type \(t\) would fix everything the pipeline uses: the student’s bundle valuation \(v_t\), constraints and budget, initial reports, and LLM response behavior. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of students with that profile. Given type masses, course capacities, proxy response distributions, and an average query budget \(B\), choose type-specific comparison-query policies and a fractional assignment \(x_{t,S}\) of each type’s mass to bundles \(S\). Require \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\), enforce course capacities, and maximize expected aggregate true welfare, \(\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)]\), after the specified learning and allocation pipeline. This captures the paper’s elicitation-to-allocation workflow, but it is a proposed problem, not a mirror of a named computational result. I would tentatively expect bundle combinatorics to remain hard as the number of courses grows; that expectation is not established by this paper.

The regime could be a very large, standardized program where many students share exact degree requirements, bundle preferences, budgets, and reporting behavior, so \(N\) is in the thousands while \(\tau\ll N\). The weakest point is that the paper’s own setup emphasizes individualized preferences and text, and trains a model for each student. Finding many students with identical assignment-relevant profiles may be difficult; treating merely similar students as one type would change the model. That makes this a possible research extension, but not an anchored positive case under the paper-result requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper gives ChoCo no named computational result to continuize. Proposition 3.1 is the closest formal anchor: under its noise assumption, a student’s true valuation minimizes the generalized cross-entropy training loss when proxy accuracy exceeds \(50\%\). That is a statistical claim about noisy labels, not a complexity result or an algorithm for a computational social choice problem. The allocation and learning gains are experimental comparisons of a particular pipeline; the paper does not analyze their computational complexity.

Even Proposition 3.1 has little to gain from making the population continuous. For exact types \(t\), one could state the same condition type by type and average the losses with weights \(\mu_t\). That gives an aggregate version of the existing statistical claim, not a new computational question. A more ambitious question about learning across types or choosing queries to maximize population welfare could be interesting, but it would need a new objective and analysis that the proposition does not supply.

The proposed large-cohort scenario is plausible: many students could share the same complete preference, constraint, report, and proxy-response type. I would not reject it because repeated types are impossible, or because assigning fractional mass across bundles is inherently wrong. But the proposed optimization—choosing type-specific query policies and fractional assignments to maximize expected welfare—does not mirror an optimization problem solved or classified in this paper. The paper fixes its query-generation and training methods, then measures their effects experimentally. Adding a global query-policy decision and a welfare-maximizing assignment creates a new problem.

There is a more faithful version: retain the paper’s pipeline and ask how it behaves when identical students share a learned model and query stream, with mass-sensitive course supplies. That is coherent, but it chiefly studies pooling duplicate elicitation work and adapting the allocation stage to a large market. It does not continuize a computational result of this paper, because no such result is stated. If query answers instead remain independent per student, the population representation also needs to preserve that sampling and its effect on learning.

So the negative case rests on a mismatch of target, not on a claim that course-allocation cohorts cannot have high multiplicity. The proposed follow-on may be worthwhile in its own right; the paper alone does not establish it as a worthwhile continuous mirror. The case is not airtight against every possible extension, but claiming that no scenario could produce a useful one would go beyond the evidence.
