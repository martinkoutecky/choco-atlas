The continuous mirror question

Given fixed \(k\), parties, utilities, a decreasing policy function \(X\), and rational masses \(\mu_{d,\theta}\) over a finite support of district-preference types, determine the limiting seat count for \(L\) and resulting policy along finite replications with odd district sizes and type frequencies converging to \(\mu\), when each finite game is solved by pure-strategy perfection. Assume each district has a unique mass median \(m_d\) and \(m_d\ne\alpha_d\), where \(\alpha_d=(X(d)+X(d-1))/2\). Is the eventual outcome \(X(d^*)\), where \(d^*=\max(\{d:m_d<\alpha_d\}\cup\{0\})\), independent of the approximating sequence?

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete district-preference types \((d,\theta)\). Compute each district’s weighted mass median \(m_d\), compare it with the seat thresholds \(\alpha_d\), and output the policy \(X(d^*)\); interpret this as the eventual perfect-equilibrium outcome of odd finite replications converging to \(\mu\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 4’s multidistrict-majority perfect-equilibrium outcome. It leaves the district-sincerity result, the proportional-election result, and the electoral-system comparisons in Propositions 1, 6, 7, and 8 aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do the strict median-threshold inequalities ensure the same eventual seat count for every odd finite replication sequence converging to \(\mu\)?
  • What runtime bound in \(k\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\) follows for computing the weighted medians and threshold scan?

The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational result. It reports no problem as NP-hard, in P, FPT, or otherwise. Its numbered propositions establish equilibrium and policy-comparison results. In particular, Proposition 4, proved in the paper, says that in the multidistrict-majority model \(X(\bar d^M)\) is the unique pure-strategy perfect-equilibrium outcome, where
\[ \bar d^M= \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if }m_1>\alpha_1,\\ \max\{d:m_d\le\alpha_d\} & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases} \qquad \alpha_j=\frac{X(j)+X(j-1)}{2}. \]
That is a strong equilibrium characterization, but it does not assert a computational-complexity result. Propositions 1 and 6 give related equilibrium results for district-sincere voting and proportional representation; Propositions 7 and 8 compare electoral systems. None supplies the required anchor.

The strongest positive follow-up I can make is therefore not an anchor-based claim about what this paper proved. It is a continuous computational question suggested by Proposition 4. Take a fixed number \(k\) of districts and a finite set of voter types \((d,\theta)\), where a type specifies both district and bliss point (and hence the relevant utility). Give each type a rational mass \(\mu_{d,\theta}\), with total mass one. The parties, seat-to-policy function \(X\), and voters’ utility functions are as in the paper. In each district, the mass of every type casts ballots for \(L\) or \(R\); voters maximize their expected utility over policies. Ask for the seat count and policy that arise in the limit of the unique pure-strategy perfect-equilibrium outcomes in finite societies whose type frequencies converge to \(\mu\) and whose district sizes are odd. The solution is that limiting equilibrium outcome, if it stabilizes.

This has a plausible regime: a very large electorate, perhaps millions of voters, divided into districts with a comparatively small number \(\tau\) of recurring preference-and-district types. A type is complete for the model because it fixes the voter’s district and preferences. The outcome remains the parliament’s seat allocation and resulting policy; it is the population that becomes a distribution over types. With unique district medians \(m_d\), ordered by district, and no positive mass at any \(\alpha_j\), the natural candidate answer is the Proposition 4 threshold formula using those mass medians. Computing it would take polynomial time in \(k,\tau\), and the input bit length: calculate the weighted median in each district and scan the seat thresholds. This mirrors only the multidistrict-majority result, not the proportional case or the comparison propositions.

The regime is credible because the paper itself studies district populations whose voters share preferences, and large elections with repeated voter types are a natural high-multiplicity instance of that model. The authors could recognize the mirror as their equilibrium-outcome problem at high multiplicity. The weakest point is that perfection in the paper relies on finite voters being pivotal. In a naïve nonatomic voting game, an individual has no effect on the outcome, so that equilibrium concept cannot simply be carried over. Defining the continuous question through limits of finite replications makes it precise, but also makes the proposed tractability result a close extension of the paper’s characterization rather than a computational result the paper actually establishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 4 is the only serious anchor, and it is not a computational result: it characterizes the unique pure-strategy perfect-equilibrium outcome. Propositions 1 and 6 give related equilibrium characterizations; Propositions 7 and 8 compare policies. None asks for an algorithm or classifies a computational problem.

The strongest objection to the proposed mirror is that perfection has no direct nonatomic version here. In the paper, an individual’s vote matters when that voter is pivotal, and perfection is defined through finite voters’ trembles. In a continuum, each voter has zero mass and cannot change the outcome. A limit of finite replications gives a coherent way to retain the paper’s selection, but it imports the finite refinement as part of the new model. A direct mass-voting Nash model would make each individual’s vote irrelevant; modeling deviations by positive-mass groups would introduce a coalition concept the paper does not study. District design or campaigning could create richer algorithmic problems, but those would add new objectives rather than mirror this result.

That objection does not defeat the best version of the mirror. For a large electorate with fixed districts and recurring district–preference types, the types are complete for the paper’s model. Under the stated genericity conditions, the finite-replication limit is stable, and its outcome can be computed from weighted district medians and the seat thresholds. The population is genuinely continuous, and the authors could recognize the question as computing their equilibrium outcome at high multiplicity.

So the negative case is weak. The paper has no named computational result to anchor, and the proposed continuous problem inherits its equilibrium selection from finite replications. But under the programme’s stated standards, neither point rules out this mirror: the high-multiplicity regime is plausible, and the limit question is well-posed. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
