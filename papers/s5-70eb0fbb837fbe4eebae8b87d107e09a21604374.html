The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest conclusion is that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor in the strict ChoCo sense. None of its numbered results proves NP-hardness, membership in P, parameterized tractability, approximation, or a computational formulation whose complexity is analysed. Its results are mechanism-design and structural theorems. Thus no fully compliant positive case can be based on the paper’s own computational results.

There is nevertheless a credible substantive mirror if structural anchors are admitted. My lead would be a continuum version of the paper’s finite-population impossibility boundary.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for every MTRA with \(p\ge2\), when agents may submit arbitrary strict linear orders over bundles, no mechanism can simultaneously guarantee sd-efficiency, sd-envy-freeness, and a decomposable assignment on every instance.

Define \(\mathsf{Continuum\text{-}MTRA\text{-}Purification}\) as follows. There is a nonatomic population of mass \(1\), with finitely many complete agent types \(\Theta\), mass distribution \(\mu\), and \(|\Theta|\ll N\) in the intended finite approximation. A type \(\theta\) specifies a complete strict order \(\succ_\theta\) over bundles \(D_1\times\cdots\times D_p\). Each item category \(o\in D_i\) has per-capita supply \(s_{i,o}\), with \(\sum_{o\in D_i}s_{i,o}=1\). The solution is a mass assignment \(P_{\theta,x}\) satisfying

\[ \sum_x P_{\theta,x}=\mu_\theta \]

and

\[ \sum_{\theta,x:o\in x}P_{\theta,x}=s_{i,o}. \]

It must be implementable by assigning every individual one whole bundle, while its type-level averages are sd-envy-free and sd-efficient. The question is whether such an assignment can always be computed, and whether the finite impossibility of Theorem 1 survives.

This is a genuine population continuization. Individuals still receive integral bundles; only a positive-mass type is partitioned into subpopulations receiving different bundles. The item supplies are scaled per capita, as high multiplicity requires. The finite decomposition obstruction disappears because a nonatomic type can be split measurably. The expected classification is Class A: compute a fractional type-level assignment, then purify it by partitioning each type’s mass. The paper’s MPS results, especially Theorem 4 and Proposition 2, provide a natural construction for the required efficient and envy-free assignment, although they are supporting machinery rather than complexity anchors.

A second, narrower structural anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here: under lexicographic preferences, LexiPS satisfies sd-efficiency and sd-envy-freeness and always outputs decomposable assignments.

The corresponding problem, \(\mathsf{Continuum\text{-}LexiPS}\), takes a finite distribution \(\mu\) over lexicographic types \(\theta=(\triangleright_\theta,\succ^1_\theta,\ldots,\succ^p_\theta)\), together with per-capita item supplies. In phase \(k\), all mass whose \(k\)-th important resource type is \(i\) simultaneously consumes its most-preferred available item of type \(i\), at rate proportional to its mass, until an item is exhausted. After \(p\) phases, the algorithm outputs the bundle-mass assignment \(P_{\theta,x}\), together with a measurable integral implementation. The task is to compute this assignment exactly and certify sd-efficiency, sd-envy-freeness, and individual integrality. This should also be Class A, via a finite event simulation whose size depends on the number of distinct preference and item types, not on the number of agents.

The authors should recognise both questions as their problem: the MTRA structure, multi-type bundles, stochastic-dominance notions, lexicographic restriction, and decomposability are all retained. The continuous change is specifically that many agents share complete types and are represented by masses. Further questions include finite-population rounding, the granularity needed to recover Theorem 1’s impossibility, and whether truthful importance-order reporting extends from individuals to positive-mass coalitions.

The weakest point is decisive: these are plausible continuations of the paper’s mechanism problems, but not mirrors of named computational results. Moreover, because the paper already studies fractional assignments through MPS, a referee could regard the first construction as an atomless implementation of existing divisible-resource machinery rather than a new computational mirror. So the strongest defensible verdict is: substantively, there is a good Class-A population mirror, led by Theorem 1; under the programme’s strict computational-anchor rule, however, the paper supplies no eligible anchor and therefore cannot support a fully compliant positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong under ChoCo’s eligibility rule: this paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 is an impossibility theorem about the coexistence of three axioms; Theorem 2 is an axiomatic guarantee for LexiPS. Theorems 4, 6, and 7 are likewise mechanism-design properties and characterizations. None asserts membership in \( \mathrm{P} \), hardness, approximation, parameterized tractability, or even a computational optimization formulation. The proponent’s concession is therefore decisive: a continuous computational problem would have to be added to the paper, not mirrored from one of its computational results.

The proposed continuum version of Theorem 1 also loses the paper’s substantive obstruction. In the finite model, decomposability means that a fractional assignment \(P\) lies in the convex hull of discrete whole-item assignments. In the proposed atomless model, let type \( \theta \) have mass \( \mu_\theta \), and suppose \(P_{\theta,x}\) assigns mass \(P_{\theta,x}\) of that type to bundle \(x\). Partition an interval of length \( \mu_\theta \) into measurable pieces \(A_{\theta,x}\) with lengths \(P_{\theta,x}\), and give every individual in \(A_{\theta,x}\) the whole bundle \(x\). The item-supply constraints ensure that this is a feasible integral assignment for every individual.

Thus the proposed “purification” is not a computational phenomenon; it is the automatic divisibility of an atomless population. The finite obstruction in Theorem 1 has disappeared because the continuum has removed the relevant integrality constraint. If one insists on the original lottery-over-finite-assignments definition, there is no comparable object once a continuum of agents and per-capita item supplies is introduced. If one restores positive indivisible cohorts, one has restored atoms and abandoned the proposed continuum.

The strongest version of the mirror could retain arbitrary complete preference types and ask whether a mass assignment satisfying sd-efficiency, sd-envy-freeness, and individual integrality exists. That is a legitimate mechanism-design question, but it is still not a computational mirror of Theorem 1. It is either a feasibility problem over the fractional assignment polytope or an axiomatic existence question. The paper supplies no computational theorem about it, and the finite impossibility no longer supplies the interesting boundary.

Theorem 2 fares no better. Under lexicographic preferences, the continuous LexiPS process is simply the mass version of the paper’s own phase-wise PS construction. Its output can again be implemented by partitioning each positive-mass preference type. The claims of sd-efficiency, sd-envy-freeness, and individual whole-bundle allocation are therefore inherited by the same aggregation and purification argument. The continuum adds a population representation, but no new computational object.

A more ambitious proposal—say, minimizing the number of measurable pieces in a purification, bounding the support of a lottery, or studying manipulation by positive-mass coalitions—could certainly become worthwhile. But each would introduce a new objective or strategic question absent from Theorem 2. It would be a new computational social-choice paper, not a continuization of this paper’s result.

There is also a scaling problem behind the proposed “better” type spaces. In the paper, \(n\) agents coexist with \(n\) distinct items of every type, and preferences rank an \(n^p\)-sized bundle domain. Under normalized population mass, each named item has mass \(1/n\), so the item-level object vanishes in the limit. Keeping fixed positive per-capita supplies instead replaces named indivisible items by resource classes or divisible copies. That may be a sensible model, but it is precisely the modification that removes the finite decomposability phenomenon.

So I would not claim that no atomless multi-type allocation problem could ever be valuable. One could build a worthwhile ChoCo project around a genuinely new optimization problem involving type masses, coalition control, robustness, or succinct preference representations. The honest negative conclusion is narrower but sufficient: neither proposed anchor is a computational result of this paper, and the paper’s only potentially distinctive obstruction—finite decomposability of indivisible multi-type assignments—becomes automatic under the atomless population model. Under the programme’s gate, there is no worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
