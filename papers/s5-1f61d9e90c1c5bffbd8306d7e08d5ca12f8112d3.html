None asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally classified. I can’t honestly use any of them as a computational anchor.

The strongest possible setting for a population-continuous extension would be a basin with many similar local water districts or municipalities. Their types might record their river position, local inflow, and any other features the allocation rule uses; mass would represent the fraction of districts of each type. But position is central to the paper’s upstream-to-downstream transfers, so preserving the model may require many distinct types. More fundamentally, this paper studies axiomatic characterizations of allocation rules, not computational problems over a population. A continuous-population optimization question would be a new problem, not a mirror of a named result here.

The paper’s allocations are continuous quantities of water, but that is continuity in the outcome, not in the population. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive difficulty is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Theorems 1–4 and Proposition 1 characterize allocation rules by axioms; the paper poses no algorithmic problem and proves no complexity or algorithmic result. A continuous version of those characterizations could be an axiomatic project, but it would fall outside ChoCo’s computational scope. To get a computational question, one would have to add a task—such as optimizing an allocation or fitting rule parameters—that the paper’s results do not specify.

The proponent’s strongest scenario is many local water districts along a river. Even there, the districts’ positions and inflows matter to the paper’s transfer rules: changing the order changes who can pass water downstream and which cumulative feasibility constraints apply. A type that omits position merges districts the rule treats differently; a type that includes position may leave nearly every district distinct. Making the chain very dense instead gives a spatially continuous river model, which continuizes locations rather than the population of rights holders. Treating residents as the population would require assigning rights to individuals, although the paper assigns them to geographically distinct jurisdictions. That could motivate a new model, but it would not preserve the paper’s characterized problem.

Replicating whole basins does not fix the gap: separate basins do not share the paper’s downstream transfer chain, while treating them as one chain changes the model. There may be worthwhile computational questions about water rights in richer settings, but they would need an independently motivated objective and computational result. I therefore find the negative case strong against a continuous mirror of this paper; the claim that no related scenario could ever be worthwhile is less airtight, because such a scenario could amount to a valuable new research direction rather than a mirror.
