The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the level of the paper’s actual results: it has no named computational result to continuize. The increase from \(18\%\) of participants finding their target choice before tradeoff navigation to \(75\%\) afterward is an experimental finding, based on 28 participants and each person’s review of a fixed catalog. The preference and confidence changes are experimental findings too. None specifies an algorithmic problem, input, output, or complexity claim.

A stronger mirror than the one proposed would model a large e-commerce audience as a distribution over complete shopper types, including preferences and responses to tradeoff prompts. That could be a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But aggregating the paper’s outcomes over those types gives a population statistic; it does not create a computational social choice problem. Each shopper has their own target apartment, and the paper studies how an interface helps that person choose. There is no collective decision rule or common winner to compute.

One could add a budget and ask which types should receive tradeoff support to maximize aggregate improvement. That would be a computational allocation problem, but its budget, objective, and model of treatment effects would all need to be supplied. It would be a new problem motivated by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its results. If support is available to everyone, the aggregate effect is simply an average over types.

So the honest negative case is narrow but substantial: plausible repeated shopper types do not rescue a mirror when the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. I cannot rule out a worthwhile adjacent project; the argument is that it would begin beyond the paper’s central empirical question.
