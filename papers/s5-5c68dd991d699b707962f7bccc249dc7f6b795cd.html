What the mirror covers

The proposed extension draws on Proposition 3’s finite guinea-pig optimization; the paper’s network characterizations, benchmark comparisons, and numerical exercises do not provide computational anchors.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying anchor in this paper. Theorem 1 characterizes which networks can be uniquely optimal for some information environment; Theorems 2 and 3 construct environments where the star or complete network is uniquely best; and Proposition 3 gives cutoffs for the optimal number of “guinea pigs.” These are results about Bayesian learning and network performance. None classifies a computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized, and the numerical exercises do not supply such a result. So there is no honest per-anchor continuous problem to state under the requested standard.

The closest positive foothold is Proposition 3, proved here: for fixed ordinary-signal accuracy \(p\), it gives a decreasing sequence of cutoffs in the conclusive-signal probability \(\varepsilon\). For odd \(N\), the optimum is \(M=1\) above the first cutoff, \(M=2k\) between successive cutoffs, and \(M=N-1\) below the last; adjacent choices tie at the cutoffs. The cutoffs increase with \(p\), and their limit as \(k\) grows determines the eventual optimum as \(N\) increases. That suggests a follow-on question about choosing a *fraction* of an organization’s agents to act independently before later agents learn from earlier actions.

But that extension exposes the main weakness: in a natural atomless population model, a positive mass of independent agents produces exact signal frequencies. Later agents may then infer the state perfectly, wiping out the finite-population trade-off Proposition 3 studies. Preserving that trade-off would require extra modeling choices, and the resulting question would be a new analytical extension rather than a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s computational result. The shared-signal, repeated-cohort scenario is plausible as a population regime, but it does not overcome the missing computational anchor or the collapse in the natural limit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that none of the anchors is a computational result. Theorem 1 characterizes, by an existence argument, which networks can be uniquely best under some information environment. Theorems 2 and 3 exhibit environments favoring the star and complete networks. Proposition 3 gives exact analytical cutoffs for the optimal number of guinea pigs. They introduce no computational problem with an input, an algorithm, or a complexity classification. A continuous question can be invented from each result, but it would be a new computational project rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Proposition 3 is the best candidate for a mirror: take \(M/N\) to a continuous fraction and ask how much of an organization should act independently. The paper gives this idea a credible setting, and the first \(M\) agents really are repeated types under the common signal structure. But the natural limit erases the cutoff trade-off. For any positive limiting fraction of guinea pigs, their actions have state-dependent frequencies. In the stated signal model, those frequencies differ between \(H\) and \(L\); an atomless cohort reveals its frequency exactly. A focal agent observing that cohort can therefore identify the state perfectly. All positive fractions yield the same terminal accuracy, so the intermediate optima and the cutoffs disappear.

The boundary case does not preserve Proposition 3’s distinctions. If \(M\) stays finite as \(N\) grows, its mass is zero and finite-sample uncertainty remains; if \(M\) grows without bound, even sublinearly, the independent cohort again reveals the state exactly. Proposition 3’s part (ii) explicitly allows the optimal \(M\) to stabilize at a finite value in some parameter regimes. A fraction of the population cannot distinguish those optima. Keeping them distinct requires a zero-mass, finite-sample scaling or added noise, which supplies extra modeling choices and an analytical extension, not a continuous-computational mirror.

The network results have a related obstacle. Their object is an ordered information process: an agent’s position and observed predecessors determine what an action means, and the overturning argument relies on the order of particular actions. In general networks, including the complete network, positions have different observation sets; treating those sets as part of type makes the type support grow with \(N\). Collapsing agents into masses loses the history order. Retaining it as continuous time leads to a continuum-time learning model, with a path of actions as the central object; that may be a worthwhile analytic project, but it does not give the paper’s results a finite-support population input for complexity analysis. Theorem 1’s rationalizability test itself is already a structural characterization, not an algorithmic classification.

There is room for a neighboring project: one could ask for algorithms to choose an observation network from a specified signal environment, or develop a continuous-time learning model for repeated cohorts. Neither is ruled out as worthwhile. The negative case is strongest—and the universal claim is weakest—at the boundary between those projects and this paper: the paper supplies no computational anchor, and its most plausible mass-based extension either loses the finite-horizon distinctions or requires a new temporal model.
