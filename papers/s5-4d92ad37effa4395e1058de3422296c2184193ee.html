The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive case with a qualifying anchor under your criteria. The paper has no numbered result asserting that an underlying problem is tractable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally classified.

The closest results are axiomatic tradeoffs: for example, Theorem 1 says no \(\epsilon\)-DP rule satisfies \(\theta\)-JR when \(\theta>e^\epsilon\); Theorem 4 bounds \(\beta\)-PE under DP; and Proposition 2 establishes privacy and approximate Pareto efficiency for the AV exponential mechanism. These concern which guarantees a randomized rule can achieve, not the computational complexity of a problem. The paper mentions the AV mechanism’s \(O(m(n+k))\) runtime and says two other mechanisms are exponential, but those observations are not named computational results of the required kind.

A continuous-population version is imaginable: types would be approval sets, masses would be population fractions, and a rule would map the distribution to a lottery over committees. But defining privacy there requires choosing what mass transfer counts as a neighboring change; that choice changes the paper’s one-voter privacy question. It would be a possible new question, not a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that there is no computational result here to mirror. The numbered theorems and propositions bound which approximate axioms a private rule can satisfy; they do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The stated runtime for the AV mechanism is an implementation bound, and the exponential runtimes are observations about particular mechanisms. Neither supplies the kind of complexity question the programme is set up to continuize.

The best candidate for a new computational question would be: given masses over approval types, compute a lottery over committees that meets specified privacy and axiom guarantees. But that is a new optimization problem, not a continuous version of one of the paper’s named results. The mechanisms already given also suggest little computational leverage from the population limit: AV scores compress directly to weighted sums over types, while the number of possible committees still depends on \(m\), not on how many voters share each type.

There is a deeper mismatch in the paper’s central ingredient, individual-level differential privacy. Its neighboring profiles differ in one voter’s ballot. A normalized mass vector \(\mu\) does not say how much mass one voter represents, so it cannot specify that adjacency without retaining the population size \(n\) and its \(1/n\) lattice. Retain those, and the input is still a finite-population model. Replace them with mass-transfer adjacency, and the privacy guarantee changes: a fixed mass step is privacy for a cohort, while a bound scaled to transferred mass is a different guarantee from the paper’s fixed per-person \(\epsilon\)-DP. Either may be worth studying, but neither preserves the paper’s central privacy question.

Axioms such as JR and PJR can certainly be given sensible mass-based definitions. That does not rescue these anchors: the paper’s contribution is the tradeoff with individual DP, and its proofs rely on changing one or a specified number of ballots. I do not see a recognized continuous-population version of those results that retains that privacy notion and creates a computational problem.

This negative case is strongest on scope, not impossibility. One could write a worthwhile paper on algorithms for private committee lotteries over type distributions, but the current paper does not establish a named computational result that such a paper would mirror.
