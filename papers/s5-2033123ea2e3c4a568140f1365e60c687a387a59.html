The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), capacities \(k_i\le 4\), an undirected acceptability graph on types, and strict rankings of acceptable partner types, decide whether a complete mass matching exists that is Pareto-optimal among all feasible mass matchings. A mass \(x_{ij}\ge0\) represents partnerships between types \(i\) and \(j\), with within-type partnerships allowed only when acceptable; completeness requires \(\sum_{j\ne i}x_{ij}+2x_{ii}=k_i\mu_i\) for every \(i\). Type \(i\)'s average partner vector has coordinate \(q_{ij}(x)=x_{ij}/\mu_i\) for \(j\ne i\) and \(q_{ii}(x)=2x_{ii}/\mu_i\). Ask whether some complete \(x\) has no feasible \(y\) with \(q_i(y)\succeq_{\mathrm{lex}}q_i(x)\) for every type and strict improvement for at least one.

The model it lives in

Use a continuum of agents partitioned into finitely many preference-and-capacity types with masses \(\mu_i\). Decision variables \(x_{ij}\) are bilateral partnership masses; each type's per-capita partner vector \(q_i(x)\) records average partners by rank and is compared lexicographically, as in the paper's fractional-matching model. The target is a complete feasible mass matching that is Pareto-optimal across types.

The objection that survived

Type-average Pareto improvement can conceal losses to some individuals of a type, and the paper's fractional preference definition does not establish that this aggregate criterion coincides with individual Pareto improvement.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.3's existence question for complete Pareto-optimal solutions in stable fixtures. It leaves the paper's strong-core results, verification hardness, near-feasible and fractional strong-core algorithms, and many-to-many maximum-size result untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • How do complete mass-Pareto-optimal solutions relate to complete individually Pareto-optimal matchings in finite markets formed by replicating the types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper by a reduction from Exact-3-Cover: deciding whether a stable fixtures instance with lexicographic preferences has a complete Pareto-optimal matching is NP-hard, even when every capacity is at most four. “Complete” means every agent is saturated. This is a good anchor because it is a hardness result about matching itself, and its question carries naturally to a population whose members fall into recurring types.

Call the mirror Complete-PO Fixture Mass. An instance has a finite set \(T\) of types, rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), capacities \(k_i\le 4\), and an undirected acceptability graph on the types. Each type specifies its capacity and its strict ranking of acceptable partner types. A type is a complete profile: its members share all the preferences and capacities relevant to this problem. The intended regime is a large population of organizations forming bilateral partnerships—say, regional service providers with up to four active collaborations—where many organizations share one of a much smaller number of profiles.

For each acceptable unordered type pair, let \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) be partnership mass. For distinct types \(i,j\), this contributes \(x_{ij}\) partner units to each type; a same-type edge contributes \(2x_{ii}\) units to type \(i\). A complete mass matching satisfies
\[ \sum_{j\ne i}x_{ij}+2x_{ii}=k_i\mu_i \quad\text{for every }i. \]
Type \(i\)’s partner vector \(q_i(x)\) records the average mass of partners at each rank in its type ranking, divided by \(\mu_i\). Compare these vectors lexicographically, as the paper does for fractional partner vectors. The question is whether there exists a complete mass matching \(x\) for which no feasible mass matching \(y\)—complete or not—makes every type weakly better off and at least one type strictly better off. A solution is such an \(x\), with Pareto-optimality understood against all feasible mass matchings.

I expect this to be Class C, though that is a conjecture, not a result. Testing whether any complete mass matching exists is just rational linear feasibility. The harder part is ruling out every Pareto improvement: for each type, its first rank at which the partner vector changes determines whether it improves, and these pivot choices interact across types. Divisibility may erase the Exact-3-Cover gadget in Theorem 4.3, so I would not claim its hardness transfers. But the type-level lexicographic choices could remain combinatorial after individual headcounts disappear. The main follow-up is to settle whether that yields NP-hardness in \(\tau=|T|\), or whether the Pareto condition admits a polynomial or parameterized algorithm; a separate question is how the mass solution relates to finite replicated markets.

This is a plausible analogue of the authors’ problem: it preserves fixtures, capacities, lexicographic bundle preferences, completeness, and Pareto-optimality. Section 5’s fractional-matching definition also makes the average partner vectors familiar within the paper. The mirror continuizes the society itself: \(\mu_i\) is a population share, while individual members can still receive integral partner bundles whose type-level average is fractional.

The weak point is that the paper ranks named partners strictly, whereas this mirror treats members of a type as interchangeable and ranks partner types. That narrows the mirror to markets where identity within a profile truly does not matter. It does not cover arbitrary named-agent fixtures, but that is a credible high-multiplicity regime—and the question remains the paper’s complete-Pareto-matching question within it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.3 is a real computational anchor, so the case against cannot rest on the paper having no complexity result. The strongest objection is that the proposed mirror changes what counts as a partner and what counts as a Pareto improvement. In the paper, an agent has a strict ranking over named agents, and Pareto comparisons concern each agent’s own bundle. In “Complete-PO Fixture Mass,” rankings are over partner types and Pareto comparisons are between type averages. A better average partner vector can conceal some members of a type losing partners they prefer while others gain them. That is a coherent aggregate criterion, but it is not the paper’s individual Pareto criterion.

Keeping the original criterion creates the opposite problem. A complete type would have to specify an agent’s ranking over particular counterpart identities. In a growing market, those rankings generally change as new agents appear, so exact types proliferate and the supposed high-multiplicity compression disappears. The Exact-3-Cover reduction makes this mismatch salient: its combinatorics are encoded in which particular agents accept one another and where they appear in each ranking. Collapsing those identities into partner classes changes that incidence structure.

There is a stronger repair: represent each type by a distribution over its members’ integral partner bundles, couple the bundle masses across mutually acceptable types, and call an outcome Pareto-dominated only if almost every individual weakly improves and a positive mass strictly improves. That would preserve individual welfare comparisons while making the population continuous. It is a serious candidate, though it is no longer the simple mass-matrix problem proposed by the other analyst. The paper’s fractional-matching section gives the authors some reason to recognize such an extension; its fractional outcomes do not, by themselves, make the population continuous or settle this question.

So the negative case is that the proposed formulation has not yet shown that its average-based Pareto notion is the right mirror, while the individual-level repair brings back substantial structure the type averages discard. But I cannot honestly claim that this defeats every plausible scenario. Large matching markets with repeated profiles and ranked partner classes are conceivable, and the repaired distributional model is coherent. Theorem 4.3 remains a worthwhile anchor; the case against is a demand for a faithful welfare model, not a convincing argument that no continuous mirror exists.
