The case FOR (proponent)

A continuous population is quite natural for the paper’s subject. Let \(A\) be a finite alternative set, \(T\) a set of weak-ordering types, and \(\mu\) a rational distribution over the types. Define the majority relation by comparing the mass that ranks \(x\) at least as high as \(y\) with the mass that ranks \(y\) at least as high as \(x\). A plausible derived problem is: given the sparse distribution \(\mu\), determine whether this relation is transitive, returning a violating triple if it is not. The regime could be a large electorate with many repeated ballots and relatively few distinct rankings. Each type is a complete preference description, and mass is the share of the electorate holding it.

That is a credible population-continuous question about the paper’s topic, but it is not anchored in a named computational result of the paper. The nearest results are mathematical conditions for transitivity: Lemma 1 says at least two additional orderings are needed to destroy a strict three-person cycle; Theorem 1, proved here, gives a sufficient condition for transitivity with one cycle and at least five concerned individuals; Corollary 1 gives a necessary and sufficient condition using ER when two antagonistic cycles are present and there are at least nine individuals; and Corollary 2 gives the corresponding condition using Inada’s list. The corollaries draw on results by Sen and Pattanaik and by Inada. None asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithmic result.

The proposed recognition problem is straightforward to compute from \(\mu\): sum the masses for each pair of alternatives, then check transitivity. So the mirror is plausible as a continuous formulation of majority transitivity, but it does not uncover a computational question the paper studies. The weakness is decisive for this task: there is no qualifying named computational anchor, and presenting one of these transitivity conditions as such would overstate the paper’s contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper contains no named computational result to continuize. Lemma 1 is a finite-cardinality claim; Theorem 1 gives a sufficient condition for transitivity; Corollaries 1 and 2 give conditions for transitivity in a special case. The bO-bI section proposes an axiomatic framework. None asks for an algorithm or analyzes computational complexity.

The proponent’s recognition problem is a reasonable standalone question: given rational type masses, compute the pairwise majority relation and check its transitivity. But the paper does not pose that task. It follows directly from the definition of majority transitivity and can be checked by summing support masses and inspecting triples. It is therefore a new computational framing of the subject, not a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

The best versions of the theorem mirrors do not fix that gap. Replacing counts with masses in Theorem 1 or the corollaries could yield continuous statements about when majority preferences are transitive. Those remain characterizations, not computational questions the paper studied. One could also ask for the least mass of voters to change to restore transitivity. That may be a worthwhile ChoCo problem, but it adds an intervention objective absent from the paper; it is a new problem inspired by the paper’s topic.

I would not argue that high multiplicity is implausible: a large electorate with repeated rankings is a sensible regime. Nor can I honestly rule out every worthwhile computational problem inspired by this work. The negative case is narrower: none of the proposed routes supplies a continuous mirror of the paper’s results under the programme’s computational remit. The universal claim is weaker here than the evidence supports, because the paper’s transitivity questions could still motivate useful new algorithms.
