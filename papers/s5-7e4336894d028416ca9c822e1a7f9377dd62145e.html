The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible anchor in this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Examples 8–13 exhibit cycles on particular finite profiles, and the conclusion generalizes that observation in prose, but neither supplies the kind of named result your criteria require. So I cannot honestly give a continuous problem *per anchor*.

The strongest plausible positive angle is a continuous version of those cycle examples. A type could record a voter’s true ranking and weight; a state would give the mass of each type currently using each declared ranking. Nauru scores would be the mass-weighted sum of reciprocal rank positions, with the paper’s Copeland and lexicographic tie-breaking. The action would transfer mass between declared rankings according to one of the paper’s manipulative-move rules. One could ask whether a permitted sequence of such transfers returns to its starting state. Large electorates with repeated preference-and-weight classes make this a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the score calculation itself is naturally expressed over masses.

That remains only a prospective mirror, not a result anchored in this paper. The main weakness is that the paper studies one voter moving at a time: in an atomless population, a single voter has zero mass and cannot change the scores. Making a positive mass move together changes the dynamics to a cohort action, whose acceptance by the authors is uncertain. The paper also does not fully specify the move rules in the supplied text. So the continuous-population question is imaginable, but this paper does not establish the computational finding needed to make a strong anchored case for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to mirror. The paper’s numbered Examples 8–13 exhibit cycles on selected finite profiles; they do not give a theorem about cycle existence, convergence, or computational complexity. The conclusion reports observed cycles and suggests further analysis, but that is not a computational result of the kind the continuization programme is meant to map. The proponent’s own case acknowledges this gap: its proposed continuous question is prospective, not anchored in a result of the paper.

The natural population model also removes the mechanism behind those examples. Each transition is one voter changing their declaration. In an atomless society, that voter has zero mass, so changing their declaration does not change the Nauru scores or the winner. With utilities determined by the winning candidate, unilateral profitable moves disappear; the paper’s individual-move cycles therefore do not carry over as such.

The strongest repair is to let a positive mass of voters move together, perhaps grouping voters by true ranking and weight. That is a coherent aggregate model, but the moving mass now acts as a bloc rather than as the individual player in the paper. Another repair is to specify a population flow driven by voters’ responses to current scores. That may produce an interesting mean-field dynamics question, but it requires a revision protocol and behavioral assumptions that the examples do not supply. A complexity question about cycles or reachability could then be posed, but it would be a new problem built around those choices, not a computational finding this paper established.

The negative case is strongest against this paper as an anchor, and weaker as a claim that no continuous study could be worthwhile. Large electorates with repeated preference-and-weight classes are a plausible setting for aggregate dynamics. But the paper’s contribution is a collection of finite, one-voter transitions, not a computational result whose continuous-population complexity landscape it opens up.
