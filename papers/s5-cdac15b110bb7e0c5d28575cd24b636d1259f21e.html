The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 1, proved here, says that in every solvable roommate problem with strict preferences, the set of S-stochastically stable networks equals the set of core stable networks. Lemma 1 and Claims 1–3 establish path and resistance facts used in that proof; none classifies a problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or otherwise, and the paper gives no algorithmic analysis.

The most plausible positive direction is a large roommate or housing cohort with finitely many preference types. One could represent the population by type masses and a matching by masses of pairs between types and singles, then ask which matching measures are core stable—or arise as limits of S-stochastically stable matchings in finite replications. That is a plausible continuous-population model, but the paper does not define or analyze it. In particular, the limit of the stochastic-dynamics result would need its own formulation, and agents identical by type may require a type-level preference model beyond the paper’s strict individual preferences.

So this is an adjacent research question, not a continuous computational mirror grounded in a named result of this paper. Under the requested anchor rule, there is no per-anchor prover question to supply.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s anchor is not computational. Theorem 1 says that, in each solvable finite roommate problem, the S-stochastically stable networks are exactly the core stable networks. Lemma 1 and Claims 1–3 support that equality; the paper asks no decision or optimization question and gives no complexity or algorithmic result. Moving from individuals to type masses does not supply one. To get a computational question, someone must add a target such as finding a core stable matching measure. That may be worthwhile, but it is a new problem rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The roommate or housing cohort proposed by the other analyst is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The objection is not that its population cannot sensibly be represented by types and pair masses. A stronger candidate mirror would ask which core stable matching measures arise as limits of S-stochastically stable networks in finite replications. If both sides are defined through the same finite replicas, Theorem 1 gives equality in every replica, so any common limit operation preserves that equality. If instead the continuous society gets its own stochastic dynamics, an individual mutation changes zero mass; choices about the size and resistance of mutations then define a new dynamic model that the theorem does not specify. Either route yields a stability question, not a computational result anchored in this paper.

A continuous algorithm for finding core stable matching measures could still be a useful project. The case against is that it would add the computational target the paper itself never studies. Across plausible population scenarios, the paper’s contribution remains an equivalence between two solution concepts. That makes the negative case strong under ChoCo’s requirement for a computational anchor, though not airtight as a claim that no worthwhile follow-on project could be built from the model.
