The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), \(k\) initial districts, a target \(c\in C\), a rational movement budget \(\beta\), and rational masses \(\mu_{i,a}\) over supported types \((i,a)\) (initial district and top-ranked candidate), decide whether there are assignments \(x_{i,a,j}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_j x_{i,a,j}=\mu_{i,a}\) and \(\sum_{i,a}\sum_{j\ne i}x_{i,a,j}\le\beta\), such that \(c\) is the unique overall winner. For \(q_{j,a}=\sum_i x_{i,a,j}\) and \(W_j=\arg\max_{a\in C}q_{j,a}\), this means \(\sum_j\mathbf{1}[c\in W_j]>\sum_j\mathbf{1}[a\in W_j]\) for every \(a\ne c\), counting every district-level tied winner as in the paper.

The model it lives in

Represent the society by rational masses \(\mu_{i,a}\), and choose mass assignments \(x_{i,a,j}\) from each type's initial district \(i\) to final district \(j\). Minimize moved mass \(\sum_{i,a}\sum_{j\ne i}x_{i,a,j}\), subject to mass conservation and the condition that \(c\) wins strictly more districts than every rival under district plurality, with ties counted for each tied candidate.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's uniform-cost, no-connectivity \(\mathrm{MRGM}\) decision problem. It does not establish continuous counterparts of the graph-connectivity or non-uniform-cost results, or of the paper's special-case algorithms.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of this decision problem in \(m\), \(k\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\); does fractional assignment permit a polynomial-time algorithm or is the continuous problem hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Continuous Minimum Reverse Gerrymandering (CMRG), based on Theorem 2, proved in this paper. The theorem says that \(\mathrm{MRGM}\) is NP-complete: given alternatives, an initial partition of voters into districts, a budget, and a target candidate \(c\), decide whether moving at most that many voters—at uniform cost per move—can make \(c\) the unique winner of the election. Districts need not be connected, and the number of alternatives is unrestricted. The paper proves hardness by reduction from Exact Cover by 3-Sets.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large online or organizational election with many members in each electoral division. Administrators can change members’ district assignments, and plurality depends only on each member’s top-ranked candidate. A type is therefore a pair \((i,a)\): the member’s original district \(i\) and top choice \(a\). If \(N\) is the population, there may be \(N\gg\tau\) members but only \(\tau\le km\) supported types, where \(k\) is the number of districts and \(m\) the number of candidates. This uses the paper’s reverse-gerrymandering setting, which explicitly allows elections without a geographic connectivity requirement.

Here is the continuous decision problem. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), \(k\) districts, a target \(c\in C\), a rational mass distribution \(\mu_{i,a}\ge0\) over supported types \((i,a)\), with \(\sum_{i,a}\mu_{i,a}=1\), and a rational movement budget \(\beta\). A solution is a nonnegative assignment \(x_{i,a,j}\), where \(x_{i,a,j}\) is the mass of type \((i,a)\) placed in district \(j\), satisfying
\[ \sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{i,a,j}=\mu_{i,a} \quad\text{for every }(i,a), \qquad \sum_{i,a}\sum_{j\ne i}x_{i,a,j}\le\beta. \]
The final plurality score of candidate \(a\) in district \(j\) is \(q_{j,a}=\sum_i x_{i,a,j}\). Let \(W_j\) be the set of candidates tied for the maximum score in district \(j\). The assignment succeeds exactly when
\[ \sum_j \mathbf 1[c\in W_j] > \sum_j \mathbf 1[a\in W_j] \quad\text{for every }a\ne c. \]
The question is whether such an assignment exists. Individuals still belong to one district; the mass allows members of a type to be distributed across districts. The budget \(\beta\) is the fraction of the population moved, corresponding to \(B/N\) in a discrete election. The natural complexity parameters are \(m,k,\tau\), and the bit length \(L\) of the rational input.

This is a close analogue of the authors’ problem: it preserves the initial districts, district-level plurality, reassignment action, uniform movement cost, and unique overall winner condition. Only the electorate is replaced by a distribution over types. It covers Theorem 2’s \(\mathrm{MRGM}\) result; it does not purport to mirror the graph-connectivity variants.

My tentative prediction is Class C, because the district winner indicators and the requirement that \(c\) win more districts than every rival retain a combinatorial choice even when voters are divisible. The question is whether that structure still makes CMRG hard, or whether a flow or continuous-optimization method can handle it in time polynomial in \(m,k,\tau,L\). Natural follow-ups include tractability for fixed \(m\) or \(k\), and how continuous solutions relate to large finite elections.

The soft spot is substantial: Theorem 2’s exact-cover proof does not establish hardness for CMRG. In its construction, each set district starts in a tie; an arbitrarily small positive mass of \(c\)-voters moved from the central district can break that tie. Fractional movement may therefore bypass the whole-voter structure that enforces exact cover. That weakness makes the complexity question more open, but it does not make it a different problem: CMRG is a direct, precise high-multiplicity version of the paper’s own \(\mathrm{MRGM}\) question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The MRGM mirror is the part of the case I cannot honestly defeat. For this problem, a voter’s initial district and top choice contain everything relevant: plurality ignores the rest of the ranking, and every move has the same cost. So types \((i,a)\) are complete, movement is a mass transfer, and the budget is naturally the fraction of the population moved. A large organization or online election with many members in each division and freely changeable division assignments is a credible high-multiplicity setting. Keeping the number of districts finite does not stop the population from being the continuous object.

The strongest objection is narrower: the paper’s geographic motivation depends on connected districts. A mass distribution over \((i,a)\) types does not preserve voters’ locations or the boundaries that make a district connected. A spatial continuous model might be worth studying, but it would be a different, more elaborate mirror. That does not defeat the MRGM anchor: the paper explicitly defines the no-connectivity variant, and organizational elections supply a plausible setting for it.

The closest continuous computational work I found studies other election attacks, but does not cover district reassignment, so it is adjacent prior art rather than a collision. [The continuous bribery study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25444) therefore does not close this question. The exact-cover proof for discrete MRGM also does not establish hardness for the mirror: divisible mass can evade the whole-voter gadgets. But that weakens the proponent’s Class C prediction, not the case for asking the question; a tractability result could be valuable too.

So the universal negative case is weak. The proposed MRGM mirror appears well-posed, population-continuous, and plausible in at least one regime. I would not claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
