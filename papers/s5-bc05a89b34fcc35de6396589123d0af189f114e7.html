The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: under assumptions (A.1)–(A.5)—including taste anonymity in consumption and production, compactness of feasible public outputs under bounded inputs, strict small group effectiveness, and desirability of private-goods endowments—an equilibrium exists. This is a substantial result about a population distributed across crowding and taste types. A plausible regime is a large metropolitan area with millions of residents but relatively few complete types: mass records each type’s share, while each jurisdiction still has a finite, integral membership.

But Theorem 5 is an equilibrium-existence theorem, not a computational result. The paper gives no named theorem or proposition about an algorithm, complexity class, or computational formulation. So it cannot serve as the required anchor. Nor can I state a precise computational mirror of it without adding an encoding and changing or restricting the model: jurisdictions may have unbounded finite sizes, public projects come from a metric space, and utilities and technologies are not given as finite computational inputs.

The paper therefore suggests a worthwhile future question—how hard it is to compute an anonymous price-taking equilibrium from an encoded distribution over types—but it does not provide a compliant anchor for that question. It also already takes the population to be a continuum. Its results do not settle computational complexity, but a proposed “mirror” of its equilibrium-existence result would need to explain what discrete problem is being mirrored, rather than simply restate the paper’s continuous model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5 is the proponent’s only anchor, and it cannot support a continuous mirror: it proves equilibrium existence in an economy whose population is already a continuum. The theorem says nothing about the complexity of a computational problem. The same is true of the paper’s other named results: they establish core and equilibrium properties, not algorithms or complexity bounds.

The proposed “compute an equilibrium” question is the strongest repair, but it adds a computational model the paper does not give. The paper permits an abstract metric space of public projects and arbitrary continuous utility and production data; it gives no finite encoding from which to define input length or running time. Restricting those primitives to finitely encoded data could make an algorithmic question well-posed, but that would be a new, restricted computational model—not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. And because the population is already continuous here, it would not be a continuization of the society either.

So the negative case is strong for rejecting this paper as an anchor in the programme: there is no computational result to mirror, and its central model already has the continuous population. The broader claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile computational research is weaker. A suitably encoded special case might be interesting, but that would be a separate research direction rather than a continuous mirror of this paper.
