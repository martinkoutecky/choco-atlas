The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is categorical: this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its formal results are probability and welfare formulas, followed by empirical calibration. None classifies an algorithmic problem by complexity or presents an algorithm whose computational question could be continuized. The proponent recognizes this, so their proposed “Mass Deterrence-Intervention Design” is a follow-on question, not an anchor from the paper.

That follow-on adds little computationally under the paper’s assumptions. Acceptance and pressure are independent for each representative doctor, and the model explicitly ignores competition among doctors. For types \(t\) with masses \(\mu_t\) and acceptance probabilities \(a_t\), the common-swap design reduces to a scalar choice: minimize \(\epsilon\sum_t\mu_t a_t^2\), subject to \(\epsilon\ge\alpha/a_t\) for every type and \(\epsilon\le1/2\). The population contributes weights and a minimum; it creates no coupling or pricing problem. Allowing a more general finite swap policy could produce an LP, but that would add a policy-design problem rather than continue a computational result in this paper.

The obvious stronger rescue is to model the actual many-doctor deferred-acceptance market, with program capacities and competition, then study how pressure and randomization affect welfare. That is a plausible research question, but it restores interactions the paper deliberately sets aside and needs a fuller market model. It is not a continuization of any result established here.

So the negative case is strong against a paper-anchored continuous computational mirror. It cannot honestly establish the universal claim that no worthwhile related scenario exists: residency markets plainly admit large populations, and a coupled model may be valuable. The case’s force comes from the paper’s lack of a computational target, not from proving that every future population model would be uninteresting.
