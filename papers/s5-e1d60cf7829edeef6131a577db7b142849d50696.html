The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest conclusion is a qualified positive one: this paper admits a sensible high-multiplicity mirror, but it does not provide a qualifying computational anchor under your rules. Theorem 1 through Theorem 5 are axiomatic characterizations, not results asserting membership in P, hardness, parameterized complexity, or an explicit computational formulation. They are proved in this paper. Thus the formal number of computational anchors is zero, and I would not relabel one of them as a complexity theorem.

If structural theorems are nevertheless admissible as provisional anchors, Theorem 4 is the strongest. It states, and proves here, that a rule satisfies scale invariance, downstream impartiality, and upstream invariance if and only if it is an \(R^\alpha\) rule. For \(\alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{n-1})\in[0,1]^{n-1}\), with \(\alpha_n=1\), agent \(i\) retains \(\alpha_i e_i\), while the remaining \((1-\alpha_i)e_i\) is divided equally among all downstream agents. Equivalently, \(R^\alpha_i(e)=\alpha_i e_i+\sum_{k<i}(1-\alpha_k)e_k/(n-k)\).

A credible continuous-population version would be the following.

Call it Continuous Riparian Rule Recognition. There are \(\tau\) ordered reach-types. Type \(i\) represents a large population of otherwise indistinguishable water users located at reach \(i\), with population mass \(\mu_i\), \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and per-capita local inflow \(e_i\). Their aggregate inflow is \(\mu_i e_i\). Let \(D_k=\sum_{j>k}\mu_j\) be the downstream population mass after reach \(k\).

Given rational \((\mu,e)\) and an observed vector of per-capita rights \(q\), the task is to find \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{\tau-1}\in[0,1]\), or report that none exists, such that the rights are generated by the mass version of Theorem 4:

\(x_i^\alpha=\alpha_i e_i+\sum_{k<i}\frac{(1-\alpha_k)\mu_k e_k}{D_k}\).

The aggregate rights \(y_i=\mu_i x_i^\alpha\) must satisfy the corresponding feasibility conditions: \(\sum_i y_i=\sum_i\mu_i e_i\), and \(\sum_{i\le k}y_i\le\sum_{i\le k}\mu_i e_i\) for every upstream prefix. The decision variable is the type-level retention vector \(\alpha\); the objective is to determine whether the observed allocation belongs to the axiomatically characterized family, and, if so, recover \(\alpha\).

This is a genuine population continuization. The mass \(\mu_i\) is population mass, not fractional water. The per-capita inflow \(e_i\) is held fixed while aggregate supply scales as \(\mu_i e_i\). The denominator \(D_k\) is forced by equal sharing among downstream people. When every type has equal mass \(1/n\), the formula reduces exactly to the paper’s finite formula.

The regime is plausible for a system of many farms, irrigation units, or water-user associations arranged along a managed river or canal. A type records the complete relevant description: reach, per-capita inflow, and any legal priority class used by the rule. A realistic instance might have millions of users but only \(5\)–\(30\) reach/inflow types. This is much more convincing than treating the five Nile countries as a continuum, but it preserves the paper’s underlying allocation problem and its ATS/UTI/TIBS interpretation.

The problem should be tractable. Given \(\alpha\), the rights can be evaluated by direct summation in polynomial time, and recognition reduces to checking linear equations and bounds on the \(\alpha_i\). There is no hardness transfer to expect, since the paper contains no discrete hardness result. The most natural further questions are whether the characterizations in Theorems 1, 2, and 5 survive with mass-weighted downstream sharing, and whether heterogeneous inflows within a reach require parameters indexed by the full type rather than by location alone.

The weakest point is decisive: this is primarily a structural and evaluation problem, not a computational-complexity problem. The paper never studies algorithms, and the resulting continuous evaluation problem is almost transparently easy. Moreover, the mass-weighted denominators and per-capita inflows are necessary co-modifications rather than results already justified by the authors. So the paper supports the claim that a meaningful continuous-population mirror exists, especially for high-multiplicity irrigation systems, but it does not support a strong ChoCo claim about a new complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this is the wrong kind of paper for the programme. It contains no computational anchor at all. Theorem 1 through Theorem 5 are axiomatic characterizations of allocation rules; they do not state a complexity bound, hardness result, algorithmic formulation, or optimization problem. The Nile application is descriptive. Thus there is no computational question here whose continuous-population version could produce a ChoCo complexity result.

The proponent’s Theorem 4 construction does not repair that gap. “Given observed rights \(q\), recover \(\alpha\)” is an inverse-identification problem invented after the fact. The paper never treats \(q\) as input and never asks whether an allocation belongs to the characterized family. This wrapper could be attached to any explicit rule in any axiomatic paper. It is not a continuous mirror of Theorem 4’s content, which is the logical equivalence between three axioms and a formula.

The more natural forward version is even less distinctive. Given \(\mu\), \(e\), and \(\alpha\), one evaluates

\[ x_i^\alpha = \alpha_i e_i+ \sum_{k<i} \frac{(1-\alpha_k)\mu_k e_k}{\sum_{j>k}\mu_j}. \]

This is a direct finite sum. If \(\alpha\) is to be chosen to fit observations or satisfy linear constraints, the resulting problem is ordinary finite-dimensional linear or convex optimization. That may be a perfectly valid water-allocation exercise, but it does not expose a complexity landscape, a pricing problem, or a high-multiplicity relaxation of a difficult discrete problem. The continuum merely changes coefficients in a formula whose evaluation was already immediate.

The proposed population model also requires more than a high-multiplicity interpretation of the paper. In the paper, \(e_i\) is the inflow associated with one territorial agent at position \(i\), and the denominator \(n-i\) counts downstream agents. Replacing this with per-capita inflow and downstream mass changes both the supply model and the rule: aggregate supply becomes \(\mu_i e_i\), and equal sharing is now by population mass. Those are defensible modelling choices, but the corresponding mass-weighted axioms and characterization would be new results, not continuizations already latent in Theorem 4.

One can certainly tell a plausible story about many farms or irrigation users grouped into a few reach-types. That is the negative case’s genuine weakness: the resulting population model is not nonsensical, and the authors might recognize it as a useful extension. But it is an adjacent axiomatic allocation model, not a worthwhile computational mirror of this paper. Holding total physical inflow fixed would require a different per-capita formulation; letting supply scale with \(\mu_i\) models many replicated sources rather than many users of the same river. Either choice requires substantive new economics.

The remaining named results do not offer stronger anchors. Theorems 1–3 and 5 are the same kind of axiomatic characterizations, and the empirical section has no computational claim. Therefore the proponent has identified a coherent continuous reinterpretation, but not a ChoCo problem. Under the programme’s stated gate, the paper should receive no continuous mirror.
