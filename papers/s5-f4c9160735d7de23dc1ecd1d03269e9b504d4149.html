The case FOR (proponent)

There is no anchor-compliant computational result in this paper. Theorem 4.1, proved here, says that every finite society satisfying compactness and continuity (\(C\)), positive population-responsiveness (\(PPR\)), and consecutive support (\(CS\)) has a Core–Tiebout equilibrium. Theorem 5.1, also proved here, gives existence when feasible sets are monotonic and each decision rule permits the coalition’s Pareto set. Proposition 5.1 shows that a single-valued decision rule can leave the coalition structure core empty, even under the paper’s stated restrictions. These are existence results, not claims about polynomial time, hardness, or another complexity class. Section 3 gives a constructive solution, but no input encoding or running-time analysis. So there is no named computational anchor here, and no honest way to supply a continuous problem *per computational anchor*.

The strongest positive case is instead a plausible new ChoCo question suggested by the paper’s subject. In a large metropolitan region, let the finitely many types describe residents’ public-policy preferences and relevant characteristics, with type \(t\) occupying rational mass \(\mu_t\). A community is described by its composition \(q\), and its feasible public alternatives are given by \(\Phi(q)\). One precise finite-input version could use a finite alternative set \(X\) and, for each \(x\in X\), a rational polytope \(Q_x\) describing the compositions for which \(x\in\Phi(q)\).

The problem would be: find a finite collection of community compositions and alternatives \((q^j,x_j)\), with \(\sum_j q^j=\mu\) and \(x_j\in\Phi(q^j)\), that is stable. Stability requires that residents do not prefer another community’s alternative, and that no feasible coalition can be formed from masses of residents who all strictly prefer its alternative to what they currently receive. There is no numerical objective in the paper’s underlying problem; a solution is a stable formation, or a report that none exists.

This has a credible high-multiplicity setting: millions of residents, but relatively few preference and characteristic types. Community membership and feasible public packages depend on aggregate composition, so masses are natural decision variables. The question preserves the paper’s central subject—endogenous coalitions and stability—rather than merely making outcomes or uncertainty continuous.

The weak point is substantial: the paper allows feasible sets to depend on the *named coalition* \(\phi(S)\), while this mirror requires a composition-based technology \(\Phi(q)\). Its proof also uses a finite ordered set of individuals and a recursively chosen largest “benevolent dictator.” Neither the complexity classification nor that construction transfers directly. This is a promising population-continuum research question, but it is not a computational result established by the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with a mismatch: this paper proves existence, not computational complexity. Theorem 4.1 says a Core–Tiebout equilibrium exists under \(C\), \(PPR\), and \(CS\); Theorem 5.1 gives a second existence condition; Proposition 5.1 gives a counterexample to existence. Section 3 supplies a recursive construction for finite societies, with no encoded input or running-time analysis. None is a computational result to mirror. Continuizing any of them as stated would ask another existence question.

The proposed search problem addresses that gap by adding a finite set of types, rational masses, finite alternatives, and polyhedral community technologies. That is a coherent model, and its population interpretation is plausible. It also makes substantial choices the paper does not make: feasible alternatives become functions of type composition, and the continuous objects receive a particular finite encoding. The paper allows arbitrary coalition-indexed feasible correspondences and compact alternative spaces. Its existence theorem therefore does not specify which computational problem this polyhedral version should answer, or which technology assumptions would make it representative.

The best replacement mirror would keep the central question—find a stable formation—and use a type-mass technology with an explicit finite encoding. Yet that remains a new algorithmic theory of community formation. The paper gives no reason to expect its consecutive-benevolence construction to survive when the finite sequence of dictators is replaced by divisible masses. Nor does it provide a pricing problem, objective, or other computational structure that would make this model a particularly strong ChoCo target. Those could emerge from studying it, but that possibility is the case for doing new work, not evidence that the paper itself supplies a promising computational mirror.

I would not claim the universal negative is airtight. A large population with repeated preference and characteristic types is a convincing regime here; mass blocking and type-level envy are meaningful, and neither vanishes merely because individuals have measure zero. The negative case is strong against treating this paper as a source of named computational results. It is much weaker against the proposed finite-type research question, which preserves the paper’s subject and could be worth pursuing.
