The case FOR (proponent)

The positive foothold is that a large participatory-budgeting electorate can sensibly be represented by a distribution over approval-ballot types: each type is an approval set \(t\subseteq P\), and its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters holding that ballot. Given project costs, impact labels and a budget, one could compute the utilitarian-greedy and equal-shares outcomes and compare their impact or novelty metrics. Large citywide elections make that population regime plausible.

But this paper contains no eligible named computational result to anchor that mirror. Its four numbered key results are empirical findings about impact and novelty, not numbered theorems, lemmas, corollaries or propositions asserting complexity or an algorithmic classification. The paper applies existing aggregation methods to datasets; it does not classify the complexity of a problem. So I can’t honestly state a continuous problem *per anchor* or predict whether an anchor’s hardness transfers, dissolves or arises specifically in the continuum. The ballot-distribution formulation is a possible research question inspired by the paper, but it would not be a mirror of one of its named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is the proponent’s own admission: there is no named computational result here to mirror. The four numbered results are empirical findings about impact and novelty in observed participatory-budgeting elections. The paper does not classify a problem’s complexity or present an algorithmic result. That is the decisive gap for a ChoCo mirror; it is not an objection about whether the answer would change, or whether high multiplicity is sensible.

The proponent’s best foothold is coherent: represent voters by a distribution \(\mu\) over approval-ballot types, define the mass version of equal shares, and compare its project outcomes with utilitarian greedy using the paper’s metrics. Large city elections make that population model plausible. But for the observed elections, \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) is a high-multiplicity encoding of the same ballot counts. Computing the outcomes and metrics in that representation reproduces the empirical pipeline; it does not continueize a computational result asserted by the paper. For hypothetical continuous societies, the paper makes no claim about the resulting outcomes. Establishing such claims would be new work.

That distinction holds for all four findings: impact loss and gain, novelty, cost and popularity controls, and the two case studies are all reported comparisons, not computational anchors. One could pose a new algorithmic question—for example, how to choose campaign interventions that improve an impact metric under a mass-transfer budget—but the paper supplies neither that optimization problem nor its intervention costs and objective. It would be a research direction inspired by the discussion, not a mirror of a result in the paper.

I would not argue that the population model is meaningless: it is a natural representation for participatory budgeting. That makes the broader assertion that *no worthwhile continuous research question could arise in any scenario* hard to defend. The stronger, supportable negative is narrower: this paper offers no named computational result for a continuous mirror to anchor on, and the proposed ballot-distribution formulation is a plausible new analysis rather than such a mirror.
