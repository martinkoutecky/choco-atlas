What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered; Theorem 1 and Corollaries 1 and 2 are axiomatic characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is the paper’s hospital appointment setting: a large service could have many patients but relatively few types defined by clinical priority class, value for an appointment, and out-of-pocket reservation price. A continuous model could represent each type by its population share \(\mu_t\), scale appointment capacity per capita, and track the mass of each type receiving an appointment. That makes a plausible high-multiplicity scenario.

But the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor that mirror. Theorem 1, proved in the paper, characterizes mechanisms: a mechanism satisfies minimal tradability, individual rationality, strategy-proofness, consistency, independence of unallocated objects, and non-wasteful tie-breaking if and only if it is a serial dictatorship with some reservation-price vector \(r\) and priority ordering \(\succ\). Corollary 1 gives the single-object version without independence of unallocated objects; Corollary 2 replaces individual rationality with non-imposition. These are axiomatic characterizations, not results about a problem being in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally tractable. The paper gives no computational problem or input representation for finding or recognizing such mechanisms.

There is also a fit issue for the candidate scenario: the theorem’s priority ordering is a strict order over individuals. A finite-type population model would need to aggregate people into priority classes, which could preserve aggregate allocations when patients in a class are otherwise identical, but the paper does not formulate or analyze that aggregation. A continuous axiomatic characterization could be imagined, yet it would not supply the computational mirror ChoCo seeks.

So I cannot state a continuous computational problem per anchor or predict its complexity: the required anchors are absent. The appointment example supports the plausibility of a high-multiplicity regime, but not a positive computational-mirror case grounded in this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that none of the paper’s named results states a computational problem. Theorem 1 characterizes mechanisms by axioms; Corollaries 1 and 2 change the axiom list, but remain characterizations. The lemmas and axiom-independence examples do not supply a complexity result either. In particular, the paper gives no finite input for “finding” the reservation prices and priority order: its mechanism is a function over all finite populations, utility profiles, and object supplies. The priority order ranges over named individuals, and the prices are indexed by them. Calling those parameters “endogenous” means they are implied by the axioms, not that the paper gives an algorithm for recovering them from encoded data.

The appointment setting is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and that is the strongest part of the case for a mirror. One could group patients by clinical priority, value, and out-of-pocket price, represent each group by a mass, and scale appointment capacity with population. But this does not make Theorem 1 computational. If priority within a group can decide who gets a scarce appointment, the group description omits relevant order; adding individual queue positions can make the support grow with the population. Treating a positive mass as tied instead requires a mass-level priority and tie-breaking model that the paper does not give. Either choice can motivate a new model, but neither supplies a computational question already anchored in the theorem.

The best repaired proposal would be to define that finite-type, mass-allocation model and ask for an algorithm to compute allocations or recognize mechanisms satisfying mass analogues of the axioms. That could be a sensible project in its own right. It would require choosing the type encoding, the treatment of priority ties, and the mass versions of consistency and the other axioms; it would not be a continuous computational counterpart of a result in this paper. So the negative case is strongest against a *mirror of this paper’s results*. It does not show that no worthwhile computational project could be built around appointment rationing—a universal claim broader than the evidence supports.
