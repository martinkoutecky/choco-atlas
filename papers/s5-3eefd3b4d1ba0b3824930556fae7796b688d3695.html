What the mirror covers

Proposition 1, Theorem 1, and Theorem 2 are axiomatic impossibility or existence results; none supplies a computational result for a continuous-population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see an honest continuous mirror with a qualifying anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered result asserting that a computational problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Its numbered results concern whether social welfare functions exist with specified axioms.

The closest anchors are Proposition 1, proved here, which says there is no SWF on \([0,1]^{\mathbb N}\) satisfying Hammond Equity and Restricted Weak Dominance; Theorem 1, also proved here, which says that if \(|\bar Y|\ge 4\), no SWF on \(\bar Y^{\mathbb N}\) satisfies Hammond Equity and Strong Pareto; and Theorem 2, proved here, which constructs an SWF on \((\mathbb N\cup\{0\})^{\mathbb N}\) satisfying Hammond Equity+, Anonymity, Weak Pareto, and Weak Dominance. None is a computational result. The construction in Theorem 2 is a construction of a welfare function, not an algorithm for a finitely encoded problem.

The most charitable population reading is to treat generations as agents and their utility levels as types. But a generation’s position in the stream matters: the axioms compare particular coordinates, and HEF distinguishes the present from the future. Include position in the type, and there are infinitely many distinct roles, so the model does not have a small support of interchangeable types. Drop position and represent the stream by a distribution over utility levels, and the representation loses the ordering and coordinate-specific comparisons the results use. A large society with repeated cohorts might motivate a new model, but it would not preserve these anchors.

The paper’s use of \([0,1]\) is continuity in the feasible utility domain; the agents remain countably many, temporally ordered generations. A continuum-time welfare model could be posed as a separate axiomatic project, but it would not supply the computational questions this programme requires. So I cannot state a prover-ready continuous problem per anchor, or classify one as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard. The paper offers a possible starting point for a different model, but no defensible ChoCo case anchored in its results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 are impossibility results about whether *any* social welfare function satisfies a set of axioms; Theorem 2 is an existence construction. Their claims quantify over all streams in \(X\), not over a finite input to an algorithm. Asking whether those claims have continuous analogues does not create the missing computational task.

The strongest candidate around Proposition 1 would replace the countable sequence of generations with a continuum of dates, then ask whether Hammond Equity and Restricted Weak Dominance can coexist for welfare functions on that population. But the relevant dominance axiom changes one coordinate and leaves all others fixed. In a nonatomic population, changing one date affects zero mass. If welfare depends only on the population distribution, that change is invisible; if it can detect the change, the model must retain date-specific information that a distribution over types does not encode. Replacing a single-date change with a positive-mass cohort change would make the axiom meaningful, but it would be a new existence question, not a computational mirror of Proposition 1.

Theorem 1 has the same problem in a particularly clear form: its \(\bar Y\) is the set of *utility levels* available to each generation. The result already allows any such set with at least four elements. Making utility levels continuous would continuize the feasible-state domain, while making dates continuous runs into the coordinate problem above. Neither move supplies a finite computational input or an algorithmic objective.

Theorem 2 is the most promising candidate because it constructs a welfare function. One might ask whether such a function can be represented and evaluated efficiently on finitely encoded streams. But the theorem concerns a function on all of \((\mathbb N\cup\{0\})^{\mathbb N}\), and its construction selects representatives from classes of streams that eventually agree. It provides no finite representation or evaluation procedure. Restricting the domain to finitely encoded streams and requiring computability would add the assumptions that define the problem; the theorem itself would not answer it. A continuum of dates would still leave the same question of how the function treats changes at individual, measure-zero dates.

A computational theory of representable intergenerational welfare functions could be worthwhile. It would need to specify finite encodings, admissible welfare functions, and how equity applies to positive-mass cohorts. Those choices would form a new model and research question. The paper offers no computational result for that question, and its axiomatic results do not make a continuous-population mirror worth ChoCo’s time.
