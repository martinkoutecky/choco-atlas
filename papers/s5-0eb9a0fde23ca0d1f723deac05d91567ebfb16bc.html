The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), a target \(c^\star\), and an explicitly listed support of \(\tau\) strict rankings \(t\) with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, promise that all induced pairwise margins \(M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t-\sum_{t:\,b\succ_t a}\mu_t\) are nonzero and have distinct absolute values; decide whether \(c^\star\) is the Simple Stable Voting winner of the tournament defined by \(M_\mu\).

The model it lives in

Each type is a complete ranking \(t\) carrying mass \(\mu_t\); compute the normalized pairwise margins \(M_\mu(a,b)\) and apply Simple Stable Voting to their tournament. There are no actions or optimization variables: the decision is whether the designated candidate wins.

The objection that survived

Every rational-mass instance can be scaled to integer pairwise margins of polynomial bit length, so the paper’s weighted-tournament result already classifies this mirror without a new complexity argument; the proponent acknowledges margin sufficiency but does not state this full equivalence.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers designated-winner determination for Simple Stable Voting, with the analogous Stable Voting question covered by Theorem 5.8; it leaves the implementation, average-case, and total-search questions untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the parameterized complexity of winner determination in the support size \(\tau\), and do natural restrictions on supported rankings yield tractable cases?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is winner determination from a distribution over complete ballots. A type \(t\) is a strict ranking of the candidates, and its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. For each pair \(a,b\), compute the per-capita majority margin
\[ M_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t-\sum_{t:\,b\succ_t a}\mu_t. \]
The distribution is the input; there is no action or optimization variable. The question is whether a designated candidate wins under the rule applied to the tournament of these margins. I would restrict inputs to nonzero margins with distinct absolute values, so the tournament is uniquely weighted as in the hardness construction.

This is a credible population regime for a large public poll or referendum: many people cast one of relatively few identical rankings, so the number of voters \(n\) can greatly exceed the number of supported types \(\tau\). In fact, the paper’s reduction can be realized in just this regime. For a tournament with \(N\) candidates and \(E=\binom N2\) pairs, Appendix A constructs a profile with \(E(E+1)=\Theta(N^4)\) voters and at most \(2E=O(N^2)\) ballot types. Turning those counts into rational masses gives \(\tau=O(N^2)\) while \(n=\Theta(N^4)\). The rankings are complete voter types, and the masses preserve the normalized margins: dividing all margins by \(n\) changes neither their signs nor their order.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It states that winner determination for Simple Stable Voting is PSPACE-complete: given a weighted tournament \(G\) and a designated candidate \(A\), deciding whether \(\operatorname{SSV}(G)=A\) is in PSPACE and PSPACE-hard. The paper proves hardness by reducing from TQBF, constructing a tournament whose winner is \(C\) exactly when the formula is true and \(D\) otherwise.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous SSV Winner. An instance consists of a candidate set \(C\), an explicitly listed support of \(\tau\) rankings over \(C\), positive binary-rational masses summing to one, and a target \(c^\star\). Form \(M_\mu\) as above, and ask whether \(c^\star\) is the SSV winner of the induced uniquely weighted tournament. The answer is yes or no. I expect this problem to be PSPACE-complete, in Class B: every finite profile gives rational type masses with exactly the same winner, and the paper’s TQBF hardness construction has a polynomial-size realization as such a distribution. Membership remains in PSPACE because the rational margins can be computed exactly in polynomial space, after which the recursive winner computation uses polynomial space.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Theorem 5.8, also proved here. It states that winner determination for Stable Voting is PSPACE-complete: given a weighted tournament and a designated candidate, deciding whether that candidate is the SV winner is PSPACE-complete. The proof reuses the TQBF construction and shows that SV’s extra undefeated-candidate condition does not change the outcome where the reduction needs it.

Its mirror, Continuous SV Winner, has the same input format and margin promise as Continuous SSV Winner, and asks whether the target is the SV winner of \(M_\mu\). I likewise expect PSPACE-completeness in Class B, by the same profile realization and normalization. This is not an independent hardness construction; it is a second rule-specific mirror of the paper’s extension of the result. Theorem 4.1 is the stronger lead because it carries the full reduction, while Theorem 5.8 shows that the additional SV condition preserves the complexity result.

These are author-recognizable questions: the paper begins from voting profiles, works with their pairwise-margin tournaments because they contain all the information SV and SSV use, and proves that the constructed tournaments can be induced by profiles. The mirror changes the electorate’s representation to rational ranking masses while keeping the same winner predicate. It covers winner determination for the two rules only; it does not mirror the implementation results, average-case questions, or the conjecture about a total-search class.

The weakest point is also clear: this is an exact but scientifically modest mirror. Once the distribution is mapped to pairwise margins, the rule depends only on that tournament; it does not use the richer type distribution. The recursive candidate-deletion structure drives the hardness, and it survives normalization unchanged. So this is a solid Class B boundary result, not an example where continuization unlocks new optimization machinery or removes multiplicity-driven hardness.

It suggests useful follow-up questions: does PSPACE-hardness persist when \(\tau\) is much smaller relative to the number of candidates, or under natural restrictions on ballot rankings? Can small support yield algorithms parameterized by \(\tau\)? And which structural restrictions on the margin tournament make the recursive computation tractable?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that their continuous versions are already covered by the paper’s computational model. Theorem 4.1 classifies SSV winner determination on weighted tournaments, and Theorem 5.8 does the same for SV. For either rule, the ranking distribution matters only through its pairwise margins and their order.

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\), compute \(M_\mu(a,b)\) for each candidate pair and clear their denominators with a common positive integer \(q\). The resulting weights \(qM_\mu(a,b)\) are integers of polynomial bit length, and they preserve the margin order and winner. So the proposed continuous decision problems reduce directly to the problems in the two theorems. In the other direction, Appendix A realizes the hardness construction as a profile with polynomially many voters and at most \(2\binom m2\) ballot types; normalizing its voter counts gives rational masses. The reduction therefore yields hard continuous instances with support size \(O(m^2)\).

This is stronger than saying a continuous version would have the same answer. The paper deliberately removes voter-level data, proves that its weighted-tournament representation suffices, and shows that its hard instances come from voting profiles. Every rational ranking distribution can be handled through that representation, and the paper’s existing PSPACE algorithms and reductions transfer. A general Continuous SSV Winner or Continuous SV Winner result would chiefly spell out this encoding equivalence.

The same point applies to the best direct versions of both mirrors: exact normalized margins, a promise of nonzero distinct absolute margins, and the same target-winner predicate. That promise avoids tie issues while retaining the reduction. SV’s undefeated-candidate condition adds no new population question because the margins determine the same paths and defeat tests.

A support-sensitive version is the strongest possible rescue. One could ask whether SSV or SV becomes tractable when the distribution has few ranking types, or analyze complexity in \(\tau\). That is a legitimate restricted-instance question, but \(\tau\) is a property of the distribution’s representation; the rules themselves see only the margins. Moreover, the paper already realizes every hard tournament with polynomial support. A small-\(\tau\) result could be valuable, but it would study the structure of tournaments induced by few rankings rather than uncover a new general continuous mirror.

I can’t make the universal negative case airtight. Voting has a credible high-multiplicity regime, the mass model does not lose information needed by these rules, and support-sensitive algorithms might be useful. The strongest honest objection is narrower: the two proposed unparameterized mirrors do not open a new complexity landscape, because the paper’s own tournament formulation and realization argument already cover them. A claim that no worthwhile question can be built in any scenario would go beyond what the paper supports.
