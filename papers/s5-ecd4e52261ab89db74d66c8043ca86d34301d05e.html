The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible computational anchor in the paper. Theorem 1, proved here, says that \(\mathrm{cost}(Q)=|C'|\,|B|\,(1-\mathrm{Var}(B))\) satisfies the paper’s four cost-function axioms: empty questions, prefix monotonicity, multiple monotonicity, and variance monotonicity. Lemmas 1 and 2 support that proof. None asserts a complexity or algorithmic result about query selection or committee approximation. The paper’s claims that some strategies perform better are experimental findings, not numbered complexity results. So there are zero anchors to restate, and no honest per-anchor tractability or hardness prediction.

The strongest positive case I can make is for a *new formalization of the paper’s central problem*, rather than a mirror of one of its proved results. Consider a recurring DAO election for a fixed slate of six candidates, with millions of participants but at most \(6!=720\) distinct complete rankings. Each ranking is a voter type, and its mass is that fraction of the population. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: the number of voters can greatly exceed the number of types.

Call the candidate problem Continuum Query-Budget \(k\)-Borda Approximation. Its instance has \(C\), \(k\), a per-capita budget \(b\), a cost function, a support bound \(\tau\), and an oracle for a hidden distribution \(\mu\) over at most \(\tau\) rankings. The oracle exposes preferences only through refinement queries. A policy may query all the mass in a cohort with the same observed query history; the oracle returns the exact mass of each response class. A query of cost \(\mathrm{cost}(Q)\) applied to a cohort of mass \(\alpha\) costs \(\alpha\,\mathrm{cost}(Q)\), and total cost must be at most \(b\). After finitely many queries, the policy outputs the \(k\) candidates with the highest aggregate imputed Borda scores, using the paper’s rule for scoring candidates tied in a partial order. The decision question is whether there is an adaptive policy whose output is within Hamming distance \(\epsilon\) of the full-information \(k\)-Borda committee for every allowed hidden distribution.

This preserves the paper’s recognizable core: limited-attention refinement queries, query costs, and approximation to \(k\)-Borda. Theorem 1 supplies a possible cost function for this problem, but does not solve it. I would leave its place in the tractability–hardness trichotomy open: the paper gives no complexity result from which to infer one. Further work would need to settle whether this oracle model has a finite-input formulation and whether worst-case error, rather than the paper’s experimental average error, is the right objective.

The weak point is substantial: the continuous policy sees \(\mu\) only through an oracle, while ChoCo’s central model takes the support and masses as the society’s input. If those masses and rankings are given directly to the selector, the query problem disappears. The oracle-access assumption keeps the paper’s information bottleneck alive, but makes this a tentative mirror of its *problem framework*, not a continuous counterpart to a result the paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to mirror. Theorem 1 and its supporting lemmas establish properties of a query-cost function; they say nothing about the complexity or approximation of query selection. The reported strategy comparisons are experiments on finite elections, not algorithmic guarantees. The proposed DAO problem therefore starts a new line of work rather than continuing a result proved in this paper.

The DAO scenario is plausible: with six candidates, millions of voters could be grouped into at most \(720\) ranking types. But preserving the query problem requires changing what the algorithm knows. If the society’s distribution \(\mu\) is supplied as ChoCo normally specifies it, the selector can compute the full-information \(k\)-Borda committee directly, so there is no preference information left to elicit. To retain the bottleneck, the proposal hides \(\mu\) behind an oracle that returns exact masses for query responses. That could define a legitimate problem, but its oracle, precision, and cost model are additional modeling choices; complexity results about it would be new results, not mirrors of the paper’s experimental findings.

That objection does not establish the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous version exists. An aggregate query oracle is a natural extension of the paper’s central question, and with rational masses of bounded bit length it could support a meaningful study of adaptive query policies. So the negative case is strong only in the narrower sense: this paper has no established computational result to mirror. The broader claim that no continuous mirror is worthwhile is weak, because the proposed query problem remains plausible as follow-on work.
