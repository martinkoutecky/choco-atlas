The strongest plausible *unanchored* lead would be a continuous version of the paper’s centralized social optimization only. Imagine a large grid built from many repeated generator or inverter modules, with \(N\) modules but a fixed, small set \(T\) of complete types. A type records every parameter the model uses: local dynamics, control inputs, costs, disturbance role, and coupling pattern. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A measurable feedback kernel \(K\) maps states across module pairs to control inputs. Given continuum dynamics and LQR cost operators, the problem is to find a stabilizing \(K\) that minimizes the normalized infinite-horizon LQR energy, subject to a bound \(\sigma\) on the normalized measure of nonzero state-to-control links. This mirrors the paper’s trade-off between global control performance and communication-link count, while continuizing the population. The natural regime is \(N\gg |T|\), with communication capacity scaled to the number of possible module pairs.

I would expect this candidate to be unresolved or hard: the paper’s LQR objective is nonconvex in \(K\), and the link-support constraint adds combinatorial structure. Its main weakness is whether the repeated-module scenario preserves the paper’s network model. If type must include each module’s full neighborhood, the number of types may grow with \(N\); if modules have genuinely repeated coupling patterns, the mirror is more convincing. A further question would be whether solutions of this continuum problem approximate finite-grid optima as \(N\) grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is one the proponent concedes: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. It formulates sparse LQR optimization and gives GraSP-based algorithms, but it proves no complexity classification or guarantee for the full optimization. In fact, it says convergence is not assured in general. The polynomial-time statement concerns a Newton-step subroutine, not the problem being optimized. The pure-equilibrium existence claim is not a computational result either. So the paper offers no theorem whose continuous counterpart could expose a tractability gain or a limit of continuization.

The best candidate is the centralized sparse LQR problem. In the paper, though, the many generators are not interchangeable voters: the dynamics, costs, disturbance matrix, and communication links depend on their positions in one coupled network. A complete type would have to capture a node’s couplings to the rest of the system. In a general growing grid, those descriptions proliferate with the number of nodes, so fixed \(\tau\) does not capture the regime. The cost-allocation game is a poorer candidate still: its players are a small set of distinct utilities, and its outputs assign costs to those named players.

One can rescue the LQR candidate by imposing repeated modules and exchangeable, mean-field coupling. But that requires a new continuum model: an operator-valued feedback kernel, a measure of allowed links, and disturbance and objective scaling that keep per-capita energy nonzero. For example, the paper’s disturbance enters at a particular generator; in a population limit, a single generator has zero mass under a normalized objective. Those choices may define a sensible mean-field control problem, but they do not follow from a high-multiplicity relaxation of the paper’s general network model. Its complexity would also depend on how the kernel and stability constraints are represented and approximated, rather than just on finite rational input parameters such as \(\tau\) and \(L\).

That leaves an honest limit to the negative case: a homogeneous sparse mean-field LQR problem could be worthwhile research in control. I cannot establish that no engineered scenario could make it so. The stronger conclusion this paper supports is narrower: it gives no qualifying computational result, and its most promising continuous candidate needs enough structural and scaling choices that it becomes a new problem rather than a mirror of a result here.
