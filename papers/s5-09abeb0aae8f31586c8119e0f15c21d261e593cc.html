The continuous mirror question

Given finite mediator and advertiser sets \(M\) and \(A\), a finite set of user cost tiers \(K\), rational cohort masses \(\mu_{m,k}\), public rational advertiser capacities \(u_a\), and a bound \(h\) on each mediator’s total cohort mass and each advertiser’s capacity, does there exist a deterministic direct mechanism that chooses assignment masses \(x_{m,k,a}\) and transfers, is truthful for each mediator’s cohort-measure report and each advertiser’s value report, is individually rational and budget balanced, and achieves at least \(1-5h/q^\star\) times optimal gain from trade, where \(q^\star\) is the optimal traded mass?

The model it lives in

Treat each user with mediator affiliation \(m\) and cost tier \(k\) as part of a population type of mass \(\mu_{m,k}\). Choose \(x_{m,k,a}\ge 0\), subject to \(\sum_a x_{m,k,a}\le\mu_{m,k}\) and \(\sum_{m,k}x_{m,k,a}\le u_a\), to maximize \(\sum_{m,k,a}(v_a-c_k)x_{m,k,a}\). The mechanism pays each mediator for assigned mass and charges advertisers; each unit of user mass receives an integral assignment, while aggregate assignments may be fractional. Mediators and advertisers remain individually strategic and finite.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.1’s PRM guarantees in the public-capacity setting; it leaves Theorem 1.2’s randomized mechanism and the paper’s broader applications aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can PRM’s leave-one-mediator-out threshold and VCG allocation be defined over cost quantiles and masses while preserving truthfulness, individual rationality, and budget balance?
  • Does the \(1-5h/q^\star\) competitive guarantee carry over, and what support-size and bit-length bounds apply?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror is the deterministic market in Theorem 1.1, proved in this paper. It is an algorithmic mechanism result, not a complexity-class theorem: the paper proves that PRM is budget balanced, individually rational and incentive compatible, and is \((1-5\gamma/\tau)\)-competitive. Here advertiser capacities are public, \(\gamma\) is a known upper bound on any advertiser’s capacity or any mediator’s number of users, and \(\tau\) is the size of the optimal trade. PRM computes a threshold separately for each mediator with that mediator’s users removed, excludes users above their mediator’s threshold, then uses a VCG allocation with a dummy advertiser to allocate the remaining users. The result’s value is precisely that it handles multidimensional reports from mediators while retaining these economic guarantees.

Lead mirror — Continuous PRM for cohort-based advertising markets. An instance has finite mediator and advertiser sets, a finite list of user cost tiers, and rational masses. Each user type is a pair \((m,c)\): a user represented by mediator \(m\), with per-ad compensation cost \(c\). Its mass \(\mu_{m,c}\) is the fraction of the user population in that cohort. This is a complete type for the users’ role in the paper’s model: users are nonstrategic, and their cost and mediator determine the relevant allocation and payment. Each advertiser \(a\) has a public capacity \(u_a\) measured in mass and privately reports a per-user value \(v_a\). Each mediator privately reports a measure over cost tiers, representing the sizes and costs of its user cohorts.

A feasible decision is an allocation \(x_{m,c,a}\ge 0\): the mass of type \((m,c)\) assigned to advertiser \(a\). It must respect reported cohort supplies and advertiser capacities:
\[ \sum_a x_{m,c,a}\le \mu_{m,c}, \qquad \sum_{m,c}x_{m,c,a}\le u_a. \]
The benchmark maximizes gain from trade,
\[ \max_x\sum_{m,c,a}(v_a-c)x_{m,c,a}. \]
A solution to the mirror problem is a deterministic direct mechanism that maps reports to such an allocation and to charges and payments. It must make truthful reports a dominant strategy for each mediator’s entire measure and each advertiser’s value, give each party nonnegative utility when truthful, and never run a deficit.

The regime is a large advertising exchange served by many privacy brokers, each representing a cohort of users with similar information-sharing costs. A useful high-multiplicity limit has many users but relatively few cost tiers per broker; no broker’s audience and no advertiser’s capacity is large compared with the total mass traded. Write \(h\) for an upper bound on those masses and \(q^\star\) for optimal traded mass. The natural analogue of Theorem 1.1 asks whether a continuous mechanism can guarantee competitive ratio \(1-5h/q^\star\), or what best ratio and structural conditions are possible. That is a question about computing a truthful, budget-balanced market allocation, not about fractional outcomes for their own sake.

I expect this mirror to be tractable, at least for finite rational support: the allocation benchmark is a transport LP, and the discrete mechanism suggests a continuous analogue using cost quantiles in place of individual users at indexed positions. That is a research direction, not a claim that Theorem 1.1 already proves the continuous guarantee. A prover could investigate whether the leave-one-mediator-out thresholds and VCG step extend to masses, preserve dominant-strategy incentives, and attain the analogous loss bound. Follow-up questions include whether the bound is tight, what happens when advertiser capacities are private as in Theorem 1.2, and whether the guarantee can be expressed in the number of supported types and their bit lengths.

The mirror is plausible because it retains the paper’s central objects and constraints: mediators report their users’ costs, advertisers report values, assignment creates gain from trade, and the mechanism must jointly satisfy incentive compatibility, individual rationality and budget balance. Replacing counts of users and slots with masses and capacities is the co-modification that makes the population continuous. It does not replace the problem with continuous outcomes while leaving the relevant population finite.

My weakest point is that the paper’s strategic players are mediators and advertisers, while its users are explicitly nonstrategic; the result is not about computational complexity or runtime, either. The continuous population here is the represented user population, and the mechanism must still handle the strategic intermediaries. Also, Theorem 1.1 assumes a public-capacity condition that restricts the mirror’s scope. Those limits make this a focused mirror of one result, rather than a claim that the whole paper’s mechanism-design programme is already a continuous computational-social-choice problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1.1, and I cannot honestly defeat its best continuous version on modelling grounds. The paper has a real mechanism result: PRM jointly guarantees incentive compatibility, individual rationality, budget balance, and an approximation to optimal gain from trade. A continuous question can preserve those same requirements.

The best objection is that the paper’s strategic agents are the mediators and advertisers, not the users whose population the proposed mirror continuizes. In a fixed exchange, those strategic actors remain discrete and identity-specific: each mediator needs an individual utility and payment, and PRM sets a threshold with that mediator’s users removed. Turning each broker’s user supply into a measure therefore gives a divisible-supply market, while the strategic market remains finite. It is not a continuous model of all the actors whose incentives drive the theorem.

That objection does not eliminate the strongest mirror. Users are nonstrategic in the paper, and users with the same mediator and compensation cost are indistinguishable for allocation and payment. A finite rational distribution over those cohorts, with public capacities measured in mass, is a coherent high-multiplicity version of the stated model. It preserves each mediator’s identity and utility, and asks a recognizable question about whether the paper’s mechanism guarantees can be maintained for cohort supply. The paper’s use of individual users and slots does not by itself make that question ill-posed.

A broader limit that also continuizes mediators and advertisers faces a sharper problem: their identities and individual incentive constraints matter. Aggregating them by type would discard those constraints; taking an atomless limit would require a new formulation of individual utilities and transfers. But that only rules against one broader mirror. It does not rule out the finite number of strategic parties with continuous user cohorts.

So the negative case is weak where it must be strongest. The user-only mirror looks like a sensible continuous-supply mechanism-design problem, and I have no fundamental reason to claim the authors would reject it. The objection is that it continues a nonstrategic part of the market while leaving the theorem’s strategic architecture discrete; that makes the mirror less central to a programme about continuous societies, but does not establish that it is not worth asking.
