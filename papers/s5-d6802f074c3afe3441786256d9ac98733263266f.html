What the mirror covers

The proposed question concerns Theorem 3.3’s Fisher-market GPoA bound; it leaves the Walrasian auction results and the regret-minimization results untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

Under the stated anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying computational-complexity result. Theorem 2.1 gives generic regret rates for a player choosing among \(K\) actions, but it does not classify a computation in the market problem. The substantive market theorems bound welfare loss.

The closest foothold is Theorem 3.3, proved here in Section 5 using Lemma 5.1: for a large Fisher market of largeness \(L\), with homogeneous, concave, continuous, monotone gross-substitutes utility and bid functions, the PoA and GPoA are at most \(e^{m/L}\), provided price-dependent demands are unique at every positive price vector or utilities are linear. This is a strategic-efficiency result, not a complexity result, so I would not present it as a valid anchor.

The positive intuition is still concrete. In a large advertising or organizational market, many buyers could share a modest number \(\tau\) of complete types, where a type specifies a buyer’s utility, budget, and allowed bids. Mass \(\mu_t\) represents the fraction of buyers of type \(t\); each buyer’s budget becomes negligible relative to total market budget. This is close to the paper’s large-Fisher-market regime, where \(L\) grows.

If a performance-theorem foothold were allowed, I would ask the following follow-up. Atomless Fisher GPoA: given \(m\) goods of unit supply, a finite set \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita budgets \(e_t\), rational linear true utilities, and finite menus of rational linear bids containing the truthful bid, compute the supremum GPoA over atomless Nash equilibria of the Walrasian market mechanism. A profile can be represented by the mass assigned to each type–bid pair; equilibrium requires each used bid to be a best response at the resulting prices. The truthful Walrasian market supplies the benchmark. The expected answer is GPoA \(=1\): an infinitesimal buyer cannot move prices, so equilibrium demands are true-utility-maximizing at clearing prices. That is a natural population limit of Theorem 3.3’s bound as \(L\) grows.

The weak point is decisive for this assignment: the mirror is an analytic limit, and the limit removes individual price influence. It gives a plausible continuous-population question, but not a question anchored in a named computational result of this paper. No continuous problem can therefore be submitted here as a qualifying anchored case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying computational-complexity result to anchor a mirror. Theorem 2.1 gives generic regret rates; Theorems 3.1–3.4 bound welfare loss in large markets. None classifies the complexity of a market problem. That leaves no named result whose computational question can be transferred to a continuous society.

The proposed atomless Fisher GPoA question is the closest candidate, but it exposes a deeper problem: atomlessness removes the strategic effect the paper measures. A single buyer’s deviation has zero mass, so it cannot change prices. With truthful bidding available, each buyer can choose a utility-maximizing bundle at the prevailing prices; under the paper’s uniqueness conditions, the resulting efficiency loss collapses. That is a continuum-specific degeneracy, not merely an uninteresting answer. Making each positive-mass type a strategic player could preserve price influence, but then the type is a coordinated cohort, not a population of independent buyers. Keeping individual buyers instead returns to the finite large-market setting, where the paper already bounds the loss in terms of \(L\).

One could still study algorithms for computing equilibria in markets with a small number of repeated types. That may be worthwhile on its own, and the paper’s market examples make high multiplicity plausible. But it would introduce a computational problem the paper does not analyze; it would not mirror one of its results. The negative case is strong for this programme’s criteria, though I cannot rule out every useful follow-up.
