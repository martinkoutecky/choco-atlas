The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no result to mirror. The paper’s findings are experimental: turnout differs from the equilibrium prediction by majority status and electoral rule, and those differences affect welfare and inequality. They are not complexity or algorithmic results. Equation (1) gives an equilibrium condition, but the paper does not claim an algorithm or analyze the complexity of finding an equilibrium. By ChoCo’s stated criteria, that is a decisive gap.

The best attempted mirror would model each group as a population over preference and cost types, then ask how equilibrium turnout and welfare change under WTA and PR. That is a sensible high-multiplicity setting; voter identity is not an objection. But it would turn the paper’s finite voting game into a new equilibrium analysis, not mirror a computational result the paper established. With fixed benefits, individual pivotality can also vanish as the population grows; scaling benefits or uncertainty to preserve turnout would add another modeling choice. Those choices might motivate a worthwhile project, but they would not supply the missing anchor.

So the case against a *continuous mirror of this paper’s results* is strong.
