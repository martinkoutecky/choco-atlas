What the mirror covers

The proposals cover Theorem 1's stationary payoffs and coalition formation, plus Theorem 2 and Theorem 4's patient path limit; they leave Theorem 3's middleman thresholds, Proposition 4.1 and Corollary 4.2's community structure, and Theorem 5's network result aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is computing equilibrium payoffs in a market of large, interchangeable cohorts. It is a plausible fit for ChoCo’s computational question, though the paper already supplies much of the continuous model.

Lead: Continuous Cohort Stationary Equilibrium. A type is a complete description of a worker’s relevant attributes: for example, their network position, discount factor, and bargaining weight. The input has a finite support \(T\) of such types, with rational population shares \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\); a family \(F\) of feasible coalitions of types; rational surpluses \(V(S)\); and rational opportunity and proposer rates. Cohorts are large enough that an event can select participants from each required type, with participants replaced by same-type clones after agreement. As in the paper’s labor-market interpretation, per-person selection rates can depend on cohort mass: \(\alpha_t\) is proportional to \(\beta_t/\mu_t\), with the opportunity rate fixing the scale.

The question is: given this continuous-cohort market, compute each type’s stationary-equilibrium payoff and identify which coalitions form. A solution is the payoff vector \(u\) and coalition slacks \(z\) solving

\[ \min_{u,z}\; \sum_{t\in T}\frac{\delta_t(1-\delta_t)}{\alpha_t}u_t^2 +\sum_{S\in F}P(S)z_S^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{t\in S}\delta_tu_t+z_S\ge V(S) \quad(S\in F). \]

Theorem 1, proved in the paper, says that every stationary-equilibrium payoff is the unique optimizer of this convex program. It also characterizes trade: if the discounted continuation payoffs of a coalition are below its surplus, the proposer offers those continuation payoffs and the members accept; if they exceed the surplus, agreement does not occur. At equality, strategies can vary, but equilibrium payoffs and the trade pattern remain determined. Thus this mirror asks about the underlying bargaining outcome, not merely about an encoding.

With \(F\) explicitly listed, the theorem gives a convex quadratic program with size polynomial in \(|T|+|F|+L\), so I expect this version to be tractable. For a succinctly represented coalition family, the answer depends on the pricing or separation problem; the theorem alone does not settle it.

A narrower, more compact-input version is Patient Path Bargaining, anchored by Theorem 4, also proved here. Its input is a trading graph whose nodes are worker types, rational link costs, and seller–buyer type pairs with rational surpluses. A feasible coalition is a path between a specified pair; its surplus is the pair’s surplus minus the path’s link costs. Cohorts contain many agents of their node type, and the limit uses the paper’s equal proposer-rate assumption. The question is to compute the per-type payoffs as the common discount factor tends to \(1\), and a sparse certificate of which paths can trade.

Theorem 4 says that these limiting payoffs equal node congestion in the associated traffic-routing game. Theorem 2 supplies the corresponding optimization formulation: compute the unique minimizer

\[ \min_{u\ge 0}\sum_{v\in T}u_v^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{v\in P}u_v\ge V_k-\sum_{e\in P}c_e \]

for every path \(P\) joining the endpoints of pair \(k\). A sparse certificate can assign nonnegative path weights \(\lambda_P\) to tight paths such that \(u_v=\sum_{P\ni v}\lambda_P\). For a candidate \(u\), finding a violated path constraint reduces to a shortest-path computation with node and edge costs. I therefore expect this path version also to be tractable through separation-based convex optimization.

The regime is credible: the paper itself describes labor markets with a continuum of workers divided into finitely many types, and its clone-replacement assumption represents steady-state entry and exit. Here \(|T|=\tau\) is the cohort support, while the number of individual workers can be vastly larger. Types must include all payoff-relevant distinctions, including network position. The authors should recognize this as their problem’s continuous-population form.

The case has a real limitation: this paper already models a continuum of workers by type and derives the equilibrium convex program. The incremental ChoCo question is the algorithmic one—how computation scales with support size and with a compact representation of feasible coalitions—not the invention of a new continuous model. Also, the paper states no named NP-hardness or polynomial-time complexity classification. Theorem 1 is the strongest algorithmic anchor because it gives a computation-ready formulation; Theorem 4 supports a tractable compact network case. These mirrors cover equilibrium payoffs and trade formation, not the paper’s full comparative-static results. And because the subject is bargaining equilibrium rather than a conventional election or collective choice rule, acceptance depends on ChoCo including market allocation problems within its scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is a prior-work collision. The paper already makes the population continuous in the relevant sense: §2.3 describes finitely many types, each with a continuum of workers or assets, and gives mass-based selection and replacement by clones. The labor-market example even derives proposer rates from cohort masses. Treating a type as a complete bundle of payoff-relevant attributes is therefore already built into the paper’s model.

The first mirror asks for the equilibrium payoffs and forming coalitions of that same type-based market. Theorem 1 already gives exactly those outputs as the unique optimizer of its convex program. When \(F\) is explicitly listed, this is a finite convex quadratic program; the remaining contribution would be a bit-complexity account for a computation the paper already characterizes. Theorem 2 also supplies the patient-agent limit used in the proposed formulation. There is no new population model in either question.

The path mirror collides even more directly. Theorem 4 already identifies limiting bargaining payoffs with traffic congestion, and its proof gives the path constraints and dual path weights. For the stronger compact-input version, a violated constraint can be found by a shortest-path computation with node costs \(u_v\) and edge costs \(c_e\). Thus the paper’s own network formulation supplies the natural separation procedure. It contains no named P-versus-NP theorem, but for these particular payoffs and paths the proposed computational questions have already been answered at the level the analyst states them.

The negative case weakens if “mirror” is expanded to mean any new complexity study of this already-continuous model. For example, §4.3 gives a natural compact family: coalitions are cliques and \(V(S)=|S|^2\), which is strictly supermodular. At a candidate vector \(u_i=q-\tfrac12\), pricing asks whether some clique violates \(\sum_{i\in S}u_i\ge |S|^2\). A clique of size \(s\) violates it exactly when \(s\ge q\), so this separation problem captures maximum-clique detection. That points to an unanalysed computational question, though it does not by itself prove that computing equilibrium payoffs is hard.

So I can defeat the two proposed anchors as *new continuous mirrors*: their central models and outputs are already in the paper. I cannot honestly make the universal claim airtight. A complexity study for a specifically defined compact coalition family could still be worthwhile; it would extend the computation of an existing continuous model rather than continuize a previously discrete population.
