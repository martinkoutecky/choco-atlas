The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give this paper a qualifying computational anchor. Its numbered propositions concern equilibrium existence and characterization, not whether a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, approximable, or parameterized-tractable.

The closest hook is Proposition 4, proved in the paper: an efficient MPE exists for all \(\delta\) sufficiently close to \(1\) if Rubinstein payoffs are in the interior of the core; such an equilibrium exists only if those payoffs are in the core. Proposition 1 also characterizes MPE values and agreement probabilities as exactly the profiles solving system (1) at every active-player set. These are substantial equilibrium results, but the paper gives neither an algorithm with a complexity bound nor a computational formulation it uses to compute solutions. The fixed-point characterization alone does not supply that anchor.

There is a plausible *follow-on* population setting: a large labor cohort with many workers and firms sharing a finite set of complete templates—side, pairwise surpluses, and proposal rates—with masses representing population shares. One might study whether a stationary bargaining equilibrium implements a surplus-maximizing mass matching. But the paper’s game selects one named individual to propose each period, and its state records which individuals remain. In an atomless population, one selected person has zero mass; replacing single-pair exits with per-capita proposal clocks and mass flows changes the bargaining process. That may be a worthwhile extension, but it is not a computational mirror anchored by the paper’s results.

So there is no eligible anchor here, and I can’t honestly assign a continuous problem per anchor or predict its complexity class. The strongest positive case is for a future large-market bargaining project; the decisive weakness is that this paper does not provide the computational result the mirror brief requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its propositions characterize equilibrium existence, payoffs, and efficiency; none studies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. Proposition 1’s fixed-point system describes MPE values and agreement probabilities over active-player sets. It is not an optimization problem or a pricing problem. Proposition 4 is the closest candidate: it relates efficient-equilibrium existence near \(\delta=1\) to Rubinstein payoffs and the core. But turning that characterization into a computational task—say, deciding efficient-MPE existence from a payoff matrix and proposer probabilities—would be an analyst’s new formulation, not a computational question the paper poses or answers.

The stronger labor-cohort proposal does not fix that gap. It is possible to define complete types by side, surplus relationships, and proposal rates, and give each type a mass. That makes high multiplicity plausible; the paper’s emphasis on thin markets does not rule out every such regime. But a continuous matching allocation by itself drops the bargaining process. To retain bargaining, one must specify how individual proposal probabilities scale with population size, how agreements create mass flows, and how continuation values depend on the evolving distribution. In the limit, one individual’s exit has zero mass. Preserving the paper’s changing-market mechanism therefore requires a new flow-based equilibrium model, not simply a continuous version of its finite-player game. Its computational questions would have to be chosen and formulated separately.

The same problem applies to Proposition 1: aggregate counts or masses might compress the state when many agents share templates, but the paper’s result is about strategies over sets of named active players. A mass-state equilibrium computation would be a substantial new mean-field bargaining project. The paper’s discussion of large-market bargaining makes that project credible; it does not already answer it, and it should not be used as a collision. Yet it also underscores how much the proposed mirror would change the object of study.

So I would not count any of these as a continuous computational mirror of this paper under ChoCo’s standard. That is a strong negative verdict on the available anchors, not proof that no useful continuous-population bargaining model could ever be built. The finite-template labor cohort is where the universal “no scenario” claim is weakest: it could motivate worthwhile research, but the paper does not provide the computational anchor that would make it a mirror.
