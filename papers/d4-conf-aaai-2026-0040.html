The continuous mirror question

Given \(m,\alpha,k\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over moderate-up-to-\(k\) ranking-with-intensity ballots, can one compute an alternative \(a\) such that, for every unit-mass electorate \(X\) whose ballot map has pushforward \(\mu\), and every compatible metric \(d\), \(\int_X d(x,a)\,d\lambda(x) \le [2+\max(\alpha,t_k)]\min_{c\in A}\int_X d(x,c)\,d\lambda(x)\), in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)? Here \(t_k\) is the bound defined in Theorem 2.

The model it lives in

The input is mass \(\mu_t\) on each reported ballot type \(t\); underlying distances remain hidden and range over all metrics compatible with the mandatory intensity reports. Set \(q(c)=\sum_t\mu_t r^k_{\operatorname{rank}_t(c)}\). For each candidate \(a\), use flow variables \(x_{t,c}\ge0\) on edges where \(t\) ranks \(a\) above \(c\), with row sums \(\mu_t\) and column sums \(q(c)\). Select a candidate with a feasible flow; the objective is the worst-case integral social-cost ratio, bounded by \(2+\max(\alpha,t_k)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's guarantee for moderate-up-to-\(k\) profiles; Theorem 1's lower bound transfers but is not a computational anchor. It leaves the varying-intensity and outlier bounds, the line-metric results, and the price-of-ignoring-intensities results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one compute an instance-optimal robust winner for each \(\mu\), beyond the theorem's universal distortion guarantee?

The case FOR (proponent)

This supports a narrow but credible mirror: metric-distortion voting with preference intensities over a continuous electorate. The lead anchor is Theorem 2, whose constructive rule can be implemented using mass capacities indexed by reported ballot types. Theorem 1 supplies a lower-bound boundary for the same design problem.

Let \(A\) contain \(m\) alternatives and fix \(\alpha\). A reported type is a complete ballot \(t=(\pi,\eta)\): a ranking \(\pi\) and an intensity mark \(\eta\in\{\succ,\succ\succ\}^{m-1}\). A continuous society is a unit-mass population \(X\) with a ballot map \(b:X\to T\); its input is the rational distribution \(\mu_t=\lambda(\{x:b(x)=t\})\), with support size \(\tau\). For a compatible metric \(d\), social cost becomes
\[ \operatorname{sc}_{\lambda}(a)=\int_X d(x,a)\,d\lambda(x), \]
and distortion is the worst-case ratio of this cost to the minimum cost of any alternative, over metrics consistent with the reports.

This keeps the paper’s information structure: the rule sees ballots, while the metric is hidden and adversarial. For a high-multiplicity scenario, imagine a large public consultation on facility sites, with many residents in a small number of residential or campus clusters. Residents in a cluster share the same location, costs, and reported intensity ballot; the rule uses the ballot-mass distribution, while its guarantee covers every compatible metric. More generally, residents with the same ballot may have different hidden metric positions, as they may in the paper’s model. Those latent differences must not be mistaken for identical full types; the continuous electorate can retain them while the rule sees only the ballot distribution.

Theorem 1 — proved in this paper — anchors a lower-bound question. It states that, for mandatory intensity elicitation and every \(\alpha\in[0,1]\), every voting rule has metric distortion at least
\[ 1+2\max\!\left(\alpha,\frac{1-\alpha^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}{1+\alpha^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}\right). \]
The paper proves this using two-agent instances. The continuous question is to lower-bound the optimal worst-case distortion over deterministic rules \(F\) that map rational ballot-mass distributions to alternatives:
\[ \inf_F\ \sup_{\mu,d}\ \frac{\int_X d(x,F(\mu))\,d\lambda(x)} {\min_{a\in A}\int_X d(x,a)\,d\lambda(x)}. \]
Theorem 1’s bound transfers: replace each witness voter by a block of equal mass at the same metric location and the distortion ratio is unchanged. The certificate is therefore supported on just two reported types. This is a transferred impossibility bound, not a claim of computational hardness; the exact continuous minimax value remains a further question.

Theorem 2 — proved in this paper — is the lead anchor. For \(k\in[m-1]\), it gives a rule that, on every moderate-up-to-\(k\) profile, selects an alternative with distortion at most \(2+\max(\alpha,t_k)\). Here moderate-up-to-\(k\) means no intensity appears before position \(k+1\), and the theorem’s bound uses
\[ w_1=\frac{\alpha+1}{3\alpha+1},\qquad t_1=\frac{1-\alpha}{3\alpha+1}, \]
and, for \(k>1\),
\[ w_k=1-\frac{2\alpha}{(1-\alpha)t_{k-1}+2w_{k-1}+2\alpha}, \qquad t_k=w_k+(1-w_k)t_{k-1}. \]
The continuous problem is: given \(m,\alpha,k\) and a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) supported on moderate-up-to-\(k\) ballots, find a winner with that distortion guarantee.

The paper’s PSM construction gives a concrete solution. Let \(r^k\) be its scoring vector, padded with zeros after position \(k+1\), and define
\[ q(c)=\sum_t\mu_t\,r^k_{\operatorname{rank}_t(c)}. \]
For each candidate \(a\), build a bipartite graph from ballot types \(t\), with mass \(\mu_t\), to alternatives \(c\), with mass \(q(c)\). Include an edge when type \(t\) ranks \(a\) above \(c\). Find an \(a\) and nonnegative flow \(x_{t,c}\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{c:\,a\succ_t c}x_{t,c}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t:\,a\succ_t c}x_{t,c}=q(c). \]
That candidate is the PSM winner; the flow certifies the matching condition. The proof’s inequalities hold voter by voter, so they extend to integrals over a population, including hidden distance variation within a reported ballot type. With rational \(\alpha\) and masses, the flow instance has polynomial size in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length. This is an expected Class A result for the continuous winner-selection task. The paper’s matching lemma (Theorem 3) is cited from Gkatzelis et al.; Theorem 2 and its PSM guarantee are proved here.

The authors should recognize this as their problem: it preserves single-winner voting, the same intensity semantics, and the same worst-case metric distortion objective. Only the electorate representation changes from a list of voters to mass by type. Theorem 2 raises natural follow-ups: can one compute the instance-optimal robust winner rather than a winner with a global bound? How does the algorithm extend to varying intensity ranks or a small mass of high-rank outliers, as in the paper’s later results?

The scope here is mandatory intensity elicitation, with Theorem 1’s general lower bound and Theorem 2’s moderate-up-to-\(k\) upper bound. I would not extend this case to the paper’s line-metric or price-of-ignoring-intensities results.

The weakest point is that this paper gives distortion guarantees and a constructive rule, not a named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity classification. The polynomial-size flow formulation is a consequence of the PSM construction, not a complexity theorem claimed by the authors. Also, when masses are rational, the society can be expanded into a finite election using a common denominator. The potential contribution is thus the high-multiplicity representation and its dependence on \(\tau\), not a new voting model or a new distortion bound.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is about fit to ChoCo’s computational aim. Theorem 1 is a lower bound on metric distortion, and Theorem 2 is a distortion guarantee for a constructed rule. Neither states a complexity result for a computational problem. Theorem 1’s continuous counterpart—minimizing worst-case distortion over ballot-mass distributions—would be a legitimate mathematical question, and its two-type witness transfers by duplication. It would carry over a distortion bound, though, rather than open a computational question the paper actually studies.

Theorem 2 is harder to dismiss. For mass \(\mu_t\) on each reported ballot type, its PSM construction becomes a capacitated fractional matching: set \(q(c)=\sum_t\mu_t r_{\operatorname{rank}_t(c)}\), then use a source node of capacity \(\mu_t\) for each type and candidate nodes of capacity \(q(c)\). The distortion proof’s voter-level inequalities extend by integration. The resulting flow has size polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and the rational input length. So there is a coherent continuous question and a direct algorithm.

One concern is that a ballot does not determine an agent’s exact distances to alternatives. If ChoCo requires each type to include those costs, the full types would carry more information than the paper’s voting rule receives. That makes a distribution over ballots an incomplete description of the true population. Still, the paper evaluates rules against every metric consistent with the reports, and its proof works pointwise before aggregation. A ballot-mass input with hidden metric uncertainty is therefore a defensible mirror; the type concern does not defeat it.

The negative case can say that the continuous PSM computation is a modest high-multiplicity implementation result: the paper already supplies the rule, and replacing individual nodes with ballot-type capacities gives the compressed flow. A more ambitious question, such as finding the instance-optimal robust winner for each \(\mu\), might be worthwhile, but the paper does not establish a finite-instance complexity result for that task to mirror.

I cannot honestly defeat Theorem 2’s anchor. Theorem 1 is weak as a ChoCo complexity anchor, but Theorem 2 supports a plausible Class A continuous mirror, even if its computational novelty is limited. That leaves the universal negative case short of its burden.
