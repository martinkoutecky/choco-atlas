The continuous mirror question

Input: \(N\) features, a common feasible-outcome set \(\mathcal O\), a finite support \(\Theta\) of recursive LCP theories \(P_\theta\) over \(\mathcal O\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta>0\) summing to \(1\), and \(s,t\in\mathcal O\). Decide whether \(\sum_{\theta:L(P_\theta)\vdash\operatorname{dom}(s,t)}\mu_\theta>1/2\).

The model it lives in

Residents are grouped into \(\tau=|\Theta|\) complete LCP preference-and-constraint types, with \(\mu_\theta\) the population share of type \(\theta\). Outcomes remain discrete; each type judges \(s\) against \(t\) using its own LCP dominance relation, and the decision asks whether more than half the mass derives \(\operatorname{dom}(s,t)\).

The objection that survived

Majority over pairwise dominance is a chosen collective aggregation rule absent from the paper, and the proponent's multi-agent context does not establish that this is the aggregation these authors would claim.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4's recursive-LCP dominance decision after aggregation over types; it leaves Theorems 3 and 5–7, Proposition 2, the compiler, and the experiments unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the combined complexity in \(N\), \(\tau\), and \(L\) for majority dominance when the type theories are flat or restricted to acyclic or tree structures?
  • Can algorithms exploit bounded \(\tau\) or repeated theory encodings to improve on evaluating each distinct type separately?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Majority LCP-Dominance. Its society consists of a finite support \(\Theta\) of preference types, each a complete recursive LCP theory \(P_\theta\) over the same \(N\) features and the same feasible outcomes. Type \(\theta\) has rational mass \(\mu_\theta\), with \(\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mu_\theta=1\). A type is complete in the paper’s sense: people grouped together have the same conditional preferences and constraints. A large group of residents comparing public-service plans, for example, might have millions of people but only a few recurring LCP preference templates.

Given two feasible plans \(s,t\), ask whether a strict majority of the population’s types derive that \(s\) dominates \(t\):

\[ \sum_{\theta:\,L(P_\theta)\vdash \operatorname{dom}(s,t)} \mu_\theta > \frac12. \]

This is a decision problem, so it has no optimization objective: the answer is yes or no. The solution is yes precisely when the displayed mass exceeds one half. The plans remain discrete; it is the population that is represented by a distribution. The input can be listed using \(N\), support size \(\tau=|\Theta|\), and total binary encoding length \(L\), including the theories and masses.

My anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: “Given a recursive LCP theory \(P\) over \(N\) features, deciding \(\operatorname{dom}(s,t)\) is EXPTIME-complete in \(N\).” Here, recursive LCP rules may invoke recursively defined predicates involving \(d/2\), and \(\operatorname{dom}\) is the transitive closure of the theory’s preference relation. The theorem is about deciding whether one outcome dominates another under one theory.

The connection to the mirror is direct: each type’s vote on \(s,t\) is exactly that dominance query. Moreover, the hardness survives in a society with two types. Given an instance \((P,s,t)\) of Theorem 4, use mass \(3/5\) for \(P\) and mass \(2/5\) for a type with the same feasible outcomes but no preference rules. The majority query is yes exactly when \(P\) derives \(\operatorname{dom}(s,t)\). Thus the expected answer is hardness transfers, in the programme’s Class B: the reduction’s combinatorics live in the recursive theory and feature space, not in population multiplicity. The mirror gives a concrete question about a high-multiplicity society, even though the continuous population does not erase this anchor’s hardness.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors the paper’s dominance result, not its consistency and optimization results, compiler, or experiments. Further questions include how the aggregate query behaves for flat or tree-structured type theories, and whether algorithms can exploit small \(\tau\) or repeated theories beyond simply querying each distinct type.

The weak point is that the paper defines dominance within one LCP theory; it does not define majority aggregation across people with different theories. The paper does, however, place recursive preferences in multi-agent contexts, and each individual comparison here uses its own LCP dominance semantics unchanged. I think authors would recognize this as a population-level extension of their dominance problem, though they might not regard the majority wrapper as part of their original problem. That qualification matters: this is a defensible continuous mirror, but its plausibility depends on accepting the natural social-choice step from individual dominance to majority dominance.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a mismatch between the paper’s problem and the proposed society. Theorem 4 is unquestionably a computational result, but its instance is one LCP theory \(P\): a compact description of one preference relation over outcomes, with \(N\) describing features of those outcomes. The theorem asks whether that agent’s theory derives \(\operatorname{dom}(s,t)\). The paper does not ask how a population combines different agents’ theories.

The proposed majority question is well-defined, and its population model is plausible: residents could share a small number of LCP templates for comparing public plans. The issue is that majority aggregation supplies the central operation. The paper does not select majority over pairwise dominance; Pareto aggregation, Kemeny-style ranking, or another rule would produce different computational problems. This is not a limit-induced adjustment to the individual dominance problem. It turns that problem into a new collective-preference problem.

A stronger version would ask for a Condorcet winner under the population’s majority-dominance relation. That would make the result a collective decision, rather than only a pairwise test. But the same gap remains: the aggregation rule and the winner question are added from outside the paper. The references to multi-agent preferences make such an extension imaginable, though; they do not establish that these authors would recognize it as their problem.

The reduction using masses \(3/5\) and \(2/5\) confirms that Theorem 4 survives inside the proposed model. It does not show that the population operation itself is a natural mirror of the paper’s result. Still, the objection is about whether the authors would own the added aggregation problem, not about whether a continuous society can sensibly be constructed. Repeated LCP types are credible, the mass is meaningful, and the paper provides a clear computational anchor. I cannot make an honest case that every worthwhile mirror fails; this anchor is a real vulnerability in the negative position.
