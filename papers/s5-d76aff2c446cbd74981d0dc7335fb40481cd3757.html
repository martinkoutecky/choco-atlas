The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of the kind the mirror exercise requires. It gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or computational formulation that it then solves.

Theorem 1, proved here, says that on the classical domain, a rule satisfies efficiency, strategy-proofness, individual rationality, and no-subsidy if and only if it is the minimum-price Walrasian rule with reserve prices equal to the seller’s benefits. Theorem 2, also proved here, gives the analogous characterization using two-sided individual rationality. Both are axiomatic characterizations, not results about a computational problem being in P, hard, or otherwise.

One could invent a continuous-market question about computing a minimum-price Walrasian allocation from masses of preference types. But it would not be anchored in a named computational result of this paper. Extending these theorems to a continuum would also continue their axiomatic question, which is outside ChoCo’s computational scope. So I don’t see a valid positive case under the stated anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are two versions of one axiomatic result: they characterize when the minimum-price Walrasian rule is the unique rule satisfying specified welfare, incentive, and participation properties. The strongest possible computational mirror would be: given masses of preference types and object supplies, compute a minimum-price Walrasian allocation. But that asks for an algorithm the paper neither states nor analyzes. The two theorems do not provide separate computational anchors; both lead to the same added question.

There is a deeper obstacle to treating that question as a mirror. The paper’s domain is the full class of classical preferences over object-price bundles, with no finite encoding or query model. Complexity in \(m,\tau,L\) is therefore undefined. Choosing a finitely represented preference family and rational type masses could make an algorithmic market problem well-posed, but it would replace the paper’s unrestricted characterization with a result about a selected parametric domain.

The population limit also strains the theorem’s defining properties. With a fixed set of indivisible objects and normalized population mass, an individual has measure zero, so their report cannot affect aggregate allocations or prices; strategy-proofness can become vacuous. Giving objects positive per-capita supply instead requires replicated capacity and aggregate allocation across types. That may be a sensible market model, but it is a new computational problem, not a continuous version of either characterization.

This is a strong reason not to count the paper as a ChoCo mirror: it has no named computational result, and turning its Walrasian rule into a computational target requires specifying both a new input model and a new population mechanism. It does not prove that no related algorithmic project could be worthwhile; it does show that such a project would be an extension of this paper, not a mirror of its results.
