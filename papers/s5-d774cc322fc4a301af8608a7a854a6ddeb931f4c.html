The continuous mirror question

For fixed \(k\), given a finite undirected type graph \(H=(T,F)\), uniform masses \(\mu_t=1/\tau\), and a budget \(p\), choose exactly \(p\) types to activate and maximize the mass of types within distance \(k\) of an activated type. What is the polynomial-time approximation threshold for this population-mass objective?

The model it lives in

A society consists of cohorts indexed by the vertices of \(H\), with each cohort carrying mass \(\mu_t=1/\tau\); every member has the same inter-cohort interaction pattern, and the campaign activates exactly \(p\) whole cohorts. The value of an activation set \(D\subseteq T\) is \(\sum_{t:\operatorname{dist}_H(t,D)\le k}\mu_t\).

The objection that survived

The mirror depends on authors accepting whole-cohort activation as the intervention unit, which extends the paper’s budget for selecting individual vertices; the proposed platform and organization scenario makes that extension plausible but cannot guarantee their recognition.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1 on Max \(k\)-hop Domination and, secondarily, Theorem 2 on Max Ext-Domination for uniform-mass cohort graphs. It leaves arbitrary rational masses, individual-level interventions, and the paper’s election and resource-allocation results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • How do approximation guarantees change for arbitrary rational masses \(\mu_t\)?
  • What complexity results hold if campaigns can activate only part of a cohort and influence responds to that partial activation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a group-level influence regime. Imagine a large platform or organization with \(\tau\) recurring cohorts. Each cohort contains many people who have the same interaction pattern: everyone in cohort \(t\) can influence everyone in each cohort adjacent to \(t\), and the campaign can activate a cohort as a unit. The type graph \(H=(T,F)\) records those interactions. With \(R\) people per cohort, the society has \(N=R\tau\) people but only \(\tau\) types, and cohort \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\) (uniformly, \(\mu_t=1/\tau\)). The objective is the fraction of people reached. This is a plausible regime when campaigns act through whole workplace, campus, or organizational groups; the members of a cohort share the relevant interaction and intervention properties.

The lead mirror is Type-Mass \(k\)-Hop Domination. Its input is a finite undirected type graph \(H=(T,F)\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), an integer \(p\), and fixed \(k\). Choose exactly \(p\) types \(D\subseteq T\), activating every member of each chosen type. With \(B_k(D)=\{t\in T:\operatorname{dist}_H(t,D)\le k\}\), maximize \(\sum_{t\in B_k(D)}\mu_t\). A solution is the chosen set \(D\); its value is the dominated population mass. This is a continuous-population objective, while the intervention is deliberately cohort-level.

The hardness anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for every fixed integer \(k\ge1\), unless \(P=NP\), no polynomial-time algorithm approximates Max \(k\)-hop Domination within any constant factor greater than \(1-1/e\). In that problem the input is an undirected graph and a number \(p\); one chooses \(p\) vertices and maximizes the number of vertices at distance at most \(k\) from a chosen vertex, counting the chosen vertices themselves as dominated. For the uniform-mass case of the mirror, set \(T=V(H)\), \(\mu_t=1/|T|\), and use the same \(p\) and \(k\). The mirror’s objective is then exactly the paper’s objective divided by \(|T|\), so approximation ratios are unchanged. The hardness transfers to this continuous-population regime: the relevant complexity lies in the number of distinct interaction types \(\tau\), even when each type represents arbitrarily many people.

A second, central-result mirror is Type-Mass External Domination. Use a uniform-mass type graph \(H\), choose exactly \(p\) types, and maximize \(\sum_{t\in B_1(D)\setminus D}\mu_t\): the mass reached outside the activated types. The anchor is Theorem 2, proved here. It states that Algorithm 2 gives a \((6e-5)/(6e+5)\)-approximation for Max Ext-Domination, the \(k=1\) problem of choosing \(p\) vertices to maximize the number of externally dominated vertices. In the uniform type model, the objective is exactly the paper’s external-domination count divided by \(\tau\), so the ratio carries over. This is a useful independent mirror of the paper’s new external objective, though the lead remains Theorem 1 because it is the hardness result.

I expect hardness to transfer for the first problem, and the stated approximation guarantee to carry over for the uniform-mass external variant. The questions that remain include what approximation guarantees hold with arbitrary rational masses, how complexity depends on \(\tau\), and what changes if a campaign can activate only a fraction of a type. In particular, if any positive activated mass reaches an entire adjacent cohort, fractional activation creates a threshold effect; a model where influence scales with the activated fraction would be a different, further question.

The soft spot is the cohort-wide action. The paper chooses individual vertices, while these mirrors commit every member of a type together. If the authors mean only individual-level interventions, they may reject this as a nearby group-control problem. I think the regime is still defensible: it keeps the graph-distance objective, makes cohort members genuinely identical for the problem, and gives the population mass an operational meaning. The claims above are correspondingly limited to this regime; they do not mirror the paper’s election results or establish the same guarantees for arbitrary weighted populations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a mismatch between the paper’s intervention unit and the proposed type. The paper chooses \(p\) individual vertices in an arbitrary graph. Treating each vertex as a type gives \(\tau=|V|\), so it supplies no repeated types. To give each type many members, one can replace every vertex by a cohort of clones and connect cohorts according to a type graph. But then the mirror chooses whole cohorts: its budget is \(p\) cohorts, or \(Rp\) people for cohorts of size \(R\). That is a group-intervention problem, while the paper allows its \(p\) choices to be individuals from anywhere in the graph. If interventions instead select arbitrary mass within cohorts, Boolean reachability creates a threshold: any positive activation can reach a whole neighboring cohort. Smoothing that effect changes the influence model.

This raises a real admissibility question for both Theorem 1 and Theorem 2: would the authors recognize cohort activation as their problem, or as a new group-control variant? The type graph also carries the reduction’s full combinatorial structure. Increasing cohort size does not compress that structure; the hard instances still require large \(\tau\).

That objection does not defeat the strongest version of the mirror. Cohort-wide campaigns are plausible, and the programme permits a co-modified model when authors would recognize the resulting question. For that model, uniform masses give the Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 objectives exactly after division by \(\tau\), preserving their approximation statements. Complexity residing in \(\tau\) is not disqualifying, and the weighted-mass extensions pose further computational questions. Neither anchor can honestly be dismissed on those grounds.

So the negative case is weak. Its best point is that the proponent needs to defend whole-cohort intervention as part of the problem, rather than simply assign masses to the paper’s vertices. That is a substantive modeling challenge, but the stated cohort scenario answers it plausibly. I cannot make a sound universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
