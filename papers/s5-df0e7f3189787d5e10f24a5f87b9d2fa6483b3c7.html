The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a strong *anchored* case for a continuous mirror here. The paper has no numbered result asserting a computational complexity classification such as membership in P, NP-hardness, or FPT. Its numbered results are structural and enumerative, so they do not meet the required anchor condition.

The closest results are Proposition 1, which says every set-alternating scheme generates a copious, maximal Condorcet domain, and Proposition 10, which gives the asymptotic size of particular such domains. If \(a(n)\) is the size of the even scheme’s domain, Proposition 10 states that for even \(n\),
\[ a(n)\sim \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\bigl(\sqrt{2+2\sqrt{2}}\bigr)^n, \]
and for odd \(n\),
\[ a(n)\sim \frac{\sqrt{\sqrt{2}-1}}{2}\bigl(\sqrt{2+2\sqrt{2}}\bigr)^n. \]
This yields the paper’s exponential lower bound for domain sizes. Neither proposition studies the computational complexity of a problem.

The nearest population model would take each permitted ranking as a type and \(\mu_t\) as its share of a continuum of voters. One could ask whether every such \(\mu\) induces a transitive majority relation. But that would recast the Condorcet-domain property; it would not mirror the paper’s results about constructing and counting domains as the number of alternatives grows. The paper’s substantive objects are sets of rankings, not a population whose mass is manipulated or optimized.

That is the weakest point, and it is decisive for this task: any continuous problem I could state would be an unanchored extension, not a continuous analogue of a named computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper gives no computational result to mirror. Its numbered propositions establish structural properties of preference domains and count their orders; none gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or an optimization problem. In particular, Proposition 1 says set-alternating domains are copious and maximal, while Proposition 10 gives their asymptotic size. Those are meaningful mathematical results, but neither is a computational claim of the kind ChoCo is meant to chart.

For Proposition 1, the strongest population version would take the permitted rankings as types and a distribution \(\mu\) over them. But the proposition concerns a domain’s property across all profiles drawn from it, not the frequencies in one society. Asking about one \(\mu\) changes the subject to a particular electorate; quantifying over every \(\mu\) leaves the domain property as the object and supplies no computational question from the paper to solve. Either way, the proposed population model does not mirror Proposition 1.

For Proposition 10, fractional mass over rankings does not represent the number of rankings in the domain. A stronger attempt might assign a distribution over those rankings and study its entropy or diversity. That requires a new objective or sampling assumption: the proposition itself specifies neither. Such a project could be interesting, but it would be a new question attached to the paper, not a continuous mirror of its growth result.

Large societies with many voters sharing each ranking are entirely plausible. That validates a high-multiplicity regime for election problems, but it does not make the paper’s domain size or structural claims depend on voter masses. The weakness in this negative case is that those domains could motivate new computational questions about elections under distributions over rankings. I see no basis in this paper, though, for treating those questions as mirrors of its results or as a reason to spend ChoCo’s effort continuizing them.
