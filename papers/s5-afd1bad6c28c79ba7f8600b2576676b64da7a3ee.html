The continuous mirror question

For five locations \(y_1,\ldots,y_5\in\mathbb S^1\), let \(\mu_y=\frac15\sum_{i=1}^5\delta_{y_i}\). Define PCD’s lottery on the distinct occupied locations by assigning each location the length of its facing gap, and let \(SC_{\mu_y}(z)=\int d(x,z)\,d\mu_y(x)\). What is the supremum over \(y\) of \(\mathbb E[SC_{\mu_y}(Z)]/\min_{z\in\mathbb S^1}SC_{\mu_y}(z)\), and which profile attains it?

The model it lives in

A society consists of five equal-mass cohorts of agents with ideal locations \(y_i\in\mathbb S^1\); coincident cohorts combine into one type. PCD selects an occupied location with probability equal to its facing gap, and the objective is the worst-case ratio of expected per-capita social cost to optimal per-capita social cost.

The objection that survived

In a literally nonatomic society, an individual deviation leaves the distribution unchanged, so the individual strategyproofness attached to PCD in the finite model does not transfer as a substantive guarantee.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4’s tight PCD approximation ratio for five equal-mass cohorts. It leaves aside the conjectured formula for general odd numbers of agents, the question of whether a better strategyproof mechanism exists, and individual strategyproofness in a nonatomic society.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: the paper has no named complexity-class result such as an NP-hardness or polynomial-time theorem. Its closest named computational anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: it establishes that the PCD mechanism’s exact worst-case approximation ratio for five agents on a circle is \(7-4\sqrt{2}\). That is an approximation-performance result, not a complexity classification.

A concrete mirror is High-Multiplicity Five-Slot PCD Ratio. Take an odd replication factor \(q\) and five ideal locations \(y_1,\dots,y_5\) on the unit circle. Make \(q\) residents at each location, so the population is \(N=5q\), and its distribution is
\[ \mu_y=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\delta_{y_i}. \]
A type is an ideal location: residents of that type have the same preferences and distance costs. Coincident locations combine into one type, so the support size is \(\tau\leq 5\), while \(N\) can grow arbitrarily large. A plausible regime is a city with five equally sized cohorts whose members share an ideal site for a public facility.

Run PCD on the \(N\) individual reports. Let \(Q_{q,y}\) be its facility-location distribution and let
\[ SC_{\mu_y}(z)=\int d(x,z)\,d\mu_y(x). \]
The problem is to determine
\[ R_q=\sup_{y\in(\mathbb S^1)^5} \frac{\mathbb E_{Z\sim Q_{q,y}}[SC_{\mu_y}(Z)]} {\min_{z\in\mathbb S^1}SC_{\mu_y}(z)} \]
and provide a profile attaining the supremum; define the ratio as \(1\) if the optimum cost is zero.

The mirror is credible because it preserves the paper’s agents, strategic reports, PCD rule, and per-capita social-cost objective. At a replicated profile, the agents at each location contribute the same total PCD probability as the corresponding location in the five-agent profile: duplication adds zero-length arcs, while the gap-facing probability remains at that location. Normalizing social cost by population size also leaves the ratio unchanged. Since \(N\) is odd, PCD’s strategyproofness follows from the result of Meir (2019) cited in the paper.

Theorem 4 therefore gives this mirror’s answer: for every odd \(q\), \(R_q=7-4\sqrt{2}\). The paper’s two-pair analysis identifies a maximizing arc pattern \(\big((\sqrt{2}-1)/2,\,0,\,1/2,\,0,\,(2-\sqrt{2})/2\big)\). I expect this mirror to be tractable—in fact, its answer follows by exact replication from the theorem. The further questions are whether analogous ratios can be established for other odd cohort counts, and what changes when type masses vary freely.

The weak point is that this mirror fixes the population proportions to those induced by five equal replications. It makes the population large while keeping the mass vector essentially fixed, so it does not expose the richer continuous-mass or complexity questions ChoCo is after. In particular, simply allowing arbitrary masses while keeping PCD’s support-gap probabilities can make the ratio unbounded as mass concentrates on one location. Thus this is a defensible high-multiplicity mirror of Theorem 4, but a limited one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is the one serious anchor, but the equal-replication construction does not give it a new continuous problem. For any five locations \(y_1,\ldots,y_5\), its population measure is fixed at \(\mu=\frac15\sum_i\delta_{y_i}\). PCD assigns each occupied location its facing gap, so replacing each agent by \(q\) coincident agents leaves the lottery and the per-capita ratio unchanged. Thus \(R_q\) is exactly the paper’s five-agent optimization in another notation. It introduces no variable mass vector or algorithmic problem; computing PCD’s lottery on a finite support just requires ordering the locations and reading off the gaps. The paper proves a distortion bound, but has no complexity result for a population-input problem of the kind ChoCo is targeting.

The stronger mirror would let the locations have arbitrary rational masses. But the finite-replication meaning of PCD forces its probability at each occupied location to depend on the gap, not on that location’s mass. With two distinct locations \(y,z\), any society \(\mu_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)\delta_y+\varepsilon\delta_z\), for \(\varepsilon>0\), gives both locations their fixed gap probabilities. At \(\varepsilon=0\), the lottery is concentrated at \(y\). So as the population measure converges to \(\delta_y\), the PCD lottery does not converge with it; meanwhile the optimal social cost tends to zero. This is a genuine continuum degeneration: an arbitrarily small positive mass can keep a location’s full gap-based influence on the outcome. The model is driven by which locations have positive mass, rather than by the population shares that continuization is meant to expose.

There is also an incentive problem with that limit. If individuals have zero mass, their deviations leave \(\mu\) unchanged, making strategyproofness vacuous. If whole positive-mass cohorts can move, that is a group-deviation model, which is not the individual strategyproofness proved for PCD. A mass-aware lottery might avoid the discontinuity, but it would be a new mechanism rather than the population limit of the paper’s PCD.

I cannot make the negative case airtight: a researcher could still study the distortion of support-gap PCD over weighted atomic societies. But that would be a distributional mechanism-analysis project, not a continuous computational mirror of this paper’s result. The proposed equal-mass mirror is already the same five-location optimization; the richer version loses the stable population and incentive interpretation that would make it a worthwhile continuization.
