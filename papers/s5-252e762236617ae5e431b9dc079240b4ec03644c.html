The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita capacities \(\beta_j\) with \(\sum_j\beta_j\ge1\), per-capita benefits \(a^k_{tj}\in[0,1]\), and \(q\) rational posterior weight vectors \(w^1,\ldots,w^q\), let \(\mathcal A=\{x\ge0:\sum_jx_{tj}=\mu_t\ \forall t,\ \sum_tx_{tj}\le\beta_j\ \forall j\}\) and \(f_w(x)=\sum_{k,t,j}w_k a^k_{tj}x_{tj}\). Compute an exact minimizer \(x^*\), a best challenger \(y^*\in\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal A}\frac1q\sum_{s=1}^q\max\{0,f_{w^s}(y)-f_{w^s}(x^*)\}\), and the value \(\min_{x\in\mathcal A}\max_{y\in\mathcal A}\frac1q\sum_{s=1}^q\max\{0,f_{w^s}(y)-f_{w^s}(x)\}\).

The model it lives in

A planner assigns a continuum of residents, grouped into complete cost-and-eligibility types \(t\) with mass \(\mu_t\), to resources \(j\). The decision variables \(x_{tj}\) are population shares, resource capacities are per-capita bounds \(\beta_j\), and the planner’s uncertain criterion weights are represented by the finite sample \(w^1,\ldots,w^q\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3’s sampled MMER solution and best-challenger computation for MAP. It leaves out Bayesian posterior updating, the full adaptive elicitation procedure, and the paper’s numerical results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the \(2^q\) dependence on the number of posterior samples be improved?
  • How closely does the type-mass optimum approximate the discrete MAP optimum at finite population sizes?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s multiobjective allocation problem. Imagine a large city assigning residents to clinics, schools, or service centres under capacity limits. A resident’s type records their criterion-specific cost at each resource, along with any eligibility details that affect the assignment. Residents of the same type are indistinguishable for this problem. In a plausible high-multiplicity regime, there may be hundreds of thousands of residents but only tens or hundreds of distinct types.

Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of residents of type \(t\). An allocation \(x_{tj}\) is the fraction of the whole population that is type \(t\) and receives resource \(j\). Thus each resident still receives one resource, while a type’s mass can be split across resources. The feasible allocations are

\[ \mathcal A(\mu,\beta)= \left\{x\ge 0: \sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\ \forall t,\quad \sum_t x_{tj}\le\beta_j\ \forall j \right\}, \]

where \(\beta_j\) is resource \(j\)’s capacity as a fraction of the population. This is a natural high-multiplicity version of the paper’s MAP: its agents, resource assignments, criteria, and capacity constraints remain, with counts replaced by population masses and capacities scaled per capita. Use normalized per-person benefits \(a^k_{tj}\in[0,1]\), equivalent to shifting normalized costs, so criterion \(k\)’s value is \(u_k(x)=\sum_{t,j}a^k_{tj}x_{tj}\). The planner’s uncertain criterion weights \(w\) and posterior over them remain as in the paper. The population mass is not that posterior: it describes the residents being assigned.

The best named anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It states that the authors’ iterative algorithm terminates and returns a minimax expected-regret solution and its best challenger for a finite feasible set \(\mathcal X\) and a finite sample \(S\) of weight vectors. The algorithm solves a restricted MMER problem over a growing challenger set \(A\), finds the best challenger over all of \(\mathcal X\), and adds it to \(A\) until that challenger is already present. At termination, the restricted value equals the full MMER value, the returned allocation minimizes maximum expected regret, and the challenger maximizes its pairwise expected regret. This is a correctness result, not a polynomial-time bound; the proof’s termination argument relies on the finiteness of \(\mathcal X\).

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is High-multiplicity MAP-MMER. An instance contains rational type masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita capacities \(\beta_j\), rational criterion benefits \(a^k_{tj}\), and a finite rational sample \(S=\{w^1,\ldots,w^q\}\) of posterior weight vectors. For \(f_w(x)=\sum_k w_k u_k(x)\), compute

\[ \min_{x\in\mathcal A(\mu,\beta)} \max_{y\in\mathcal A(\mu,\beta)} \frac{1}{q}\sum_{s=1}^{q} \max\{0,f_{w^s}(y)-f_{w^s}(x)\}. \]

A solution consists of an optimal mass allocation \(x^*\), its best challenger \(y^*\), and the exact MMER value. This mirrors the underlying problem Proposition 3 computes: choose a low-regret recommendation and identify the comparison that is most informative to ask the planner next.

I expect this sampled continuous problem to be fixed-parameter tractable in \(q\), the number of posterior samples, though this does not imply polynomial time when \(q\) is unrestricted. For each subset \(B\subseteq[q]\), let \(h_B=\max_{y\in\mathcal A}\sum_{s\in B}f_{w^s}(y)\). Since

\[ \sum_s\max\{0,d_s\}=\max_{B\subseteq[q]}\sum_{s\in B}d_s, \]

each \(h_B\) is the value of a linear program over the allocation polytope, and the outer minimization is another linear program with one constraint per subset \(B\). Enumerating these subsets gives an exact \(2^q\operatorname{poly}(K,\tau,r,L)\) algorithm, where \(K\) is the number of criteria, \(\tau\) the number of positive-mass types, \(r\) the number of resources, and \(L\) the input bit length. An optimizing subset and its LP solution also give a best challenger. This is a derived algorithm for the mirror, not a result claimed by the paper. It leaves open whether the dependence on \(q\) can be improved enough for large samples.

The scope here is deliberately narrow: the sampled MMER recommendation-and-challenger computation for MAP. It does not mirror the paper’s Bayesian posterior update, its full interactive session, or its numerical performance claims. The authors could plausibly recognize this as their allocation problem under high multiplicity: the costs and capacities are exactly the quantities in their model, now given by type and population share.

My weakest point is that the paper’s elicited preference belongs to one decision maker, while the continuum is the population receiving the allocation. The mirror preserves the planner’s uncertainty and query task, but it does not turn the residents into the people answering queries. Also, the paper’s exact termination proof does not transfer directly: \(\mathcal A(\mu,\beta)\) has infinitely many allocations, so its finite-set challenger-generation argument alone gives no termination guarantee here. The case rests instead on MAP being a sensible high-multiplicity allocation regime and on the continuous MMER problem having its own exact optimization route.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 3 is a thin anchor for a complexity mirror. It proves that the restricted-challenger algorithm returns the right answer and terminates because \(\mathcal X\) is finite; in the worst case it adds every feasible solution. It gives no running-time bound or complexity classification. So the proposed MAP question is not a continuous version of a complexity result from the paper. It is a new computational question extracted from the paper’s regret calculation and experiments.

That objection does not defeat the improved mirror. A city assigning residents to clinics or schools is a plausible high-multiplicity MAP regime: a type records the complete criterion-specific cost vector, and allocations split each type’s mass while respecting per-capita capacities. The planner can remain the single decision maker. For the paper’s sampled regret objective, the subset identity reduces the continuous problem to an LP with one constraint per subset of the \(q\) sampled weights, giving an exact \(2^q\operatorname{poly}(\tau,r,K,L)\) algorithm. That is a well-posed population-continuous computational question, and the model is one the authors could recognize.

The mirror is narrower than the paper’s full procedure: it covers sampled MMER for MAP, not Bayesian updating or the full elicitation session. And the proposition’s finite-set termination proof does not itself carry over. Those are limits on the mirror, not fundamental obstructions; the LP formulation supplies a route around the termination issue.

So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor. The case against is weak: Proposition 3 is not a complexity theorem, but MAP supplies a credible high-multiplicity population model and a meaningful continuous optimization question. Claiming that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario would go beyond what the paper supports.
