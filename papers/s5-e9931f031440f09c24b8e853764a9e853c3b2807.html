The continuous mirror question

Given a finite compatibility graph \(H=(T,F)\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational equal-sharing rewards \(r_{ts}\), find a stable mass matching \(x\) with welfare \(W(x)\geq \tfrac12 OPT\), in time polynomial in \(\tau=|T|\), \(|F|\), and input bit length \(L\). Here \(x_{ts}\geq 0\), \(\sum_{s:\{t,s\}\in F}x_{ts}\leq\mu_t\), and \(OPT\) maximizes \(W(x)=\sum_{\{t,s\}\in F}r_{ts}x_{ts}\) over feasible mass matchings. Stability means there are no compatible types \(t,s\) with positive-mass groups assigned partner types \(u,v\), respectively, such that \(r_{ts}>r_{tu}\) and \(r_{ts}>r_{sv}\), taking the unmatched payoff to be \(0\).

The model it lives in

Types are cohorts with identical compatibility and equal-sharing reward schedules; \(\mu_t\) is each cohort’s population mass. The decision variables \(x_{ts}\) are masses of one-to-one matches between types, with unmatched mass allowed. Welfare is \(W(x)=\sum_{\{t,s\}\in F}r_{ts}x_{ts}\), and stability rules out mutually beneficial rematching between positive-mass groups.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the no-friendship, equal-sharing specialization of Corollary 1. It leaves the friendship and unequal-sharing results, and the contribution-game results, unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a stable mass matching with welfare at least \(\tfrac12 OPT\) be computed in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|F|\), and \(L\), with polynomial-bit output?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s equal-sharing stable matching result. The paper contains no named hardness result, so this is an algorithmic mirror, not a claim that population continuization dissolves hardness.

My anchor is Corollary 1, proved here from the paper’s convergence and price-of-stability results. It says that when \(\alpha_1=\alpha_2\), starting from a centrally optimal matching, best-blocking-pair dynamics reaches a stable matching in \(O(m^2)\) time and returns one whose welfare is at most a factor \(\frac{2+2\alpha_1}{1+2\alpha_1+\alpha_2}\) below optimum; here \(m\) is the graph’s number of edges. In the no-friendship case \(\vec{\alpha}=\mathbf{0}\), this gives a stable matching with at least half the optimum welfare.

A plausible regime is a large collaboration or matching platform with many participants but a modest number \(\tau\) of recurring profiles. A profile must include everything relevant to this problem: compatibility with other profiles and the reward from matching with each of them. For example, \(N\) participants might fall into a few dozen or hundred cohorts with the same compatibility and reward schedule. This is a complete-blow-up version of the paper’s matching graph: every participant in one profile has the same possible counterpart profiles and the same reward for each. The specialization to no friendship is deliberate; it preserves the paper’s matching and blocking-pair question without needing to invent a continuum analogue of its graph-distance social effects.

Here is the lead problem, *Stable Mass Matching with a Half-Optimal Guarantee*. The input is a finite undirected compatibility graph \(H=(T,F)\), binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and binary-rational equal-sharing rewards \(r_{ts}\geq 0\) for each \(\{t,s\}\in F\). Write \(\tau=|T|\), and let \(L\) be the total encoding length. A solution is a nonnegative mass \(x_{ts}=x_{st}\) of type-\(t\)/type-\(s\) pairs on each edge, with \(\sum_{s:\{t,s\}\in F}x_{ts}\leq\mu_t\). The leftover mass \(x_{t\bot}=\mu_t-\sum_s x_{ts}\) is unmatched. Thus each individual still gets either one whole partner or no partner; only the aggregate assignment is fractional.

Call \(x\) stable if there is no compatible pair of types \(t,s\), and no positive-mass groups currently assigned partner types \(u,v\) (where either may be \(\bot\)), such that both groups strictly prefer matching with each other: \(r_{ts}>r_{tu}\) and \(r_{ts}>r_{sv}\), taking \(r_{t\bot}=r_{s\bot}=0\). Ask for a stable \(x\) with \(W(x)=\sum_{\{t,s\}\in F}r_{ts}x_{ts}\geq \tfrac12 OPT\), where \(OPT\) is the maximum of this same objective over all feasible mass matchings, stable or not. The requested algorithm should run in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(|F|\), and \(L\), and output a rational \(x\) of polynomial bit length.

I expect this problem to be tractable: the paper’s maximum-weight starting point becomes a fractional matching LP, and its best-blocking-pair process suggests a type-level batch process that moves positive masses at once. This is recognizable as the authors’ problem: participants still form one-to-one matches, and instability still means two agents can both improve by matching with each other. Replacing counts of identical participants with masses is the population continuization; the fractional entries describe how many agents receive each integral match.

The weakest point is that Corollary 1’s \(O(m^2)\) bound does not itself prove a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\) and \(L\). Fractional batches and multiple partner groups per type may complicate termination and bit growth. That is a real proof obligation, and the mirror covers only the no-friendship, equal-sharing case. The next questions are whether the factor \(2\) is tight for mass assignments and whether the best stable mass matching can be computed exactly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best objection is that Corollary 1’s algorithm exploits a finite graph of named players. Its \(O(m^2)\) bound counts edge events in that graph; it does not show that the dynamics can be compressed when many players of one type split across different partner types. In a mass matching, a blocking type pair may involve only some of each type’s mass, so a deviation must choose how much to move and update the remaining partner groups. The paper gives no bound on how many such changes are needed in terms of \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

That objection narrows the mirror, but does not defeat it. The proponent’s complete-blow-up regime is coherent: agents in a type class have the same compatibility and reward schedule, and an aggregate assignment can represent their individual one-to-one matches. Their blocking condition can also be stated using the positive-mass groups assigned to each partner type. This is a well-posed population-continuous version of the stable matching question. The fact that the original graph’s friendship structure would often make types nearly unique is not decisive, since the mirror can specialize to the paper’s no-friendship result.

A stronger version of the objection is about payoff: this mirror covers only equal sharing without friendship, and the finite theorem already supplies a polynomial algorithm. Perhaps its mass version is just a routine batching of the same dynamics; perhaps exact computation requires work beyond that. The paper settles neither possibility. The gap between an algorithm polynomial in the number of cloned agents and one polynomial in \(\tau\) and binary input length is a genuine computational question, not evidence that the mirror is pointless.

So the negative case is weak. I can argue that the proposed regime is narrower than the paper’s main social-network setting and that Corollary 1 does not itself establish the claimed mass algorithm. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists: this anchor passes the programme’s high-multiplicity test, and determining whether the dynamics compress efficiently is exactly the kind of computational question the programme is meant to ask.
