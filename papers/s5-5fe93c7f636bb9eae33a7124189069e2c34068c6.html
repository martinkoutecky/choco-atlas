The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of valuation types \(T\), masses \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), item-type count \(t\), additive valuations \(v_a\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{t}\), and per-capita supplies \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{t}\), find integral bundles \(b_a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{t}\) satisfying \(\sum_{a\in T}\mu_a b_{a,z}=q_z\) for every item type \(z\), maximizing \(\min_{a\ne a'}v_a\cdot(b_a-b_{a'})\); report whether the optimum is nonnegative, or report infeasibility.

The model it lives in

A grouped high-multiplicity goods model in which valuation types \(a\) carry mass \(\mu_a\), each unit of a type receives an integral bundle \(b_a\), item supplies are specified per unit population by \(q\), and fairness is measured by the minimum additive envy gap. For proportionality, type mass may instead mix over integral bundles while each individual unit remains integral.

The objection that survived

Theorem 3's sufficient bound depends on the chosen integer representation of the same rational \((\mu,q)\), so it does not itself provide a representation-invariant per-capita threshold; the continuous problem must therefore be proved separately.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The lead mirror directly covers Theorem 3's goods envy-free guarantee; analogous cost formulations cover the chores results, and a configuration version covers Theorem 7's proportionality result. Cake cutting, stochastic corollaries, and the tEFX result remain unanchored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the exact integral-bundle problem admit an FPT algorithm in \(t+|T|\), or a polynomial-time algorithm under the paper's dissimilarity threshold, with complexity invariant under rescaling a rational representation?
  • What replaces \(k_z\equiv0\pmod g\) and the Frobenius threshold when \(\mu\) and \(q\) are real-valued or have large denominators?
  • Can the proportionality configuration LP over integral bundles be priced or approximated efficiently while preserving the guarantee for every unit of mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make Theorem 3 the lead anchor, with Theorem 7 as a supporting anchor. The paper contains no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity classification; these are theorem-level existence and algorithmic guarantees. Thus the mirror is of the underlying allocation problems, not of an existing complexity result.

My lead problem is \(\mathrm{CEFA}_{\infty}^{\mathrm{goods}}\), continuous high-multiplicity envy-free allocation for goods. There is a finite set \(T\) of valuation types and a population distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\). Type \(a\) has an additive valuation \(v_a\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{t}\) over \(t\) item types. The supply is specified per unit of population by \(q\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{t}\). Every unit of mass of type \(a\) must receive the same integral bundle \(b_a\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{t}\), with complete allocation requiring \(\sum_a\mu_a b_{a,z}=q_z\) for every item type \(z\).

The optimization question is to maximize the minimum envy gap
\[ \Delta^\star=\max_b\min_{a\ne a'}v_a\cdot(b_a-b_{a'}) \]
subject to those supply constraints. The output is the bundles \(b_a\), or a certificate that exact complete allocation is infeasible. The allocation is envy-free exactly when \(\Delta^\star\ge0\).

This is a genuine high-multiplicity population model. A finite realization with \(N\mu_a\) agents of type \(a\) and \(Nq_z\) copies of item \(z\) is precisely the finite grouped problem studied in the paper. Conversely, a group-symmetric finite allocation becomes a point in this continuous problem after dividing population and inventories by \(N\). The regime is a large food-bank, school-meal, or public-assistance system with millions of recipients but finitely many dietary or need profiles, and standardized goods supplied in many copies. The type count remains small relative to the population, and the valuation type includes every feature relevant to allocation.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. With \(d\) groups of sizes \(n_1,\ldots,n_d\), total population \(n\), \(t\) item types, \(g=\gcd(n_1,\ldots,n_d)\), and \(\theta=g(n_1/g-1)(n_d/g-1)\), it proves that if every item type \(z\) has \(k_z\) copies satisfying \(k_z\equiv0\pmod g\) and
\[ k_z\ge \frac{4n\bigl(d^2+t(\theta+n+nd-d-1)\bigr)} {\min_{i\ne i'}\|\widetilde v_i^{(2)}-\widetilde v_{i'}^{(2)}\|_2^2}, \]
then an envy-free integral allocation exists. Here \(\widetilde v_i^{(2)}\) is the one-copy \(\ell_2\)-normalized valuation of group \(i\). The theorem also yields the asymptotic statement \(\mu\in\mathcal O(tn^3/(g\eta))\) when every pairwise squared distance is at least \(\eta\).

The continuous question is not merely “replace \(n_i\) by real numbers.” It asks for the exact population-limit allocation problem: how large must the per-capita inventory \(q\) be, as a function of \(\mu\), the valuation geometry, and the number of types, before an envy-free integral bundle can be assigned to every unit of population? I expect \(\mathrm{CEFA}_{\infty}^{\mathrm{goods}}\) to be tractable in the intended fixed-type regime. The pure type-homogeneous formulation is an integer program with \(t|T|\) integer variables, so fixed-dimensional integer programming gives an FPT algorithm parameterized by the number of item and valuation types, independent of the number of named agents. If the number of types is unrestricted, hardness is likely to remain, but its combinatorics would live in the item and valuation types rather than in population multiplicity: a Class B-style boundary, not continuum-specific hardness.

The second anchor is Theorem 7, also proved here. For the single-copy setting \(k=1\), let \(S=\frac1n\sum_i\widetilde v_i^{(1)}\), let \(D^\star=\min_iD_{\chi^2}(\widetilde v_i^{(1)}\|S)\), and let \(\widetilde v_{\max}^{(1)}\) be the largest normalized item value. The theorem proves that if \(\widetilde v_{\max}^{(1)}\le D^\star/n\), then a proportional allocation exists; strict inequality gives a strongly proportional allocation. Its proof combines a trading-post fractional allocation with an integral rounding theorem.

The corresponding continuous problem, \(\mathrm{CP}_{\infty}^{\mathrm{goods}}\), uses the same \(T,\mu,v,q\), but does not require all agents of one valuation type to receive the same bundle. Let \(x_{a,B}\) be the mass of type \(a\) receiving an integral bundle \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{t}\). Require \(\sum_Bx_{a,B}=\mu_a\) and \(\sum_{a,B}x_{a,B}B_z=q_z\). Every supported bundle must satisfy \(v_a\cdot B\ge\alpha\,v_a\cdot q\). The objective is to maximize \(\alpha\); proportionality means \(\alpha\ge1\), and strong proportionality means \(\alpha>1\). Thus type-level averages may be fractional, but every individual unit of mass receives an integral bundle and satisfies the guarantee ex post.

This is especially plausible because the paper’s aggregate valuation already has the exact continuous replacement \(S_\mu=\sum_a\mu_a\widetilde v_a^{(1)}\). The trading-post LP becomes a configuration LP over integral bundles, with mass variables \(x_{a,B}\). For fixed item types, its pricing problem is a fixed-dimensional integer covering problem, suggesting a Class A result or at least an efficient approximation scheme in the high-multiplicity regime. With unrestricted item types, pricing may retain packing hardness, but again that hardness would be driven by item structure, not by the population being continuous.

The main further questions are whether the paper’s \(n\)-dependent copy bound has a meaningful per-capita limit, what replaces the divisibility condition \(k_z\equiv0\pmod g\) when \(\mu\) is genuinely real-valued, and whether approximate envy-freeness removes the remaining integer obstruction. The chores results should admit analogous mirrors using costs, KL divergence, and the harmonic-mean aggregate.

My weakest point is that the pure envy-free mirror is close to a compact encoding of the paper’s already-grouped finite problem: population mass appears mainly as coefficients in an integer program. Moreover, Theorem 3’s sufficient bound grows with the finite population size, so it does not by itself yield a nontrivial theorem for fixed per-capita supply in the continuum limit. The proportional mirror is stronger on this point because it uses genuine mass splitting while preserving integral bundles for individuals. I would therefore present Theorem 3 as the clearest high-multiplicity bridge, but Theorem 7 as the cleaner computationally continuous formulation. I would not use the paper’s cake-cutting Theorem 6 as an anchor: its continuity is in the outcome space, not in the population, and that would violate the programme’s scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that neither proposed anchor is actually a computational result. Theorem 3 is an existence threshold, and Theorem 7 is another existence threshold obtained from a fractional allocation and an integral-rounding lemma. Neither states a complexity classification, an approximation algorithm, or a parameterized algorithm. The proposed optimization problems are therefore new fair-division problems inspired by the paper, rather than continuous versions of named computational results.

Theorem 3 also has a serious population-limit problem. In the paper’s own grouped setting, write

\[ \mu_i=\frac{n_i}{n},\qquad q_z=\frac{k_z}{n}, \]

and let \(b_i\) be the integral bundle received by group \(i\). The proposed continuous feasibility equation

\[ \sum_i \mu_i b_{i,z}=q_z \]

is exactly the finite grouped allocation equation after clearing denominators. Thus, for rational \(\mu\) and \(q\), the “continuum” is merely a compressed encoding of the same finite high-multiplicity instance. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity regime, but it produces no population limit.

More damagingly, the same continuous instance has arbitrarily many finite representations: replacing \((n_i,k_z)\) by \((rn_i,rk_z)\) leaves \((\mu,q)\) unchanged. Yet Theorem 3’s threshold changes, roughly quadratically in \(r\), because \(g\) and \(\theta\) change with the representation. Hence its quantitative content is not a function of the proposed continuous instance. It is a statement about the arithmetic presentation of that instance, driven by divisibility and the Frobenius coin argument. Choosing a primitive denominator would be a new normalization, not a genuine limit theorem.

The proponent’s stronger repair is to allow each valuation type to split its mass across several integral bundles. That does make a coherent configuration LP, but it abandons the paper’s central group-symmetric allocation restriction. Its computational core is then a new bundle-pricing and decomposition problem; Theorem 3 supplies no result about it. If splitting is disallowed, the model collapses to the finite integer formulation above; if splitting is allowed, the anchor has changed.

Theorem 7 is not stronger as an anchor. Its condition

\[ \widetilde v_{\max}^{(1)}\le \frac{D^\star}{n} \]

is tied to a finite population of \(n\) named agents and a one-item rounding loss. Duplicating the agents changes \(n\) without changing their normalized valuations, so the condition is not invariant under the proposed population representation. To obtain a meaningful limit, one must simultaneously scale the item supply or replace single items by repeated item types. That is a new per-capita allocation model, not the \(k=1\) problem proved in Theorem 7.

The configuration-LP version may still be worthwhile in its own right. But the replacement of the finite average by

\[ S_\mu=\sum_a\mu_a\widetilde v_a^{(1)} \]

is only a formal averaging step. The difficult part is decomposing fractional type-level allocations into integral bundles while guaranteeing proportionality for every supported individual bundle. Theorem 7’s rounding lemma does not provide that decomposition. Again, the proposed mirror is a new computational programme rather than a continuization of the theorem.

The remaining results do not rescue the paper as a population-continuization source. The cake-cutting result continuizes the resource, not the population; the stochastic results continuize uncertainty; and the similar-agent result remains a finite-agent allocation theorem.

This negative case has an important weakness: the paper is unusually well suited to a high-multiplicity interpretation. A large food-bank or school-meal system with finitely many valuation types and many repeated goods is a sensible regime, and existing high-multiplicity work supports rather than collides with it. The repaired configuration problem is therefore genuinely defensible. The honest conclusion is not that no such mirror can exist, but that the paper itself offers no named computational anchor, while its two proposed anchors become either arithmetic encodings of the finite problem or new allocation problems whose computational interest must be established independently.
