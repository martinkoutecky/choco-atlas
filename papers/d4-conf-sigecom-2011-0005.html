The case FOR (proponent)

There is a numbering caveat: the introduction’s direct computational-hardness theorem is printed only as “Theorem:” with no number. I won’t invent one. My lead anchor is Theorem 5.14, proved in this paper. It is a structural result with a computational consequence, rather than a theorem that itself states an NP-hardness or polynomial-time bound.

Theorem 5.14 says that if \(A\) is a truthful, scalable mechanism for \(m>2\) items and two bidders with approximation ratio strictly better than \(2\), then some constant \(w>0\) exists such that, whenever both bidders’ valuations are degenerate—\(v(1)=u(1)=0\) and \(v(m-1)=v(m-2)\), \(u(m-1)=u(m-2)\)—the mechanism must output an allocation attaining \(\max_k\bigl(v(k)+w u(m-k)\bigr)\). In other words, even on that restricted domain, the incentive and approximation requirements force the mechanism to solve a weighted welfare-allocation problem. The paper’s unnumbered theorem in the introduction gives the conditional computational-hardness consequence; I treat it as context for this anchor, not as a numbered result.

A plausible population regime is a large auction for identical units—say, cloud-compute credits—where many buyers share a small number of complete valuation schedules. A type \(t\) is a buyer’s valuation function \(v_t\) over integer bundles \(k\in\{0,\ldots,K\}\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of buyers with that schedule. The number of buyers \(n\) is large while the number of types is small. Each buyer still receives an integer number of units; only the amount of a type’s mass assigned to each bundle can be fractional. Let \(\rho\) be the supply per buyer in the population limit.

Lead question — Atomless Multi-Unit Welfare Mechanism. Given a finite report domain \(\mathcal T\) of explicitly listed, normalized, nondecreasing rational valuations \(v_t:\{0,\ldots,K\}\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), a rational society distribution \(\mu\), and supply \(\rho\), does there exist a direct, scalable, truthful population mechanism that maximizes welfare? A feasible population allocation is \(x_{t,k}\ge0\), where \(\sum_k x_{t,k}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,k}k x_{t,k}\le\rho\); \(x_{t,k}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(k\). The mechanism takes reported masses, assigns bundles to that mass, and charges transfers. Truthfulness means that an individual agent cannot gain by reporting another type; the aggregate distribution is unchanged by one individual’s report in the atomless model. Scalability means that scaling all valuations by \(\alpha>0\) preserves allocations and scales transfers by \(\alpha\).

I expect this explicit-input version to be Class A. Its welfare benchmark is the linear program \(\max\sum_{t,k}x_{t,k}v_t(k)\). Strong duality gives a common per-unit price \(\lambda\): mass of type \(t\) is assigned only to bundles maximizing \(v_t(k)-\lambda k\). Charging \(\lambda k\) makes truthful reporting optimal at that price; a reported type can only select a bundle from its demand at \(\lambda\), while truthful reporting offers a utility-maximizing bundle. The resulting mechanism reaches the LP optimum, and the LP has size polynomial in \(|\mathcal T|\), \(K\), and the input bit length. The allocation can split a type’s mass among tied, integer-bundle choices; no individual receives a fractional bundle.

This is a credible population extension of the paper’s allocation and incentive problem: preferences remain over the same bundles, welfare remains the objective, and truthfulness still constrains what an agent gains by misreporting. The continuous feature is that cohorts can be divided while each buyer’s outcome remains integral. The mirror covers the welfare-allocation obstruction behind Theorem 5.14; it does not claim to characterize Triage auctions or reproduce the paper’s two-bidder theorem.

The weak point is substantial. An atomless buyer cannot influence the market price, so the mirror’s truthfulness condition removes the strategic externality central to the finite-bidder result. Authors could reasonably regard this as a price-taking market question rather than a continuous version of their deterministic finite auction. Also, the polynomial bound above uses explicit valuation tables: with succinct circuits or black-box values, the pricing problem \(\max_k(v_t(k)-\lambda k)\) may itself be hard. The next questions are whether an appropriate finite-\(n\) limit recovers the atomless mechanism with a quantified approximation or incentive error, and whether succinct valuation representations preserve any tractability gain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.14 is the proponent’s only anchor, and its statement is a characterization of finite mechanisms, not a complexity bound: for two bidders with degenerate valuations, a truthful scalable mechanism with approximation ratio below \(2\) must choose an allocation maximizing \(v(k)+w u(m-k)\). The paper’s computational result is a separate, unnumbered conditional theorem in the introduction. A stronger case would anchor the mirror there, but it faces the same modeling issue.

The proposed LP is a coherent atomless auction. Its truthfulness guarantee comes from each buyer having zero mass: a unilateral report cannot change the reported distribution or the price. In the paper, dominant-strategy truthfulness applies to a bidder whose report can affect the allocation and the other bidder’s payment. The LP therefore poses a price-taking market question, rather than continuing the incentive constraint characterized by Theorem 5.14. It solves the welfare-allocation problem while leaving the paper’s strategic question behind.

A closer repair would take a finite auction with many buyers sharing a small set of valuation schedules, set supply proportional to population, and require exact dominant-strategy truthfulness. Then one buyer’s report changes a type’s mass by \(1/n\), and that effect must remain in the incentive constraints. Taking the atomless limit first erases it; retaining it requires tracking finite deviations on the \(1/n\) grid. Treating each whole type cohort as a strategic bidder preserves mass-level influence, but changes the players from individual buyers to coordinated coalitions.

That is the strongest case against this anchor: the clean continuous mechanism derives its tractability from a change in what “truthful” means, while repairs that preserve individual incentives retain finite-agent effects. Still, this does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. A large auction with many buyers sharing complete valuation types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s model provides those types naturally. The negative case defeats this particular atomless witness; it is weak as a universal argument against every continuous mirror.
