Why no mirror

Theorem 3 is a computational result, but its convergence argument depends on a single voter being able to change the winner. In an atomless population, that unilateral move has no effect; allowing positive mass to revise would change the paper’s update process. The proposed limit is well-defined, but it studies a sequence of finite-electorate processes rather than a continuous-population analogue of the theorem.

What the mirror covers

The proposed limit touches only deterministic, unit-weight best-reply convergence in Theorem 3; it leaves aside the paper’s weighted, randomized, arbitrary-better-reply, and truth-biased cases.

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is a high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s serial best-reply dynamics. I would not replace a voter with a strategic mass player: that would let a whole group coordinate, contrary to the paper’s setup. Instead, the society is a distribution over preference types, and the continuous question asks which outcomes remain possible as that distribution is realized by larger and larger electorates.

The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that under deterministic tie-breaking, if every strategic voter has unit weight and uses the paper’s best-reply rule, then sequential single-voter updates reach a Nash equilibrium from any initial vote profile. A Nash equilibrium here is a profile where no voter can improve the winner by changing their vote alone. The proof also gives a bound of \(m^2k^2\) steps, where \(m\) is the number of candidates and \(k\) the number of strategic voters. This is an algorithmic convergence result, though not a conventional complexity classification such as NP-hardness or membership in P.

Here is the continuous problem I would put to a prover:

High-Multiplicity Iterative Plurality Limit. An instance consists of candidates \(C\) with a fixed deterministic tie-breaking order, and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings. Each ranking is a type; \(\mu_t\) is its share of the population. Start from the truthful state, represented by action masses \(x_{t,c}\), with \(x_{t,\operatorname{top}(t)}=\mu_t\) and all other \(x_{t,c}=0\).

Let \(D\) be a common denominator of the masses. For each positive integer \(r\), form the exact replication with \(rD\mu_t\) unit-weight voters of type \(t\). Run the paper’s sequential best-reply process from the truthful state, allowing any legal choice of the next voter. Let \(W_r(\mu)\) be the set of winners at terminal states over all such update sequences. The problem is to compute

\[ \mathcal{L}(\mu)=\{c\in C : c\in W_r(\mu)\text{ for infinitely many }r\}. \]

Thus the action variable at each finite scale is a ballot, and \(x_{t,c}\) records the fraction of type \(t\) currently casting ballot \(c\). The requested solution is the set of terminal winners that persist along arbitrarily large exact replications. Theorem 3 ensures that every finite run used in this definition terminates.

This mirrors the paper’s underlying question closely: the ranking types, plurality rule, tie-breaking, truthful start, unilateral vote changes, and best replies are all retained. What changes is the society representation and the question asked of it: rather than report convergence for one finite electorate, compute the limiting terminal-winner correspondence from the type distribution. The paper’s own discussion makes the scale relevant: it says the analysis is most suitable for small electorates and points to non-atomic voting models as more suitable when populations grow.

I expect this limit problem to be Class A, though Theorem 3 does not prove that. For truthful starts, the paper’s discussion bounds possible winners to candidates tied with the truthful plurality winner or one vote behind. Under exact replications of a fixed \(\mu\), a one-vote gap vanishes as a share when \(r\) grows. So \(\mathcal{L}(\mu)\) should be contained in the set of candidates with maximum first-place share, which is easy to compute. The remaining question is which of those tied candidates are reachable under some sequence of individual best replies. I would look for a finite transition structure over that tied set, with feasibility determined by the supported rankings and their masses. Further questions are whether this can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and how the answer changes for non-truthful starts or randomized tie-breaking.

The scope is deliberately narrow: this mirrors the truthful-start case of Theorem 3’s deterministic, unit-weight setting, not the paper’s weighted, randomized, or truth-biased variants. The paper proves that finite replications converge; it does not characterize their limiting winner set or provide a bit-complexity bound for computing it.

My weakest point is that this is a limit of finite-agent dynamics, not a primitive best-reply process for an atomless population. In a literal atomless game, one voter’s deviation has zero mass and generally cannot change the winner. I think the limit correspondence is still a credible mirror because it keeps individual, non-coordinated moves intact while making the input society the distribution \(\mu\). But an author or referee could reasonably ask whether that is continuous population dynamics, or a high-multiplicity question about discrete dynamics.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the paper’s strongest anchor, and it has real algorithmic content: under its assumptions, every sequential best reply path terminates, with a polynomial bound in the number of voters. The problem is that the moves making the theorem interesting are indivisible, one-voter pivots.

Represent the population by ballot masses \(x_{t,c}\), with plurality scores \(s_c(x)=\sum_t x_{t,c}\). In an atomless population, one voter’s deviation changes none of these masses. The outcome therefore stays the same, even at an exact tie, and the voter cannot improve it by changing their ballot. The theorem’s notion of a ballot that can make a candidate win has disappeared. With a unique plurality leader, this degeneracy is already visible in large finite replications: a fixed positive score-share gap eventually exceeds any single voter’s swing, so a truthful start has no improving move. Nontrivial behavior is confined to exact ties or gaps that shrink like \(1/n\). Exact ties have measure zero among possible share vectors, and an atomless voter still cannot break one.

The proposed \(\mathcal L(\mu)\) limit is a thoughtful repair. It preserves individual moves and asks a well-defined question about exact replications. But its answer is carried by those vanishing-scale pivots: each step changes the mass state by \(1/(rD)\), while whether that step changes the winner depends on integer score margins. Computing terminal winners across replication factors is thus an asymptotic reachability problem over finite transition systems. Replacing those systems with an atomless best-reply process erases the behavior the limit is meant to capture. The fact that \(\mu\) is a sensible high-multiplicity description of an electorate does not resolve this mismatch between the population representation and the dynamics.

A richer continuous process could allow positive masses to revise together, or prescribe revision rates and study time-rescaled trajectories. Those choices give nontrivial mass-level movement, but they add coordinated updates or a revision mechanism absent from the paper’s unilateral, asynchronous game. They would pose new computational questions, rather than continuize Theorem 3’s process.

So the strongest negative case is a scale singularity: generically the large-population dynamics stop immediately, while the interesting cases depend on an individual’s finite pivotal weight. I would not claim that \(\mathcal L(\mu)\) could never be worthwhile; it may be an interesting high-multiplicity reachability problem. But the paper gives less reason to expect a useful continuous-population mirror than the proponent suggests.
