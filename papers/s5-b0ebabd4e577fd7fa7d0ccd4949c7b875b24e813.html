What the mirror covers

It covers the MTC/MT Strong-PR claim and its scaled transfer invariant; it leaves the remaining axiomatic theorems, empirical simulations, greedy variants, and explicit complexity classification alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can finite-support rational-input MTC on \((\mu_t,v_t,\lambda,\kappa)\) be given a polynomial bit-complexity bound?
  • Does the continuous Strong-PR invariant hold under precise mass-transfer semantics and tie-breaking rules?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow. This paper contains no numbered theorem stating NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), parameterized complexity, or a similar computational classification. Its named results are axiomatic. Thus it cannot support a conventional Class B or Class C complexity claim. It can, however, support a genuine continuous-population mirror of its central MTC rule, with a plausible Class A algorithmic continuation.

My lead mirror is Continuous MTC Participatory Budgeting. Let \(P\) be the finite project set, let \(\lambda>0\) be the budget available per unit of population, and let \(\kappa(p)\) be the cost of project \(p\) per unit population. A voter type is a complete cumulative ballot \(v_t\in\Delta(P)\); it specifies the fraction of that type’s virtual coin assigned to each project. The society is a finite type distribution \(\mu\), with mass \(\mu_t\) on type \(t\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A feasible outcome remains an integral project bundle \(B\subseteq P\) satisfying \(\kappa(B)\le\lambda\). Thus the population is continuous, but the projects are not being fractionally funded.

This is exactly the high-multiplicity scaling of the paper’s model. With \(n\) voters of these proportions, take \(L_n=n\lambda\) and \(c_n(p)=n\kappa(p)\). The paper’s support condition becomes \(s_\mu(p)=\lambda\sum_t\mu_t v_t(p)\ge\kappa(p)\). Hence the continuous model is not merely an analogy: replication of the type distribution produces the corresponding discrete instance, while the decision remains a bundle of projects.

The action variable is the type-level support redistribution used by CSTV. MTC uses the GSC priority \(s_\mu(p)/\kappa(p)\), applies Minimal-Transfers when no project is initially eligible, redistributes excess proportionally after a project is funded, and uses Acceptance of Undersupported Projects at the end. In the continuous version, the transfer of support from project \(q\) to project \(p\) by type \(t\) is represented by a quantity \(z_{t,q\to p}\); there is no need to manipulate individual voters. The objective is the same rule-defined aggregation objective as in the paper: output the MTC-selected feasible bundle, together with its transfer history. The paper does not define a scalar welfare objective, so inventing one would no longer be its problem.

The named anchor is Theorem 6, proved in this paper: “MT and MTC satisfy Strong-PR but EwT and EwTC fail it.” Strong-PR says that for every \(\ell\in[L]\), every voter group \(V'\) with \(|V'|\ge \ell n/L\), and every project set \(P'\), if all voters in \(V'\) support every project in \(P'\) and no other project, then either all of \(P'\) is funded or every unfunded \(p\in P'\) satisfies \(c(p)+c(P'\cap R(E))>\ell\).

The continuous question is therefore:

Given \(P,\lambda,\kappa,\mathcal T,\mu\), a mass \(a\), a real budget share \(\ell\in[0,\lambda]\), and \(P'\subseteq P\), compute the MTC bundle \(B_\infty\) and determine whether the following continuous Strong-PR condition holds: if \(a\ge\ell/\lambda\) and every type in a mass-\(a\) coalition assigns positive support exactly to projects in \(P'\), then either \(P'\subseteq B_\infty\), or every \(p\in P'\setminus B_\infty\) satisfies \(\kappa(p)+\kappa(P'\cap B_\infty)>\ell\).

This is a faithful mirror: the population mass replaces \(|V'|/n\), per-capita costs replace \(c(p)/n\), and the funded object remains an integral bundle. The proof of Theorem 6 is based on aggregate support invariants, so its reasoning should carry over essentially unchanged from finite sums to \(\sum_t\mu_t(\cdot)\). Its evaluation problem also appears Class A for finite rational type support: every operation is over \(m\) projects and \(\tau\) types, and the rule performs only finitely many funding and transfer stages. The paper itself calls MTC computationally efficient, although it does not state a numbered bit-complexity theorem; that formal polynomial bound would be a result of the mirror project, not a result already proved here.

A natural regime is a municipal or organizational PB election with hundreds of thousands of residents, a few dozen or hundred projects, and a small collection of repeated ballot templates: for example, residents of the same ward, school association, housing cooperative, or civic organization using the same allocation among transport, parks, schools, and social services. Then \(n\) is very large while \(\tau\ll n\). This is more convincing than claiming that every free-form cumulative ballot in the paper’s simulations repeats exactly. The type includes the whole cumulative vector, so the mirror does not erase relevant voter information; it assumes a high-multiplicity instance regime.

The weakest point is that exact repetition of rich cumulative ballots is less obviously natural than repetition of approval ballots or rankings. If voters independently enter arbitrary real-valued splits, \(\tau\) may be close to \(n\), and the high-multiplicity gain disappears. A second weakness is that Theorem 6 is axiomatic rather than a complexity result, so this is not a strong answer to the programme’s NP-hardness-dissolution question. Still, the mirror is technically clean and recognisably the authors’ problem: it continuizes the electorate, preserves integral project selection and the MTC transfer mechanism, and asks whether the paper’s central proportionality guarantee survives in the resulting high-multiplicity model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper supplies no eligible computational anchor. Theorem 6 is an axiomatic theorem: it says that MT and MTC satisfy Strong-PR. It does not define a complexity problem, prove polynomial-time solvability, establish hardness, or give an approximation or parameterized result. The paper’s statement that MTC is “computationally efficient” is an informal description of a finite procedure, not a numbered computational result.

The proposed continuous question therefore changes the object being studied. “Given a continuous society, compute the MTC bundle” is simply execution of the paper’s rule with weighted sums. “Determine whether continuous Strong-PR holds” is a universal axiom-verification theorem, not a computational problem posed by the paper. A formal bit-complexity bound for the procedure could be useful in isolation, but it would be a new audit of an already explicit finite loop, not the kind of population-complexity question ChoCo is meant to expose.

The best version of the mirror does not repair this. Let each type \(t\) have cumulative ballot \(v_t\) and mass \(\mu_t\). At every stage of MTC, the relevant quantities are weighted sums such as

\[ \lambda\sum_t \mu_t v_t(p) \]

and, for transfer eligibility, weighted sums over the types currently supporting \(p\). Proportional redistribution updates the current ballot of each type. Thus an explicit finite-support implementation stores \(\tau\) type states and performs a bounded number of project-selection, elimination, and rescaling rounds. Continuization replaces voter-indexed sums by mass-weighted sums; it does not create an LP with an exponential type space, a pricing problem, or any population-driven combinatorial search.

The continuous Strong-PR statement is even more transparently inherited. Replace \(|V'|/n\) by coalition mass \(a\), \(c(p)\) by \(\kappa(p)\), and finite sums by integrals. The proof’s invariant is an aggregate-support invariant, so the discrete argument transfers almost verbatim. That is a legitimate high-multiplicity reformulation, and the municipal setting makes repeated ballot templates plausible. But it is precisely a linearity extension of the paper’s axiom, not a new computational mirror. Existing high-multiplicity intuition supports the model’s plausibility; it does not turn this axiomatic result into a complexity result.

One could improve the proposal by asking for the minimum mass of voters whose cumulative ballots must be changed to make MTC fund a target project, or by studying winner robustness under mass perturbations. Those could be worthwhile ChoCo problems. They would, however, be new campaigning and robustness problems surrounding MTC, not continuations of Theorem 6. Likewise, allowing a density over the whole simplex \(\Delta(P)\) would introduce integration and representation issues; with explicit finite support it remains routine, while with a succinct density the difficulty comes from that encoding rather than from the paper’s result.

So the proponent has shown that a mathematically sensible continuous-population version of MTC exists. They have not shown a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper. The paper’s central contributions are axiomatic and empirical, and its only proposed anchor becomes a nearly automatic weighted-profile restatement. The universal modelling claim— that no future MTC-related continuous control problem could ever be valuable—cannot honestly be proved. But under ChoCo’s stated anchor standard, the negative verdict is strong: this paper has no qualifying computational result to continuize.
