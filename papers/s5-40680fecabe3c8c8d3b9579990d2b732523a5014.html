The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of types \(t\), each specifying a strict school ranking and a priority tier at every school, rational masses \(\mu_t\), and rational school capacities, compute a student-proposing mass-SOSM baseline \(b_{t,s_0}\). At each proposal round, schools accept applicants in priority-tier order up to capacity, splitting a cutoff tier proportionally. Then find rational assignment masses \(x_{t,s_0,s}\) minimizing \(\sum_{t,s_0,s} x_{t,s_0,s}(\operatorname{rank}_t(s)-1)\), subject to assigning all mass from each baseline cohort, respecting school capacities, and allowing a cohort to move only to a school it ranks at least as highly as \(s_0\).

The model it lives in

Types carry school rankings and priority tiers; \(b_{t,s_0}\) is the mass of type \(t\) initially assigned to school \(s_0\); \(x_{t,s_0,s}\) is the mass transferred to \(s\). Schools have population-scaled capacities, and the objective is the aggregate preference reverence index. A solution can be accompanied by a decomposition of its transfers into mass trading cycles.

What the mirror covers

Covers selection of a minimum-preference-index allocation among Pareto improvements of the mass SOSM baseline, corresponding to the paper’s TADAM reachability result. It leaves aside coalition manipulation, strategic properties, and comparisons with TTC.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does proportional acceptance at a cutoff tier give a mass-SOSM baseline the authors would regard as a faithful aggregate of their tie-breaking setting?
  • Can every rational assignment-mass vector that Pareto improves on the baseline be realized by a finite rational decomposition into trading cycles, including null cycles?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a mirror of the paper’s TADAM result. The paper has no named theorem claiming a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified by complexity. Its best computational anchor is instead a constructive result.

Anchor — Proposition 3.6, proved in this paper. If a matching \(M\) Pareto dominates the SOSM matching \(M^*\), then \(M\) is realizable by TADAM, up to null cliques. In other words, every matching that makes no student worse off than SOSM and makes at least one student better off can be reached by a sequence of trading cliques; null cliques can complete the rearrangement without changing anyone’s assignment. The proposition’s proof constructs the cycles from the permutation of students’ SOSM assignments induced by \(M\).

A plausible regime is a large district in which many students share a relatively small set of complete types: each type specifies a school ranking and a priority tier at every school. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the district with type \(t\), and let each school’s capacity be specified as a fraction of the population. Priorities can be coarse tiers, with a cutoff splitting tied mass proportionally, as an aggregate representation of tie-breaking. The number of students can then be large while the support size \(\tau\) stays moderate.

My proposed question is Minimum-Preference-Index Continuous TADAM. An instance gives the finite type support and rational masses, school capacities, and a baseline allocation \(b\) from a mass version of student-proposing SOSM. Treat each positive amount \(b_{t,s_0}\) as a cohort of type \(t\) endowed with school \(s_0\). Find a mass allocation \(x_{t,s_0,s}\) that assigns every cohort to schools, respects school capacities, and sends no mass to a school that type \(t\) ranks below \(s_0\). Among these allocations, minimize the aggregate preference index
\[ \sum_{t,s_0,s} x_{t,s_0,s}\bigl(\operatorname{rank}_t(s)-1\bigr). \]
A solution is the rational assignment-mass vector \(x\), together with a decomposition into mass trading cycles.

This is the population version of selecting a good outcome from TADAM’s Pareto improvements. The paper itself suggests minimizing the preference index when choosing among efficient outcomes. Any optimum is Pareto efficient among allocations that do not worsen anyone’s SOSM assignment: a further Pareto improvement would strictly lower the rank sum. Proposition 3.6 is the bridge to TADAM: its finite matching claim has a natural mass analogue, where balanced trades decompose into cycles among cohorts holding the initial school endowments. The underlying problem is assignment optimization; the LP is a likely way to solve it, not the object being mirrored.

I expect this version to be tractable: with at most \(\tau m\) baseline cohorts, the assignment variables and capacity constraints are polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. That is a prediction for the proposed mirror, not a result established by this paper. Further questions include whether every rational Pareto-efficient mass allocation has a finite rational cycle decomposition, and how closely fractional tie-breaking represents discrete SOSM outcomes in large districts.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors TADAM’s post-SOSM trading and selection of Pareto improvements, not the paper’s claims about coalition manipulation, strategyproofness, or TTC. The weakest point is the baseline. The paper’s SOSM uses strict individual priorities, while a continuous model with priority tiers may split tied mass and therefore changes the baseline mechanism. That is a real co-modification, but the paper itself discusses large priority classes and tie-breaking as an efficiency concern. If the authors accept proportional cutoff mass as the aggregate account of that tie-breaking, the post-baseline question remains recognizably theirs: which Pareto-efficient assignment improvements can be made after starting from SOSM?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor, Proposition 3.6, is a reachability characterization, not a computational result. It says which finite matchings TADAM can realize; it gives no complexity claim, encoded optimization problem, or running-time analysis. The paper’s numbered results concern welfare, incentives, and mechanism properties. Its remark that searching all coalitions could take substantial computing power never becomes a formal computational question. That leaves no computational result here for a continuous mirror to classify.

The proposed minimum-preference-index problem is the best attempt to supply one. Given a baseline allocation \(b\), it is a coherent mass assignment problem: move each endowed cohort only to schools it likes at least as much, respect capacities, and minimize aggregate rank. But that is an added selection problem, not Proposition 3.6 in continuous form. Proposition 3.6 characterizes outcomes of TADAM; the proposed LP selects one outcome using a welfare criterion drawn from elsewhere in the paper. Its variables and constraints describe a general transportation problem. A cycle decomposition may explain the resulting transfers, but it does not recover a computational claim made by the paper.

There is also a substantive obstacle to carrying over the paper’s baseline. Its types would need to record student preferences and priority positions at every school. With strict priorities, those positions distinguish individuals, so repeated complete types—and hence a large population with moderate support—are generally unavailable. Grouping students by priority tier makes repetition possible, but then the model needs a new rule for allocating seats when a cutoff splits a tier. A proportional cutoff may be sensible, but it defines a new mass version of SOSM. The LP then studies improvements after that newly defined baseline; it does not compute the paper’s SOSM-to-TADAM transformation. The finite proof’s permutation of individually endowed students has become a flow decomposition over divisible cohort mass.

I would therefore reject this as a compelling mirror of this paper’s computational contribution: there is no such contribution to mirror, and the strongest proposed question adds a standard mass-assignment objective while requiring a newly specified priority mechanism. This is not an airtight case against every possible ChoCo extension. If the authors accept a nonatomic tie-breaking model and want to study welfare selection after its mass-SOSM baseline, the question is coherent and close to their policy discussion. That possibility makes the universal negative weak; the stronger conclusion is that the paper itself does not furnish a worthwhile computational anchor for it.
