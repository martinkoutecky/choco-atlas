What the mirror covers

The proposed ranking-mass reinterpretation could restate Theorems 2–7 and Corollary 1 as axiomatic claims; it covers no computational result, and the cited quantum voting security protocols are left alone.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a population reinterpretation of the paper’s quantum Condorcet construction. Let each type \(t\) be a complete ranking of the candidates, and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the electorate with that ranking. For each pair \(x,y\), the mass preferring \(x\) to \(y\) is \(p_{xy}=\sum_{t:x\succ_t y}\mu_t\). A population version could compute Condorcet scores from these pairwise masses, then form an output state over the linear orders extending the resulting weak order, with minority and unanimity steps keyed to positive mass and unanimous mass. A large election with many repeated ballot types is a plausible regime for this model.

But the paper has no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror to. Theorem 2, proved here, says Quantum Condorcet Voting satisfies sharp unanimity; Theorem 3, proved here, says it satisfies unsharp unanimity; Theorem 4, proved here, says it satisfies sharp IIA; Theorem 5, proved here, says it satisfies unsharp IIA; Theorem 6, proved here, says it does not satisfy sharp dictatorship; and Theorem 7, proved here, says it does not satisfy unsharp dictatorship. Corollary 1, proved here by combining those results, says Arrow’s impossibility theory is not valid in quantum voting. Theorem 1 is the classical Arrow result, cited from Arrow: for three or more candidates, a social welfare function satisfying unanimity and IIA cannot satisfy nondictatorship. These are axiomatic claims, not results about complexity or algorithms.

So the population construction is a possible neighboring research question, but it asks whether axioms hold for a population-level rule, outside this programme’s computational scope. Making it fit would also require a defensible population analogue of dictatorship and a choice about how to represent the quantum states and correlations in a population distribution. I would not submit it as a prover-ready continuous problem per anchor: there is no eligible anchor here. The weakness in the positive case is fundamental to this paper’s results, rather than to the plausibility of large populations with repeated ballots.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest point stands: every result they name is axiomatic. Theorems 2–5 assert unanimity and IIA properties; Theorems 6–7 assert failures of dictatorship; Corollary 1 draws an Arrow-style conclusion. Theorem 1 is the classical background result. None studies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. That leaves no computational result here for ChoCo to continuize.

The most natural population version is coherent: treat rankings as types with masses, compute pairwise support from those masses, and adapt the minority and unanimity steps. It could yield continuous versions of the paper’s axiom claims. But that would still be an axiomatic treatment of a social welfare rule, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. The existence of many voters sharing rankings makes the population model plausible; it does not turn those theorems into complexity results.

A stronger computational proposal would add a mass-transfer problem: find the least-cost way to change a population so that Quantum Condorcet Voting gives a candidate or ranking a desired outcome. That could be an interesting project, but the paper specifies no intervention, costs, manipulation objective, or robustness question. Adding those would create a new problem around its rule, rather than mirror a result in the paper. Its mention of future work on the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem likewise points toward another axiomatic question, not computational complexity. And a ranking-type model covers the classical basis-ballot regime; representing the full quantum input would also need to preserve correlations among voters, which a distribution over individual types does not capture.

So the negative case is strong on the paper-specific criterion: its plausible continuous versions are either axiomatic or newly invented computational problems. That does not show that no future computational study of quantum voting could be worthwhile. It does show that this paper offers no worthwhile continuous mirror within ChoCo’s stated programme.
