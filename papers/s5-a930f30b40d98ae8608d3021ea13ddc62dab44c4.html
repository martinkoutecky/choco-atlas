What the mirror covers

The proposed population setting could extend the paper’s aggregation questions to distributions over support-relation types. The paper’s preservation results remain axiomatic, so none is covered by a continuous computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no named computational result in this paper to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Its numbered results concern which aggregation rules preserve argumentation properties, not the complexity of a computational problem.

The closest candidate is Theorem 8, proved in this paper: when \(|\mathrm{Arg}|\ge 5\), any aggregation rule that is unanimous, grounded, neutral, and independent must be dictatorial if it preserves the property of being a \(d\)-preferred, \(s\)-preferred, or \(c\)-preferred extension. Theorem 9 gives the corresponding impossibility for necessary support, and Theorem 10 gives it for stable extensions. These are axiomatic impossibility results; they do not assert that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or has another computational-complexity classification.

A plausible population setting would be a large policy consultation in which many participants share exactly the same view of which arguments support which others. A type would be a complete support relation on the fixed argument and attack framework; its mass would be the fraction of participants with that view. One could ask whether an aggregation rule on the resulting distribution preserves a specified semantic property while satisfying population versions of the paper’s axioms.

That is a recognizable continuization of the population, but it does not provide the requested mirror: the paper has no computational question or named computational result for it to mirror. Moreover, dictatorship in the finite model selects a named individual, which does not directly carry over to a distribution over types. Since the paper’s actual results are axiomatic, their continuization also falls outside the programme’s computational scope. I therefore have no qualifying anchor or prover-ready continuous problem to offer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 8 says that certain axioms plus preservation of preferred extensions force dictatorship; Theorems 9 and 10 give related impossibilities. Those are claims about which aggregation rules satisfy a set of axioms, not about the complexity of a computational problem. The paper does not analyze the time or space needed to find an aggregation rule, compute an aggregate, or test preservation.

The policy consultation is a sensible high-multiplicity setting: each complete support relation can be a type, and its mass the fraction of participants holding it. That makes the population model plausible, but it does not supply the missing computational question. Under a mass-based version, a quota rule can aggregate each argument pair from its total support mass. Asking which such rules preserve preferred or stable extensions remains an axiomatic question. Asking how hard it is to compute the extensions of the resulting finite argumentation framework would be a different computational question, with the difficulty in the argumentation instance rather than in continuizing the population.

A stronger proposal could ask for an algorithm that synthesizes a non-dictatorial rule or optimizes a trade-off between preservation and consensus. But the paper specifies no such task or objective; either would add a new research problem rather than mirror one of its results. Moving to fractional or probabilistic collective support would introduce continuity in the outcome, not make the population the computational object.

So I would reject Theorems 8–10 as ChoCo anchors. The negative case is weak only as a universal claim: a future computational paper could build a worthwhile population-based problem on this setting. But on this paper’s results, the natural continuous model stays in the axiomatic study of aggregation, outside the programme’s computational scope.
