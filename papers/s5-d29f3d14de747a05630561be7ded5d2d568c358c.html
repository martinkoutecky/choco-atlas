What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named result that meets your computational anchor requirement. Its named results classify set inclusion between manipulability domains; none says that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or a related class. I can’t present one as a complexity result.

The strongest structural result I would use if that requirement were relaxed is Theorem 1, proved here: for every voter count \(n\), candidate count \(m\), and \(i<j\), \(i\)-approval and \(j\)-approval are incomparable under Pathak–Sönmez manipulability. That is, neither rule is manipulable on every profile where the other is manipulable.

A plausible regime is a large electorate with many voters but relatively few distinct complete rankings: for example, a large membership vote with a small set of recurring ballot patterns. Let \(T=L(C)\) be the rankings of \(m\) candidates. A continuous society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), with support size \(\tau\). To preserve the paper’s unilateral-manipulation question, give each voter a unit of mass and let the total population size \(N\) be rational. A voter of type \(t\) can be selected whenever \(N\mu_t\ge1\); changing that voter’s report to \(t'\) changes the society to \(\mu+\frac1N(\delta_{t'}-\delta_t)\). The winner remains a single candidate, with the paper’s lexicographic tie-breaking.

My lead candidate is Continuous-Background PS Incomparability Witness. Its input is \(m\) and \(i<j\). Its solution is two finite-support rational societies, \((N^+,\mu^+)\) and \((N^-,\mu^-)\), such that:

  • at \((N^+,\mu^+)\), some voter can manipulate \(j\)-approval and no voter can manipulate \(i\)-approval;
  • at \((N^-,\mu^-)\), some voter can manipulate \(i\)-approval and no voter can manipulate \(j\)-approval.

Here “manipulate” means that changing one voter’s reported ranking makes the winning candidate strictly better according to that voter’s true ranking. The construction keeps the paper’s candidates, rankings, scoring rules, individual action, and objective. Only the background counts become fractional.

I expect the answer to this witness question to be yes for every \(m,i,j\): set \(N\) to an integer and \(\mu\) to the empirical distribution of a finite profile. Those profiles are included in the continuous domain, so Theorem 1’s two counterexamples transfer directly. That establishes continued incomparability; it establishes no complexity classification. A worthwhile follow-up would ask how manipulation varies across nonintegral masses, or how to find a profitable report for a given rational society. The paper proves no result about either question.

The weakest point is that the focal voter must retain mass \(1/N\). In a fully nonatomic population, an individual has zero mass and cannot change a scoring outcome; requiring a positive-mass bloc to deviate would change the paper’s unilateral-manipulation concept. The other limitation is more fundamental for ChoCo: although the continuous population mirror is precise and faithful to Theorem 1, that theorem is structural rather than computational. I would cover Theorem 1 alone and present this as a plausible high-multiplicity extension, not as an anchor-backed computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result here to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 1 proves a set-inclusion fact: each approval rule is manipulable on some profile where the other is not. It gives no complexity classification for finding a manipulation, and the paper does not formulate an algorithmic problem whose complexity it studies.

The proponent’s witness-search question doesn’t supply that missing target. Its answer is yes because the discrete counterexample profiles are included among the proposed societies: choose \(N\) and \(\mu\) to reproduce their voter counts. That establishes the same incomparability witnesses in a larger domain; it asks no new computational question about genuinely fractional populations. This is more than an objection that the answer happens to be unchanged: the proposed task is settled by the paper’s existing constructions.

A richer version could ask how manipulability behaves at nonintegral masses, or how hard it is to find a profitable report for a rational society. But preserving the paper’s unilateral move requires preserving an individual voter’s influence. In a population normalized to mass one, that influence is \(1/N\); the society therefore needs \(N\) as well as \(\mu\). In a genuinely nonatomic model, one individual has zero mass and cannot change the winner. Positive-mass deviations can change it, but then the question concerns a coalition: its size must be specified, or made an optimization objective. That is a plausible neighboring research direction, but it adds a strategic unit and an objective the paper does not provide.

I would not claim that elections lack high-multiplicity regimes; large electorates with repeated rankings are quite plausible. The negative case is narrower: this paper’s central comparison is about unilateral, profile-by-profile manipulability, and its named results are structural rather than computational. One can invent continuous strategic questions around it, but none is a computational mirror anchored by the results here. The case is strongest on that point and weaker against the possibility that the authors would recognize a carefully chosen coalition or finite-resolution extension as worthwhile.
