The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval ballots, an initial size-\(k\) committee \(W_0\), and the paper's candidate order, compute the complete trajectory of strict PAV-improving swaps under lexicographic better response through the first swap-free committee. Characterize the worst-case trajectory length in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(k\).

The model it lives in

Types are approval ballots \(A\subseteq C\) with masses \(\mu_A\); committees remain integral and have size \(k\). The per-capita potential is \(\Phi_\mu(W)=\sum_A\mu_A H_{|A\cap W|}\), and the state is the committee, with swaps accepted exactly when they strictly increase \(\Phi_\mu\). The fixed lexicographic pivot rule determines the next swap, and the requested output is the full trajectory. Setting \(\mu_A=n_A/n\) recovers each discrete PAV score up to the positive factor \(n\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9's fixed-pivot trajectory lower bound; Theorem 3's adversarial sequence has the same mass-based analogue. It leaves the empirical comparison, the best-response question, and the complexity of finding a terminal local optimum without replaying the trajectory outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can best-response dynamics on rational approval-type distributions have superpolynomially long trajectories?
  • Can a swap-local optimum be found in polynomial time without outputting or replaying the prescribed trajectory?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s Theorem 9, about \(0^+\)-ls-PAV with lexicographic better response. It is a close fit because PAV already evaluates voters through an aggregate score: replacing a sum over people with an expectation over voter types preserves the very quantity that drives every swap.

Let the candidates be \(C\), and take the voter types to be approval ballots \(A\subseteq C\). A type’s mass \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of the population with that ballot; the society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) with support size \(\tau\). This is a complete type description for the problem: agents with ballot \(A\) have identical PAV utility for every committee. For a size-\(k\) committee \(W\), define its per-capita PAV welfare by
\[ \Phi_\mu(W)=\sum_{A\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\mu_A H_{|A\cap W|}, \qquad H_r=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac1j,\quad H_0=0. \]
The society stays fixed. The decision variable is a sequence of swaps in the finite committee: remove \(a\in W\), add \(b\notin W\), and accept the swap exactly when \(\Phi_\mu((W\setminus\{a\})\cup\{b\})>\Phi_\mu(W)\).

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large town, university, or organization choosing \(k\) public projects from \(m\) candidates. Many residents may share the same approval ballot, while the number of ballot types is small relative to the population. The paper’s constructed elections have polynomially many voters in \(k\), and their voters can be grouped into at most that many approval types. Replicating those voters makes the population arbitrarily large without changing \(\mu\), the support size, or the swap dynamics.

Theorem 9 states that for every \(k\ge 1\), there is a committee election with \(\operatorname{poly}(k)\) voters and an initial size-\(k\) committee \(W_0\) such that \(0^+\)-ls-PAV with lexicographic better response makes \(\Omega(k^{\log k})\) swaps. Here the pivot rule scans incoming candidates in a fixed order and, for each one, scans committee members in order for a strictly improving swap. The theorem is proved in the paper’s Appendix A; its proof is omitted from the conference body. The paper also gives the intermediate Theorem 3, proving an \(\Omega(k^{\log k})\)-length sequence of strictly improving swaps when swaps may be chosen adversarially. Theorem 9 is my sole anchor: it establishes the stronger claim for a fixed pivot rule.

The corresponding continuous question is Continuous Lexicographic PAV Trajectory. An instance consists of \(C\), \(k\), a rational type distribution \(\mu\), an initial size-\(k\) committee \(W_0\), and a linear order on \(C\). Starting from \(W_0\), run the strict-improvement rule above, using the lexicographic pivot rule, until no improving swap remains. The requested solution is the resulting swap sequence and terminal committee; equivalently, one can ask whether the trajectory makes at least \(q\) swaps. The model keeps committees integral and continuizes the population only.

Theorem 9 carries over directly. If the discrete construction has \(n_A\) voters with ballot \(A\), set \(\mu_A=n_A/n\). Then the discrete PAV score of every committee is \(n\Phi_\mu(W)\). Every swap therefore has the same sign in both models, and the fixed pivot rule follows the same path. Since \(m\), \(\tau\), and the binary encoding length \(L\) of these masses are polynomial in \(k\), the continuous mirror has instances with a superpolynomially long prescribed trajectory in those parameters. This is a hardness-transfers result in the programme’s sense, but it is specifically a lower bound on this local-search procedure’s execution. It is not an NP-hardness result, and it does not prove that finding a terminal committee by some other method takes superpolynomial time.

That distinction supplies the natural follow-up questions: can best-response dynamics also have a superpolynomial trajectory in this continuous model, and can one find a swap-local optimum in polynomial time without replaying the lexicographic trajectory? The paper leaves both questions open.

The weakest point is that this mirror mostly preserves an existing discrete trajectory under normalization. The combinatorial difficulty remains in the candidate agenda and pivot rule; continuous population does not dissolve it or create a new algorithmic advantage here. But that is also why the mirror is faithful: PAV is already defined by population aggregation, and the high-multiplicity version asks the same local-search question for a society represented by type masses.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against this anchor is that it measures the run of one specified pivot rule, not the difficulty of finding a fair committee or even a swap-local optimum. The paper itself says a different algorithm might find an outcome in polynomial time. If the mirror asks for the entire trajectory, its length is part of the output; if it asks only for the terminal committee, Theorem 9 gives no lower bound. The unanswered best-response question is interesting, but it is a new algorithm question rather than a result this theorem mirrors.

The population model also contributes little to this particular lower bound. For masses \(\mu_A=n_A/n\), every PAV score is the discrete score divided by \(n\), so each comparison and lexicographic choice is preserved exactly. The theorem’s instances already have polynomially many voters and candidates. That makes the mirror look like a faithful restatement of a finite algorithmic phenomenon, rather than a case where treating a large society as masses reveals new structure.

But this does not defeat the anchor under ChoCo’s stated standard. Local-search runtime is a computational question, approval ballots are complete types, and a large community choosing public projects is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. The exact correspondence is not disqualifying: it is a discrete-to-continuous identity, which the programme treats as a possible result. A stronger mirror could ask about trajectories on arbitrary rational type distributions, or whether a local optimum can be found without replaying the trajectory; neither is settled by Theorem 9.

So the negative case is weak. I can argue that this is a lower-priority mirror because it follows a particular dynamics, but I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists for this anchor.
