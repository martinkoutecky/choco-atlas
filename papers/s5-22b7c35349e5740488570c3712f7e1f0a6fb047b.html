The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a named computational result in the supplied text to anchor a continuous mirror. The closest results are Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, both proved here. Theorem 1 characterizes unanimous, strategyproof social choice functions in the baseline model as locally bilateral top selections. Theorem 2 gives the constrained-model characterization: such functions are constrained-top selections with locally bilateral assignment rules, and every nonconstant component must be iso-constrained. These are structural mechanism-design theorems; neither establishes a complexity class, an algorithm, or a computational formulation. The numbered lemmas are structural as well.

There is a plausible population setting nearby: a very large society with relatively few recurring complete SEU preference types, each type specifying both valuations and beliefs, and \(\mu_t\) giving its population share. One could ask whether a mass-based extension of the theorems’ characterization exists. But that would be an axiomatic characterization question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. Making it computational would require adding a problem the paper does not pose.

There is also a substantive modeling obstacle: the theorems use unilateral reports. In an atomless population, one person’s report does not change an aggregate distribution, so strategyproofness for aggregate-based rules may become vacuous. Replacing unilateral deviations with deviations by positive mass would change the incentive notion. Likewise, assigning fractions of states to types risks turning the act into a lottery, continuizing outcomes rather than just the population.

So the honest positive case is limited: high multiplicity is plausible for the underlying population, but this text supplies no eligible computational anchor, and a precise computational mirror would have to be invented rather than mirrored from a named result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes unanimous, strategyproof social choice functions as locally bilateral top selections. Theorem 2 gives a corresponding characterization with state-dependent feasibility constraints. The lemmas establish those structural claims. None poses an algorithmic problem with encoded instances, an objective, or a complexity measure.

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is still axiomatic: take a large society with a distribution over complete preference types and ask whether the characterizations extend to that setting. That could be a sensible question. In fact, I would not object that repeated types are implausible: with finitely many acts, people can share the same strict preference ordering over acts. But classifying which mechanisms satisfy axioms in a continuum society remains outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

There is also a problem with carrying over the incentive condition. The original theorems concern unilateral reports by individuals. For a rule that depends only on the population distribution, one person’s report has zero mass and leaves that distribution unchanged. Replacing unilateral deviations with positive-mass deviations may avoid that degeneracy, but it changes the incentive notion and calls for new theorems. Keeping the state space, feasible acts, and deterministic outcomes finite does not resolve this.

One could invent a computational task around these mechanisms—for example, optimize a social objective over the strategyproof rules—but the paper supplies neither that objective nor an input representation for rules. Its theorems do not single out such a task. So neither named result survives as a computational anchor, even under a plausible high-multiplicity scenario. The universal claim has a limit: related computational mechanism-design questions could be worthwhile, but they would be new questions built around this paper, not continuous mirrors of its results.
