The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case stops at the anchor requirement: this paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its closest results are Table 1, which compares simulated and observed government-type frequencies, and Table 2, which compares portfolio-allocation regressions. The paper also reports simulated comparative statics for delays and other outcomes. Those are substantive findings, but they are not qualifying anchors. The ergodicity argument in a footnote concerns the simulated Markov process; it is not a numbered complexity result either.

A plausible population regime would be a large legislature whose members fall into relatively few complete types. One could let type \(t\) encode a member’s policy ideal point and relevant party characteristics, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of seats of that type. A government would then involve a coalition of types, a policy, and a portfolio allocation. But the paper’s proposal-makers are parties: each party proposes independently and must be included in its own proposal. Replacing parties with mass types changes who proposes and makes that self-inclusion rule unclear. Treating \(\mu\) as the distribution of party types also requires separately representing each party’s seat share and proposal opportunity.

So there is a conceivable high-multiplicity extension, but I cannot state it as a continuous problem anchored in a named result of this paper, or assign it a tractability or hardness class, without going beyond the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has not supplied a qualifying anchor. Table 1’s government-type frequencies, Table 2’s portfolio regression, and the delay results are substantive findings, but none asserts the complexity of a computational problem. The ergodicity discussion supports the simulation procedure; it is not a complexity result either. Under the requested standard, that leaves no named result whose continuous mirror the case for can defend.

The suggested population of legislators does not fix this. In the model, legislators matter through seat shares and majority support, while parties are the agents that propose governments, evaluate them against the status quo, and can become formateurs. Making many legislators of the same party type into a mass therefore aggregates a quantity the model already represents through seat shares; it does not continuize the proposal-making society or yield a new computational question anchored in the paper’s findings.

The stronger alternative is to continuize parties themselves, with types encoding seat share, ideal point, and relevant institutional role. But the paper’s proposal process is defined over a finite set of parties: each proposer must include itself, coalition membership is sampled over subsets of those parties, and cabinet shares are allocated among them. A continuum of party mass makes an individual proposer’s mass vanish and requires a new rule for proposal intensity, self-inclusion, and selecting an enacted proposal. There may be reasonable ways to supply those rules, but they define a new stochastic formation model. Computing its stationary outcome distribution would be a plausible companion project; it would not be a continuous version of a named computational result in this paper.

The same applies to the other apparent anchors. A continuous calculation of government-type frequencies would recast Table 1; one of portfolio proportionality would recast Table 2; one of delays would recast the simulated delay distribution. Each could be a worthwhile research question on its own. None is a complexity claim the paper makes, and the paper’s computational contribution is to use simulations to study aggregate predictions, not to formulate an algorithmic problem over a society distribution.

So the negative case is strongest as a verdict on this paper’s fit for the programme: its central actors are a small number of distinct parties, and its reported results are simulated outcome patterns rather than computational-complexity results. I would not claim that no plausible continuous party model can be built. The weaker point is that such a model would be a new problem motivated by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its computational results.
