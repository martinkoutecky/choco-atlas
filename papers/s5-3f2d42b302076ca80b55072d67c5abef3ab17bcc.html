The continuous mirror question

Given rational parameters \(\mu,p_1,p_2\) with \(p_1<1/2<p_2\), derive \(a,b\) as in the paper. An atomless unit population has signal types \(L,H\). For each state \(\omega\in\{0,1\}\), an adversary chooses a probability law \(\rho_\omega\) over the mass \(\nu\in[0,1]\) of type \(H\), subject to \(\mathbb{E}_{\rho_0}[\nu]=a\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{\rho_1}[\nu]=b\). The decision maker observes \(\nu\) and chooses a measurable rule \(f:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) to minimize \(\sup_{\rho_0,\rho_1}\bigl(P^*(\rho_0,\rho_1)-\mu\int f\,d\rho_1-(1-\mu)\int(1-f)\,d\rho_0\bigr)\), where \(P^*(\rho_0,\rho_1)\) is the accuracy of a decision maker who knows both laws. Find an optimal rule and the minimum regret.

The model it lives in

The society is an atomless unit mass of agents with complete types \(L,H\), identical signal marginals and interests, and unknown cross-agent correlations. The adversary's state-conditional laws over aggregate type mass have fixed means \(a,b\); the decision variable is the measurable aggregation rule \(f(\nu)\), and the objective is worst-case regret against a Bayesian decision maker who knows those laws.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest defensible case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s large-population regret problem. One qualification up front: the paper has no named complexity result such as a P, NP-hardness, or parameterized-complexity theorem. My anchor is an exact optimization theorem about the best aggregation rule, not a complexity classification.

The anchor is Theorem 4.3 (Main), proved in this paper. It says that if \(1/n\leq a<b\leq (n-1)/n\), then the random dictator rule is the unique regret-minimizing aggregation rule, and the minimum regret is \(1-(1-\mu)(1-a)-\mu b\). Here \(a\) and \(b\) are the probabilities that an agent reports \(H\), conditional on states \(0\) and \(1\), respectively. This is a useful anchor because it gives both the optimizer and its value for the problem of choosing an aggregation rule.

The mirror: Continuous Robust Anonymous Signal Aggregation. Give an instance by rational parameters \(\mu,p_1,p_2\), where \(\mu\) is the prior probability of state \(1\), and \(p_1<1/2<p_2\) are the agents’ posteriors after signals \(L\) and \(H\). Derive \(a,b\) from those parameters as in the paper. The society is an atomless population of total mass one, with two possible signal types, \(L\) and \(H\). In each realized society, the mass reporting \(H\) is some \(\nu\in[0,1]\), so its type distribution is \((1-\nu)\delta_L+\nu\delta_H\).

Conditional on state \(0\), let the adversary choose a probability law \(\rho_0\) over possible masses \(\nu\); conditional on state \(1\), let it choose \(\rho_1\). The feasible laws satisfy \(\mathbb{E}_{\rho_0}[\nu]=a\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{\rho_1}[\nu]=b\). This is the natural atomless counterpart of the paper’s feasible-frequency characterization: randomizing the placement of each type across the continuum can give every individual the same signal marginal while producing any law of aggregate masses with the required mean.

The decision maker sees \(\nu\), knows \(\mu,p_1,p_2\), but does not know the laws \(\rho_0,\rho_1\). Its decision variable is a measurable rule \(f:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), where \(f(\nu)\) is the probability of guessing state \(1\). For fixed feasible laws, its accuracy is
\[ P_f(\rho_0,\rho_1) =\mu\int f(\nu)\,d\rho_1(\nu) +(1-\mu)\int(1-f(\nu))\,d\rho_0(\nu). \]
Let \(P^*(\rho_0,\rho_1)\) be the accuracy of a decision maker that knows the laws and uses the observed \(\nu\) to make the best Bayesian guess. The problem is to find an \(f\) minimizing
\[ \sup_{\rho_0,\rho_1} \bigl(P^*(\rho_0,\rho_1)-P_f(\rho_0,\rho_1)\bigr), \]
and return an optimal rule and its value.

I expect the solution \(f(\nu)=\nu\), the continuum version of random dictator: choose the \(H\) recommendation with probability equal to its population mass. Its regret is \(1-(1-\mu)(1-a)-\mu b\). Indeed, its accuracy is constant across feasible laws; the adversary can make the Bayesian benchmark perfect by choosing \(\rho_0=\delta_a\) and \(\rho_1=\delta_b\). The matching lower bound for any rule follows by restricting it to a sufficiently fine frequency grid and applying Theorem 4.3 there. So the exact value has a strong route from the paper’s theorem, even though the paper itself does not state this continuum model.

The regime is a very large anonymous pool of forecasters or residents reporting on a shared issue, such as residents responding to a standardized public-health signal. Agents share the same signal meanings, posteriors, and interests; overlapping sources of information make their signals correlated in unknown ways. There may be millions of agents but only two signal types, so \(n\gg\tau=2\). The population mass is the continuized object; the decision remains binary. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s symmetric large-\(n\) analysis is evidence that the regime makes sense for this problem.

I expect this narrow mirror to be Class A, tractable: the uncertainty is over one-dimensional mass distributions with fixed means, and the finite theorem supplies the value and a succinct optimal rule. It covers the large-\(n\), symmetric, binary-state regret result, not the paper’s small-\(n\) concavification analysis or its broader extensions. Natural follow-up questions are how the characterization changes with more signal types, heterogeneous agent marginals, or more than two states and actions.

The soft spot is that the mirror is close to an asymptotic version of a problem the paper already solves: its Corollary 4.4 establishes random dictator optimality for all sufficiently large finite populations. Also, its main contribution is robust optimization, not computational complexity. The continuum formulation is a credible population mirror, but a modest case for the broader ChoCo complexity programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest version of the proposed mirror is sound as a population model. A large pool of otherwise identical forecasters can have two complete types, \(L\) and \(H\), and an atomless society can realize any law \(\rho_\omega\) over the mass \(\nu\) of \(H\)-types with the required mean. Randomly shifting a block of mass \(\nu\) around the population gives each individual the same signal marginal. So I cannot honestly defeat this anchor by claiming that multiplicity is implausible, that identities matter, or that the continuum is ill-defined. The paper itself supplies plausible large-pool settings.

The case against is narrower: Theorem 4.3 is a theorem about the robustly optimal rule and its value, not a theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. Its instance data are just \(\mu,p_1,p_2\); the continuous version gives the decision maker a scalar \(\nu\), and the adversary’s laws are constrained by two scalar means. There is no encoded combinatorial instance, pricing problem, or parameter such as a growing support size \(\tau\) whose complexity the paper studies. Asking for the continuous optimizer is well-posed, but it does not turn this result into the kind of complexity-landscape question ChoCo is set up to chart.

The strongest alternative mirror does not change that assessment. Section 6.1 allows richer private signals, but agents still submit binary recommendations and the analysis collapses them to the same frequency \(\nu\). The arbitrary-state extension likewise keeps two recommendations and a one-dimensional mass. To create a more substantial computational problem, one would need to introduce, for example, multiple reported actions or new restrictions on feasible correlations. That could be worthwhile follow-up research, but it would no longer be a mirror of Theorem 4.3.

So the honest negative case is about programme fit, not about whether a continuous mirror exists. The proponent’s model survives the modelling objections; I would argue only that it is a modest continuous reformulation of a robust rule-design result, rather than a strong ChoCo complexity mirror. That does not support the universal claim that no scenario makes a worthwhile mirror.
