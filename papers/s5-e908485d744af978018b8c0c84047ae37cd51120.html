What the mirror covers

The proposed climate-policy model might use the welfare criterion characterized in Theorem 1 and extensions in Theorems 2–5, but the paper establishes no computational result about choosing policies.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive reading is that the paper’s welfare criterion could be useful in a continuous-population climate-policy model: treat each generation as a large cohort of similar people, let mass represent shares within cohorts, and evaluate a policy by the long-run average utility it gives generations. Theorem 1, proved here, gives an exact characterization of that average on streams where it exists. Theorems 2 and 3 characterize extensions to bounded streams; Theorems 4 and 5 characterize fully additive extensions through bounds on long-run averages. These are substantial results about social welfare over infinite streams.

But that is not a valid ChoCo mirror. In the paper, the coordinates are generations over time, and their order matters—especially for the catching-up and fixed-step criteria. A distribution over utility types would discard that chronology. Adding a continuous mass of people within each generation is plausible in a climate-policy scenario, but it adds a population continuum to the paper’s model; it does not turn one of the paper’s results into a computational question about that continuum.

The decisive problem is that the paper contains no named computational result: no theorem or proposition establishes tractability, hardness, or an algorithm for an underlying problem. Its numbered results are axiomatic characterizations and functional representations. For example, Theorem 1 says which axioms characterize the Cesàro ordering; it does not compute an intervention or classify the complexity of doing so. There is therefore no qualifying anchor, and I cannot honestly state one continuous computational problem per anchor. Optimizing a policy trajectory subject to resource constraints under a Cesàro objective could be a new research question, but it would need its own model and complexity results; it is not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive point is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes the Cesàro ordering on \(\ell^{\text{Ces}}\); Theorems 2 and 3 characterize extensions to bounded streams; Theorems 4 and 5 characterize fully additive extensions through inequalities. None gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or even a computational problem with an instance and output. So none supplies an anchor for a continuous computational question.

The strongest repair is the climate-policy scenario: let each generation contain a large, homogeneous cohort, let policy \(p\) induce a utility stream \(u(p)\), and choose a policy maximizing its Cesàro average. That may be a sensible new model, but the paper specifies neither the feasible policies nor how they generate utility streams or how those streams are represented as inputs. Add those choices and the computational problem comes from the new policy model; the paper contributes a welfare criterion for evaluating its outputs. A continuum of people within each generation does not change what the paper’s theorems establish.

There is a further mismatch with ChoCo’s central model. The paper’s equal weighting is over an ordered sequence of generations. Treating generation as type would require preserving infinitely many chronological positions, each with equal weight; a distribution over a finite support of types does not do that. Treating people within each generation as a continuum can preserve the sequence, but leaves the theorems as axiomatic results about that sequence, not computational results about population mass. The authors’ remarks that some extensions may not be constructible sharpen the problem for Theorems 4 and 5: their characterizations need not specify an evaluable rule.

This is a strong case against a *mirror of this paper*, not a proof that no worthwhile computational project could ever use its welfare criteria. A policy-optimization project could be valuable, but it would need its own model and computational results; calling it a continuous mirror would stretch the programme’s scope.
