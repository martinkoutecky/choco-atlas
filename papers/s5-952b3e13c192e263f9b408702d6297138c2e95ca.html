The continuous mirror question

For a fixed scoring rule \(r\) in either anchored class, given candidates \(C\), a target \(c^\star\), and a finite support \(T\) of ordered-partition types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), determine whether there are \(x_{t,\pi}\ge 0\) for each strict refinement \(\pi\) of type \(t\), such that \(\sum_{\pi}x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\) for every \(t\), and \(\sum_{t,\pi}x_{t,\pi}\alpha^{(m)}_{\mathrm{pos}_{\pi}(c^\star)}\ge \sum_{t,\pi}x_{t,\pi}\alpha^{(m)}_{\mathrm{pos}_{\pi}(d)}\) for every \(d\in C\).

The model it lives in

Types are ordered partitions of the candidate set, each carrying rational mass \(\mu_t\). Decision variables \(x_{t,\pi}\) split that mass among the type’s strict refinements; the objective is feasibility of the mass-conservation and co-winner score inequalities, with no cost-minimization objective.

The objection that survived

The pricing method appears to apply broadly across positional scoring rules, so the continuous result may reveal less about the paper’s particular hardness reductions; this limits novelty but not the value of asking the question.

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers the hardness claims of Theorems 16 and 17. The paper’s polynomial results for two-valued rules and the \((2,1,\ldots,1,0)\) rule also fit the same formulation, but are not needed for this grade; other details of the paper’s reductions are not separately mirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one give a bit-complexity analysis and an explicit polynomial-time separation algorithm in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\) for both anchored scoring-rule classes?
  • What additive rounding or approximation guarantee relates a feasible mass assignment to finite electorates with specified type counts and score margins?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s possible-winner problem itself, with only the population made continuous. Consider a large product-ranking or public-consultation setting: millions of respondents sort the same alternatives into ordered tiers—say “strongly support,” “support,” and “oppose”—but do not order alternatives within a tier. If many respondents use the same tier pattern, the number of respondents \(n\) can be far larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct patterns. Each pattern is a genuine voter type: two respondents share a type exactly when the partitioned preference description the problem uses is the same.

The two hardness anchors are:

  • Theorem 16, proved in the paper: in its pure positional-scoring setting, with unweighted voters and an unbounded number of candidates, POSSIBLE-WINNER is NP-complete for every scoring rule whose scoring vectors have at least four distinct values. The paper’s proof combines its own reductions with the differentiating-rule hardness attributed to Dey and Misra [7, Theorem 6].
  • Theorem 17, proved in the paper as a direct corollary of Lemma 14: for a 3-valued scoring rule, POSSIBLE-WINNER is NP-complete if the number of positions assigned score \(0\) or score \(2\) in its size-\(m\) scoring vector is unbounded as \(m\) grows.

I would give Theorem 16 the lead. Both results concern the same underlying question—whether some completion of the given partitioned preferences lets the designated candidate co-win—but cover distinct scoring-rule classes.

Here is a precise continuous version for each anchor. Call it Continuous Partitioned Possible Co-Winner, with the scoring-rule restriction in its name. An instance has a candidate set \(C\) of size \(m\), a fixed scoring rule \(r\), a distinguished candidate \(c^\star\), and a finite support \(T\) of ordered partitions \(t=(A_1\succ\cdots\succ A_q)\) of \(C\). Each type has a positive binary-rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). The input gives the support and its masses; \(\tau=|T|\), rather than the number of all possible partitions, is the number of types in the instance.

For each type \(t\), let \(\mathrm{Ext}(t)\) be the complete rankings that preserve its tier order. A solution assigns mass \(x_{t,\pi}\ge 0\) to each \(\pi\in\mathrm{Ext}(t)\), with \(\sum_{\pi\in\mathrm{Ext}(t)}x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\). Each infinitesimal voter still casts one complete, ordinary ranking; \(x\) says what fraction of a type’s mass uses each refinement. The resulting score is
\[ S_x(c)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{\pi\in\mathrm{Ext}(t)} x_{t,\pi}\,\alpha^{(m)}_{\mathrm{pos}_{\pi}(c)}. \]
The question is whether an assignment exists with \(S_x(c^\star)\ge S_x(d)\) for every \(d\in C\). A feasible assignment is the solution. This is a decision question, as in POSSIBLE-WINNER; there is no cost-minimization objective.

For Continuous Partitioned Possible Co-Winner\(_{\ge4}\), restrict \(r\) to Theorem 16’s class of rules with at least four distinct score values. For Continuous Partitioned Possible Co-Winner\(_{3U}\), restrict \(r\) to Theorem 17’s 3-valued class with unboundedly many positions scored \(0\) or \(2\). These are separate mirror questions for the two anchors. In both, I expect Class A: tractable.

The reason is the shape of the feasibility problem. It is a linear program with one variable for each type and each of its complete refinements, potentially exponentially many variables, and only linear mass-conservation and winner constraints. Its pricing problem appears polynomial: a dual query assigns weights to candidates, and for each ordered partition the best refinement can be found by assigning the available positional scores within each tier to candidates in sorted weight order. That is a polynomial-time optimization over refinements. Thus the usual separation/ellipsoid machinery should give a polynomial algorithm in \(m,\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\), for either scoring-rule restriction. The discrete reductions encode combinatorial choices in indivisible voters’ completions; mass splitting lets the continuous model take convex combinations of those choices.

This is a plausible mirror because it keeps the paper’s central ingredients intact: the same candidates, scoring rule, partitioned preferences, and existential completion question. It does not make outcomes or rankings fractional. Only population mass is continuous. The paper’s polynomial results for 2-valued rules and \((2,1,\ldots,1,0)\) also fit this formulation, though they are not anchors in this case.

The weakest point is that a continuum can split one type’s mass among refinements in proportions that a finite electorate may not realize, especially when a type has few voters. That changes the answer to a finite instance in some cases. The defence is that this is exactly the high-multiplicity relaxation: when many respondents share each of a modest number of types, the mass assignment describes the distribution of their integral rankings, and finite-count restrictions become the approximation issue. A useful next question is to bound how well a feasible continuous assignment can be rounded to an electorate with specified type counts, in terms of \(m\), \(\tau\), and the target’s score margin.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative argument is semantic: POSSIBLE-WINNER asks whether *some* completion of incomplete ballots makes a candidate win. If a tiered ballot records genuine indifference, its refinements may not be choices the voters could make; if it records missing data, the question may concern the unobserved records rather than a population-level intervention. But that objection depends on the story attached to the ballots. In a large ranking or consultation dataset where tiers are coarse reports of underlying preferences, many people can share a tier pattern, and asking whether their latent rankings could make a candidate co-win is coherent. The high-multiplicity interpretation survives.

Theorem 16 has a direct mirror: give each ordered partition a rational mass and ask whether the mass can be distributed over its strict refinements so that \(c^\star\) co-wins. The discrete theorem’s NP-completeness for rules with at least four score values does not undermine that question; a tractable continuous version would be a useful result. In fact, the proposed LP has an evident polynomial pricing oracle: within each tier, assign its available positions to candidates in sorted order by their dual weights. Theorem 17’s three-valued class uses the same model and oracle. Both anchors therefore look like credible continuous questions, not failed mirrors.

A narrower skeptical point is that this may be a generic convexification rather than a deep consequence of the paper’s particular reductions: the LP and pricing routine appear to apply across positional scoring rules. That could make the result less surprising than a new structural dichotomy. But it does not show that the question is unimportant. It would still identify what the population relaxation does to two NP-complete classes, and the programme explicitly treats that boundary as a worthwhile finding.

So the honest negative case is weak. I cannot defend the universal claim that neither anchor has a worthwhile mirror: neither relies on individual identity, neither is already answered by a continuous computational treatment in the supplied material, and both admit a plausible repeated-type population. The strongest reservation is about whether the latent-completion interpretation fits a particular application; it does not rule out the mirror across scenarios.
