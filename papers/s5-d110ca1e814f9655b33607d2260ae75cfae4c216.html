The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for a continuous population model of the paper’s club-good problem. But the paper contains no named computational result of the kind ChoCo requires: no theorem says a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results concern mechanism existence and axiomatic characterization. So I can give a concrete candidate mirror, but I can’t present it as a valid computational anchor.

The closest named result is Theorem 1, proved in the paper’s Appendix 1. It says that in the service setting—where every nonempty group has positive production cost, some group has weak returns to scale, and agents’ valuations lie in \(\mathbb{R}_+\)—there is no direct rule satisfying all four conditions: no deficit, production efficiency, voluntarism, and strategy-proofness. This is a mechanism-design impossibility, not a computational-complexity result.

A continuous analogue I would take seriously is Atomless Club-Mechanism Feasibility. Its instance is an atomless unit-mass community with measurable valuations \(v(z)\in\mathbb{R}_+\), drawn from a finite set of possible types with rational masses. The club good can be provided to any measurable set \(W\), at cost \(c(q)\), where \(q=\lambda(W)\), \(c(0)=0\), \(c(q)>0\) for \(q>0\), and there are \(a,b>0\) with \(a+b\leq1\) and \(c(a+b)<c(a)+c(b)\). A mechanism takes reported valuations and returns a measurable served set \(W\) and integrable net transfers \(\tau(z)\).

The question is whether such a mechanism can satisfy, for every report profile:

  • efficiency, by maximizing \(\int_W v(z)\,d\lambda(z)-c(\lambda(W))\);
  • no deficit, by requiring \(\int \tau(z)\,d\lambda(z)+c(\lambda(W))\leq0\);
  • voluntarism, by giving each agent utility \(v(z)\mathbf{1}_{z\in W}+\tau(z)\geq0\); and
  • dominant-strategy truthfulness against unilateral report changes.

The proposed mirror is plausible because it keeps the paper’s central choices intact: agents have private valuations, the mechanism chooses who receives an excludable good, and production costs depend on the served group. A credible regime would be a very large membership base for a shared utility or broadband service: many households, but relatively few valuation types, with a common setup technology and production cost determined by the share served. Type mass is the fraction of the community, and the action is the measurable set served. Making cost depend on mass is a natural high-multiplicity restriction on the paper’s group cost function.

I would expect the free-rider impossibility to persist in this model, but that is a conjecture to test, not a result inherited automatically from Theorem 1. The key complication is that an individual in an atomless population has zero mass, so their report cannot change aggregate demand or production cost. The continuous mechanism must still give that individual a meaningful assignment and payment response to their own report. A proof would need to handle that incentive structure directly.

The paper’s Theorems 2–4 offer further material for continuous mechanism design: they characterize citizen-shareholder rules, impose no-deficit constraints on their social-dividend schedules, and identify Pareto-undominated schedules. But these are characterizations, not computational results. A continuous study of those rules could be mathematically rich; it would not supply the missing computational anchor or, by itself, fit ChoCo’s complexity programme. Nor can I responsibly assign the candidate existence question to ChoCo’s tractable/hardness trichotomy: that trichotomy concerns computational complexity, while this question concerns feasibility of mechanisms.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is a named result, but it says that four mechanism axioms cannot be satisfied together in the service setting. It does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The proponent’s proposed “Atomless Club-Mechanism Feasibility” question preserves the economic subject, but asks for existence of a truthful, efficient mechanism under axioms. With measurable valuation profiles and transfers, that is a mechanism-theory question; the proposal has no finite input, output, or running-time measure in \(m,\tau,L\). So it does not pass ChoCo’s computational gate.

The proposed broadband scenario is nevertheless sensible as a high-multiplicity regime: many households can share valuation types, and a mass-based production cost is natural. That is not the weakness. The bridge to Theorem 1 is less direct: its service setting allows costs to vary across named groups, while a cost \(c(q)\) restricts them to depend only on the mass served. And in an atomless model, an individual report changes zero mass. A model can give that person a report-dependent assignment and payment, but its incentive constraints then describe a price-taking environment, not the pivotal incentives of the finite-agent mechanism. This is a real modelling question to resolve, not a reason the continuous model is incoherent. Resolving it still yields an existence or characterization problem, not a complexity result.

The best computational reframing would be to compute an efficient served set from the reports. With finitely many valuation types and a cost depending only on served mass, sort types by valuation and optimize over the mass served. If the cost is given as a manageable convex or piecewise-linear function, that calculation is straightforward. If hardness is made to appear by retaining arbitrary coalition costs or encoding a complicated cost schedule, it comes from that input representation; it is not a computational result of the paper, nor a complexity question about its mechanism axioms. Asking an algorithm to construct a mechanism instead would still need a new finite input model and a computational objective.

Theorems 2–4 do not supply another anchor. They characterize strategy-proof, fair, no-deficit, and Pareto-undominated rules. A continuous version would extend those characterizations. In the object setting, the paper already parameterizes rules by social-dividend schedules and gives finite constraints on those schedules; optimizing among them would require adding an objective the paper does not pose.

So no named result survives as a ChoCo computational anchor. I would not claim that no continuous study of this paper could be worthwhile: an atomless club-good mechanism study could be a legitimate contribution to mechanism theory. The honest negative verdict is narrower: that prospect does not make this paper a good continuous computational mirror.
