What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror concerns only Proposition 4.1’s procedure for determining g-criticality ranks; it leaves the paper’s other propositions, power indices, and electoral analysis without a continuous-population question.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-based case for a continuous mirror: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Its propositions establish definitions and mathematical properties of criticality, and its electoral application computes indices for a few finite party games; it does not classify an underlying problem as tractable or hard.

The closest result is Proposition 4.1, proved here. It characterizes g-criticality using minimal winning or blocking coalitions: for a losing coalition \(S\), player \(i\) is g-critical exactly when \(i\) belongs to some minimal winning coalition after removing \(S\), and the rank is the smallest size of such a remainder. For a winning \(S\), the analogous condition uses minimal blocking coalitions after removing \(S^c\). This is a finite combinatorial characterization, not a stated complexity result: the paper gives no input model or running-time bound.

A continuous critical-mass question for large groups of interchangeable players might be worth developing, but it would be a new problem rather than a mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Its propositions characterize notions of criticality and prove their mathematical properties. Section 4 gives a way to compute ranks from minimal winning or blocking coalitions, but it specifies no computational problem, input model, or complexity bound. The electoral section evaluates indices for a few finite party games. The other analyst’s closest candidate, Proposition 4.1, is a characterization, not a complexity result.

The best repair would be to ask for continuous critical masses in a high multiplicity weighted voting game. That is a plausible new problem, but it does not preserve what Proposition 4.1 characterizes. Its g-criticality requires every individual in a group to be essential. In a nonatomic population, removing one individual has no effect, so individual essentiality disappears. One could instead make a positive mass of a type the actor, or define essentiality through removing some positive mass. Either choice changes the unit of agency and the definition of criticality; it asks about pivotal blocs or mass transfers rather than the paper’s groups of players. Keeping parties as the actors avoids that change, but the Italian application has a small set of distinct parties, not a high multiplicity population. Expanding parties into individual legislators allows coalitions to split parties and changes the game being measured.

A broader weighted voting model might still be worth studying. But its computational questions would need to be supplied by the new model; they are not problems this paper formulates or results it reports. So the case against a worthwhile mirror of this paper is strong. The universal claim that no scenario could produce worthwhile related work is weaker: continuous pivotal mass could be a sensible extension, though it would be a new research direction rather than a continuization of a named computational result here.
