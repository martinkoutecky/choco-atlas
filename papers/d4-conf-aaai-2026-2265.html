The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give an anchor-compliant positive case: the paper contains no numbered result classifying a computational problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results are analytical. For example, Proposition 3 identifies the Calculus of Voting pivotality with a PPM, while Theorem 4 says that under weakly vanishing, \(q\)-tie-sensitive pivotality, each pivot point below \(q\) is the limit of left and right equilibria, and—when there are finitely many pivot points—every equilibrium limit is either \(0\) or a pivot point. Neither is a complexity result.

A plausible *follow-up* would represent voters by a mass distribution over preference and cost types, then ask for stable equilibrium thresholds satisfying \(c=p\!\left(N(s_A(c)+s_B(c)),\,|s_A(c)-s_B(c)|/(s_A(c)+s_B(c))\right)\), and classify their limits and winner probabilities as \(N\) grows. But that would be a new computational question based on the paper’s model, not a mirror of one of its named computational results.

The weak point for that follow-up is substantial: the paper’s winner probabilities come from finite-population sampling fluctuations. A deterministic continuum population removes those fluctuations; retaining them requires specifying a stochastic finite-\(N\) model alongside the mass description. So there are no qualifying anchors, and I would not claim an expected complexity class for this proposed problem from the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no named computational results in this paper to anchor a continuous mirror. Its propositions and theorems concern equilibrium existence, limiting thresholds, stability, and winner probabilities. None makes a claim about complexity or algorithms. The proponent identifies this correctly, so there is no paper result for the reader to green as a computational mirror.

The proposed follow-up—compute stable equilibrium thresholds and classify their limits and winner probabilities—does not supply an anchor. The paper already represents voter costs and preferences through a distribution \(\mathcal D\) and its support functions \(s_A,s_B\); its equilibrium condition is the scalar fixed-point equation
\[ c=p\!\left(N(s_A(c)+s_B(c)),\frac{|s_A(c)-s_B(c)|}{s_A(c)+s_B(c)}\right). \]
Turning that analysis into an algorithmic problem would require choosing finite encodings for the support functions and pivotality model, along with a precision and output specification. Those choices define a new computational problem. For ordinary finite descriptions, the unknown remains a single threshold; any hardness would need to come from the chosen representation or an expanded model, rather than from a computational result in this paper.

The stronger obstacle is that the paper’s jury-theorem classification depends on finite-population sampling fluctuations. Its slow, moderate, and fast cases turn on the scale \(\sqrt{N}|c_N-c^*|\). An exact continuum of mass removes the random vote counts whose fluctuations determine those winner probabilities. Retaining them requires keeping a finite sample of \(N\) voters from \(\mathcal D\), which preserves the paper’s model rather than making the realized society a continuous mass distribution.

One could formulate a new problem using repeated cost cohorts with fractional masses, perhaps allowing partial participation by a marginal cohort. That might be worth studying. The paper assumes an atomless positive-cost distribution, though, and does not analyze that cohort model or its algorithmic complexity. It would be a related project, not a continuous mirror of one of the paper’s results. The negative case is therefore strong for rejecting a mirror of this paper; the broader claim that no useful computational project could be built from its ideas is less certain.
