The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. So it has no anchor that meets your formal requirement. The strongest substantive anchor is its unnumbered claim in §2 and the Conclusion: winner determination is NP-complete because it is an instance of weighted set packing. The paper’s problem is to choose package bids that maximize total reported value, subject to each buyer receiving at most one package and each item being allocated at most once. The paper asserts this as an established result; it does not prove it here or give it a numbered result.

The best positive case I can make from that claim is Per-capita Continuous Winner Determination, the lead mirror. Imagine a large market of buyers—say, many branches of public agencies or firms buying combinations of standardized services from one provider. A type is a buyer’s complete valuation for every package, including any other buyer attributes the allocation problem uses. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) supported types. The regime is one where the number of buyers \(N\) is large relative to \(\tau\), and each item’s supply grows with the market: item \(j\) has \(Nq_j\) units, or \(q_j\) units per buyer. This is a co-modification of the paper’s single-unit supply, but it is in keeping with its discussion of multi-unit auctions.

For an instance, give \(m\) items, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), and each type’s rational value \(v_t(S)\) for every package \(S\subseteq M\). The value table is explicit. A solution is a mass \(y_{tS}\) of type \(t\) assigned package \(S\). Every individual still receives either one whole package or none; \(y_{tS}\) records how the continuum of indistinguishable buyers is divided among those choices. The problem is

\[ \max \sum_t\sum_{S\subseteq M} v_t(S)y_{tS} \]

subject to

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq M} y_{tS}\leq \mu_t \quad \forall t, \qquad \sum_t\sum_{S\ni j} y_{tS}\leq q_j \quad \forall j\in M, \qquad y_{tS}\geq 0. \]

This is the paper’s winner-determination question in a proportional-stock market. It is also close to machinery the paper already discusses: its equation (2) is the LP relaxation of winner determination, and equations (2)–(3) connect that allocation problem to dual prices. That makes it plausible that the authors would recognize the mirror as their underlying problem, rather than a different question about ANP or dynamic priorities.

I expect this version to be Class A: it is an LP with \(\tau 2^m\) variables, so rational LP algorithms solve it in time polynomial in the explicit valuation-table size and the bit length of the instance. That qualification matters. It is not a polynomial-in-\(m\) result when valuations are given succinctly; then tractability depends on whether the pricing problem \(\max_S\bigl(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}p_j\bigr)\) can be solved efficiently. The mirror covers winner determination only. It does not continuize the paper’s ANP preference-elicitation procedure or DNP’s time-dependent priorities.

The mirror also raises useful follow-up questions: how large can the gap be between the continuous optimum and the integral allocation; which package or valuation structures make the LP exact, as the paper’s discussion of special constraint matrices suggests; and which compact valuation representations admit efficient pricing?

My weakest point is that this is mathematically the familiar LP relaxation, now given a population interpretation. Also, keeping the paper’s fixed one-unit supply while letting the number of buyers grow would make per-capita supply vanish, so the proportional-stock regime is essential. The paper’s discussion of multi-unit auctions supports that change, but a referee might still see it as a substantial move from its specific one-seller, single-unit formulation. And under the requested anchor rule, the NP-completeness claim remains an unnumbered prose assertion, not a qualifying named result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper supplies no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its only complexity claim is the standard statement that finite-bid winner determination is NP-complete; the article neither proves that result nor contributes an algorithm or complexity classification of its own. Its ANP/DNP sections propose a modeling approach and report experiments, but do not state a computational problem with a complexity result. So under the requested anchor rule, there is no qualifying result to continuize.

The proponent’s winner-determination mirror is the strongest possible counterexample, but it changes the problem’s economic regime. With the paper’s fixed supply of one unit per item, a society normalized to mass one has per-capita supply tending to zero as the number of buyers grows; the limiting allocation and normalized welfare degenerate. Scaling supply with the population avoids that, and the paper does mention multi-unit auctions. But the resulting question is about a replicated, proportional-stock market, not the single-unit winner-determination problem the paper formulates.

That proportional-stock model is well posed, and I would not dismiss it as a continuous mirror in the programme’s broader sense. But with explicit values for all \(2^m\) packages, its mass-allocation LP is essentially the LP relaxation already written in the paper. Calling fractional assignments a distribution over indistinguishable buyers adds a population interpretation; it does not continuize a computational result established by this article. If instead the values are meant to be compactly encoded by the paper’s ANP machinery, the paper never specifies how those pairwise judgments produce package values or an efficient pricing problem, so that version lacks a defined computational model.

That is the strongest paper-specific case against the proposed anchor. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists under any scenario: the proportional-stock version is plausible and fits the programme’s allowance for population-scaled supplies. The negative case is therefore strong on the absence of a qualifying result and weaker on the broader claim that the question is not worth asking.
