The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is a large certification or compensation programme with many claimants but relatively few complete preference-and-evidence types. A type \(t\) records everything relevant to incentives: utility over allocations, feasible evidence, and evidence costs. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a small support \(T\), with \(\tau=|T|\) and population size \(N\gg\tau\). If the total budget grows with the population, the continuous outcome can be a per-person allocation \(x_t\) for each type, subject to \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t x_t\leq B\). That is a plausible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s output-verification example.

The paper’s best substantive hook is Theorem 2, proved here: with at least three agents in an economic environment, every evidence-monotonic social choice function is Nash-implementable. Evidence monotonicity means there is a costless evidence profile for each state such that, if no agent’s preferences over outcome-and-evidence pairs reverse between two states, the target outcomes in those states must agree. The theorem constructs a mechanism; it does not give a complexity classification.

A precise follow-on question would be Uniform High-Multiplicity Evidence Implementation. Its input is a finite set of states and claimant types, their utilities and evidence technologies, a binary-rational distribution \(\mu\), and a target allocation rule \(f\). For each compatible population size \(N\), where \(N\mu_t\) is integral, the agents are \(N\mu_t\) individual claimants of type \(t\). The question is whether there is an \(N_0\) and a mechanism for every compatible \(N\geq N_0\) such that, in every state, every pure Nash equilibrium produces \(f\)’s target allocation and uses only costless evidence. A yes-solution is \(N_0\) and the mechanism family. This covers the paper’s full pure-strategy Nash implementation objective and its zero equilibrium evidence-cost requirement. The paper gives no basis for predicting whether this new decision problem is tractable or hard.

Under the anchor rule in the prompt, though, this is an exploratory question, not a qualifying mirror: the paper has no named result asserting that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity. Theorems 1–4 and the propositions are implementation characterizations or existence results, not computational-complexity results.

The weakest point is the Nash limit. In a literal atomless population, one person’s deviation has zero mass and may not change an aggregate allocation or evidence distribution. Treating a whole type’s mass as a deviator would change the paper’s equilibrium concept. Finite replications preserve individual deviations, but make the continuous distribution an input to a family of finite games rather than the paper’s direct model. So there is a plausible high-multiplicity research lead here, but I would not present it as an anchored ChoCo mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive gap is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 2 is an existence theorem: under evidence monotonicity, an economic environment, and \(n\geq 3\), it constructs a mechanism implementing the target rule. The other named results are also implementation characterizations or conditions. None classifies an algorithmic problem or gives a complexity bound. The proponent acknowledges this, so their Theorem 2 anchor does not qualify as a mirror under the programme’s stated test.

A stronger follow-on might encode a finite environment—states, outcomes, utilities, evidence sets, and a target rule—and ask whether the rule is implementable, or whether implementation holds for every sufficiently large replication of a type distribution \(\mu\). That could be a worthwhile new mechanism-design project. The paper, however, does not define such an input or computational task, and Theorem 2 supplies no complexity question for it. A distribution over replicated agents would add a population description to a new decision problem; it would not expose a computational result already present here.

There is a credible high-multiplicity setting in the paper’s output-verification example: a large compensation programme could have many claimants sharing complete preference and evidence types. The obstacle is that implementation turns on each person’s unilateral deviations and on the outcomes of every Nash equilibrium. With an atomless population and aggregate mechanisms, one claimant’s deviation changes no mass. Theorem 2’s construction relies on individual deviations. Keeping those deviations meaningful requires retaining individual strategic choices; treating a whole type’s mass as the deviator instead changes the equilibrium notion. Scaling the budget and defining per-capita allocations could make the application more natural, but it does not settle this strategic issue.

So I would reject the proposed anchor as a ChoCo mirror. I would not claim that no useful continuous-agent implementation research could ever grow from this paper: the claimant setting gives a plausible adjacent direction. The negative case is strongest on the programme’s gate—there is no computational result here to mirror—and weaker as a universal claim that every possible continuous extension lacks value.
