The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s loss-sharing model. But it does not meet the required anchor standard: the paper contains no named computational result about complexity or algorithms.

The only numbered theorem is Theorem 2.1, proved in the paper. It says that, for a fixed loss \(L\), a sharing rule satisfies Symmetry, Independence of Rank-and-Mean-Preserving Changes, Efficiency, and Monotonicity in Contributions if and only if, for each fixed mean endowment \(\bar\omega\), there is an \(\alpha\) such that
\[ f_i(\xi)=\frac{L}{n}+\alpha\bigl(\omega_i-\mu(\xi)\bigr). \]
That is an axiomatic characterization, not a result that a computational problem is tractable or hard. The paper’s calculations comparing proposals with rules are empirical analyses, not named complexity results. So I would not present Theorem 2.1 as a qualifying computational anchor.

A plausible follow-up question, if a proposed mirror is useful despite that gap, is Mass Loss-Rule Fitting. Imagine a large organization with a deficit to share among many members on standardized pay grades. A type \(t\) is a pay grade with per-member endowment \(w_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of members in that grade. The organization has a per-member loss \(\ell\), and \(\bar w=\sum_t\mu_t w_t\). Given rational \((\mu_t,w_t)\), \(\ell\), and a target proposed burden \(a_t\) for each type, choose \(\alpha\) to minimize
\[ \sum_t \mu_t\bigl(\ell+\alpha(w_t-\bar w)-a_t\bigr)^2 \]
subject to \(0\le \ell+\alpha(w_t-\bar w)\le w_t\) for every type. The output is the best-fitting feasible \(\alpha\) and the resulting per-member burdens. The regime would have many members but relatively few standardized grades, so mass is meaningful and \(\tau\ll n\).

This is recognizable as a population version of the paper’s endowment-based rule and its comparison of observed proposals to rule-based allocations. The natural expectation is tractability: it is a one-variable convex optimization problem over rational data. The scope would be the sharing rule and proposal fit, not the paper’s bargaining protocol or its statistical claims. Further questions could allow several candidate rules, or ask how the fitted rule behaves as the type distribution changes.

The weak point is decisive for this assignment: Mass Loss-Rule Fitting is a new computational task built from the paper’s model and empirical method, not a problem whose computational status the paper establishes. Theorem 2.1 supplies the formula, but it does not supply a complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies the decisive gap: the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 2.1 characterizes a sharing rule by axioms. The paper’s other results describe experimental proposals and their proximity to selected rules; they do not establish the complexity of a computational problem. So there is no qualifying anchor here.

The proposed Mass Loss-Rule Fitting task is a reasonable question about a larger population, but it adds an objective the paper never studies: choose \(\alpha\) to minimize squared error against target burdens. For the proposed model, that task reduces to weighted least squares in one variable, with \(\alpha\) clipped to the interval imposed by the feasibility constraints. It may be useful as an applied fitting exercise, but it does not turn the paper’s characterization or experiment into a computational problem.

A stronger population story—many people in standardized endowment groups sharing a public deficit—does make mass meaningful. That helps the model travel to a high-multiplicity setting; it does not supply the missing computational result. And the experimental contribution is harder to lift: proposers make sequential offers, other named participants accept or veto them, and the group may end with a random proposal. Replacing people with masses removes that interaction; retaining it leaves the paper’s four-person bargaining structure in place.

So the honest negative case is about fit to the programme, not impossibility: one can devise a sensible continuous loss-sharing application, but the computational task then has to be invented around the paper. On the evidence here, that is a weaker ChoCo candidate than a mirror of a computational result the paper actually proves.
