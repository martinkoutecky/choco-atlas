The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is at the programme’s first gate: this paper states no computational problem and proves no complexity or algorithmic result. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 equate two definitions of equilibrium; Propositions 1–4 concern efficiency. Examples 1–4 exhibit profitable manipulation in specified economies. None gives a computational claim to mirror. The general model also treats production functions abstractly, without specifying an input encoding for a complexity question.

The most plausible rescue is to make firms of the same production technology and per-capita endowment a type, then ask whether a positive-mass group can improve its payoff by withholding or destroying endowments and inducing a new equilibrium. That is economically coherent: the paper itself mentions groups of producers withholding output. But it changes the strategic actor. In an atomless population, one firm has measure zero and cannot move market prices—the price effect at the heart of the paper’s unilateral manipulation vanishes. Letting a whole type act together restores that effect by introducing a coordinated bloc, a question about collective manipulation rather than the paper’s individual deviation. Keeping a price-moving individual instead leaves an atom in the model.

Such a bloc question might be worth studying with a carefully specified finite representation of technologies and a clear equilibrium-selection rule. But those choices would define a new computational problem; the paper supplies neither that problem nor a result about its complexity. So I would reject Theorems 1 and 2, the propositions, and the examples as anchors for a ChoCo mirror.

That is a strong case against a mirror *of this paper*. The broader claim that no related continuous computational question is worthwhile is harder to sustain: a formalized model of producer blocs could be interesting.
