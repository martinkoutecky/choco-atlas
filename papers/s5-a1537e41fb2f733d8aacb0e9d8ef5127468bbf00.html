What the mirror covers

Theorem 3 is the closest candidate; Theorems 1–2 and Corollaries 1–3 also characterize stable partitions. None states a computational result with a continuous-population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive side is that the marriage-market model has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large cohort in which many people share a small number of relevant types, each defined by gender and economic-success class. A society could be represented by rational type masses, and a current matching by mass flows \(x_{ij}\) between women’s and men’s types.

The closest named result is Theorem 3, proved in the paper. It says that with equal numbers of women and men and a symmetric supermodular couple-worth function, the only \(\chi\)-stable partitions pair each woman \(w_\ell\) with the man \(m_\ell\) of the same rank. This is a useful structural characterization, but it does not state that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classify its complexity. The other numbered results are also stability characterizations, not computational results. So under your anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying named computational anchor. I would not present Theorem 3 as one.

As a follow-on question, one could give rational masses to finitely many gender–quality types and a current matching \(x_{ij}\), then ask whether that mass matching is stable in the high-multiplicity limit. To preserve the paper’s definition, lift the masses and matching to every sufficiently large integer replication, compute the finite \(\chi\)-payoffs and apply its stability condition; the decision problem is whether all sufficiently large lifts are \(\chi\)-stable. A natural conjecture is a tractable, assortative characterization related to Theorem 3. This is a concrete research extension, but it is not a mirror anchored by a computational result in this paper.

The weak point is substantive too: \(\chi\) uses Shapley values over permutations of named players, and stability compares deviations by finite coalitions. A continuum version needs a principled way to carry both constructions into a mass model. Replacing them with ordinary pairwise blocking would be simpler, but would change the paper’s stability question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 3 and the other numbered results characterize \(\chi\)-stable partitions under particular assumptions; they do not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The proponent correctly concedes this, so there is no qualifying anchor for their mirror to survive.

Their proposed question—whether a mass matching is stable in every sufficiently large integer lift—is a plausible follow-on, but it keeps stability defined through a sequence of finite games. Making the masses the actual population object exposes a deeper problem. In the paper’s team game, a coalition has positive worth only when it consists of exactly two players. A positive-mass coalition in an atomless society is never such a pair, while a two-person deviation has zero mass and is not a deviation in the mass-flow model. The paper’s \(\chi\)-value also depends on Shapley payoffs over permutations of the finite player set; the paper supplies no continuum definition of that value.

The finite lifts show why this matters. With a fixed finite set of types and bounded couple worths, each player’s Shapley payoff is \(O(1/n)\): only singleton predecessor coalitions can give a nonzero marginal contribution. For a matched pair \(\{i,j\}\), the formula gives \(\chi_i = v(\{i,j\})/2 + (\mathrm{Sh}_i-\mathrm{Sh}_j)/2\). Thus the outside-option term that motivates the \(\chi\)-value vanishes as the market grows. One can keep meaningful mass-matching stability by defining infinitesimal bilateral blocking and per-capita couple payoffs, but that replaces the paper’s \(\chi\)-stability question with a different continuous matching model. Making coalition worth aggregate over many couples could also repair the scaling, but it changes the paper’s characteristic function and its Shapley calculation.

So the proposed extension may be worth studying on its own; the paper does not establish a computational result whose continuous mirror it would be. My negative case is strongest on that narrower point. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous matching model the authors might recognize, but the proponent’s construction does not provide one anchored in a computational result of this paper.
