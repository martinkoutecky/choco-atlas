The continuous mirror question

Given a characteristic-zero representable matroid whose elements are partitioned into categories and whose feasible bases are invariant under relabeling elements within each category, let \(\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{T}\) be its feasible integral basis profiles. For identical recipients of total mass \(1\), a rational per-capita supply vector \(q\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{B})\), and nonnegative category values \(w\), compute a probability distribution \(\pi\) over \(\mathcal{B}\) with \(\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\pi_b b=q\) that minimizes \(\max_{\pi_b>0}w\cdot b-\min_{\pi_b>0}w\cdot b\). Each recipient receives an integral basis; \(q\) is the average bundle profile.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population of identical recipients receives integral bases from a type-symmetric matroid. The distribution \(\pi\) assigns mass to basis profiles, its mean is the rational per-capita supply \(q\), and its objective is to minimize the utility spread across profiles in its support.

The objection that survived

Theorem 1.9 does not optimize spread at a prescribed mean supply; that objective is an added computational question, and the proposed category-count model restricts the theorem to symmetric matroids.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.9's weighted equitability result in a symmetric, many-recipient regime. It leaves the exchange theorems, the local-search robustness result, and the separate two-agent EF1 application outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the minimum spread be computed efficiently when basis profiles are represented implicitly by a matroid representation?
  • Under what explicit scaling assumptions does the optimal spread vanish as resource categories or bundle sizes grow?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 1.9, proved in this paper. For a matroid \(M\) representable over a field of characteristic zero, if its ground set can be partitioned into \(k\) bases and \(w:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\), the theorem proves there is an ordering of those bases such that
\[ w(B_1)\ge \cdots \ge w(B_k)\ge w(B_1)-\max_{e\in B_1}w(e). \]
It also proves that if the weights are integers in \(\{0,\ldots,W\}\) and a representation is supplied, such a partition can be found by a randomized algorithm in expected time polynomial in \(|E|\) and \(W\), assuming unit-time field operations. The paper gives no named hardness result to anchor on, so this is the best computational anchor: it connects a many-recipient allocation problem to an algorithm.

The regime I would defend is a large population of identical service centers, each receiving a feasible package of indivisible resources. A center’s type, in the programme’s sense, includes its additive values and its matroid constraint; in this case the population has one type, so \(\tau=1\). The number of centers grows while the number of resource categories stays small. Copies of a category must have the same value and be interchangeable in the matroid constraints. This symmetry is a real restriction, but it is exactly what makes grouping copies into types faithful. Total resource supplies scale with the population, and supplies per center are rational.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Continuous Weighted Equitability for Matroid Bundles. Let \(T\) be a finite set of resource categories, let \(\mathcal B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^{T}\) be the type-count vectors of integral bases of a type-symmetric, characteristic-zero representable matroid, and let \(w_t\ge0\) be the value of one item of category \(t\). A continuum of agents of mass \(1\) shares this valuation and constraint. The input includes a rational per-agent supply vector \(q\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal B)\). Find a probability vector \(\pi\) over \(\mathcal B\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{b\in\mathcal B}\pi_b=1,\qquad \sum_{b\in\mathcal B}\pi_b b=q, \]
that minimizes
\[ \Delta(\pi)= \max_{\pi_b>0} w\cdot b-\min_{\pi_b>0}w\cdot b. \]
Here \(\pi_b\) is the mass of agents assigned an integral bundle with profile \(b\). The average bundle and aggregate supplies may be fractional; every agent still receives an integral basis. The input can be given by the matroid representation, \(T\), \(q\), and \(w\); the desired output is an optimal \(\pi\) and its spread.

This is recognizable as their weighted equitability problem: \(k\) in the theorem is the number of recipients, and the bundles are bases of the same matroid. The theorem’s bound says that a finite partition gives a mass allocation with spread at most \(\max_{e\in B_1}w(e)\), which is at most \(\max_{t\in T}w_t\). When bundles grow and the value contributed by any one item tends to zero, that bound supports exact equitability in the limit. The mass vector over bundles is the continuous-population object; fractional per-agent averages arise from assigning different integral bundles to different mass shares.

I would treat the fixed-tolerance version as a Class A candidate: for a specified utility interval, feasibility is a convex-hull question over basis profiles, and column generation suggests a route through matroid optimization. The obstacle is the pricing problem restricted to bases whose value lies in that interval. The paper’s algorithm is polynomial in \(W\), rather than necessarily in the bit length of the weights, so I would expect a pseudo-polynomial route before claiming a bit-polynomial one. Further questions include the complexity of that pricing problem, whether the optimal spread vanishes under explicit scaling assumptions, and whether the model extends to multiple agent types with different valuations.

My weakest point is that the cleanest regime has identical agents, and allowing mass shares over bundles may remove some of the coupling that makes a finite partition difficult. The mirror also uses type-symmetric matroids, whereas Theorem 1.9 allows arbitrary representable matroids. I would keep the claim narrow: the theorem’s \(k\)-recipient weighted-equitability result has a credible high-multiplicity mirror in symmetric, few-category settings. Its separate two-agent EF1 application is not itself the anchor; two agents alone do not provide the population regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1.9 is a real computational anchor: it gives an algorithm for a partition problem, and \(k\) is the number of recipients. I would not argue that the paper has no algorithmic result, or that many identical recipients are an implausible regime. In fact, this anchor is stronger than the paper’s exchange theorems for a population mirror: identical service centers receiving feasible bundles is a coherent high-multiplicity story.

The best objection is to the proponent’s type-count model. A matroid basis is a set of particular ground elements, and their joint feasibility can depend on their identities. Grouping goods into categories is faithful only if the matroid has enough symmetry that category counts determine feasibility. The proposed \(\mathcal B\) of count vectors assumes that property; the paper’s theorem does not. Enforcing it would leave a narrower family of matroids than the theorem covers.

One can repair that by keeping a sequence of matroids \(M_N\), with the same agent type at every population size, and representing the inventory by exact per-agent proportions \(q_N\). Then a distribution over integral bases with mean \(q_N\) is a sensible continuous allocation, and minimizing the utility spread is a well-posed computational question. But it changes what the theorem contributes: the theorem partitions a fixed inventory and proves a bound for that partition; the proposed optimization chooses a distribution over bundles. Its objective and aggregate-supply constraint are additions, not a limit statement supplied by the paper. That weakens the claim that this is a mirror of Theorem 1.9, though it does not make the new question unworthy.

I cannot make that objection decisive. The authors already connect weighted equitability to fair allocation, and identical recipients with integral bundles remain recognizable under a mass interpretation. The type-symmetry requirement is a genuine restriction, but a coherent restricted instance regime is enough for this programme. So my negative case narrows the proposed mirror and casts doubt on how directly it follows from the theorem; it does not defeat the repaired version. The honest conclusion is that the case against a worthwhile mirror is weak.
