The continuous mirror question

Given a finite group set \(G\), rational masses \(\mu_{g,s}\) on supported types \((g,s)\) with \(s\in[-1,1]\), symmetric nonnegative rational interaction rates \(a^+_{gh},a^-_{gh}\), and a budget \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), choose a measurable reassignment \(y(x)\in[-1,1]\) that changes at most mass \(\kappa\) from its baseline opinion. For each group, require equilibrium expressed opinions to satisfy \((1+d_g)\bar z_g=\bar y_g+\sum_h\mu_h(a^+_{gh}-a^-_{gh})\bar z_h\), where \(d_g=\sum_h\mu_h(a^+_{gh}+a^-_{gh})\); maximize \(\int z_y(x)\,d\mu(x)\).

The model it lives in

A finite-type population with mass \(\mu_{g,s}\) on each group-opinion type, symmetric signed interaction kernels \(a^+_{gh},a^-_{gh}\), and group-level FJ equilibrium. The decision assigns changed mass from each baseline type to new opinions under total budget \(\kappa\); the objective is mean equilibrium expressed opinion, with changed mass sent to the endpoint favored by its group’s marginal influence.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the approximate evaluation result in Theorem IV.11 and the fixed-budget intervention result in Theorem V.2 for cohort-structured populations; it does not cover the paper’s arbitrary sparse-graph solver performance, random-walk interpretation, or experiments on individual network datasets.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the optimum be computed exactly from the finite support and group system, and what is its running time in the support size, number of groups, and input bit length?
  • Which graph families admit a controlled approximation by this mass-scaled group model while preserving the intervention objective?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is OpinionMax. The paper’s named computational results are algorithmic rather than hardness results, so the case is that a high-multiplicity version remains tractable—not that a known discrete hardness dissolves.

Consider a large online community divided into a small number of stable groups, such as issue communities and broad stance categories. Members of a group have the same initial opinion and the same supportive and antagonistic exposure rates to each other group. There may be millions of members but only a fixed number of such types. This is a natural high-multiplicity regime: the intervention target is a fraction of the population, and exposure rates describe group-level interactions.

Here is a precise model. Let \(G\) be the set of interaction groups, and let the finite support consist of types \(t=(g,s)\), where \(g\in G\) and \(s\in[-1,1]\) is the initial opinion. The input gives each type’s rational mass \(\mu_{g,s}\), summing to one, and symmetric nonnegative rational interaction intensities \(a^+_{gh}\) and \(a^-_{gh}\). They describe supportive and antagonistic exposure from group \(g\) to group \(h\), scaled per unit population mass. Write \(\mu_g=\sum_s\mu_{g,s}\), \(w_{gh}=a^+_{gh}-a^-_{gh}\), and \(d_g=\sum_h\mu_h(a^+_{gh}+a^-_{gh})\).

For an opinion assignment \(y\) after intervention, the mean expressed opinion \(\bar z_g\) of group \(g\) satisfies
\((1+d_g)\bar z_g=\bar y_g+\sum_h\mu_h w_{gh}\bar z_h\).
An individual \(x\) in group \(g\) has expressed opinion
\(z_y(x)=(y(x)+\sum_h\mu_h w_{gh}\bar z_h)/(1+d_g)\).
These are the signed Friedkin–Johnsen equilibrium equations at the population scale.

Lead question — Continuous Type-Mass OpinionMax. Given this instance and a budget \(\kappa\in[0,1]\), choose a measurable reassignment \(y(x)\in[-1,1]\) that changes at most mass \(\kappa\), meaning \(\mu(\{x:y(x)\ne s(x)\})\le\kappa\), to maximize the population’s mean expressed opinion \(\int z_y(x)\,d\mu(x)\). A solution is the reassignment itself, or a finite description of how much mass of each baseline type is shifted to each new opinion. Since the objective is linear in the internal opinions, an optimum can be represented by shifting selected mass to the appropriate endpoint, \(+1\) or \(-1\).

The anchor is Theorem V.2, proved in this paper. It says that, for a signed graph, initial opinions in \([-1,1]\), a node-change budget \(k\), and error parameter \(\epsilon\), APPROXOPIN runs in expected \(\tilde O(m)\) time and returns a modified vector \(y\) with \(\|y-s\|_0\le k\) whose overall opinion is within additive \(\epsilon\) of the optimum. The continuous question keeps the same underlying decision—choosing whom to change and how to change their initial opinions—and scales \(k\) to a population fraction \(\kappa\). Its likely classification is Class A: solve the finite group equilibrium system, compute each group’s marginal value, then allocate the allowed mass to the types with the largest gains. It also raises natural extensions with group-specific budgets or intervention costs.

A second, more limited mirror is Continuous Signed-FJ Quantity Evaluation. Given the same finite-type population and interaction rates, compute the equilibrium and the population versions of the paper’s quantities: internal conflict \(\mathcal I=\int(z-s)^2\,d\mu\), polarization \(\mathcal P=\int z^2\,d\mu\), and, for example, positive-edge disagreement and negative-edge agreement
\(\mathcal F=\tfrac12\iint a^+_{gh}(z(x)-z(x'))^2\,d\mu(x)d\mu(x')\) and
\(\mathcal E=\tfrac12\iint a^-_{gh}(z(x)+z(x'))^2\,d\mu(x)d\mu(x')\).
The total disagreement is \(\mathcal D=\mathcal F+\mathcal E\). A solution returns these quantities to specified tolerances.

Its anchor is Theorem IV.11, also proved here. For a signed undirected graph and \(\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac12)\), APPROXQUAN runs in expected \(\tilde O(m)\) time and approximates internal conflict, disagreement, and polarization within relative error \(\epsilon\), while approximating disagreement with friends and agreement with opponents within additive error \(\epsilon\). In the finite-type mirror, the likely classification is again Class A: equilibrium reduces to a finite linear system, and the requested quantities follow from the resulting opinions and type-level moments. Further questions include how efficiently to handle approximate or much richer interaction kernels.

The quantity-evaluation mirror covers the paper’s measures and their computational evaluation; the OpinionMax mirror covers its fixed-budget intervention result. Neither claims to reproduce every arbitrary signed network. The paper itself treats individual nodes and an explicit graph, while this mirror uses a weighted, mass-scaled interaction kernel. That is a meaningful population regime: in a large cohort, a member can have approximately the same exposure rates to each cohort, and the paper explicitly notes that its analysis extends to weighted signed graphs. This remains a computational question about equilibrium quantities and opinion intervention, rather than an analytic question about existence or structure of dynamics alone.

My weakest point is the type compression. A few group-level exposure profiles preserve the FJ equations in this regime, but they cannot represent the topology of a sparse, irregular social graph without losing information. The case therefore rests on the plausibility of large, cohort-structured populations, not on a claim that the mirror covers every network studied in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is that both replace the paper’s graph with a mean-field interaction model. In the paper, an agent’s equilibrium depends on its particular signed neighbors: \(z=(I+L)^{-1}s\). For that problem, a complete type must capture the agent’s relevant interaction pattern, not just its opinion and average exposure to a few groups. In an arbitrary sparse network, those patterns can be unique, so the number of types grows with the number of agents.

The proposed group model avoids that growth by supplying interaction rates between groups. But those rates define a different input object: a signed interaction kernel whose equations close on group averages. They do not, in general, preserve the paper’s local neighbor sums. This is a reasonable mean-field FJ model, but it changes the network structure and its scaling. With per-person interaction strength held finite, it amounts to diffuse, population-wide interactions; keeping the paper’s unweighted edges instead makes total degree grow with population and changes the role of the stubbornness term.

That distinction weakens Theorem V.2 as an anchor. In the paper’s problem, the value of changing a node depends on its network-derived structure centrality. An exact population representation of an irregular network must retain those node-specific values and the information needed to compute them; mass allocation then amounts to carrying the original list of candidates into the limit. In the group model, members share group-level influence values, so OpinionMax becomes a simple allocation of intervention mass by marginal gain. That is coherent, but it is an intervention problem for the homogenized model, not a high-multiplicity version of the network problem proved in Theorem V.2.

The same gap applies to Theorem IV.11. Its algorithm evaluates quantities using the signed graph’s sparse Laplacian structure. Under the proposed finite-group model, evaluation instead reduces to solving a system indexed by groups and calculating moments from its solution. That is a computational question, but the paper’s graph solver and its dependence on the original edge set have disappeared. A richer graphon mirror could preserve more irregular structure, but it would need a specified representation and computational model; the finite-group formulas do not establish that bridge.

This case is strongest against treating either anchor as a mirror of the paper’s stated network problems: an exact account of arbitrary neighborhoods keeps essentially \(n\)-scale information, while a fixed-type account gets tractability by imposing a different, highly mixed interaction regime. It is weakest against the broader claim that the mean-field version is itself worth studying. The paper notes extensions to weighted graphs, and its authors might well recognize a signed-kernel FJ intervention problem as theirs. I cannot honestly rule out that scenario; the negative case is a substantive scope objection, not a universal refutation.
