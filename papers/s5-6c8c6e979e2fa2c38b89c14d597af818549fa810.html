There is still a plausible setting for a *new* computational project: a large recurring organization or market with many agents drawn from a small number \(\tau\) of complete preference types, where type includes how agents rank whole partition outcomes, including externalities. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of agents of type \(t\). One could represent outcomes as coalition arrangements of this population and ask whether a stable arrangement exists or compute one under the paper’s myopic blocking rule. That looks most plausible where many interchangeable people form teams or partnerships. It is an adjacent high-multiplicity question, though, not a continuous mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper; the paper does not specify a succinct computational representation of preferences over continuum-scale partitions.

That is also the weak point: moving from finite agents to population mass would require choices about which coalition arrangements and deviations are meaningful in a continuum, and then an algorithmic question and input encoding absent from the paper. The mirror may be a sensible direction for the authors, but presenting it as a continuization of one of their named computational results would overstate the case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a *paper-anchored* mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes the core using an effective top-partition collection; Theorem 3 gives a uniqueness conclusion under order-preserving preferences; Theorem 5 equates absorbing sets for associated problems. None classifies a computational problem or gives an algorithm, running time, or hardness result. Their continuous analogues could be interesting structural theorems, but ChoCo’s stated target is computational.

Theorem 1 does not supply a hidden optimization problem. Its condition refers to agents’ rankings over the full set of admissible partitions, and the paper gives no succinct representation or method for finding the collection \(V\). In a continuum model, one could define configurations by distributions of coalition compositions and ask whether a corresponding core equals \(V\). That would be a new structural question. Turning it into an algorithmic one requires a finite encoding for preferences and configurations; the paper supplies neither.

Theorem 3 has the same gap. Uniqueness of a stable partition is a structural conclusion, not a procedure for finding that partition. Theorem 5 is a reduction of one finite transition relation to another. Extending it to mass distributions may be mathematically possible, but computing absorbing sets would require representing and exploring a transition system over those distributions. The theorem offers no complexity handle for doing that.

The best proposed setting—large organizations or markets with repeated agent types—does make high multiplicity plausible. It also exposes what a mirror would need to add. A type’s preference here ranks whole partitions, whose meaning changes with population size. To use types across scales, one needs a compact preference functional over aggregate coalition configurations, plus a definition of feasible mass partitions and positive-mass deviations. Those choices may yield a worthwhile new computational model. They are substantial new modeling inputs, however, and the paper’s arbitrary rankings do not identify a natural encoding, objective, or pricing problem from which to derive the algorithmic question.

So the negative case is strong for the claim that this paper already contains a worthwhile continuous computational mirror: none of its three nearest candidate anchors does. It is weaker as a universal claim that no scenario could make the subject worthwhile. A well-specified high-multiplicity model of coalition formation with externalities might be valuable; the honest objection is that it would start a new computational project inspired by this paper, rather than continuize one of its computational results.
