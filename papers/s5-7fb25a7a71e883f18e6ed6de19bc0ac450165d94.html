What the mirror covers

The proposed analogue covers Theorem 1's three-value impossibility; Corollaries 1 and 2 remain axiomatic results, and the paper contains no computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest lead is High-Multiplicity Ownership-Rule Feasibility. There is a plausible continuization here: a very large group of co-owners resolving the sale or transfer of one company, with a small number of valuation and ownership classes. The society is a distribution over types \(t=(v,\beta)\), where \(v\) is a member’s value for the object and \(\beta\) is their scaled ownership share. For rational masses \(\mu_t\), require \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t\beta=1\). In an \(N\)-person approximation, there are \(N\mu_t\) people of type \(t\), each with actual share \(\alpha_i=\beta/N\). Thus the population grows while the number of types \(\tau\) stays small.

The closest named anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for any ownership profile, if every agent has the same preference domain of exactly three values, then no rule can satisfy both strategy-proofness and the ownership lower bound. The theorem is about existence of a rule, not computational complexity; the paper contains no named result establishing a problem as NP-hard, in P, W[1]-hard, or FPT.

A precise high-multiplicity question suggested by that theorem is: given rational masses \(\mu_{v,\beta}\) on \(T\subseteq\{a,b,c\}\times\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), with \(\sum\mu_{v,\beta}=1\) and \(\sum\mu_{v,\beta}\beta=1\), does there exist a family of deterministic rules, one for each sufficiently large compatible \(N\)? Each rule takes reports in \(\{a,b,c\}^N\) and assigns the single indivisible object to exactly one person, with transfers satisfying \(\sum_i m_i\le0\). It must be strategy-proof and, for every person \(i\), satisfy \(v_i x_i+m_i\ge(\beta_i/N)v_i\). A solution is such a family of rules; the paper supplies no welfare objective to optimize. Theorem 1 says no such family exists: every sufficiently large finite approximation already has at least two owners and is ruled out. That is the only anchor I would use.

This is a credible population regime for the authors’ underlying problem: many people with public ownership shares and private values decide who receives one indivisible asset and what compensation they receive. The mirror preserves the indivisible object, private reports, money, and both axioms. Its expected direction is transfer of the impossibility to every finite approximation. That is the nearest analogue to hardness transferring, but it is not computational hardness, so the ChoCo trichotomy does not properly classify it. The scope is Theorem 1’s three-value impossibility, not a computational account of the paper’s other corollaries.

The weakness is decisive for the programme: this is still an axiomatic mechanism-existence question, which ChoCo places outside scope. Also, a direct rule on a nonatomic population would make one person’s report change zero mass, potentially making individual strategy-proofness vacuous. The sequence formulation preserves meaningful individual incentives by retaining finite \(N\), but that makes it a high-multiplicity limit question rather than a theorem about a rule directly on a continuous society. A computational follow-on might ask how to minimize the worst strategy-proofness violation while keeping the ownership lower bound, or vice versa, for these finite-support high-multiplicity instances. The paper does not anchor either problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the wrong kind of anchor for ChoCo: it proves that two axioms cannot be satisfied together. The paper gives no named complexity result, algorithm, or computational optimization problem. Corollaries 1 and 2 have the same axiomatic character. Describing the population by masses does not turn these results into computational ones. Even if one phrases rule existence as a decision problem, that is still an axiom-compatibility question; studying how to synthesize rules computationally would require additional structure and a finite encoding of the rules.

The proposed shareholder scenario is plausible, and the scaled ownership types \((v,\beta)\) are a sensible high-multiplicity description. The difficulty is that \(\mu\) describes the population’s composition, while strategy-proofness constrains a rule over every individual report profile. For finite \(N\), the rule still maps profiles in \(\{a,b,c\}^N\) to allocations that award the single object to one named person. An individual deviation changes a mass by \(1/N\), so the sequence of finite mechanisms preserves the paper’s incentives only by retaining its finite-agent structure. It gives a family of mechanism-existence questions, not a computational problem whose input is a continuous society.

Taking the limit directly creates a deeper mismatch. A single person has zero mass, so their report does not change the population distribution; individual strategy-proofness can become vacuous. And a distribution over types does not identify which individual receives the indivisible object. Treating each type class as a bloc would give deviations aggregate impact, but it changes the agents and their incentives. Each repair either keeps the finite mechanism-design problem or introduces a different model.

The suggested follow-on—minimizing strategy-proofness violations or ownership-bound violations—could become a research problem, but it needs a new error metric, objective, and rule representation. Theorem 1 does not anchor those choices. So the case against is strong on ChoCo’s stated scope: the paper has a credible high-multiplicity setting, but no computational result to continuize, and the natural continuum limit weakens the very individual incentive condition its theorem studies. The absolute claim that no worthwhile extension exists anywhere would be too strong; any computational project here would be a new extension rather than a mirror of this paper’s result.
