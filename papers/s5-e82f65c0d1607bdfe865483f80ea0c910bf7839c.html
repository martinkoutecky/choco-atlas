The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that the paper’s setting has a natural continuous population: a statewide referendum electorate can be represented by a distribution \(\mu\) over voter types. A type could record, across the referendum’s propositions, a voter’s material and ideological interests, preferences, and vote-credit allocation. Its mass would be that type’s share of the electorate. This fits the paper’s distinction between interested and disinterested voters, including its claim that interest can lie on a spectrum.

But the article contains no numbered computational result to anchor a mirror: no Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic result, and no formal computational problem. It develops a philosophical argument about vote-credit systems and impartiality. Turning it into a problem such as computing winners from type masses and credit allocations would add a task the paper does not study; modeling strategic allocation would also require assumptions about voter preferences and behavior that the article does not formalize.

So the population regime is plausible, but I cannot give the required named anchor or an anchored continuous problem for this paper. Its argument is a possible motivation for future computational work, not a computational result with a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no named result to defend: they identify no computational theorem or formal problem in the paper. That is the strongest objection here. The paper’s contribution is a philosophical argument about what vote-credit systems may lose when they reduce disinterested voting. Its central claims concern impartial judgment, social inclusion, and moderation; it presents no complexity result, algorithm, or defined computational task to continuize. It even leaves the relevant behavioral claims open to empirical and experimental examination.

The best candidate mirror would take a statewide referendum electorate and represent it as a distribution over voter types: each type could encode issue-specific interests, preferences, and credit allocations. That is a credible high-multiplicity regime, and the article’s discussion of voter types gives it a natural foothold. But if the allocations are given, computing referendum outcomes is just aggregating their mass. To ask a harder question—say, how strategic voters allocate credits or how a proposed rule changes the balance of interested and disinterested voting—we must supply behavioral assumptions, a model of strategy, and a formal measure of the paper’s social functions. Those additions may support worthwhile research, but they create a new computational project rather than mirror a result in this paper.

A still stronger proposal might optimize vote-credit rules against some measure of lost impartiality or reduced inclusion. The paper does not define such a measure or optimization problem. Its point is that the value of disinterested voting is contested and context-dependent, and that its effects need study. Turning that argument into a computational objective would decide modeling choices the article leaves unresolved; it would not chart the complexity of a problem the article posed.

So the negative case is strong on the paper-specific question: there is no computational result here for ChoCo to mirror, even though the population itself can sensibly be continuized. The broader claim that no adjacent computational study could be worthwhile would go too far. A formal study of referendum outcomes or mechanism design might be valuable; on the evidence in this paper, it would be an extension of its subject, not its continuous mirror.
