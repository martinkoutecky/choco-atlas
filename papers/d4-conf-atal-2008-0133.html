The continuous mirror question

Given a finite rational value set \(T\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), and \(q\in(0,1)\), assume some \(\lambda\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\) satisfies \(\mu(>\lambda)<q<\mu(\ge\lambda)\). For finite populations with empirical value distributions converging to \(\mu\) and numbers of indivisible slots \(m_N\) satisfying \(m_N/N\to q\), what is the greatest common limiting per-buyer rebate achievable by anonymous feasible VCG redistribution rules that preserve the VCG allocation and make each buyer’s rebate depend only on other buyers’ reports?

The model it lives in

Buyers have types \(v\in T\) with mass \(\mu_v\), and the auction supplies \(q\) indivisible slots per buyer in the limit. Finite rules give rebates \(r_i^{(N)}(\theta_{-i})\), remain feasible at every report profile, and preserve truthfulness; maximize the common limiting rebate \(R(\mu)\).

The objection that survived

The limit \(R^*(\mu)\) is justified by aggregate budget feasibility rather than Theorem 1’s pointwise one-agent deviation equality; the case acknowledges this loss but does not connect the two characterizations.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers regular anonymous unit-demand multi-unit auctions with capacity ratio \(q\); it leaves the full profile-dependent characterization, priority-based nonanonymous construction, and combinatorial-auction results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize limiting rebates at cutoff boundaries where \(\mu(>\lambda)=q\) or \(\mu(\ge\lambda)=q\).
  • Establish finite-\(N\) convergence rates and corrections to \(R^*(\mu)=q\lambda\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It characterizes a redistribution mechanism \(r\) as undominated exactly when, for every agent \(i\) and report profile, \(r_i(\theta_{-i})\ge 0\) and
\[ r_i(\theta_{-i})= \min_{\theta_i'\in\Theta_i} \left\{ \mathrm{VCG}(\theta_i',\theta_{-i}) -\sum_{j\ne i}r_j(\theta'_{-j}) \right\}. \]
Here \(\theta'_{-j}\) is the profile of everyone other than \(j\) after \(i\)'s report is replaced by \(\theta_i'\). In words: each agent’s refund is the greatest amount compatible with feasibility against every possible report by that agent, given the refunds to the others. The paper uses this characterization in its construction methods. It is a structural, computationally used result, not a theorem that places a problem in P or proves hardness; the paper contains no named complexity-class result. I would use Theorem 1 as the sole anchor.

A plausible regime is a large auction for identical service slots, with one slot at most per buyer and capacity scaling with the number of buyers. For example, many firms might bid for standardized contracts, with valuations on a common finite tariff grid. A type is a buyer’s value \(v\) for one slot; agents of that type share all the information the mechanism uses. With \(N\) buyers and \(\tau\) value types in the society, \(N\) can be very large while \(\tau\) remains modest. Let \(\mu_v\) be the fraction of buyers with value \(v\). If the auction supplies roughly \(qN\) slots, where \(q\in(0,1)\), the population distribution and the supply ratio are natural aggregate inputs. Agents still receive indivisible slots; a type’s average allocation can be fractional because only some members of the marginal type need receive one.

The continuous question I would hand to a prover is the Asymptotic Anonymous VCG Rebate Problem. An instance consists of a finite rational value set \(T\subseteq\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) containing \(0\), a rational capacity fraction \(q\in(0,1)\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\). Restrict to regular instances for which there is a value \(\lambda\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\) such that
\[ \mu(>\lambda)<q<\mu(\ge\lambda). \]
Thus the marginal value class straddles the capacity: a positive fraction of that class wins and a positive fraction loses. The question is: what is the greatest common per-buyer rebate in the continuum limit of anonymous, feasible VCG redistribution mechanisms? A solution gives the rebate rule on this domain and a sequence of finite-population mechanisms realizing it. Each finite mechanism must base a buyer’s rebate only on the other buyers’ reports, preserve the VCG allocation, and never return more than the VCG revenue.

The expected answer is tractable. In the continuum, the VCG price is the marginal value \(\lambda\), so normalized total VCG revenue is \(q\lambda\). Feasibility bounds any common limiting rebate \(R(\mu)\) by \(q\lambda\): returning \(R(\mu)\) to each of \(N\) buyers costs \(NR(\mu)\), while total VCG revenue approaches \(Nq\lambda\). The bound is attained by the finite Cavallo rule
\[ r_i^{(N)}(\theta_{-i}) =\frac{1}{N}\min_{\hat v_i\in T} \mathrm{VCG}_N(\hat v_i,\theta_{-i}), \]
which the paper identifies as feasible in revenue-monotone settings such as multi-unit auctions with unit demand. Under the strict cutoff condition, changing one buyer’s report does not move the cutoff for all sufficiently large populations, so this rule’s refund converges to \(q\lambda\). The pointwise maximal continuous rule is therefore \(R^*(\mu)=q\lambda\); it is undominated. Computing it requires finding the marginal value in the sorted support and multiplying by \(q\), which takes \(O(\tau)\) rational operations.

This is a close but deliberately limited mirror of Theorem 1. It keeps the paper’s VCG allocation, anonymous redistribution, individual refunds, and no-deficit constraint, and takes the population—not the outcome space—to the high-multiplicity limit. The finite Cavallo sequence also supplies a truthful, feasible implementation of the limiting rebate.

The soft spot is that a continuum distribution forgets which individual made a unilateral report. Theorem 1’s central minimization over one agent’s alternative report is therefore not preserved in full by the limiting question; the limit retains aggregate feasibility and the asymptotic refund, rather than the exact finite-profile characterization. The regularity condition also leaves cutoff-boundary distributions for later work. Natural follow-ups are to characterize those boundaries and their possible limits, quantify convergence rates and finite-\(N\) corrections, and test whether comparable results hold for combinatorial auctions where revenue monotonicity may fail.

So the scope is one meaningful slice of the paper: anonymous redistribution for unit-demand multi-unit auctions. It does not mirror the nonanonymous priority method or the experimental combinatorial-auction results. The paper supplies a named characterization anchor, but no named P/NP-hardness anchor; the tractability claim above is a result of the proposed continuous problem, not a claim made by the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 1 is not a computational-complexity result. It characterizes undominated redistribution rules by a pointwise equality over every report profile. The paper’s later techniques use minimizations to improve mechanisms and test them experimentally, but it gives no complexity classification or running-time guarantee for computing those rules. So the proposed mirror is not continuizing a computational result the paper established; it poses a new mechanism-design problem inspired by a structural characterization.

The unit-demand auction is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, and its proposed limit is largely correct. With \(m_N\) slots and marginal value \(\lambda\), total VCG revenue is \(m_N v_{(m_N+1)}\), which approaches \(Nq\lambda\) when \(m_N/N\to q\). An equal rebate to every buyer cannot exceed total revenue divided by \(N\), and Cavallo’s rule approaches that bound under the stated regularity condition. But this makes the answer \(q\lambda\): find the cutoff in the support and multiply by \(q\). The calculation is a revenue bound plus a known feasible rule, rather than a computational mirror of Theorem 1’s characterization.

The deeper mismatch is that Theorem 1’s equality depends on what happens when one agent changes their report. In an atomless population, that agent has zero mass, so their report does not change the aggregate distribution. If the mirror keeps only \(\mu\), the individual incentive constraint disappears; the remaining common-rebate bound is aggregate budget feasibility. To retain the theorem’s content, a model must track the effect of a \(1/N\) change in the population and the resulting \(O(1)\) changes in payments. Those corrections can depend on the population size, rounding, ties, and the full refund schedule—information the limiting distribution alone discards. Treating a positive-mass group’s deviation as the replacement would preserve an effect, but it would ask about coalition deviations instead of the paper’s individual-report constraint.

One could formulate a richer problem using directional derivatives of welfare or VCG revenue, or study finite-population corrections in combinatorial auctions. That may be worthwhile, but it requires new choices about how the continuum represents an individual’s deviation and how to select among marginal values at ties. It would be a new computational problem built from the paper’s ideas, not a direct continuous version of a named complexity result.

The case against the *specific anchor* is strong; the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror is not. Large markets with standardized buyers are a credible high-multiplicity regime, and a carefully defined derivative-level problem could merit study. I would reject the proposed rebate calculation as a convincing ChoCo mirror, while acknowledging that the paper’s lack of a complexity theorem does not rule out every useful follow-up.
