What the mirror covers

Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, along with the paper’s other propositions, characterize or analyze axioms; none provides a computational result for a continuous mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case for a population mirror is quite natural. Take a large electorate whose voters share a relatively small set \(T\) of exact weak-order rankings. A type is a ranking \(t\in T\), and \(\mu_t\) is its share of the electorate. Define the pairwise mass in favour of \(x\) over \(y\) as
\[ p_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{t:\,x\succ_t y}\mu_t, \]
and let \(CW_\mu=\{x:p_\mu(x,y)\ge p_\mu(y,x)\text{ for every }y\ne x\}\). Top-shifting \(x,y\) becomes a transformation of the distribution over types. This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: a national electorate can have many voters but comparatively few distinct ballots.

The obstacle is the required computational anchor. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that an SDR is Condorcet-consistent exactly when it satisfies top consistency, top symmetry, top monotonicity, and top rationality. Theorem 2, also proved here, gives the corresponding characterization on the Condorcet domain: Condorcet consistency is equivalent to weak top consistency, top symmetry, top monotonicity, top rationality, and Maskin monotonicity. These are axiomatic characterizations. The propositions likewise establish implications and properties of axioms; none asserts a complexity classification, gives an algorithm, or computes with an encoding. The paper has no named computational result of the kind this task requires.

One can pose a precise continuous-population question inspired by the theorems: characterize rules \(F\) mapping rational distributions over weak-order types to nonempty winner sets, using mass versions of those axioms. But that is still an axiomatic question, not a computational mirror of a result in this paper. Computing \(CW_\mu\) from an explicitly listed rational support is an easy separate task, not what the theorems establish.

So the positive case is that the population model itself is plausible and recognizable. The weakest point is decisive for this assignment: there is no qualifying anchor, and the paper’s contribution is axiomatic rather than computational. I would not present a continuous computational problem as a mirror of its results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The national electorate in the proposed case is a credible high-multiplicity regime: ballots can be grouped by weak-order type, and rational masses preserve pairwise comparisons. That removes any objection based on the population model itself. It does not, however, supply the missing computational target. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 characterize classes of social decision rules through axioms. Neither gives an algorithm, complexity bound, optimization problem, or encoded computational instance to carry over to a continuous society.

For Theorem 1, the strongest computational reformulation is to ask whether a candidate is a Condorcet winner in a society given by rational masses over \(\tau\) types. Pairwise masses can be summed and compared in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\). That is a sensible calculation, but it does not mirror the theorem’s characterization. The theorem fixes a rule’s output when a Condorcet winner exists; on profiles with no Condorcet winner, different rules in the characterized class may behave differently. Asking for an output there requires choosing a particular rule the paper does not specify. Asking whether a specified rule satisfies the mass versions of the axioms instead requires an encoding of that rule and a way to handle axioms quantified over whole domains. The paper supplies neither, so the resulting complexity question would depend on new modeling choices rather than on its result.

Theorem 2 has the same limitation on the Condorcet domain. In a mass profile, checking whether that domain condition holds and identifying the winner is still pairwise comparison. Maskin monotonicity and weak top consistency are properties used in the characterization, not computational tasks the theorem solves. Testing them globally for a represented rule again requires a representation and computational model absent from the paper.

One could fix Copeland or another named rule and study its winner determination, or introduce an optimization objective over population changes. Those might be worthwhile ChoCo projects. They would add a computational problem that this paper neither states nor analyzes; they are not stronger versions of its two theorem anchors. So the negative case is strong for the paper’s results, though no universal argument can rule out every valuable follow-on someone might build around them. The proposed mirrors do not survive as computational mirrors of these results.
