The case FOR (proponent)

No result in this paper meets the stated anchor requirement: it has no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity classification or giving an algorithm. The conclusion explicitly leaves computational aspects for future work.

The strongest candidate for a computational mirror is Proposition 2, which says that the consensus ranking under Kendall distance elects the Kemeny winner. Appendix A.1 justifies the equivalence by matching the Kendall-distance objective to the cited definition of Kemeny voting. This is a result of the paper, but it is not a complexity result.

A plausible computational question would be: given candidates \(C\) and a support \(T\subseteq\mathfrak S_m\) of rankings with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute
\[ \arg\min_{\sigma\in\mathfrak S_m}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,d_K(\sigma,t). \]
The output is an optimal ranking, or the set of candidates placed first by some optimal ranking. A type is a complete ranking; its mass is the fraction of the electorate holding it. This fits a large electorate with many repeated ballots and relatively few distinct rankings, and it preserves the Kemeny question rather than simplifying it. Its expected classification is hardness transfers: an ordinary Kemeny election maps directly to masses \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), so its discrete hardness carries over. The paper itself does not establish that hardness. Further questions include how complexity depends on \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length, and whether useful approximation or parameterized algorithms exist.

The serious weakness is that the paper already defines its depth functions on probability distributions over permutations and defines the Fréchet objective using expected distance. The proposed mirror therefore adds a computational input convention and complexity questions, but its continuous-population objective is already present in the paper. Proposition 2 is the best substantive anchor for that research direction, yet it is not a qualifying computational anchor under your rule—and the mirror faces a model-level collision. I would present it as a promising computational follow-up, not as a clean positive case that satisfies the requested anchor test.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–6 identify voting rules with depth or Fréchet-mean objectives; Propositions 7–11 establish axiomatic properties. None gives a complexity classification or algorithm, and the conclusion leaves computational aspects for future work.

Proposition 2 is the strongest candidate, but its continuous formulation is already present in the paper. It defines Fréchet means for a probability distribution \(\Pi\) over rankings and minimizes expected Kendall distance. For a finite-support rational society, that objective is exactly
\[ \arg\min_{\sigma\in\mathfrak S_m}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,d_K(\sigma,t). \]
This is not a new population model or a limit: it is the paper’s existing distributional objective written using support masses. Describing the input by \(m,\tau,L\), then studying algorithms for it, adds a computational lens; it does not create a continuous mirror of Proposition 2. The same issue applies to the other rule-equivalence propositions. Continuizing the axiomatic propositions would instead leave the programme’s computational scope.

A richer type space could add costs, prices, or other voter attributes, but then it would pose a new problem rather than mirror a result in this paper. A standalone complexity study of the distributional Kemeny objective could still be worthwhile. The negative case is strongest on the narrower claim that this paper offers no non-colliding continuous-computational mirror; it cannot establish that every adjacent computational project would lack value.
