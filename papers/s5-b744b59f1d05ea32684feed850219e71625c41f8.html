What the mirror covers

The proposed problem captures the Condorcet-cycle calculation for one society. It leaves out the empirical prevalence across elections, bootstrap and rating-noise analyses, descriptions of winners and losers, and electoral-system comparisons.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous-population mirror of the paper’s core calculation. Let a type be a voter’s complete preference ordering over the \(\kappa\) parties or candidates, using the paper’s conversion from ratings to preferences. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the electorate with type \(t\). Given the supported types and their rational masses, ask whether the strict-majority relation contains a directed cycle; a yes answer returns a cycle of alternatives, and a no answer certifies that none exists. This is a natural high-multiplicity scenario: a national electorate may contain millions of voters while the number of alternatives is modest, so the number of possible types is bounded by \(\kappa!\), independently of electorate size.

The problem is exact and computational, but likely straightforward: compute each pairwise margin from the \(\tau\) type masses, then check the resulting graph for a cycle. That takes polynomial time in \(\tau\), \(\kappa\), and the input bit length. It covers the paper’s Condorcet-cycle classification, not its bootstrap and noise analyses, descriptions of winners and losers, or comparisons of electoral systems. It asks no optimization question because the paper specifies no intervention or decision to optimize.

The obstacle is the required anchor. The supplied paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its reported election counts and empirical conclusions are not such anchors, so I cannot present them as one or restate them as a qualifying theorem. The cycle-detection problem is a credible continuous formulation of the calculation the authors perform, but it does not give this paper a named hardness or algorithmic result to mirror.

That is also the mirror’s weakest point: replacing sampled voter counts with exact population masses captures the population scale, but it leaves the paper’s central empirical contribution—measurement and inference from survey data—outside the model. The positive case is therefore a clean, author-recognizable formulation of the underlying Condorcet calculation, but not a strong case for a computational-complexity mirror of the paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named result about the complexity of a computational problem to mirror. Its main findings are empirical claims drawn from surveyed elections, with bootstrap and rating-noise analyses. The Condorcet calculation is part of the method; the paper does not present it as an algorithmic contribution or make a claim about its complexity.

The proposed mirror is coherent: represent voters by preference types with rational masses and ask whether their majority relation contains a cycle. But that reframes one calculation in the paper as a standalone decision problem. It does not preserve the paper’s central question: how often cycles appear across real elections, and how reliable that finding is under sampling and measurement uncertainty. A single population distribution \(\mu\) has a cycle status; it cannot by itself express prevalence across elections. Recovering that prevalence would require a model of elections or survey uncertainty, shifting the work toward statistical inference rather than computational complexity over a continuous society.

The strongest alternative is a continuous robustness question: how much preference mass must change before a cycle appears? That is a plausible adjacent problem, but the paper’s bootstrap resampling and random rating noise do not define an intervention, a cost for changing preferences, or a distance between societies. Adding those choices could yield a worthwhile new study, but it would no longer mirror a result of this paper. Treating sampling uncertainty directly would keep closer to the paper’s aim, but would likewise make the contribution statistical.

I would not claim that a continuous population is implausible here: large electorates with a modest set of preference types are a sensible regime. The negative case is narrower, and strongest, on the required anchor: the paper supplies no computational-complexity result, and its cycle calculation does not make its empirical contribution a complexity result in disguise. That leaves the case against an adjacent continuous robustness project less conclusive than the case against a mirror of this paper’s actual contribution.
