The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I can defend is Weighted LP-list Agreement\(_\infty\). But the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result, so it supplies no anchor that meets your stated requirement. It does make computational claims: most usefully, it says that learning an LP-list satisfying the maximum number of examples is NP-hard, citing Schmitt and Martignon (2006) and Liu and Truszczynski (2015). That claim is unnumbered and attributed to earlier work, not proved here. I can use it as the closest candidate anchor, but cannot honestly supply a theorem number.

Here is the continuous problem I think the authors would recognize as their underlying task. An instance has attributes \(A=\{X_1,\ldots,X_n\}\), finite domains \(D_i\), and universe \(U=\prod_i D_i\). It also has a finite support \(\mathcal S\) of labeled comparisons \((\alpha,\beta)\), where \(\alpha,\beta\in U\), \(\alpha\ne\beta\), and the label means that \(\alpha\) is preferred to \(\beta\). Each support element has a nonnegative rational mass \(\mu_{\alpha,\beta}\), with the masses summing to \(1\). Return an LP-list \(L\)—an ordered subset of attributes, each assigned a total order on its domain—that maximizes
\[ \sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal S} \mu_{\alpha,\beta}\,\mathbf 1[\alpha\succ_L\beta]. \]
The solution is a maximizing LP-list and its agreement mass. A comparison left undecided by \(L\) does not count as correctly classified.

The regime I have in mind is a large collection of pairwise judgments about items from a finite, attribute-described catalogue: for example, repeated crowd judgments on popular product comparisons. A judgment’s type is the complete labeled comparison \((\alpha,\beta)\); mass is its fraction of all judgments. The number \(N\) of judgments could be much larger than the number \(\tau=|\mathcal S|\) of distinct labeled comparisons, while user identity is irrelevant to the paper’s objective. This is a distribution over training judgments, not a continuization of the LP-list’s outcome space.

The cited NP-hardness is the lead candidate anchor: the paper reports that finding an LP-list that satisfies the maximum number of examples is NP-hard. The continuous problem inherits that hardness: give each of \(q\) discrete examples mass \(1/q\) (or aggregate repeated examples by their multiplicity), and maximizing agreement mass is exactly maximizing the number of satisfied examples. I therefore expect this mirror to be Class B, hardness transfers. The paper’s Algorithm 2 and its runtime analysis offer supporting algorithmic evidence, though they are not qualifying anchors: they compute an optimal LP-list in time exponential in the attribute and domain parameters, while replacing example counts by rational masses gives the weighted version of the same dynamic-programming approach. Natural follow-up questions include how the problem behaves with fixed attribute and domain sizes, and whether useful approximation guarantees hold for the weighted objective.

The mirror is plausible because it keeps the paper’s hypothesis class and objective intact: the list still must maximize agreement over pairwise examples; only the dataset’s counts become proportions. Its weakest point is the population story. The paper speaks of training examples, not people, and real datasets may have nearly as many distinct labeled comparisons as observations. The mirror is most convincing in repeated-judgment regimes where \(N\gg\tau\). It is a strong candidate for continuization, but the missing numbered result means it cannot satisfy the requested anchor standard as written.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a scope objection: this paper learns from a dataset of labeled object pairs; it does not model a society of agents. Its dynamic program maximizes the number of examples it classifies correctly. A distribution over those examples measures the frequency of query-and-label records. In the crowd-judgment scenario, that mass can reflect which pairs were asked about and how often, rather than the share of a population holding a preference type.

To turn it into a population model of actual people, a type would need to include a person’s preferences and the process that selects and records their comparisons. The objective would then be expected prediction accuracy under that process. That is a coherent problem, but it adds a data-generation model the paper does not study. On a strict reading of ChoCo’s population scope, this is the best reason to say the proposed mirror is a new population-learning problem rather than a continuization of the paper’s task.

The anchor requirement also weakens the case for a mirror *of this paper’s results*: its NP-hardness claim is attributed to earlier work, and the paper states its algorithmic analysis without a numbered theorem or lemma. Algorithm 2 is still a genuine computational contribution, though, and the absence of a numbered theorem does not make its optimization problem disappear.

I cannot honestly defeat the proponent’s strongest version. If each crowd respondent contributes one comparison, the pair and response can be a complete task-specific type; mass over those types gives exactly weighted agreement. The paper’s objective is preserved, and the high-multiplicity regime is plausible. Uniform rational masses recover the finite-example objective, while the support size \(\tau\) can be much smaller than the number of judgments. The weighted version may be a worthwhile mirror even if it inherits the same dynamic program. So the negative case rests on treating example frequency as distinct from population mass; the one-judgment-per-respondent scenario makes that distinction hard to sustain. I would reject the mirror as unsupported by the paper’s stated setting, but I would not claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario.
