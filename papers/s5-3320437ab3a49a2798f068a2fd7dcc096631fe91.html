Why no mirror

Neither anchored result has computational character under the required rule: both are existential theorems, and their LP or integer-programming arguments serve only as proofs. The proposed continuum matching problems are plausible high-multiplicity extensions, but they are newly formulated computational questions rather than mirrors of named computational results. Therefore the computational-anchor gate fails.

fails bit a — no named computational result to mirror

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers high-multiplicity mass versions of the transferable-utility and discrete matching questions associated with Theorems 1 and 2, but not Theorem 3, Propositions 1 and 2, or the paper's structural counterexamples.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the balanced high-multiplicity transferable-utility model always admit a stable mass allocation, and can one be computed efficiently?
  • When do positive-mass blocking and finite-denominator clone semantics coincide?
  • For implicitly represented bundle families, is the associated pricing or separation problem tractable?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow. This paper does not contain a qualifying named complexity result: no theorem says that a problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its numbered results are existence and structural theorems. Thus, under the strictest ChoCo anchor rule, it cannot supply a fully compliant case. The best positive argument is conditional on admitting a theorem whose proof computes through an LP.

The strongest candidate is Theorem 1, proved in the paper using LP duality and the balanced-matrix result of Fulkerson et al. It states in full: in a many-to-one matching market with transferable utilities, a stable matching exists whenever the firm-worker hypergraph has no nontrivial odd-length cycle, where such a cycle has an odd number of edges and every edge contains exactly two vertices of the cycle. Its proof constructs the primal LP (2.3)–(2.4), whose variables are agent utilities and whose constraints dominate every singleton or potential blocking coalition, and the dual LP (2.5)–(2.7), whose variables are coalition weights. Balancedness makes the dual integral and forces equality with the maximum aggregate matching value.

A natural mirror is Balanced Continuum TU Matching. There are finitely many worker types \(\mathcal W\) and firm types \(\mathcal F\), with rational masses \(\mu_w\) and \(\nu_f\). A type is complete: it includes preferences, valuations, acceptable bundles, and all other parameters. Each firm type \(f\) has a finite family \(\mathcal B_f\) of integral worker-type bundles \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{\mathcal W}\), with value \(v_f(b)\); each worker type \(w\) has utility \(v_w(f)\) for each acceptable firm type. A feasible solution consists of masses \(x_{f,b}\) of firms of type \(f\) receiving bundle \(b\), worker–firm masses \(q_{w,f}\), unmatched masses, and type-level wages \(p_w\), satisfying \(\sum_bx_{f,b}=\nu_f\), \(q_{w,f}=\sum_b b_wx_{f,b}\), and \(\sum_fq_{w,f}\le\mu_w\). Every bundle used with positive mass must maximize \(v_f(b)-\sum_w p_wb_w\), and every worker type assigned to \(f\) must maximize \(v_w(f)+p_w\), with unmatched workers receiving wage \(0\).

The computational question is: given a balanced type-level firm-worker hypergraph, output such a stable mass matching and wage vector, or certify that none exists outside the balanced regime. This is genuinely population-continuous: individual bundles remain integral, but a fraction of a large interchangeable type can be assigned to each firm type. For rational masses, clearing denominators gives an exact finite clone market, so the high-multiplicity dictionary is explicit.

The plausible expectation is Class A when the bundle families are explicit: the paper’s primal–dual argument points toward a finite LP, complementarity system, or column-generation algorithm. If bundles are implicit, however, the pricing problem over \(\mathcal B_f\) may itself be hard, potentially producing a continuum-specific boundary. The paper does not establish the algorithm, so this is a research question generated by Theorem 1, not a result already proved there.

A second, weaker anchor is Theorem 2, also proved in the paper using Huang’s integer-feasibility program, Che et al.’s stable schedule matching, and balanced-matrix integrality. It states in full that a stable matching exists in a discrete many-to-one matching market whenever its firm-worker hypergraph has no nontrivial odd-length cycles.

Its mirror is Balanced High-Multiplicity Discrete Matching. The same finite type sets and rational masses are given, but there are no wages. Each unit of firm mass receives an integral bundle \(b\), each worker unit is assigned to one firm type or remains unmatched, and a blocking deviation consists of some \(\varepsilon>0\) firm mass moving from a strictly worse bundle to \(b\), together with \(b_w\varepsilon\) worker mass of every type \(w\) whose current firm is weakly worse than \(f\). The task is to output a mass assignment with no such deviation. This directly mirrors the paper’s underlying stable-matching problem while making population mass, rather than named-agent identity, the scalable object.

The natural regime is the paper’s own specialized-labour scenario: millions of workers in recurring skill or certification profiles, and many firms or teams with recurring technology paths, capacities, and bundle preferences. A worker type can mean “AI-systems specialist preferring employers in this order”; a firm type can mean “employer possessing this technology path and valuing these specialist bundles in this order.” Here \(N\gg\tau\) is credible, and the complete-type assumption is not an objection: it is exactly the high-multiplicity premise. Theorem 3’s technology-roadmap condition gives further support, since it identifies a realistic structured market in which the hypergraph is balanced.

The weakest point is serious. The original paper’s stability notion is defined over finite named workers and exact worker sets. Replacing those sets by worker-type bundle vectors is a necessary co-modification, and ordinary atomless blocking can collapse to a support-only condition: an arbitrarily small positive mass may block whenever every required type has positive willing mass. One must therefore choose carefully between a positive-mass blocking definition and an exact finite-denominator clone semantics. The former may change the economics; the latter is closer to high multiplicity but makes compressed computation the central open problem.

So the best positive verdict is: the paper supports a very natural continuous-population matching programme, especially through Theorem 1 and its explicit LP proof. It offers a credible Class A candidate involving balanced type hypergraphs, configuration LPs, pricing, and stable-mass realization. But because its named results are existential rather than computational, this is a strong proposed mirror of the paper’s mathematics, not a fully qualifying ChoCo mirror under the strict computational-anchor rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are existence theorems, not complexity results: neither specifies an input encoding, an output algorithm, a decision problem, or a running-time claim. The LP and integer-feasibility programmes are proof machinery. Asking a continuum model to *compute* a stable matching therefore invents a new computational problem rather than continuizing one stated by the paper. Under ChoCo’s explicit anchor rule, that is fatal.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 1 also changes more than the population. The paper’s hypergraph has named workers and edges consisting of particular worker sets. After aggregation, an edge becomes a worker-count vector, potentially with coefficients larger than one. The \(0\)-\(1\) balanced-matrix argument no longer directly applies. Moreover, the paper’s wage vector \(p(w)\) is attached to individual workers; a single type-level wage \(p_w\) imposes symmetry that the original model does not. Allowing workers of one type to receive different wages requires a distribution over contracts or employer assignments, producing a new continuum market whose existence and integrality theory are not supplied by Theorem 1.

Clearing denominators does not repair the anchoring problem. It shows that a rational mass allocation can sometimes be expanded into a finite clone market, but it does not turn the paper’s existential theorem into a compressed algorithm. With explicitly listed bundles, the resulting task is an ordinary finite LP; with implicitly represented bundles, the pricing problem is an additional modelling and complexity choice absent from the paper. Either version may be worth studying, but its interest comes from the newly imposed computational formulation, not from a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 2 fails for the same primary reason. It is again only a sufficient existence condition. Indeed, Section 5.3 explicitly shows that the condition is not necessary: a hypergraph may contain a nontrivial odd cycle while every market with that hypergraph still has a stable matching. Thus “find a stable mass matching, or certify none outside the balanced regime” is not a faithful computational reading of the theorem. A general existence or search problem would have to be formulated afresh.

The high-multiplicity labour scenario is genuinely plausible, so “no repeated types” is not a defensible objection. But its plausibility does not rescue the anchors. In the discrete model, blocking is defined at individual granularity; in the atomless model, one must choose between positive-mass blocking, which can ignore measure-zero deviations, and exact finite-denominator clone semantics, which is a family of finite models rather than a canonical continuum problem. Both are legitimate research choices, but neither is determined by Theorem 2.

So the strongest negative verdict is narrow but firm: this paper does not itself motivate a qualifying ChoCo mirror. A high-multiplicity continuum matching project could certainly be worthwhile, especially with compressed bundle descriptions and wage distributions, but that would be a new computational programme built around the paper’s proof techniques—not a continuous mirror of any named computational result. If the anchor rule is relaxed to permit such newly invented problems, the negative case becomes weak; under the stated rule, both proposed anchors fail.
