The continuous mirror question

Given a hidden PL population over complete rankings with positive rational weights \(\theta\), normalized by \(\max_i\theta_i=1\) and having a unique best item, a learner adaptively queries sets of \(k\) items and observes the top-ranked item of a freshly sampled member restricted to that set. What is the instance-dependent number of queries needed to identify \(\arg\max_i\theta_i\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\)?

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings \(\pi\) of the \(n\) items, with mass \(\mu_\theta(\pi)=\prod_{r=1}^{n}\frac{\theta_{\pi_r}}{\sum_{q=r}^{n}\theta_{\pi_q}}\). The masses are hidden; each query selects a \(k\)-item set and samples a respondent whose ranking induces the observed winner. The objective is to identify the item with greatest PL weight using as few adaptive queries as possible.

The objection that survived

The learner receives samples from a hidden distribution; with explicit mass input, the best item can be computed directly, so this mirror depends on accepting sampling access to the population.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers exact best-item identification with fixed confidence under winner feedback, through Theorem 3 and Theorem 7; it leaves the approximate, Top-\(m\), fixed-budget, and experimental results outside its claim.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the upper and lower instance-dependent query bounds be matched beyond logarithmic factors when the PL ranking masses remain hidden?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is exact best-item identification from a large population of ranking respondents. Its lead anchor is Theorem 3; Theorem 7 supplies the matching information-theoretic boundary. The paper contains no NP-hardness result, so this case is about sample complexity, not a claim that continuization dissolves computational hardness.

Let an agent’s type be a complete ranking \(\pi=(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_n)\) of the items. For positive rational PL weights \(\theta\), normalized so \(\max_i\theta_i=1\), define the population mass of ranking \(\pi\) by
\[ \mu_\pi=\prod_{r=1}^{n}\frac{\theta_{\pi_r}}{\sum_{q=r}^{n}\theta_{\pi_q}}. \]
These masses are rational and sum to one. Each round, the learner chooses a set \(S\) of \(k\) items and receives the top-ranked item of a fresh agent sampled from this population. Restricting a PL ranking to \(S\) gives exactly the paper’s PL winner feedback on \(S\). With Top-\(m\) feedback, the same construction gives the paper’s ordered list. The item with largest \(\theta_i\) is also the pairwise majority winner: against any \(j\), its population share of pairwise victories is \(\theta_i/(\theta_i+\theta_j)>1/2\).

A credible regime is a large recommendation or product-testing platform with a fixed, moderate catalogue and a much larger stream of customers. Types are complete customer rankings; mass is the fraction of customers with each ranking. The PL population can have all \(n!\) rankings in its support, so for fixed moderate \(n\), the number of customers can greatly exceed the number of types. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, though the support is not small when \(n\) grows.

The lead problem, Continuum-PL-PAC-Winner, mirrors Theorem 3 (“PAC-Wrapper(0, \(\delta\))-PAC sample complexity bound with Winner feedback”), proved in this paper. The instance is a hidden PL population \(\mu_\theta\), a menu size \(k\), and confidence level \(\delta\); the learner can adaptively query \(k\)-item sets and observe one sampled agent’s favourite in each set. The task is to return the unique item \(i^\star=\arg\max_i\theta_i\) with probability at least \(1-\delta\). A solution is an adaptive querying policy with a bound on its number of queries. Theorem 3 gives PAC-Wrapper’s bound
\[ O\!\left(\frac{\Theta_{[k]}}{k}\sum_{i=2}^{n}\max\!\left(1,\frac{1}{\Delta_i^2}\right) \ln\frac{k}{\delta}\,\ln\frac{1}{\Delta_i}\right), \]
where \(\Delta_i=\theta_1-\theta_i\), \(\theta_1=1\), and \(\Theta_{[k]}=\max_{|S|=k}\sum_{i\in S}\theta_i\). This is the strongest anchor because it gives a concrete instance-dependent algorithm for the population-query problem.

The companion problem, Continuum-PL-PAC-Winner Lower Bound, mirrors Theorem 7 (“Sample complexity lower bound: (\(0,\delta\))-PAC or Probably-Correct-Best-Item with Winner feedback”), also proved here. For the same winner-feedback population and any policy that terminates almost surely and identifies the unique best item with probability greater than \(1-\delta\), the task is to establish a lower bound on its expected number of queries. Theorem 7 gives
\[ \Omega\!\left(\max\!\left\{ \sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\theta_i\theta_1}{\Delta_i^2}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}, \frac{n}{k}\ln\frac{1}{\delta} \right\}\right). \]
Its proof uses a change-of-measure lemma of Kaufmann et al. (2016); the paper also cites Chen et al. (2018) for the coverage term. Together, these anchors show that the population-query problem is statistically tractable and that its instance-dependent sample cost is essentially pinned down up to logarithmic factors. They do not establish polynomial running time or a P-versus-NP classification.

The authors should recognize these questions: the item set, adaptive \(k\)-subset queries, PL response law, exact best-item objective, and PAC guarantee are unchanged. Only the source of each random response is described as sampling a member of a large population with fixed ranking-type masses. This mirror covers the paper’s fixed-confidence, exact best-item results with winner feedback; it does not claim to mirror its fixed-budget objective or to classify other PL-learning tasks. Natural extensions ask for the corresponding population formulation with Top-\(m\) responses, fixed budgets, and ranking distributions outside the PL family.

The weakest point is the access model. The population is observed through fresh sampled agents, exactly as the paper observes stochastic PL draws, and the masses are hidden from the learner. ChoCo’s usual mass-input formulation instead gives the support and masses as input; giving those masses here would remove the identification task. Also, the PL ranking population may have \(n!\) types, which is a poor fit for a theory whose advantage often comes from moderate support size \(\tau\). The case survives as a faithful continuous-population interpretation of the paper’s sample-complexity problem, but it is a statistical-query mirror, not evidence that the paper fills ChoCo’s computational-complexity gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is an access-model fork. The population interpretation is mathematically exact: assign each complete ranking \(\pi\) its Plackett–Luce probability \(\mu_\pi\), then sampling a voter and asking for their top choice from a queried subset reproduces the paper’s feedback. The paper’s best item is also the population’s plurality winner, since its first-place share is \(\theta_i/\sum_j\theta_j\). So I would not argue that rankings are an implausible type, or that a large population lacks multiplicity.

The problem is that the faithful mirror needs sampling access to a hidden society, while ChoCo’s central model gives the society as its support and rational masses. With those masses in hand, compute each item’s first-place share as
\[ \sum_{\pi:\,\pi_1=i}\mu_\pi \]
and compare the results. This takes \(O(\tau n)\) mass operations. The PAC task disappears because the learner already knows the distribution it was trying to learn from. That is a change in information access, not a technicality in the limit.

Keeping the PAC task nontrivial requires hiding the masses and letting the learner obtain noisy responses through adaptive queries. Then Theorem 3 is a sample-complexity result for a statistical query process: it bounds how many random observations the learner needs. It does not analyze the cost of computing over an explicit continuous society, nor does it expose a mass optimization problem for the continuous-optimization tools ChoCo is meant to bring into ComSoC. Its exact ranking-population interpretation is compelling, but it remains a sampling-oracle formulation.

Theorem 7 has the same fork. Its change-of-measure lower bound explains how many noisy observations distinguish nearby PL instances. With explicit population masses, there is no such uncertainty about the winner; with a hidden sampling oracle, the lower bound remains a statistical information bound. The Top-\(m\) and fixed-budget results vary the feedback or sampling budget, but do not change this mismatch.

One could instead ask how much mass must move between ranking types to make an item win. That is a plausible continuous social-choice problem, but it adds an intervention, costs, and a new objective absent from the paper. It would be a new project inspired by this model, not a continuous mirror of Theorems 3 or 7.

This is a substantial objection to placing the paper inside ChoCo’s explicit-mass computational programme, but it does not establish the requested universal negative. A large product-testing population with hidden ranking types and fresh sampled respondents is a credible regime, and the proposed population exactly generates the paper’s observations. If ChoCo includes sample complexity under sampling access, at least Theorem 3—and likely Theorem 7—survives as a worthwhile continuous statistical question. The negative case is strongest on the boundary of ChoCo’s present input model; it is weak against that broader reading.
