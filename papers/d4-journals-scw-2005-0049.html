The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a computational follow-up, not a qualifying anchored mirror: the paper contains no named result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. Its numbered results analyze equilibrium existence and platform choices. That leaves zero anchors under your stated rule, so I can’t honestly present a continuous problem as mirroring one of the paper’s computational results.

The closest result is Proposition 5, proved in the paper. It says that any completely interior symmetric equilibrium in the four-party proportional game satisfies
\[ \frac{1}{2}f\!\left(\frac{\alpha_{EL}+\alpha_L}{2}\right)(\alpha_{EL}-\alpha_L) +F\!\left(\frac{\alpha_{EL}+\alpha_L}{2}\right)=0, \qquad \alpha_L=-\frac{1-4F\!\left(\frac{\alpha_{EL}+\alpha_L}{2}\right)}{2f(0)}. \]
A decreasing \(f(x)/F(x)\) is sufficient for the second-order condition. If \(f(0)>1/2\), any symmetric equilibrium is completely or partially interior, and the moderate parties’ platforms are closer to zero than in the two-party case. This is a substantive analytic equilibrium result, not a computational-complexity result.

A plausible computational follow-up would be Four-Party Proportional Equilibrium Search. An instance gives a symmetric rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite set of voter blisspoints in \([-1,1]\), and four ordered party blisspoints with specified quadratic utilities. Voter type \(b\) votes for the closest announced platform, with ties split; if \(n_i\) is party \(i\)’s vote mass, the implemented policy is \(x=\sum_i n_i\alpha_i\). The task is to return a symmetric pure-strategy Nash equilibrium platform tuple, or report that none exists. A solution must satisfy, for each party, that no unilateral platform change in \([-1,1]\) improves its utility. A natural regime is a large electorate with many voters sharing a small number \(\tau\) of blisspoint types, so \(n\gg\tau\). I would leave its complexity classification open: Proposition 5 offers no algorithm or hardness result from which to infer one.

The main weakness is that the paper already makes the electorate continuous: voters’ blisspoints are represented by a distribution \(F\) with continuous density \(f\). A finite-support mass model is a plausible high-multiplicity version of its voting game, but it does not continuize a previously discrete population in this paper. So there is a worthwhile computational question adjacent to the paper, while the required named computational anchor—and hence a fully compliant positive mirror case—is absent.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that there is no computational result here to mirror. Proposition 5 is an analytic equilibrium characterization: it derives first-order conditions, gives a sufficient condition for the second-order condition, and compares the moderate parties’ platforms with the two-party case. It says nothing about the complexity of an algorithmic problem.

More decisively, the paper already models the electorate as a continuous population. Voters’ blisspoints have a distribution \(F\) with continuous density \(f\); vote shares are masses under that distribution, and the implemented policy is weighted by those shares. So Proposition 5 already studies equilibrium in a continuous society. Replacing a finite electorate by masses over blisspoint types would be a valid high-multiplicity model, but it would not create a new population-continuous mirror of this paper.

The proponent’s best candidate—finding or certifying a four-party proportional-equilibrium platform tuple for a rational mass distribution and specified utilities—could be a worthwhile computational question. But it is a new algorithmic problem built on the paper’s already continuous model, not a computational counterpart to a discrete result in the paper. Proposition 5 supplies no complexity claim for that task. Under the programme’s novelty rule, the closest named result is therefore not an anchor that survives.

This is a strong case against a *continuous mirror* of this paper. It does not establish that equilibrium computation on continuous electorates would be unworthy as separate ChoCo work; the negative case is weaker if the programme broadens from mirrors to new computational questions on continuum societies.
