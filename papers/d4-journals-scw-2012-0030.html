The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a candidate \(p\), determine whether \(p\) minimizes \(\delta_\mu(c)\) over \(c\in C\), where \(\delta_\mu(c)\) is the infimum total mass whose ranking is changed so that \(c\) becomes a strict Condorcet winner.

The model it lives in

Represent the society as ranking types \(t\in T\) with masses \(\mu_t\). For each candidate \(c\), let \(a_d=\sum_{t:c\succ_t d}\mu_t\). Choose mass \(y_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) to replace from each type where \(c\) is not ranked first, minimizing \(\sum_t y_t\), subject to \(\sum_{t:d\succ_t c}y_t\ge\max\{0,\tfrac12-a_d\}\) for every \(d\ne c\). The optimum is the infimum replacement mass; compare these values across candidates.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 9’s voter replacement winner problem. It leaves the paper’s Young’s rule and Maximin rationalizability results and Theorem 8’s separation example unmodeled.

Open questions for a prover

  • How do normalized discrete replacement scores relate to the continuous LP values under electorate duplication?
  • What finite-population approximation error arises, especially at pairwise ties?
  • How should ties between continuous replacement scores be interpreted for the limiting winner set?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a direct population-continuous version of the paper’s voter replacement rule. Its anchor is Theorem 9, proved in this paper: given an election \(E=(C,V,O)\) and a candidate \(p\in C\), deciding whether \(p\) wins under the voter replacement rule is NP-hard. That rule gives each candidate the minimum number of voters whose rankings must be changed to make that candidate a Condorcet winner, then elects the candidates with the lowest such score. The proof reduces Vertex Cover to this winner-determination problem. The theorem says NP-hard, not NP-complete.

Continuous Voter Replacement Winner Determination. An instance consists of candidates \(C\), a finite support \(T\) of strict rankings, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a candidate \(p\). Each ranking is a type: voters with that ranking are interchangeable because the paper charges the same unit cost for changing any voter’s preference order. A solution reports \(p\)’s infimal replacement mass and whether it is minimum among the candidates’ scores.

A mass transfer \(x_{t,t'}\geq 0\) moves part of type \(t\)’s population to ranking \(t'\), with total mass conserved. Its cost is the fraction of the population whose ranking changes, \(\sum_{t\ne t'}x_{t,t'}\). For any candidate \(c\), define \(\delta_\mu(c)\) as the infimum of that cost over transfers for which \(c\) is a strict Condorcet winner in the resulting society: for every \(d\ne c\), more than half the mass ranks \(c\) above \(d\). The question is whether \(p\in\arg\min_{c\in C}\delta_\mu(c)\). This is the normalized Hamming replacement operation from Theorem 9, with individual ballots replaced by fractions of ballot types.

This problem has a natural tractability route. Let \(a_d=\sum_{t:c\succ_t d}\mu_t\), and let \(y_t\) be mass changed from type \(t\) to a ranking with \(c\) first. Such a change costs one unit per unit of mass and helps \(c\) against every rival ranked above \(c\) in \(t\). Moving mass to any other destination cannot help more. Thus \(\delta_\mu(c)\) is the value of the linear program
\[ \begin{aligned} \min\quad &\sum_{t:c\text{ is not first in }t} y_t\\ \text{s.t.}\quad &\sum_{t:d\succ_t c} y_t\ \geq\ \max\{0,\tfrac12-a_d\} &&\text{for every }d\ne c,\\ &0\leq y_t\leq\mu_t &&\text{for every }t. \end{aligned} \]
The constraints use weak inequalities because \(\delta_\mu(c)\) is an infimum: strict Condorcet inequalities can make a minimum unattained at a tie boundary. The LP gives the exact infimum; arbitrarily small additional transfers make the tied comparisons strict. Solving one such LP per candidate and comparing the rational values is polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). I therefore expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable. In the discrete theorem, whole-voter choices encode Vertex Cover; allowing fractional mass makes a covering LP possible. That is a reason to investigate whether the hardness dissolves in the continuum, not a claim that the paper’s NP-hardness theorem is wrong.

A plausible regime is a large electorate described by a much shorter list of observed rankings: for example, millions of voters over tens of candidates, with thousands of distinct ballot types. People sharing a ranking are indistinguishable under the paper’s replacement cost, and “replace \(0.4\%\) of this ballot type” is a natural aggregate quantity in that setting. The authors should recognize this as their problem: it preserves the Condorcet consensus, the replacement action, and the Hamming cost, while replacing ballot counts by mass. The mirror covers Theorem 9’s voter replacement winner problem only; it does not try to continuize the paper’s Young or Maximin results.

The weakest point is that fractional replacement changes finite-electorate behavior, especially at exact pairwise ties: the continuous score may be an infimum of strict-majority repairs rather than an attained repair. The mirror also permits splitting a type, which removes the indivisibility central to the Vertex Cover reduction. Those are consequences of making the population continuous, and the question remains recognizably the paper’s distance-to-Condorcet problem. Further questions are whether normalized discrete replacement scores converge to these LP values under electorate duplication, how large the finite-population approximation error is, and how ties between continuous scores affect the limiting winner set.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only serious computational anchor is Theorem 9: deciding whether a candidate wins under the voter replacement rule is NP-hard. The strongest negative line is that its reduction encodes Vertex Cover in the pattern of candidate comparisons, while a continuous population can split each ranking type fractionally. That could make the mirror an easy relaxation with little bearing on the discrete hardness.

But that line does not defeat the mirror. The paper’s replacement cost is uniform across voters, so a ranking is a complete type: voters with the same ranking are interchangeable. A large electorate represented by fractions of its ballot types is a natural regime, and the replacement score has a direct continuous definition. For each candidate \(c\), let \(a_d\) be the initial fraction preferring \(c\) to \(d\), and let \(y_t\) be the mass changed from type \(t\) to a ranking with \(c\) first. The infimum replacement mass is given by the LP
\[ \min \sum_{t:\,c\text{ is not first in }t} y_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{t:\,d\succ_t c} y_t \ge \max\{0,\tfrac12-a_d\} \ \ (d\ne c),\qquad 0\le y_t\le \mu_t. \]
Changing mass to a \(c\)-first ranking is at least as useful as changing it to any other ranking. The weak inequalities give the infimum for strict Condorcet victories; arbitrarily small extra transfers handle a boundary tie. Solving this LP for each candidate and comparing the values is polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

One could object that fractional replacement does not preserve every finite-election winner near a tie. That is a limit and approximation question, not a flaw in the continuous problem: asking how the continuous scores relate to normalized discrete scores is itself a useful bridge. The theorem’s reduction having large support also does not rule out realistic instances with many voters and relatively few ballot types.

So the negative case is weak here. The mirror changes exactly the population axis, retains the paper’s Condorcet consensus and Hamming replacement cost, and produces a well-posed computational question. Its LP may make the answer tractable, but that is precisely a worthwhile outcome for this programme.
