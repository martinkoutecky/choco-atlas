The case FOR (proponent)

I can see a plausible continuous population model here, but I cannot make a compliant positive case anchored in the paper: it contains no named computational result. Its numbered theorems concern game theoretic implementation. For example, Theorem 2 proves that the desirable and MS-desirable correspondences are Nash implementable, and Theorem 5 proves strong Nash implementability of the desirable correspondence. Theorem 4 concerns minimal upper hemi-continuous implementable solutions; Theorem 6 constructs a mechanism. None classifies an underlying problem by complexity or gives an algorithm for computing its answer.

The natural mirror seed would be a population of households grouped by complete valuation functions \(v_t:G\to\mathbb{R}\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\). The planner chooses a network \(g\) maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t v_t(g)-C_g\) and assigns per-capita charges \(p_t\) satisfying \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t=C_g\) and \(p_t\le v_t(g)\). One could then ask whether a mechanism over reported type masses fully implements these outcomes when no positive-mass coalition can make all its members better off by deviating. That is a recognizable continuous extension of the paper’s implementation question, and a large regional population with a modest number of shared valuation types is a plausible regime.

But that extension needs its own formal equilibrium model: in a nonatomic population, a single agent’s unilateral report has zero mass and may not affect the outcome. More decisively, it is not an anchor from this paper, and assigning it a tractability or hardness class would invent a computational result the authors did not establish. The lack of an eligible anchor is the case’s weakest point—and, under your stated criterion, decisive.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about the paper’s contribution, not whether a large population with repeated valuation types is plausible. Such a population is easy to imagine here: households could share complete value functions over a finite set of networks. Theorem 2 and Theorem 5 are the closest anchors, but they establish Nash and strong Nash implementation. None of the numbered results classifies the complexity of a computational problem, gives an algorithm, or studies a pricing problem. Theorem 4’s minimality result is also about properties of solution correspondences, not computing one.

The best upgraded mirror would give a rational mass distribution over valuation types and ask either for a welfare-maximizing network and individually rational cost allocation, or for a mechanism that implements those outcomes against deviations by positive-mass coalitions. Both are intelligible questions. But the first is a new optimization problem: the paper allows arbitrary values for every graph, and does not specify a compact encoding or structural restrictions that would make its computational complexity informative. With values listed for every graph, one can scan the list; with succinct values, the chosen representation determines the problem. The second requires a new equilibrium model. In an anonymous nonatomic mechanism, one person has zero mass and cannot change the outcome, so ordinary Nash deviations lose their role. Positive-mass coalition deviations may be a sensible replacement, but that is a different implementation theory from the one proved in Theorems 2 and 5.

So the proponent’s household scenario clears the high-multiplicity test, but does not supply a paper-anchored computational mirror. A computational study of network choice under a carefully specified valuation language and continuum coalition model could be worthwhile on its own. The negative case is weaker if “mirror” is allowed to include that new project; it is strong under the programme’s stated requirement to chart computational questions resulting from this paper’s results.
