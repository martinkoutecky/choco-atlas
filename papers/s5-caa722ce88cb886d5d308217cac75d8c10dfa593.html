The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous-population mirror here, but the paper gives no numbered computational result that meets your anchor rule. The nearest candidate is Lemma 1, proved in Appendix H: it says constraints (13) make the CEUM formulation respect Assumption 4. In full, when CEUM has multiple optimal reported lists, students choose a maximum-length list of acceptable schools and resolve ties in favor of the schools with the highest true utilities; constraints (13) enforce that choice. This is a formulation-validity fact, not a complexity classification or a theorem giving the full formulation. The paper’s NP-hardness statement for the single-scenario capacity-expansion problem is attributed to Bobbio et al. (2022), and is not a numbered result proved here. I would not present either as a qualifying anchor.

The strongest unanchored candidate I see is Fluid-UM-2STMMP, mirroring the paper’s truthful-behavior capacity-planning problem. Take a large school-choice or residency cohort, with many applicants but a moderate number \(\tau\) of recurring preference-and-priority profiles. A type \(t\) specifies a student’s ranking of schools, including whether they prefer being unassigned, and their priority class at each school. In each of finitely many scenarios \(\omega\), the population is a rational distribution \(\mu^\omega\) over those types, with scenario probability \(\pi_\omega\). Capacities and the population are expressed in per-capita units.

Before the scenario is known, the clearinghouse chooses extra capacities \(e_c\geq 0\), with \(\sum_c e_c\leq b\). After scenario \(\omega\) occurs, it assigns mass \(z^\omega_{t,c}\) of each type to schools or to the unassigned option. Assignments conserve each type’s mass, respect capacities \(q_c+e_c\), and are stable: no positive mass of a type prefers a school to its assignment while that school has a vacancy or admits lower-priority mass. Among stable assignments, choose the one minimizing total reported-rank cost. The objective is to minimize expected per-capita rank cost, including the paper’s penalty for being unassigned. The solution is the capacity vector and the scenario-by-scenario stable mass assignments. This mirrors the paper’s UM regime; it does not claim to cover its strategic CEUM or IEUM regimes.

A natural setting is a district planning for a large annual grade cohort whose applicants fall into recurring preference lists and priority categories. The population might be in the hundreds of thousands while the observed support has only hundreds of profiles. Fractional type-level assignments are averages over individuals, each of whom still receives one school assignment. That is a credible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s central question: where to add capacity before preferences are revealed, to improve the expected student-optimal stable matching.

For this fractional-capacity version, my initial expectation is tractable (Class A) if fluid student-optimal stable matching admits a polynomial-size exact flow or separation formulation. With explicit finite scenarios and type support, that could turn the whole problem into a continuous optimization problem. The main questions are whether that formulation exists, and how its complexity depends on \(m,\tau,|\Omega|\), and the input bit length. Extending it to CEUM is a further question: capacity changes alter reported lists, so the tractable UM formulation may not carry over. The discrete NP-hardness cited in the paper is useful boundary evidence, but does not establish hardness for this fractional mirror.

The soft spot is the type regime. The paper gives every student a strict school priority order, and its experiments generate individual-level preferences and priorities. If those orders are mostly unique, then \(\tau\) approaches the population size and the high-multiplicity benefit disappears. The mirror needs markets with genuinely repeated priority and preference profiles—such as priority categories with exchangeable tie-breaking. That scenario is plausible for some school systems, but it is not the regime demonstrated by this paper’s experiments.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Problem 1 defines the two-stage optimization problem; the numbered Lemma 1 only verifies a tie-breaking constraint in the CEUM formulation. The NP-hardness result for deterministic capacity expansion is attributed to Bobbio et al., and the paper’s own computational claims are experimental comparisons of heuristics. Its continuous random utilities describe uncertainty over individual students, not a continuous population. So Fluid-UM-2STMMP is a proposed new model, not a continuization of a computational result established in this paper.

The proposed fluid model also loses a defining part of the paper’s matching input. Schools have strict rankings of named students, and stability depends on those rankings at the capacity cutoff. A complete student type must therefore include that student’s priority position at every school. In a strict total order, students have distinct positions, so grouping students by preference while omitting priority does not produce interchangeable types; the full type support grows with the population. The paper’s randomly generated school rankings make this especially clear.

The better version—large cohorts sharing priority categories, with lottery tie-breaking and fractional assignments—could be worth studying. But it changes the strict-priority model to a tied-priority or lottery model. A fluid cutoff may then be a sensible way to allocate mass, yet the outcome depends on how ties and lotteries are resolved. Preserving individual lottery priorities instead restores identity-specific types; replacing them with cutoffs defines a different matching problem. The paper discusses matching with ties as a separate extension, which makes that boundary material.

This is a strong case against the proposed mirror of the paper as written: its exact priority structure defeats the moderate-support high-multiplicity model, and there is no theorem-level result to carry over. It is not an airtight case against every related scenario. If the authors accept priority categories and a particular fluid tie-breaking rule, that follow-on model could be worthwhile. The honest negative claim is therefore narrower than “no scenario can work”: this paper does not itself supply a qualifying computational result or an exact high-multiplicity regime for the proposed mirror.
