The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a narrow positive case, but the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor it. Propositions 1–4 are proved here, and establish structural or welfare properties; none says that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity.

The closest support for a mirror is Proposition 2, proved here: for every assignment \(a\), \(F(a)\) is nonempty; \(F(a)=\{a\}\) exactly when \(a\) is stable; and every assignment in \(F(a)\) is stable. Proposition 4, also proved here, says that for every assignment problem the expected rank of MRRE is at most that of RSD, with strict inequality for some problems. The paper’s example gives \(162/24\) for RSD and \(150/24\) for MRRE. These are meaningful results about rank-minimizing improvements and their welfare, but neither is a computational anchor under your criterion.

The strongest candidate mirror is Mass Minimum-Rank Improvement, a continuous version of the inner optimization defining \(F\). Consider a large housing or school-assignment population. A type \(t\) records both a strict ranking of \(K\) object classes and the class initially endowed to that cohort; \(\mu_t\) is its rational population share. Object classes contain interchangeable indivisible units, with class \(j\)’s total supply equal to the mass initially endowed with \(j\). An allocation is a matrix \(x_{tj}\geq 0\): the mass of type \(t\) receiving class \(j\). It must satisfy \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\), preserve every class’s total supply, and assign zero mass to any class that type \(t\) ranks below its endowed class. The question is to find a feasible \(x\) minimizing \(\sum_{t,j}x_{tj}\operatorname{Rank}_t(j)\); any minimizing allocation is a solution.

This is a genuine population continuization: many residents are represented by a small number of preference-and-endowment types, while fractional type-level flows record which fractions of each cohort receive each class. Each person still receives one indivisible unit. The question preserves the paper’s rank objective and its requirement that assignments weakly improve on the initial endowment. Its LP formulation makes this inner problem tractable, so I would expect Class A for this narrow mirror. The scenario is plausible where a large cohort is assigned among many copies of a small number of room or seat classes and a modest number of shared preference-and-endowment profiles.

The mirror covers the rank-minimizing correspondence \(F\), not the whole MRRE lottery. A fuller question would need to aggregate these solutions over random endowments and account for ties among minimizers. That raises the main weakness: the paper chooses an endowment uniformly from individual permutations and splits probability uniformly among \(F(a)\). There is no automatic uniform distribution over a continuous space of mass allocations or over a continuum of minimizers. A specified limiting law could make the extension precise, but its existence and whether Proposition 4’s expected-rank comparison survives would need to be established.

So the positive case is that the paper contains a natural inner optimization that has a sensible, tractable mass version. The weakest point is also decisive for the requested anchoring: no named computational result in the paper supports that complexity claim, and the LP mirror alone does not continuize MRRE’s defining lottery.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–4 establish efficiency and welfare properties of assignment rules; none classifies an algorithmic problem or asks about its complexity. Proposition 2’s definition of \(F(a)\) does involve minimizing rank, but the paper presents that minimization as part of a lottery construction, not as a computational problem. Proposition 4 compares expected ranks; it does not claim that computing either lottery or that comparison is hard. So these propositions are weak anchors for a programme about the complexity landscape.

The proposed mass version of \(F\) is still coherent. With types recording preferences and initial endowments, and supplies scaling with population, one can ask for a minimum-rank allocation of mass that respects supplies and weakly improves each type’s endowment. That is a sensible high-multiplicity assignment question. But it isolates one step of MRRE and changes its output from a correspondence of named assignments to a fractional type-level allocation. A fuller mirror would have to preserve the paper’s lottery: a uniform draw over individual endowments, followed by a uniform draw among minimum-rank assignments. At population scale, the latter can have many minimizers, and the paper gives no continuum tie-breaking rule. Taking a limit of the finite lotteries could supply one, but then the question becomes an asymptotic welfare analysis of that added stochastic model, not a complexity result in the paper.

The same issue applies to Proposition 4. One could ask whether its expected-rank comparison persists in a large-population model, but that would be a new question about limiting lottery distributions. It would not turn the proposition itself into a computational claim. The paper’s \(162/24\) versus \(150/24\) example illustrates the welfare comparison, not an algorithmic obstacle.

This is not an airtight case that no useful continuous mirror exists. The cohort assignment setting makes the mass optimization plausible, and the absence of a complexity theorem in the paper does not make that neighboring question meaningless. It does, however, leave the negative case strongest on scope: the proposed optimization is a new computational question suggested by the paper, while the paper’s actual results concern lottery properties.
