The continuous mirror question

Given a finite \(\mathrm{MCSP}(\Gamma_X^{\mathrm{cons}})\) instance with variables \(V\), sort assignment \(\delta\), and constraints \(\mathcal{C}\), take a continuum of identical modules indexed by \(u\in[0,1]\), with one role type \(v\) of mass \(\mu_v=1/|V|\) for each \(v\in V\). Does there exist a measurable assignment \(a_v(u)\in A_{\delta(v)}\) such that, for every constraint \(((v_1,\ldots,v_k),R)\in\mathcal{C}\), \((a_{v_1}(u),\ldots,a_{v_k}(u))\in R\) for almost every \(u\)?

The model it lives in

A continuum of copies of one finite MCSP module. Each type \(v\) records its sort, domain, and role in the constraint template and has mass \(\mu_v=1/|V|\). Each unit of mass receives a domain value; the objective is feasibility of every template constraint almost everywhere.

The objection that survived

The population units are CSP role slots rather than voters submitting complete patterns in \(X\), which weakens the tie to the paper’s aggregation model; the anchor itself, however, is the paper’s MCSP.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 10’s tractability and NP-completeness dichotomy for \(\mathrm{MCSP}(\Gamma_X^{\mathrm{cons}})\). It does not mirror the algebraic characterizations of aggregators or the meta-problem observations.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the dichotomy persist for a finite distribution over heterogeneous module types when feasibility is required only in aggregate across modules, rather than separately within each repeated module?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case I would make is narrow: Continuum-Copy Conservative MCSP, anchored on the paper’s Theorem 10. The paper proves there that, for fixed \(X\), the multi-sorted conservative CSP language \(\Gamma_X^{\rm cons}\)—containing \(X\) and every unary subset of each sort’s domain—has a tractable MCSP if \(X\) is a uniformly possibility domain, and an NP-complete MCSP otherwise. “Uniformly possible” means that some supportive aggregator of some arity preserves \(X\), and on every issue and every two-element subset of its values, its operation is not a projection. The paper proves Theorem 10 from its own aggregator characterization and Bulatov’s cited conservative-CSP dichotomy.

Here is the mirror. Take a finite MCSP instance with variable set \(V\), sorts, and constraints. Imagine a continuum of identical modules indexed by \(u\in[0,1]\), each containing one copy of every variable role \(v\in V\) and the same constraint template. A type is a role \(v\), including its sort, allowed values, and all its roles in the constraint template. Give each type mass \(\mu_v=1/|V|\). The population is then the continuum of decision slots \((u,v)\); its size is continuous while its type support is \(\tau=|V|\).

Call the problem Continuum-Copy Conservative MCSP: does there exist a measurable assignment \(a_v(u)\in A_{\delta(v)}\) such that, for every template constraint \(R(v_1,\ldots,v_k)\), the tuple \((a_{v_1}(u),\ldots,a_{v_k}(u))\) belongs to \(R\) for almost every \(u\)? Each unit receives an ordinary domain value; fractions such as \(x_{v,a}=\lambda\{u:a_v(u)=a\}\) describe the type-level averages. The objective is feasibility.

This is an exact mirror of the underlying CSP question. There are finitely many constraints, so if a measurable solution exists, there is some \(u\) at which all constraints hold, giving a solution to the finite instance. Conversely, any finite solution can be repeated across every module. Thus Theorem 10’s split carries over exactly: tractable when \(X\) is uniformly possible, NP-complete otherwise. The continuous population does not erase hardness here; it repeats the same finite module throughout the population. The hardness remains in the number of distinct roles and the constraint template, not in the mass scale.

The regime is plausible for repeated multi-issue policy or compatibility modules: many interchangeable copies use the same domains and rules, with comparatively few distinct roles. The paper’s authors should recognize this as a continuous-population version of their MCSP, since it retains their relation language, sorts, and feasibility question. I would carry Theorem 10 alone; the other numbered results are chiefly algebraic characterizations, not comparable named complexity anchors.

The weak point is also the reason this mirror is exact: it is a population of repeated modules, not a fractional relaxation of an arbitrary CSP. It says little about whether hardness dissolves when one allows mass to move among types or asks for an aggregate assignment across heterogeneous modules. Those are the natural next questions: does the same dichotomy persist when modules vary within a finite type set, and can hardness survive when the type support stays small as population mass grows?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 10 is the one serious anchor. It really is a named computational result: for fixed \(X\), it classifies the complexity of the multi-sorted CSP built from \(X\) and unary restrictions. I would not dismiss it on the ground that it is “already high multiplicity.” The issue is what the theorem’s computational objects are. Its variables are slots in a constraint instance, each assigned one value; they are not voters, who in the paper’s aggregation model submit complete feasible patterns in \(X\). The aggregator results characterize the CSP’s algebraic structure, but the CSP itself does not aggregate a society’s votes.

The proposed continuum-copy construction preserves that distinction. A module is the entire finite CSP instance, including its constraint pattern. At every \(u\), the assignment must satisfy that same finite instance. Since there are finitely many constraints, an assignment satisfying each one almost everywhere satisfies all of them at some common \(u\). Conversely, one feasible finite assignment can be repeated for every \(u\). Thus the construction adds a continuum of copies around the instance; it does not turn its variables into a population whose mass distribution is the computational input. The masses \(\mu_v=1/|V|\) normalize the roles, but do no work in the decision problem. All the structure that matters remains inside one module.

That makes the repeated-module scenario the best version of this mirror, and also its main limitation. A population of identical compatibility or policy modules is conceivable, but if each module must be feasible independently, its size and constraint structure—not the population mass—carry the problem. This is a reasonable interpretation of batch repetition, but a weak case for spending time on a continuous-population mirror of the theorem.

Making mass do real work requires changing the question. If each variable-role gets a distribution over values and constraints require only locally compatible marginals, the model becomes a fractional CSP relaxation. For example, an odd cycle can have consistent two-color marginals on every edge even though it has no global proper two-coloring. Requiring one global distribution over feasible assignments restores the original feasibility question, but then the mass averages add no new decision content. Adding aggregate resource or outcome requirements might make a continuous model useful, but those requirements are not in Theorem 10.

So I would not claim that no plausible continuous CSP study exists. The negative case is weaker than that: the one computational anchor has no clear population axis to continuize. The exact mirror treats whole instances as repeated units; a more substantive mass-based version changes MCSP into a different problem. That leaves this paper without a strong continuous mirror of its own computational result.
