The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), baseline seed-membership masses, campaign-specific rational contact intensities \(\lambda^a_{st}\), and per-capita budget \(\kappa\), choose additional seed masses \(x_{t,10},x_{t,01},x_{t,11}\) subject to the available-mass constraints and \(\sum_t(x_{t,10}+x_{t,01}+2x_{t,11})\leq\kappa\). Return an allocation whose limiting balanced mass \(B(x)=\sum_t(z_{t,00}+z_{t,11})\) is at least \(\mathrm{OPT}-\epsilon\), where \(z\) is induced by the independent mean-field cascade with \(q^r_{t,j}=1-\exp(-\sum_s\lambda^j_{st}a^r_{s,j})\).

The model it lives in

A population distribution \(\mu\) over contact-role types, refined by baseline seed status, with campaign-specific type-to-type mean-field intensities \(\lambda^a_{st}\). Additional seed-mass variables \(x_{t,10},x_{t,01},x_{t,11}\) obey a per-capita budget, and the objective is the final mass in exposure states \(00\) or \(11\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror I see is BALANCE with a high-multiplicity population of network roles. Take a platform cohort large enough that users can be grouped into a relatively small set \(T\) of types: users of a type have the same campaign-specific contact behavior and are interchangeable for the model. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A plausible setting is a large platform deciding how to seed two opposing articles among user groups with similar community position and retweet behavior.

Here is a concrete mean-field version of the paper’s heterogeneous propagation model. For each ordered pair of types \(s,t\) and campaign \(a\in\{1,2\}\), the input gives a nonnegative rational contact intensity \(\lambda^a_{st}\). It represents the limit of independent-cascade contact probabilities \(\lambda^a_{st}/N\) in a population of \(N\) agents. The input also gives rational initial seed masses for the two campaigns and a per-capita budget \(\kappa\). The decision variables \(x_{t,10},x_{t,01},x_{t,11}\) specify the extra mass of type \(t\) seeded for campaign 1 only, campaign 2 only, or both. They must fit within the type’s initially unseeded mass, and
\[ \sum_t (x_{t,10}+x_{t,01}+2x_{t,11})\leq \kappa, \]
so seeding someone for both campaigns costs twice.

The limiting cascade is specified by type masses in exposure states \(00,10,01,11\). If \(a^r_{s,j}\) is the mass newly reached by campaign \(j\) in type \(s\) during round \(r\), an as-yet-unreached type-\(t\) user receives campaign \(j\) in that round with probability
\[ q^r_{t,j}=1-\exp\!\left(-\sum_s\lambda^j_{st}a^r_{s,j}\right). \]
In the heterogeneous version, the two campaign contact events are independent. These transitions determine the next-round exposure masses and, at the limit, the balanced fraction
\[ B(x)=\sum_t\bigl(z_{t,00}+z_{t,11}\bigr), \]
where \(z_{t,b}\) is the final mass of type \(t\) in exposure state \(b\). This preserves the paper’s objective: users exposed to both campaigns or neither count as balanced.

My lead question, BALANCE-H∞-ZERO, is: given these rational type masses, intensities, initial seed masses, and \(\kappa\), do real seed-mass allocations \(x\) satisfying the constraints exist for which
\[ \sum_t(z_{t,10}+z_{t,01})=0? \]
A solution is such an allocation together with its induced limiting exposure masses.

The anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It states that, for a given graph, initial seed sets, and budget, deciding whether additional seed sets of total size at most the budget can make the expected symmetric difference between the two reached sets zero is NP-hard. The proposition applies to both heterogeneous and correlated propagation, even when \(p_1=p_2=1\). That is the paper’s exact-balance hardness result, not just hardness of a related influence-maximization problem. The continuous question keeps its two campaigns, central seed allocation, budget, and zero-imbalance condition.

I would initially expect the continuous zero question to be Class B: the set-cover reduction’s combinatorics sit in the network’s incidence structure, which can be represented among types, rather than in the number of interchangeable people. But that is a conjecture, not a hardness transfer. Fractional seed mass may erase the integral choices the reduction depends on; whether the continuous problem remains hard is exactly what needs proving.

There is also a worthwhile optimization companion, BALANCE-H∞-MAX: on the same input, return a feasible seed-mass allocation with \(B(x)\geq \mathrm{OPT}-\epsilon\), for rational \(\epsilon>0\). Its anchor is Proposition 3, also proved here. It states that the paper’s greedy algorithm for maximizing \(\Omega\), compared with choosing no extra seeds, guarantees
\[ \max\{\Phi(S_1,S_2),\Phi(\varnothing,\varnothing)\} \geq \frac{1-1/e}{2}\,\Phi(S_1^\star,S_2^\star), \]
where \((S_1^\star,S_2^\star)\) maximizes the original balance objective. This is a genuine global-balance approximation result, not a claim that the objective itself is submodular. For the mass version I would expect a Class A additive-approximation result to be plausible: aggregate controls invite continuous optimization, and the paper’s proof already separates a monotone submodular gain term from the part it cannot directly optimize. Whether an analogous diminishing-returns structure survives the mean-field cascade is an open question, so I would not claim the same ratio without a proof.

Both mirrors cover the paper’s centralized seed-selection problem and its symmetric-difference balance objective. They do not continuize outcomes or probabilities while leaving the population finite.

The weak point is the coarsening of the network. The paper permits arbitrary individual edges and probabilities; this mirror assumes a small set of exchangeable roles and a mean-field contact limit. The authors could reasonably reject that as too restrictive for a particular network. I think the mirror survives that objection as a meaningful instance regime: the decision still allocates campaign seeds, and the objective still balances information exposure in a social network. The exact-zero hardness anchor is the most exposed part of the case, because its discrete reduction may not survive fractional mass decisions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the weaker anchor. In the proposed heterogeneous mean-field model, every finite contact intensity gives an exposure probability strictly between zero and one whenever contact is possible. Since the campaigns’ contact events are independent, any positive mass with a nontrivial chance of exposure to either campaign has a positive chance of being exposed to exactly one. Exact zero imbalance therefore rules out the very stochastic spreading that motivates the model; it mostly asks whether the seeds and zero-contact types can be aligned. The paper’s reduction uses deterministic edges with probability one, which finite rational intensities under the \(\lambda/N\) scaling cannot reproduce. A correlated limit could preserve exact agreement between campaigns, but that would be a different, specially aligned question. Replacing zero by a tolerance would avoid the degeneracy, but it moves the anchor toward the optimization question below.

The deeper concern is that the proposed type masses do not generally retain the network information BALANCE uses. Balancing depends on which particular paths each campaign can reach and on the overlap between those reached sets. For a sparse social network, equal campaign contact rates to type groups do not determine those path overlaps. To retain them, types may need to encode local neighborhoods and network position; then the number of types can grow with the population, defeating the proposed low-support regime. The finite-rate equations instead assume dense, exchangeable mixing between types. That is a coherent population model, but it is a substantial restriction on the paper’s graph model.

This objection reaches Proposition 3 and the proposed BALANCE-H∞-MAX mirror, but it does not dispose of them. In a large platform with genuine exchangeable cohorts and dense cross-cohort contact, allocating seed mass to maximize the balanced fraction is a well-posed computational problem. It retains the paper’s central intervention and objective, and it asks a complexity or approximation question over a continuous population. Neither the mean-field literature nor the paper’s finite-network results answer it. The restriction to dense mixing may make the mirror less representative of ordinary social graphs, but it does not make the scenario implausible or the question uncomputational.

So the strongest case against is that Proposition 1’s exact-zero version degenerates, while Proposition 3’s mirror is supported only after a modeling choice that discards much of the graph structure responsible for the paper’s problem. That weakens the anchors; it does not defeat the best optimization mirror. I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario: the type-cohort BALANCE optimization survives the objections.
