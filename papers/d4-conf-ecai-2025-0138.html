The continuous mirror question

Given four fully connected stages of workstation classes, rational capacities \(\kappa_{jv}\) summing to \(1\) at each stage, and rational transition costs, find feasible path masses \(x_p\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{p:p_j=v}x_p=\kappa_{jv}\) for every stage \(j\) and class \(v\), while minimizing \(\max_{p:x_p>0}\gamma(p)-\min_{p:x_p>0}\gamma(p)\), where \(\gamma(p)\) is the additive cost of complete path \(p\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic population of standardized orders, all one type, is assigned complete paths through four stages. Rational mass capacities \(\kappa_{jv}\) represent pools of interchangeable workstation replicas, and path mass \(x_p\) is the share of orders taking path \(p\); every order receives an integral path with the original additive edge costs.

The objection that survived

Worst-individual envy is not continuous under vanishing mass: a single exceptional path has mass \(1/n\) and disappears as \(n\to\infty\), so the mirror captures disparity among positive-mass cohorts rather than every individual.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the \(K=4\) slice of Theorem 1's envy-minimization hardness; it leaves Theorem 2, the C-Balance and DC-Balance guarantees, cost-of-fairness results, and experiments unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the cost-range problem remain tractable when the number of stages \(K\) is part of the input?
  • Can a convergence notion preserve discrete worst-individual envy guarantees as the smallest positive cohort mass tends to zero?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is the fixed four-stage hardness result in Vibulan Ja, Swapnil Dhamal, and Shweta Jain, “The Multi-Stage Assignment Problem: A Fairness Perspective,” *Proceedings of the 28th European Conference on Artificial Intelligence*, 2025.

Anchor — Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For an arbitrary balanced fully connected multi-stage graph with \(K\ge4\), finding an envy-minimizing assignment is NP-hard. The proof reduces Numerical 3-Dimensional Matching, already hard at \(K=4\): node labels encode the three numbers, and a path’s cost is their sum. Envy zero is achievable exactly when the numbers can be partitioned into triples with the required sum. I would mirror the \(K=4\) case specifically; the theorem’s reduction shows that the population size, rather than a long sequence of stages, carries the hardness.

Lead mirror: Continuous Four-Stage Envy Minimization. Consider a large population of standardized orders for one client type. At each of four stages, workstation classes are pools of exactly interchangeable slots; their normalized capacities \(\kappa_{jv}\) are rational and sum to \(1\) within each stage. Adjacent-stage transitions are fully connected, with rational cost \(w^j_{uv}\). A path \(p=(v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4)\) has cost \(\gamma(p)=\sum_{j=1}^{3}w^j_{v_jv_{j+1}}\).

An assignment is a distribution \(x_p\ge0\) over complete paths, with
\[ \sum_p x_p=1,\qquad \sum_{p:p_j=v}x_p=\kappa_{jv}\quad\text{for every stage $j$ and class $v$}. \]
Here \(x_p\) is the fraction of orders taking the whole path \(p\): each order still receives one workstation at every stage. Its envy is
\[ E(x)=\max_{p:x_p>0}\gamma(p)-\min_{p:x_p>0}\gamma(p). \]
The problem is to find a feasible \(x\) minimizing \(E(x)\). A prover can return the path masses and the minimum envy.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: a manufacturer processes many near-identical orders, while many workstation slots share a small set of exact cost profiles. The order count can grow while the number of agent types remains \(\tau=1\); station classes are finite and may remain few as capacity scales. In a finite instance with \(n\) orders, a class containing \(r\) interchangeable slots has capacity \(r/n\). This preserves the paper’s path assignment and cost-disparity objective. Its example of replicating a facility to represent capacity also motivates treating such slots as a pool.

For this fixed four-stage mirror, I expect tractability. Let \(\mathcal C\) be the set of costs of all complete paths. There are at most \(\prod_j |V_j|\) paths, hence polynomially many for four stages. For each pair \(a,b\in\mathcal C\) with \(a\le b\), test whether the capacity constraints have a feasible distribution supported only on paths whose costs lie in \([a,b]\). That is a rational linear program with polynomially many path variables. The smallest feasible \(b-a\) is the minimum envy: any assignment’s actual lowest and highest used path costs are among the enumerated endpoints. Thus the N3DM hardness can dissolve because mass may be distributed fractionally across complete paths, even though no individual order gets a fractional route. This is a Class A result for the fixed-stage mirror, not a claim that the original indivisible assignment is easy.

The scope is Theorem 1’s four-stage hardness slice. It does not continuize the outcome: each order still takes an integral path. The main co-modification is that capacity is recorded as a fraction of the population, as it must be when the population itself is a continuum.

My weakest point is that grouping workstation replicas into exact cost-equivalence classes and assigning them rational mass capacities changes the paper’s unit-capacity vertex model. The replication discussion gives that change a foothold, and each individual order still receives a full path, but authors who regard every workstation identity as essential could reject this as their problem’s continuous form. Further questions are whether tractability persists when \(K\) is part of the input, whether Theorem 2’s partition-based hardness survives fractional path masses, and how agent-specific cost types change the LP.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the case against any honest negative verdict. Ja, Dhamal, and Jain prove a named computational result, and its \(K=4\) reduction places the hardness in distributing agents across paths. The allowed “no computational result” objection does not apply.

The strongest concern is that the paper measures envy by the worst individual cost gap. A distribution \(x_p\) over paths records how much population takes each path, so its natural disparity is the range of costs occurring on paths with positive mass. That captures disparities affecting a positive share of the population. But a single exceptional agent in a society of size \(n\) has mass \(1/n\); as \(n\) grows, that mass vanishes. At zero mass, the distribution no longer records the agent or their path, though that agent could determine the paper’s envy. So the mirror’s worst-agent guarantee has a boundary at measure zero.

This objection does not defeat the proposed model. For every positive rational mass, however small, the path still counts toward the disparity. And the authors could reasonably treat fairness across positive-mass cohorts as the population-level version of their objective. A stronger mirror could also retain several ex ante client types, with type-specific eligibility or costs, and distribute each type’s mass across integral paths. That remains a population model; it does not give an individual a fractional workstation bundle.

The scenario is credible enough to make that mirror worth studying. Large cohorts of standardized orders or clients can each receive a full path, while workstation capacity scales with demand. The paper itself allows facilities to be replicated to represent capacity. For four stages, the proposed path-mass formulation has polynomially many paths and can test candidate cost ranges by linear programming. That is a substantive computational question, not merely continuity in the outcome.

So the best negative case is a qualification: this mirror answers a population-level, positive-mass fairness question, and it may miss disparity borne by a vanishingly small group. I cannot honestly use that to rule out every sensible scenario or defeat the four-stage anchor. The negative case is weak.
