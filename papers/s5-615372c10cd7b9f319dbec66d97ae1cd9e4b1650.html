The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative ground is the one the attached case identifies: this paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing the complexity of a computational problem. Its contribution is an empirical comparison of six ranking methods on one primary election. Tables 1–4 report rankings, distances, and cluster satisfaction; they do not make a complexity claim for a continuous-population version.

The best upgraded mirror would take a rational distribution over approval-ballot types and ask for the resulting Approval, SPAV, or Phragmén ranking and the groups’ mass-weighted satisfaction. That is a sensible model of a primary, but it mainly restates the paper’s empirical calculation with masses in place of voter counts. The paper’s dataset already has 3,713 distinct ballots among 4,507 voters, so its evidence does not demonstrate the compressed high-multiplicity regime where the programme’s continuous optimization machinery could change what is tractable. A further question about bribery, control, or robustness under mass changes could be worthwhile, but it would add a new intervention problem the paper does not study.

So the case against a mirror of this paper is strong, though not airtight as a universal claim: a larger, more repetitive primary could motivate a continuous computational study. What is missing here is a result for that study to mirror. The article’s findings are empirical, and its algorithmic methods are inputs to that analysis rather than computational results of the kind ChoCo is charting.
