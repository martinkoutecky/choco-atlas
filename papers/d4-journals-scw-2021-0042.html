The continuous mirror question

Given a set of alternatives \(A\) with \(|A|\le 5\) and a finite support \(S\) of complete rankings, each with positive rational mass \(\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t\in S}\mu_t=1\), decide whether there are distinct alternative positions \(x_a\in\mathbb{R}\) and type positions \(y_t\in\mathbb{R}\) such that, for every \(t\in S\) and distinct \(a,b\in A\), \(a\succ_t b\) if and only if \(|x_a-y_t|<|x_b-y_t|\); return such an embedding if one exists.

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings over \(A\), and \(\mu_t\) is each type’s share of society. The decision variables are positions \(x_a\) for alternatives and \(y_t\) for supported types; the objective is feasibility, with no cost term.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the representation results in Theorems 2 and 3, including the two-type construction and the criterion for at most five alternatives. It leaves the paper’s profile counts and its higher-dimensional follow-up questions aside. The opponent cites Doignon and Falmagne (1994) for polynomial recognition on general finite support; that outside-literature answer is a collision, not a reason to downgrade the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named hardness result or theorem explicitly classifying a problem as being in P. Its best anchors are constructive and characterization results. The strongest case is that one-dimensional Euclidean representability itself has a natural continuous-population mirror.

Take a finite set \(A\) of alternatives and let each type \(t\) be a complete ranking of \(A\). A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). A continuous Euclidean representation asks whether there are distinct positions \(x_a\in\mathbb R\) for the alternatives and a position \(y_t\in\mathbb R\) for each supported type such that, for every \(t\) and every pair \(a,b\), \(a\succ_t b\) exactly when \(|x_a-y_t|<|x_b-y_t|\). A solution is those positions; the objective is feasibility. Each type’s mass is a share of society, and every agent of that type has the same ranking and can occupy the same position.

My lead is Theorem 3, proved here by exhaustive computation. It states that every preference profile with at most five alternatives is one-dimensional Euclidean if and only if it is both single-peaked and single-crossing. The paper’s proof enumerates the relevant profiles and uses CPLEX to find an embedding for each profile satisfying both conditions; the necessary direction uses the known fact that one-dimensional Euclidean profiles are single-peaked and single-crossing.

The mirror question is: given \(A\) with \(|A|\le 5\) and a rational-mass society \(\mu\) over rankings of \(A\), does the supported set of types admit the embedding just defined? Return an embedding if it does, and NO otherwise. Theorem 3 says the answer is YES exactly when the support profile is single-peaked and single-crossing. This is tractable in the bounded-alternative regime, but the paper does not give a scalable complexity result for unbounded \(m\). The regime is plausible: a large electorate deciding among a shortlist of five options could have many people but relatively few distinct ranking types. There are at most \(120\) rankings on five alternatives, and a single-crossing support has at most \(\binom{5}{2}=10\) distinct rankings. The paper itself motivates shortlists as a setting with few alternatives.

A second, narrower anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: a profile with two voters is one-dimensional Euclidean if and only if its two preference orders are jointly single-peaked. Its constructive direction is Theorem 1: given two jointly single-peaked orders, Algorithm 1 returns a one-dimensional Euclidean representation. The mirror is the same feasibility problem restricted to societies with exactly two supported ranking types, with arbitrary positive rational masses. Theorem 2 gives the YES/NO condition, and Theorem 1 supplies the embedding when the answer is YES. The paper’s Lemma 1 gives a forbidden-quadruple test for joint single-peakedness, so this restriction is tractable in \(m\); Algorithm 1 constructs the representation. The scenario is two large, stable preference blocs: \(N\gg 2\), with their shares represented directly as \(\mu\). This is a narrow but faithful high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s two-voter result.

These mirrors keep the authors’ underlying question intact: whether rankings can arise from points on a line with voters preferring closer alternatives. They do not turn Euclidean representability into a voting outcome or an optimization problem the paper never studied. Repeated voters of one ranking type are interchangeable for this question, so representing them by mass is a natural high-multiplicity description; the paper also explicitly observes that identical rankings can be embedded at the same position.

The weakest point is that the masses do no work in either mirror: representability depends only on which rankings have positive support, not on their proportions. Theorem 3 also concerns a bounded number of alternatives and relies on exhaustive computer-assisted verification. So this is a credible mirror of the paper’s domain-recognition results, but it does not reveal a mass-sensitive complexity landscape. The main further question is whether recognition and construction for arbitrary \(m\) and support size \(\tau\) admit useful bounds in \(m,\tau,L\), and whether a natural population-level robustness question would make the masses computationally consequential.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The difficulty for the proposed mirrors is that their answers depend only on which rankings have positive mass. If \(S=\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\), then the society is representable exactly when the finite profile containing one voter of each type in \(S\) is representable. The masses and their bit lengths never enter the embedding constraints. That is a particularly close fit to this paper: it assumes voters have distinct rankings, and it notes that voters with the same ranking can share an embedding position. So these mirrors append population weights to the paper’s support profile without changing the problem.

That defeats the Theorem 3 anchor directly. For at most five alternatives, asking whether a rational-mass society has a one-dimensional Euclidean representation asks whether its support profile has one. The theorem already gives the stated single-peaked-and-single-crossing criterion for that profile. The large electorate scenario makes the application plausible, but it does not create a new computational question: the representation treats ten people of one ranking exactly as it treats one.

Theorem 2 and Algorithm 1 collapse the same way. A society with exactly two supported rankings is just the paper’s two-voter input with masses attached. The joint single-peakedness test and the construction give the answer and embedding; changing the two population shares changes neither. These are valid high-multiplicity settings, but multiplicity is invisible to the result being mirrored.

The proposed next step—recognition for arbitrary \(m\) and support size \(\tau\)—does not restore a mass-sensitive problem. It remains recognition of a one-dimensional Euclidean profile after duplicate rankings are removed. Moreover, the cited literature already contains a polynomial-time algorithm for that recognition task: see [Doignon and Falmagne, “A Polynomial Time Algorithm for Unidimensional Unfolding Representations”](https://doi.org/10.1006/jagm.1994.1010). I would not treat an outside answer by itself as a reason to reject a well-posed mirror; here it does show that arbitrary \(m\) is not the unfilled extension suggested by the proponent. The central issue is that the continuous input reduces to the paper’s finite support object.

A mass-sensitive extension is conceivable—for example, minimizing the fraction of society whose rankings must change to obtain a Euclidean profile. But that replaces exact representation with a weighted repair problem, introducing an objective the paper does not study. Its interest would need to be argued as a new project, rather than as a mirror of Theorems 2 or 3. I cannot honestly rule out that the authors would find such a robustness question worthwhile; that is the weak point in a universal negative. The strongest case against the anchors actually offered is narrower: they are faithful, but their continuous population adds no computational content.
