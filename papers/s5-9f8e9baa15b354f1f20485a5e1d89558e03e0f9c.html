What the mirror covers

No computational mirror survives: Theorems 4, 6, 7, and 8 are axiom incompatibility results, and the witness-synthesis integer program is mentioned only as a technique without a stated computational guarantee.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a strong population-domain mirror here. Let \(T\) be the linear rankings of the paper’s five candidates, and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of voters with ranking \(t\). The profile \(\mathbf P_1\) in the proof of Theorem 4 already has just six ranking types among 219 voters; replicating those voters makes the electorate arbitrarily large while keeping the support size at six. Its normalized pairwise margins are \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\,\sigma_t(x,y)\), where \(\sigma_t(x,y)\) records whether ranking \(t\) puts \(x\) above or below \(y\). This is a sensible high-multiplicity society.

But the paper supplies no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 4, proved here, says that no voting method can satisfy the Condorcet winner and loser criteria, positive involvement, and resolvability together. Theorem 6 extends that incompatibility to \(n\)-voter resolvability for every positive integer \(n\). Theorem 7 gives the corresponding result with negative involvement, and Theorem 8 gives variants using independence of clones, with an additional block-preservation condition in its negative-involvement case. These are axiomatic impossibility theorems, not results that classify a computational problem as tractable or hard.

One could define a continuous-population analogue by asking whether a voting rule on distributions over rankings can satisfy continuous versions of those axioms. That would require choosing how “adding a voter” and “up to \(n\) voters” translate into changes of mass; the paper’s unit-voter steps do not specify a continuous mass threshold. More fundamentally, the resulting question would still be about axiomatic compatibility, not computation. The footnote’s use of an integer linear program to synthesize a small witness profile is a possible lead for a separate witness-minimization problem, but it is not a named complexity result in the paper.

So the honest positive case is that continuous populations are a natural way to represent the paper’s electoral domain. It does not support a continuous computational problem per the programme’s scope: there is no eligible anchor, and no per-anchor complexity prediction to make. The weak point is also the decisive one: the paper studies which rules can exist, not what an algorithm can compute.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The six-type profile \(\mathbf P_1\) is a sensible high-multiplicity society: replicating its 219 voters preserves the same ranking proportions. But that establishes a natural population representation, not a computational problem. The paper’s Theorem 4 is an impossibility statement about whether a voting method \(F\) exists at all. Theorem 6 extends it to \(n\)-voter resolvability; Theorem 7 switches to negative involvement; Theorem 8 uses independence of clones. None gives an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a hardness result for computing an outcome. Replacing voter counts with masses leaves the central question—whether a rule defined over all profiles can satisfy a set of axioms—axiomatic.

A stronger proposed mirror might define \(F\) on distributions over rankings and translate involvement and resolvability into mass perturbations. That is a coherent continuous axiom question, but it remains an axiom question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope. Asking an algorithm to return \(F(\mu)\) for one distribution would not answer it: the paper’s claim quantifies over a rule on the whole domain, and supplies no representation of such a rule as an algorithm. Giving rules succinct representations and asking whether they satisfy the axioms would create a new, representation-dependent verification problem.

The paper’s integer-programming footnote suggests a better computational follow-up: search for impossibility witnesses minimizing voter count, support, or some margin-based quantity. That could be worthwhile proof-synthesis work, but it is not a mirror of a computational result in the paper. The central contradiction already has a fixed five-candidate, six-type witness; replicating it changes no underlying search problem, and a five-candidate witness suffices even for larger candidate domains. The article reports using an integer program to find a small profile, but states no complexity result for that synthesis task.

So every named anchor in the proponent’s case fails the same eligibility test. The negative case is strong for ChoCo’s remit: this paper offers a natural continuous representation of its electorates, but no continuous computational mirror of its results. The limit of the claim is that an independently designed witness-minimization project might still be useful; it would be a new project, not a mirror anchored by these theorems.
