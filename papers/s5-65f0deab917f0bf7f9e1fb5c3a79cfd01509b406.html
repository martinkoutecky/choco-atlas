What the mirror covers

It covers Proposition 14's comparison of optimal vote-count and threshold decisions; it leaves the shift identity, mirrored-performance results, Gaussian pit, and majoritarian-group characterization untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

On the strict anchor rule, there is no qualifying computational result in this paper: none of its numbered results claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. The closest positive anchor is Proposition 14, proved here. For a fixed proposal generator and a nonempty target set \(C\), it says that these are equivalent: a threshold rule maximizes \(C\)’s expected total gain among vote-count rules; some monotone vote-count rule does so; and there are no positive-probability vote totals \(k<\ell\) whose conditional expected gains satisfy \(M^C(k)>0>M^C(\ell)\). The proposition further implies that the best vote-count rule strictly beats every threshold exactly when such a pair exists. This is an exact optimization characterization, not a stated complexity result, so the mirror below is a candidate based on the nearest available anchor.

Consider a large regional electorate deciding whether to accept randomly selected public projects. Residents fall into a finite set \(T\) of exposure and behavior types: members of a type have the same per-capita gain schedule across project classes and vote as individualists. Type \(t\) has population mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The agenda has finite support \(\Omega\); project \(\omega\) occurs with rational probability \(p_\omega\) and gives type \(t\) gain \(x_{t\omega}\), also rational. Millions of residents can thus be represented by a small number \(\tau=|T|\) of masses. A fractional vote share is the natural quantity for the decision.

The continuous problem is: given \((T,\mu,\Omega,p,x)\) and a nonempty target set of types \(C\), find a vote-share rule \(V:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p_\omega \left(\sum_{t\in C}\mu_t x_{t\omega}\right)V(q_\omega), \qquad q_\omega=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t \left(\mathbf 1[x_{t\omega}>0]+\tfrac12\mathbf 1[x_{t\omega}=0]\right). \]
Here \(q_\omega\) is the mass of support, counting neutral votes as one half, and \(V(q)\) is the probability of accepting at support share \(q\). A solution is an acceptance probability for every realized share; the further question is whether an optimal solution can be chosen as a threshold in \(q\).

For each realized share \(q\), let \(M_C(q)\) be the conditional expected total gain of \(C\) given that share. An optimal rule accepts when \(M_C(q)>0\), rejects when \(M_C(q)<0\), and may choose either action when it is zero. A threshold is optimal exactly when no lower realized share has positive \(M_C\) while a higher one has negative \(M_C\). That is Proposition 14’s criterion with vote mass in place of an integer vote total. This mirror looks tractable: group the agenda states by their rational vote share, compute the conditional gains, and sort the shares. With the agenda listed explicitly, this takes polynomial time in its support size, \(\tau\), and the input bit length. The question mirrors the paper’s comparison of optimal vote-count and threshold decisions; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s Gaussian pit or its result about majoritarian groups.

The weak point is that this scenario gives everyone of a type the same gain from a given project. The paper instead assumes independent, identically distributed individual gains. A continuum of residents with such idiosyncratic shocks would generally have deterministic limiting vote and gain shares, which can erase precisely the rare vote-count events that drive some of the finite-electorate results. So this is a plausible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s fixed-strategy, stochastic-agenda decision problem, but it changes the dependence structure and yields a modest aggregation result—not a new complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper does not establish a computational complexity result. Proposition 14 is the nearest anchor, but it gives an exact decision criterion: accept vote totals with positive conditional expected gain and reject those with negative conditional expected gain; a threshold is optimal exactly when those signs do not reverse as the vote total rises. That is a useful characterization, but it does not pose the kind of complexity question ChoCo is set up to chart.

The proposed regional-project mirror is a sensible high-multiplicity model: residents with the same exposure schedule can be represented by a type mass. But with an explicitly listed agenda, its optimization separates completely. For each support share \(q\), sum the probability-weighted gains of the agenda states producing \(q\), then accept or reject according to the sign. Sorting the resulting shares tests whether a threshold is optimal. The computation is linear in the listed agenda size and type count, up to sorting; the population masses create no coupled optimization problem. If the agenda is represented succinctly and that computation becomes hard, the difficulty lies in the agenda representation and would arise in a finite electorate too. This is a direct translation of Proposition 14’s sign test, not a new complexity landscape.

A more faithful continuation of the paper’s independent individual gains runs into a different problem. In a continuum, the law of large numbers makes vote shares and aggregate gains deterministic for a given generator. For individualists, the paper itself shows that with no zero gains the conditional expected gain rises with the number of supporting votes, so a threshold is already optimal. In the large-population limit, the finite-sample vote-count fluctuations that drive the paper’s interesting comparisons disappear. The majoritarian-group effect is also finite-size: its information about the group’s total gain scales as \(\kappa(g)\sim\sqrt{2g/\pi}\), while the group’s vote is counted with weight \(g\). At population scale, the former is negligible; rescaling to retain it would study fluctuations around the continuum, not a mass-based decision problem.

There is a real weakness in this negative case: the proposed type-level project model is plausible, and Proposition 14’s question can be asked of it. That makes the mirror defensible as a small modeling extension. But it changes the paper’s individual stochastic gains into common type-level gain schedules and reduces the optimization to a finite Bayes decision rule. I do not see a strong basis for claiming that no author-recognizable continuous mirror exists; the stronger case is that the surviving mirror is too thin to justify ChoCo’s complexity programme.
