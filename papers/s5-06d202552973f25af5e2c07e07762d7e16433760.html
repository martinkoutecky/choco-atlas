The case FOR (proponent)

I can make a plausible case for a continuous-population extension, but this paper has no named computational result to serve as the required anchor. Theorems 1–4 characterize which social evaluation functions satisfy particular axioms; none establishes an algorithm, a complexity bound, or a hardness result. For example, Theorem 3, attributed in the paper to Gajdos and Weymark (2003), characterizes the social evaluations satisfying ORD, CONT, MON, ANON, UPM, SAS, WCA, and HOM when \(q\ge3\). It gives the weighted, rank-based functional forms in equations (25) and (26). That is a normative characterization, not a result about computing or optimizing anything. I can’t honestly present it as a computational anchor.

The strongest candidate question I can extract from that characterization is Continuous Generalized-Gini Social Comparison. Let \(q\ge3\), and fix positive rational \(\gamma_j\) summing to \(1\), nondecreasing rational step functions \(a_j:[0,1]\to\mathbb Q_{>0}\) with integral \(1\), and \(r=2\). An input is two societies \(\mu,\nu\), each a finite-support distribution with rational masses over positive rational attribute bundles \(x\in\mathbb Q_{>0}^q\), and with the same mean for each attribute. For each attribute \(j\), let \(Q^\downarrow_{\mu,j}\) be its descending quantile function in society \(\mu\), and set
\[ z_{\mu,j}=\int_0^1 a_j(u)Q^\downarrow_{\mu,j}(u)\,du, \qquad W_2(\mu)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^q\gamma_j z_{\mu,j}^2\right)^{1/2}. \]
The question is whether \(W_2(\mu)\ge W_2(\nu)\); the solution is the corresponding yes/no answer. This is a continuous-population version of the paper’s rank-weighted social evaluation: type is a complete bundle of the \(q\) attributes, and mass is the fraction of society with that bundle. The added step-function profiles specify how the paper’s finite rank weights extend across a continuum.

A plausible regime is a large population measured using standardized income, education, and health categories. Many people may share the same bundle, so the population can be large while the number \(\tau\) of distinct bundles stays moderate. For this specified question, computing each marginal quantile integral amounts to sorting the supported bundles by each attribute and integrating piecewise-constant functions; comparing the two evaluations is tractable in \(q,\tau\), and the input bit length. The paper’s established social-evaluation formula gives a natural reason to ask for this comparison, and authors working on those evaluations could recognize the population distribution as a meaningful extension.

The limitation is decisive for the requested case: the paper neither asks this computational question nor proves its tractability. The extension also requires a limiting rank-weight profile \(a_j\), which the paper does not specify; its finite rank weights can vary with population size. So this is a coherent new question suggested by the paper, not a mirror anchored in one of its named computational results. The paper’s actual contribution is normative and axiomatic, and a complexity study would change that contribution. That is the weakest point—and under the stated anchor requirement, it means the positive case does not qualify as an anchor-backed case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against this anchor is that Theorem 3 is not a computational result. It characterizes which social evaluations satisfy a set of axioms and gives their functional forms. It does not pose a problem of computing or optimizing an outcome, let alone establish its complexity. The proposed comparison is a new computational task attached to that characterization, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

Even taken on its own terms, the proposed task has little algorithmic substance. With finite support, each marginal quantile function is a step function. For each attribute, sort the \(\tau\) bundle values, form the cumulative mass intervals, and intersect those intervals with the steps of \(a_j\) to compute \(z_{\mu,j}\). These are sums of rational quantities. Since the compared values are nonnegative, for \(r=2\) one can compare \(\sum_j\gamma_jz_{\mu,j}^2\) directly, without computing square roots. The work is polynomial in \(q\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. The finite-society counterpart is the same calculation using ranks and weights. Continuization changes the notation from a weighted rank sum to an integral; it does not expose a computational question whose answer needs to be discovered.

The paper’s separability assumption makes the extension still thinner: Theorem 3’s evaluation depends on each attribute’s marginal distribution, not on how attributes are bundled together across types. The society can be represented by those marginals alone for this purpose. A continuous type distribution is perfectly sensible here, and large populations with repeated attribute bundles are plausible. Those facts support the *model*; they do not supply a worthwhile complexity problem.

A more ambitious version would ask for the least-cost mass redistribution that raises \(W_2\) to a target. That could be a meaningful policy problem for a large population. But it would require adding transfer costs, feasible changes, and a target—none of which the paper specifies. It would be new intervention research using the paper’s index, rather than a computational mirror of Theorem 3. A limit theorem for finite rank weights converging to \(a_j\) would stay closer to the paper, but it would be an analytic convergence question, and evaluating the resulting functional remains the sorting calculation above.

So the proposed anchor does not survive as an anchor for ChoCo’s computational programme. The limitation of this negative case is that it cannot establish the universal claim that *no* worthwhile continuous-population question could arise from the paper: its subject matter admits plausible mass-level redistribution questions. It can establish the narrower point that the proponent’s comparison question is computationally routine and that a more substantive version would add a new problem the paper does not study.
