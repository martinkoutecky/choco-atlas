The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its theorems classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its closest anchor is Theorem 11, proved here. It bounds the number \(a(n,m,\Gamma_{\mathrm{sp}})\) of ordered single-peaked elections by
\[ \frac{m!}{2}\,2^{(m-1)n}(1-\epsilon(n,m)) \le a(n,m,\Gamma_{\mathrm{sp}}) \le \frac{m!}{2}\,2^{(m-1)n}, \]
where \(\epsilon(n,m)\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\) for fixed \(m\). This is a counting result, not a complexity result, so it cannot serve as one of the required anchors.

The strongest positive opening I see is a follow-on to Theorem 11. Let the voter types be the rankings \(T\) on \(C\), and let \(q\) be a rational distribution over those types. Define Most Likely Single-Peaked Limit Society as follows: choose an axis \(A\) and a population distribution \(\mu\), supported on rankings in \(T\) that are single-peaked with respect to \(A\), to minimize
\[ D(\mu\|q)=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\log\frac{\mu_t}{q_t}. \]
The output is a minimizing pair \((A,\mu)\). Here \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of a large population holding ranking \(t\); the objective measures how atypical that population composition is under the baseline type distribution \(q\).

For a fixed axis, the minimizing population is \(q\) conditioned on the rankings single-peaked on that axis. Thus the optimization reduces to choosing an axis maximizing \(q(S_A)\), where \(S_A\) is its set of single-peaked rankings. With uniform \(q\), \(q(S_A)=2^{m-1}/m!\), so the minimum relative entropy is \(\log(m!/2^{m-1})\). That is precisely the exponential rate in Theorem 11’s likelihood bound under the Impartial Culture model. A plausible regime is a large consultation or election with many people but a stable, relatively small set of shared ranking types. The paper’s likelihood question then has a natural population-frequency interpretation.

I would expect any general computational difficulty in this follow-on to come from choosing the axis, so any hardness should transfer from the corresponding finite weighted-profile problem; with fixed \(m\), enumerating axes is straightforward. Further questions would be whether axis selection is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), and whether it admits useful approximation or parameterized algorithms.

The soft spot is decisive for the requested standard: this optimization is a proposed variational reformulation, not a result the paper proves or computes with. Theorem 11 establishes an asymptotic counting rate for uniform draws; it does not establish the complexity of axis selection or endorse the continuous optimization problem. So there is a plausible population-level follow-on, but no eligible named computational anchor in this paper for a properly anchored continuous-mirror case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a *mirror of this paper* is that it supplies no computational problem to mirror. Theorems 8–17 count elections or bound probabilities under specified sampling models. They give no algorithm, running time, or complexity classification. The paper’s references to computational voting work are background, not results it proves. That leaves no named computational result for ChoCo to continuize.

The entropy proposal is a good stress test. For a fixed axis \(A\), its optimizer is just \(q\) conditioned on the rankings single-peaked on \(A\), and its value is \(-\log q(S_A)\). Under the paper’s uniform IC model, every axis has the same mass, so the proposed optimization returns the rate already implicit in Theorem 11: \(\log(m!/2^{m-1})\). That is a clean continuous interpretation of the counting result, but it does not create a computational question for the paper’s stated model.

Making the problem computationally less trivial requires supplying a variable, nonuniform input distribution \(q\) and asking which axis captures the most mass. That is a coherent weighted axis-selection problem, but it is an extension: the paper does not pose axis selection as an optimization task. Likewise, adding costs and asking for the least mass transfer needed to make a society single-peaked would be a plausible continuous repair problem, but the paper studies neither interventions nor costs. Those may be worthwhile ChoCo projects; they are not computational mirrors of results established here.

I would not base the negative case on the claim that large populations or fractional type masses are nonsensical. They are quite natural for rankings. The case is narrower: this paper studies the likelihood of a structural property under finite-election sampling models, and its main results are counting and probability bounds. The entropy formulation is a respectable adjacent connection, so the honest negative is weak against *any worthwhile continuous research inspired by the paper*. It is stronger against calling that research a continuous mirror of the paper’s computational results, because the paper proves none.
