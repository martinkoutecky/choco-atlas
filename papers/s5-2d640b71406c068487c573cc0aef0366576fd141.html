The continuous mirror question

Given rational locations \(a_1,\ldots,a_\tau\in[0,1]\), positive rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\), and rational \(\varepsilon>0\), can one compute a rational facility location \(\hat y\in[0,1]\) in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\varepsilon\), such that \(G_\mu(\hat y)\ge \max_{y\in[0,1]}G_\mu(y)-\varepsilon\), where \(G_\mu(y)=\prod_{j=1}^{\tau}(1-|y-a_j|)^{\mu_j}\) and positive-mass types at zero utility give welfare zero?

The model it lives in

A distribution over location types \(a_j\in[0,1]\) with rational masses \(\mu_j\); the decision variable is one facility location \(y\in[0,1]\), and the objective is normalized Nash welfare \(G_\mu(y)=\prod_j(1-|y-a_j|)^{\mu_j}\).

The objection that survived

The normalized additive-error guarantee does not follow from Theorem 2's guarantee for the unnormalized product, and combining repeated locations into weights may make the new algorithm a short extension.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the facility-location optimization anchored by Theorem 2; it leaves the paper's welfare-ratio bounds, fairness properties, and strategy-proof mechanism results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the weighted log-utility optimization be approximated to additive error \(\varepsilon\) in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(L\), and \(1/\varepsilon\)?
  • How does an additive guarantee for \(G_\mu\) relate to the paper's additive guarantee for the unnormalized finite-agent product?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a continuous, high-multiplicity version of the paper’s single-facility Nash-welfare optimization. The anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: given a finite profile of \(n\) agent locations on \([0,1]\), the authors show that a facility location whose Nash welfare is within additive error \(\varepsilon>0\) of the maximum can be computed in time polynomial in the input and \(1/\varepsilon\). Their proof breaks the line at consecutive reported locations and solves a convex optimization problem on each interval. The paper gives no comparable named hardness result for this single-facility problem.

Here is the mirror I would put to a prover: Continuous Nash Facility Location. An instance consists of \(\tau\) rational locations \(a_1,\ldots,a_\tau\in[0,1]\), positive rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\), and a rational error tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\). The masses describe a population distribution over location types. A facility at \(y\in[0,1]\) gives type \(a_j\) utility \(u(y,a_j)=1-|y-a_j|\). Define the population’s normalized Nash welfare by

\[ G_\mu(y)=\prod_{j=1}^{\tau}(1-|y-a_j|)^{\mu_j}, \]

with welfare zero if a positive-mass type receives zero utility. The question is to output a rational location \(\hat y\) such that

\[ G_\mu(\hat y)\ge \max_{y\in[0,1]}G_\mu(y)-\varepsilon. \]

The algorithmic target is running time polynomial in \(\tau\), the binary input length \(L\), and \(1/\varepsilon\). This is a Class A candidate: between support locations, maximizing \(G_\mu\) is equivalent to maximizing the weighted sum of log utilities, a concave one-dimensional objective. The paper’s interval-by-interval convex-optimization approach appears to extend directly to those weighted terms. That compressed-support bound is a question for the mirror; Theorem 2 itself only proves the bound for an explicitly given finite profile.

The population story could be residents along a service corridor, represented by occupied building locations or census cells. The type is a resident’s location, mass is the fraction of residents there, and the decision remains the placement of one facility. A corridor might have many thousands of residents but only hundreds or thousands of represented location types. Agents at the same location are interchangeable for this problem because the paper defines their utility solely by distance. This is a meaningful regime when those shared locations are part of the data, rather than an assumption that arbitrary residents have identical preferences.

The connection to the paper’s objective is exact on finite high-multiplicity profiles. If \(n_j\) of \(n\) residents are at \(a_j\), then \(\mu_j=n_j/n\) and

\[ G_\mu(y)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-|y-x_i|)\right)^{1/n}. \]

Taking the \(n\)th root preserves the maximizing facility location. For general masses, the weighted geometric mean is the population-normalized version of the same Nash objective. The society is what becomes continuous; the facility stays a single point on the line. That is a mirror the authors could plausibly recognize as their optimization problem.

My weakest point is that Theorem 2 already makes the explicit finite problem polynomial-time solvable. This mirror does not claim that population continuization dissolves hardness. Its positive case is narrower: the paper’s proof suggests an algorithm that works with aggregate type masses, with complexity governed by support size rather than the number of residents. The new result would need to establish that compressed-input guarantee and its approximation bound for the normalized welfare. It could also ask how that guarantee relates quantitatively to Theorem 2’s additive error for the unnormalized product, and whether the weighted problem admits exact output when its optimizer is irrational.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The honest negative case is weak because Theorem 2 is a real computational result, and its continuous-population mirror is sensible. Residents who share a location along a service corridor can be treated as one type; the problem depends on their location, not their identities. The paper’s finite profile is already a high-multiplicity model whenever many residents share locations.

The strongest objection is that the mirror appears to be a short aggregation of the paper’s existing method, rather than a new computational landscape. For masses \(\mu_j\), maximizing the normalized Nash welfare is equivalent to maximizing

\[ \log G_\mu(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\log(1-|y-a_j|). \]

Between support locations, this is a concave one-dimensional function. Its derivative is

\[ -\sum_{a_j<y}\frac{\mu_j}{1-y+a_j} +\sum_{a_j>y}\frac{\mu_j}{1+y-a_j}, \]

and is strictly decreasing on each such interval. The optimizer can therefore be found by checking the support intervals and locating a root, with work governed by \(\tau\) and the rational input size. The paper already sets up the same log-utility optimization for individual agents; repeated locations simply combine into weights. The proposed mirror is valid, but its algorithmic content seems to follow directly from the proof.

There is also a narrower mismatch: the paper’s approximation guarantee concerns the unnormalized product, while the mirror uses the normalized geometric mean. Taking the \(n\)th root preserves the optimizer for a finite profile, but not the additive-error guarantee. That weakens the claim that the mirror directly extends Theorem 2, though it does not make the new question ill-posed.

The other results offer less promising computational anchors. The welfare bounds and fairness guarantees are analyses of mechanisms, not complexity results. A continuum version of individual strategyproofness also needs care: with infinitesimal agents, one person’s report has zero mass, so the usual notion can become vacuous; a positive-mass deviation instead asks a different, coalition-level question.

So the case against is about payoff, not legitimacy: this looks more like an elementary support-compression corollary than a substantial ChoCo complexity result. But that may still be useful as a clean high-multiplicity result. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario; this anchor survives that test.
