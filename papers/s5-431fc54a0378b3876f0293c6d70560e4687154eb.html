The continuous mirror question

Given supported types \(a\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_a\), \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\), integer endowments \(e_a\), acceptable supplier-type sets \(A_a\), quotas \(q_{ab}\), and a priority order on types, choose rational flows \(x_{ab}\ge0\) and retained masses \(r_a\ge0\) satisfying \(r_a+\sum_b x_{ab}=\mu_a e_a=r_a+\sum_c x_{ca}\), \(x_{ab}=0\) when \(b\notin A_a\), and \(x_{ab}\le\mu_a q_{ab}\) when \(b\in A_a\). Maximize \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\), then lexicographically maximize each type’s received exchange mass in priority order, requiring that the aggregate flows admit a distribution over members’ integral, balanced bundles.

The model it lives in

Each type groups members with the same service, integer endowment, acceptable supplier types, quotas, and priority tier. The decision variables \(x_{ab}\) are units of mass supplied by type \(b\) to type \(a\); \(r_a\) is type \(a\)’s retained mass. The constraints enforce balanced aggregate exchange and per-type quotas, while the objective maximizes total exchange and then applies type-level priority.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest computational anchor is Proposition 1, proved in this paper. The paper gives no named NP-hardness result or other complexity classification, so this is an algorithmic anchor. Its content is that, for a fixed priority order \(\pi\), a mechanism selecting a maximum-weight matching in the paper’s bipartite graph selects a \(\pi\)-priority allocation. The graph has an agent on one side and a clone of each agent on the other; an edge exists for an acceptable supplier or the agent’s own clone. Acceptable edges have the recipient’s reported quota as capacity, and both sides have quota \(t_i\). A self-edge has weight \(-1\); other edges to recipient \(i\) have weight \(\epsilon_{\pi(i)}\), chosen so a higher-priority agent’s gain outweighs all possible gains for lower-priority agents. Thus the matching first minimizes self-retention, giving a maximal allocation, then refines among maximal allocations in priority order. The paper proves this equivalence; the underlying matching or flow problem is computable by standard network-flow algorithms, with Orlin (1993) cited for the relevant flow machinery.

I would mirror that computation in a large time bank or federated volunteer exchange. A type \(a\) consists of members with the same standardized service, endowment \(e_a\), acceptable supplier types \(A_a\), per-type quotas \(q_{ab}\), and priority class. These are complete types for the purposes of the problem. Let \(\mu_a\) be the fraction of the population in type \(a\), with \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\). A plausible regime has thousands of members but dozens or hundreds of types: many people offer the same standardized service and use the same acceptance and quota profile.

Call the mirror Type-Priority Balanced Exchange\(_\infty\). Its input is the supported types, rational masses \(\mu_a\), integer endowments and quotas, acceptable-type sets, and a priority order on type classes. Its decision variable \(x_{ab}\) is the mass of units supplied by type \(b\) to type \(a\), excluding units a member keeps for themself; \(x_{aa}\) can represent exchanges between different members of the same type. Let \(r_a\) be the mass of type-\(a\) units retained by their owners. Feasibility requires, for every type \(a\),

\(\displaystyle r_a+\sum_b x_{ab}=\mu_a e_a,\qquad r_a+\sum_c x_{ca}=\mu_a e_a,\)

and \(x_{ab}=0\) when \(b\notin A_a\), while \(0\le x_{ab}\le \mu_a q_{ab}\) when \(b\in A_a\). The first equation accounts for each type’s members receiving their endowment; the second accounts for their members supplying it. For \(a=b\), acceptability and the quota concern other members of that type, while \(r_a\) separately records actual self-retention.

The solution maximizes total exchange, \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\), then lexicographically maximizes the exchanged amount received by each type class in priority order. It must be realizable by assigning members integral bundles: each member retains an integer number of units and receives and supplies the same integer number of external units, subject to their type’s quotas. The aggregate variables can be fractional because they describe mass. This is not an extra relaxation of individual bundles: for each type, the allowable integer inbound bundles form a capacitated simplex with an integral convex hull; outgoing units have no additional provider-side restrictions, so members’ inbound and outbound bundles can be paired at the same trade quantity.

This looks like a Class A problem. The aggregate constraints give a capacitated circulation on \(\tau\) type nodes with \(O(\tau^2)\) type-to-type flows; the priority refinement is a sequence of at most \(\tau\) linear optimizations. With rational masses and binary-encoded quotas and endowments, the natural question is whether this admits a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The flow formulation suggests yes. For finite populations whose type shares are \(n_a/n\), scaling by \(n\) gives integer flow capacities; this also invites a precise comparison with the grouped high-multiplicity discrete problem. Since the paper supplies no hardness anchor, I would not claim that this mirror dissolves discrete hardness.

The case is plausible because the paper explicitly models time banks and says its mechanism applies to other time-exchange settings. The mirror keeps the central problem—maximal individually rational, balanced exchange—and the type-level flow computes that allocation. The paper’s discussion of rational, non-integer endowments is not itself a continuous-population model: it keeps a finite set of named agents.

The weakest point is the priority rule. The paper orders named agents, while a useful type model makes equivalent members indistinguishable. This mirror therefore orders cohorts and treats each cohort’s members together. If the application requires a strict individual lottery order, or if every member has idiosyncratic service, quota, or priority data, the number of types may approach the population size and erase the high-multiplicity advantage. The cohort rule is a real co-modification, but the maximal exchange computation and the underlying time-exchange question remain intact. I would not extend this case to the paper’s non-manipulability theorem: the continuous, cohort-priority version would need its own incentive analysis.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the paper’s only clear computational anchor. It proves that a priority mechanism can be computed through a maximum weight matching; Theorem 1 is an incentive result, not a complexity classification.

The proposed type model does not preserve Proposition 1’s priority rule as stated. The paper orders *individual agents*. A complete type for this computation must therefore include an agent’s place in that order, as well as their endowment and partner-specific acceptability and quota data. If those attributes vary within a cohort, faithfully representing them can make the support as large as the population. If they do not vary, aggregation may preserve the exchange totals, but the proposal’s lexicographic priority for an entire type is still a different selection rule from the paper’s sequential priority for named agents.

That problem is structural in the exchange graph. Collapsing agents to types works only when compatibility and quotas are uniform across each pair of types. Otherwise, a type-to-type flow can combine exchanges that no individual-level matching can realize. Enforcing the needed uniformity means replacing the paper’s agent-specific goods and bilateral preferences with class-level goods and relationships. That may suit some markets, but it is a substantive change to the model.

A cohort-priority exchange problem could still be a sensible co-modification: give each class a priority tier and seek a balanced maximal flow between classes. It would not compute the paper’s individual-priority outcome, and the paper’s non-manipulability result would not establish its incentive properties. The negative case is therefore that the proposed mirror has not yet shown that it preserves the paper’s computational question.

That is not enough to support the universal claim. A standardized exchange market with many members per class and class-level compatibility could make the cohort model natural, and the paper explicitly allows different time-exchange settings. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror in every scenario; this case is strongest against the exact Proposition 1 mirror, and weak against the broader programme.
