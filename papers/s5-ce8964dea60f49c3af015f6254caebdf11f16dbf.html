Why no mirror

The paper's named results are analytical limits and institutional-design statements, not algorithms, approximation guarantees, computational formulations, or hardness results. The proposed mass-based inverse-Shapley formulation is a coherent continuous-population extension, but it introduces a finite-council computational problem whose continuum mainly replaces \(n_i\) by \(a_i\). Thus bit (a) fails even though the proposed question could be worth studying.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the strong-affiliation regime behind Theorem 2 and Corollary 3; it leaves Theorem 1, Corollary 1, and the square-root Corollary 2 outside the computational formulation.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of deciding whether some \(w\in\{0,\ldots,B\}^m\) satisfies \(\max_i\left|\phi_i(v_w)/a_i-1\right|\le\varepsilon\)?
  • Can target Shapley vectors \(a\) be approximated efficiently under bounded weights, or is the problem hard in \(m\)?
  • Can a high-multiplicity model retain the square-root effect without reintroducing finite sampling blocks?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that a continuous mirror exists, but this paper fails the brief’s strict anchor gate: it contains no named computational-complexity result. Its numbered results are asymptotic probability and design theorems, not claims of membership in \(P\), NP-hardness, W[1]-hardness, or similar. Thus there is no qualifying computational anchor. The following is the strongest mirror the paper itself supports.

The closest named result is Theorem 2, proved in Appendix B. It considers \(m\) constituencies, quota \(q\in[0.5,1)\), and representative ideal points \(\lambda_i=t\mu_i+\tilde\epsilon_i\), where the \(\mu_i\) are independent and identically distributed with a common bounded density, while the \(\tilde\epsilon_i\) are mutually independent with finite means and variances. As \(t\to\infty\), the ratio of the pivot probabilities of representatives \(i\) and \(j\) converges to the ratio of their Shapley values: \(\pi_i/\pi_j\to\phi_i(v)/\phi_j(v)\).

Its direct design consequence is Corollary 3, “Linear Shapley rule,” also proved in the paper. If individual ideal points have an i.i.d. individual component and an i.i.d. constituency-specific component, each representative adopts the constituency median, and constituency-level variance is sufficiently large relative to individual-level variance, then choosing weights whose Shapley values are proportional to constituency populations achieves approximately equal representation, for any quota \(q\in[0.5,1)\). This is the strongest support for a continuous mirror, although it is an analytical result rather than a computational one.

My lead mirror would be Continuous Egalitarian Council Design. Let there be \(m\) constituencies with population masses \(a_1,\ldots,a_m>0\), \(\sum_i a_i=1\). A type records constituency membership and policy ideal point. In a high-multiplicity regime, constituency \(i\) contains a continuum of citizens of total mass \(a_i\), with a common constituency-level ideal point \(Z_i\); the \(Z_i\) are independent draws from a continuous distribution. Equivalently, a realized society is the mass distribution \(\mu_Z=\sum_i a_i\delta_{(i,Z_i)}\). A less degenerate version can give each constituency a finite symmetric collection of repeated offset types around \(Z_i\), without changing its median.

The council assigns nonnegative integer weights \(w_i\), with quota \(q\sum_iw_i\), and chooses the weighted median of the constituency representatives’ ideal points. Let \(\pi_i(w)\) be the probability that constituency \(i\)’s representative is pivotal. Because the \(Z_i\) are i.i.d. and continuously distributed, every ordering is equally likely, so \(\pi_i(w)=\phi_i(v_w)\), the Shapley value of the weighted voting game \(v_w\).

The precise computational problem is:

Given rational masses \(a_i\), quota \(q\), a weight-encoding bound \(B\), and tolerance \(\varepsilon\), find \(w\in\{0,\ldots,B\}^m\) minimizing
\(\Delta(w)=\max_i\left|\frac{\phi_i(v_w)}{a_i}-1\right|\),
or decide whether some \(w\) satisfies \(\Delta(w)\le\varepsilon\).

The solution is the council weight vector \(w\). The objective says that a unit of population mass in every constituency has approximately equal expected influence. The exact version, \(\varepsilon=0\), asks whether the target mass vector \(a\) can be realized exactly as a Shapley-value vector.

This is a genuine population continuization. The number of citizens is infinite or very large, while the number of distinct repeated types is only \(O(m)\). Mass \(a_i\), rather than the identities of \(n_i\) citizens, is the relevant quantity. The council’s weighted-median outcome remains exactly the paper’s outcome; what changes is that the finite electorate is replaced by repeated population types. The common constituency shock is also not an artificial relaxation: it is precisely the strong-affiliation regime used in Theorem 2 and Corollary 3.

The authors should recognize this as their problem. Their finite condition is \(\pi_i/n_i\approx\pi_j/n_j\); replacing \(n_i/n\) by population mass \(a_i\) gives \(\pi_i/a_i\approx\pi_j/a_j\). Their recommendation \(\phi_i\propto n_i\) becomes \(\phi_i=a_i\). Nothing essential about the council, quota, weighted median, pivot event, or egalitarian objective has been removed.

I would expect the exact or finely approximated version to be Class B, with hardness transferring from the discrete inverse weighted-voting problem. The continuum removes the multiplicity of individual citizens, but not the combinatorics of coalitions among the \(m\) constituencies. Computing or inverting Shapley values remains a problem over subsets of constituencies. The paper itself warns that the inverse problem may have no exact solution, so the optimization and tolerance versions are more natural than insisting on equality.

The main weakness is that this mirror covers the paper’s strong-affiliation result, not its square-root benchmark. In a genuine continuum, idiosyncratic within-constituency variation averages out, so the finite-sample \(1/\sqrt{n_i}\) effect behind Corollary 2 disappears. Preserving the square-root rule would require retaining finite blocks or another source of mass-level sampling variation, which might look like reintroducing noise rather than continuizing the population. That is why Corollary 3 is the defensible lead.

So the substantive positive verdict is: the paper has a convincing continuous-population mirror in its linear-Shapley, strongly affiliated regime. The formal negative qualification is decisive under the stated rubric: there is no named computational result in the paper to serve as the required anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive negative point is the one the proponent concedes: this paper contains no qualifying computational result. Theorem 2 and Corollary 3 are asymptotic probability and institutional-design statements. They specify neither a computational problem with an input/output pair nor an algorithm, approximation guarantee, parameterized classification, or hardness result. Under ChoCo’s anchor gate, there is therefore no computational theorem here to continuize.

Theorem 2 is especially weak as an anchor. Its limit \(t\to\infty\) scales constituency-level shocks while keeping the number of constituencies \(m\) finite. That is not a high-multiplicity or population-size limit. In the proposed model, \(\mu_Z=\sum_i a_i\delta_{(i,Z_i)}\), all substantive randomness is still in the ordering of \(m\) named constituency representatives. The continuum of citizens contributes only the labels \(a_i\). If one asks for weights satisfying \(\phi_i(v_w)\approx a_i\), one has introduced a new inverse-Shapley problem on a finite weighted voting game; one has not exposed computational content already present in Theorem 2.

Corollary 3 does not repair this. The finite paper’s individual pivot probability satisfies

\[ p^\ell=\frac{\pi_i}{n_i} \]

for a voter in constituency \(i\). In a nonatomic constituency, every individual has zero pivotal probability. Replacing this by \(\pi_i/a_i\) is a reasonable normalized influence density, but it is an additional modelling choice, not the limit of the paper’s pivotal event. It turns the question into “can a finite council realize a target Shapley vector?” The bottom-tier continuum has then disappeared from the computational object.

The best rescue is to retain common constituency shocks and a continuum of repeated offset types. That does produce a coherent high-multiplicity model, and it is evidence that the proposed inverse-Shapley problem is mathematically sensible. But its solution is still governed entirely by the finite coalition structure of the \(m\) constituencies. The common shock supplies an exogenous random ordering; it does not create a continuous-population optimization problem. This may be a worthwhile separate project, but it is not a computational mirror of the paper’s named theorem.

The other natural anchors fare worse. Theorem 1 takes \(m\to\infty\), not the number of citizens per constituency. Continuizing the representatives makes each delegate measure-zero and destroys the finite Shapley pivot event; retaining finite \(m\) leaves the population continuum computationally inert. Corollary 2’s square-root rule relies precisely on finite-sample median variance of order \(1/\sqrt{n_i}\), which vanishes under high multiplicity. Preserving it requires adding sampling noise or finite blocks, rather than taking the population continuum seriously.

So the honest negative conclusion is narrow but decisive: the paper may motivate a new inverse-Shapley design problem, and that problem could be worth studying. It does not, however, supply a computational result whose continuous-population mirror belongs to ChoCo’s programme. I cannot honestly claim that no imaginable reformulation could ever be useful; I can claim that none of the proposed anchors survives the programme’s computational anchor test.
