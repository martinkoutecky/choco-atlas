The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete or truncated rankings of \(C\) candidates, a seat count \(m\), and a fixed tie-breaking rule, use WIGM with normalized quota \(q=1/(m+1)\). What is the minimum \(\frac{1}{2}\sum_r|\mu(r)-\nu(r)|\) over ranking distributions \(\nu\) whose WIGM winner set differs from that of \(\mu\)?

The model it lives in

Ranking types \(r\) carry population masses \(\mu(r)\); an alternative society has masses \(\nu(r)\). The objective is their total variation distance, and the decision rule is WIGM with fractional ballot mass and normalized quota \(1/(m+1)\).

The objection that survived

Theorem 1 validates a supplied graph but gives no guarantee on the time needed to construct one or find the best certificate; this limits what the theorem establishes about solving the exact continuous margin problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the \(\mathrm{MoV}\) lower-bound certificate and winner-stability question. It leaves the paper’s finite-ballot RLA sampling tests, mismatch and delta methods, and empirical sample-size results unchanged.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of minimizing the total variation distance as a function of \(C\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length?
  • Can coherent, weakly secure audit graphs be found efficiently, and how closely can their certified bounds approximate the exact continuous margin?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 1, stated in §3.1 and proved in Appendix B. It says that, provided \(M\leq q\), a coherent and weakly secure \(M\)-plausible audit graph certifies that the election’s margin of victory is greater than \(M/2\). Here “weakly secure” requires every edge leaving the graph to have plausibility threshold greater than \(M+2\sum_{w\in\mathcal W(v)}D_w\), where the \(D_w\) terms account for double-negative transfer mass. The paper uses this guarantee to compute lower bounds. It is a computationally relevant certificate theorem, though the paper gives no numbered result classifying a problem as polynomial-time or hard. The NP-hardness of deciding whether STV’s margin is \(1\), mentioned in §3.1, is cited from elsewhere and is not a numbered result of this paper, so I would treat it as context rather than a second anchor.

My lead mirror is Continuous STV Margin of Victory. An instance consists of \(C\) candidates, \(m\) seats, a fixed WIGM tie-breaking rule, and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete or truncated rankings. A type is a ranking; its mass is the fraction of the electorate casting it. For a population-level version of the rule, use the normalized Droop quota \(q=1/(m+1)\), and apply the paper’s Gregory transfer rule to real-valued ballot mass. Given a rational \(\varepsilon\), ask whether there is a distribution \(\nu\) over rankings with a different winner set from \(\mu\) and
\[ \frac12\sum_r |\mu(r)-\nu(r)|\leq\varepsilon. \]
A yes answer can supply such a \(\nu\); a no answer certifies that every population change within that total-variation radius leaves the winner set unchanged. The optimization version asks for the infimum such radius.

The connection to Theorem 1 is direct: normalize \(M\) by the electorate size to obtain a mass margin \(\delta\), and normalize the double-negative masses in the same way. The continuous question is whether a coherent, weakly secure \(\delta\)-plausible graph certifies a continuous margin greater than \(\delta/2\), and how hard it is to find the largest such certificate. The theorem’s proof tracks aggregate mass changes and transfer values; replacing counts by rational masses is a natural extension, but the paper itself proves the finite-profile statement. That extension should be established, not attributed to the authors.

A plausible regime is a large multi-seat election with many voters casting ballots from a relatively small set of shared slates or group voting tickets: \(N\) may be in the hundreds of thousands or millions while the number \(\tau\) of distinct rankings is far smaller. Voters with the same ranking are interchangeable for the tabulation and margin question. This mirror covers the paper’s margin-of-victory certificate and stability analysis. It does not continuize the RLA’s sampling and hypothesis tests: those still concern sampling individual paper ballots.

I would expect the mirror to be hard, with a Class B hypothesis as the first one to investigate: continuous mass removes voter-count granularity, but the branching over candidate eliminations and seatings remains. The paper’s cited hardness result makes that a worthwhile direction, but it does not establish hardness for this continuous problem; its reduction would have to survive fractional mass changes and the normalized quota. Further questions include whether the audit graph can be searched in time polynomial in \(C,\tau,L\), and how much its local, reset-per-round perturbation model overestimates the true continuous margin.

The soft spot is that the paper studies finite profiles and does not analyze this complexity question. The normalized quota is also a co-modification of the model: the finite Droop quota includes rounding and an additive ballot. I still think this mirror is plausible because it preserves the paper’s underlying problem—how much ballot mass must change to alter an STV winner set—and Theorem 1 supplies a certificate method for that exact stability question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole named anchor is Theorem 1, but it is not a complexity result. It proves a conditional certificate statement: given a coherent, weakly secure \(M\)-plausible graph, the election’s margin of victory exceeds \(M/2\). It gives no running time for constructing that graph, finding the largest useful \(M\), or computing the exact margin. The paper’s cited NP-hardness result is from other work. On the programme’s usual terms, then, the paper has no named complexity result to mirror.

A continuous version of Theorem 1 looks likely to be a direct extension of its proof. Normalize ballot counts by \(N\), use quota \(1/(m+1)\), and replace the number of changed ballots divided by \(N\) with total variation distance between ranking distributions. For empirical distributions, that distance is exactly the smallest fraction of ballots that must change to obtain the new distribution. The paper already writes its proof in terms of aggregate ranking mass and \(L^1\) distance, so the certificate’s soundness argument appears to transfer with normalized double-negative mass. That would validate a certificate, but it would not itself chart the complexity of finding one. The audit’s sampling and hypothesis tests, meanwhile, still concern finite paper ballots.

That is the strongest negative point, but it does not defeat the better mirror. In a large STV election with many voters sharing a relatively small set of rankings, the exact continuous margin question is coherent: what is the least total variation change to the ranking distribution that changes the winner set? The type is a complete ballot ranking, and the quantity has a clear interpretation as a fraction of the electorate. A positive normalized quota and the sequential elimination and seating decisions leave a substantive robustness problem in the limit. Nothing in the paper shows that this continuous problem has already been answered.

One can object that exact continuous margin is broader than Theorem 1’s certificate guarantee. But it is still the paper’s own margin-of-victory question, and the theorem is presented as a way to obtain lower bounds on that quantity. The graph’s reset-per-round perturbations may overapproximate a single global change; that limits the certificate, but it does not make the exact continuous margin ill-posed or uninteresting.

So the negative case is narrow: the proponent overstates Theorem 1 as a computational result, and its continuous certificate analogue may be straightforward. I cannot honestly extend that objection to the exact continuous STV margin problem. The large-election, few-ranking-types regime is plausible, and this text gives no basis for claiming that its complexity is already settled. The universal claim that there is no worthwhile continuous mirror is therefore weak on the material provided.
