The continuous mirror question

For a finite-support society \(\mu\) over locations \(x\in\mathbb{R}\) on a road, with binary-rational masses, consider deterministic rules \(M\) that place a facility in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Require that for every type \(x\), every movable mass \(\eta\leq \mu_x\), and every finite-support report distribution \(\rho\) of mass \(\eta\), \(\|M(\mu-\eta\delta_x+\rho)-(x,0)\| \geq \|M(\mu)-(x,0)\|\). What is the infimum, over such cohort-strategyproof rules, of the worst-case ratio between maximum distance to the support and the smallest enclosing-circle radius of the support?

The model it lives in

Types are road locations \(x\in\mathbb{R}\); the society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) with binary-rational masses. A rule \(M\) chooses one point in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), and a cohort of mass \(\eta\) at \(x\) may move its reports to any finite-support distribution of the same mass. The objective is to minimize the worst-case ratio of maximum support distance to the support’s smallest enclosing-circle radius.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the deterministic augmented-line mechanism and its approximation guarantee in Theorem 4.1, with Theorem 4.3 supplying the lower-bound bridge; it leaves the randomized, higher-dimensional, and circle-setting results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the infimum remain \(\sqrt{2}\) over all deterministic cohort-strategyproof rules on finite-support societies?

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly under ChoCo’s complexity-theoretic anchor rule, this paper has no qualifying result: it gives no runtime bound or classification such as polynomial-time solvability or NP-hardness. Its closest computational results are mechanism-design theorems about strategyproofness and approximation. The best positive mirror is therefore a mechanism-synthesis question, not a new complexity classification.

Take a road, with a facility allowed anywhere in the plane. A type is a location \(x\in\mathbb Q\) on the road; people of that type have the same Euclidean cost and the same available reports. The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over locations, with positive binary-rational masses and support size \(\tau\). A deterministic mechanism maps \(\mu\) to a facility \(M(\mu)\in\mathbb R^2\). Its egalitarian cost is
\[ \max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}\left\|M(\mu)-(x,0)\right\|, \]
and the benchmark is the smallest enclosing-circle radius of the support.

For incentives, let a co-located cohort of mass \(\delta\leq\mu(\{x\})\) move its reports from \(x\) to any distribution \(\rho\) of the same mass. Require that the cohort cannot lower its distance from its true location:
\[ \left\|M(\mu-\delta\delta_x+\rho)-(x,0)\right\| \geq \left\|M(\mu)-(x,0)\right\|. \]
This is a population-scale version of the paper’s argument that a group sharing one true location cannot profit by jointly changing its reports.

The plausible regime is many road users served by one antenna, with residents grouped by location or service zone: \(n\) is large while \(\tau\) is modest. Mass means a fraction of the users in each zone. The facility remains a single point, and the objective remains worst-resident distance. This makes the population continuous without making the outcome space the object of continuization.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper: for any deterministic strategyproof mechanism in the augmented-line setting with \(n\geq2\), approximation ratio \(\lambda\) must satisfy \(\lambda\geq\sqrt2\). The continuous question is: *what is the infimum approximation ratio over deterministic cohort-strategyproof mechanisms defined on all such \(\mu\)?* Its lower-bound side has a direct bridge: restrict to distributions \(\tfrac12\delta_a+\tfrac12\delta_b\). Moving one type’s half-mass corresponds to one of two agents changing their report, so the paper’s two-agent lower-bound argument applies.

Theorem 4.1, also proved here, supplies the matching constructive anchor: the Augmented Midpoint Mechanism is strategyproof and achieves ratio \(\sqrt2\). Its distribution version takes \(l=\min\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\) and \(r=\max\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\), then returns
\[ \left(\frac{l+r}{2},\frac{r-l}{2}\right). \]
It uses only the support extremes, so it can be evaluated in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the coordinate bit length. The paper’s co-located-group argument supports the cohort incentive condition, and the mechanism’s worst-case ratio is unchanged by fractional masses.

Together these anchors pose a precise continuous mechanism-design problem: construct a rule with ratio at most \(\sqrt2\), and determine whether any rule can do better. I expect the answer to mirror the discrete result: the lower bound transfers from the embedded two-agent case, and the endpoint rule attains it. In the programme’s trichotomy this resembles a hardness-transfer result, though the underlying claim is an approximation barrier, not computational hardness. Further questions include whether randomization can improve the ratio without translation and scale-invariance assumptions, and whether analogous mass models work for higher-dimensional input domains.

The case covers only deterministic facility placement for agents on a line with output in the plane. It does not mirror the paper’s other randomized or circle results. Its weakest point is the incentive model: a single infinitesimal resident cannot affect a distribution, so individual strategyproofness there would be vacuous. The cohort condition gives mass a strategic role and is supported by the paper’s co-located-deviation argument, but authors might regard it as a different mechanism-design question. The result is a credible continuous-population analogue; it is not evidence that this paper fills ChoCo’s complexity-theory gap.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The cleanest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper proves no computational complexity result. Its theorems bound the approximation ratios of strategyproof mechanisms and establish incentive properties. They do not classify an algorithmic problem by running time, or give an algorithm for computing a solution to one. A continuous-population version could be a sensible mechanism-design question, but it would not fill the computational gap ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 4.3 is mathematically credible. Given a mechanism on distributions, restricting it to \(\tfrac12\delta_a+\tfrac12\delta_b\) recovers a two-agent mechanism: moving one half of the mass corresponds to one agent changing their report. The lower bound therefore transfers. But the resulting question—what is the best approximation ratio among cohort-strategyproof rules—is a frontier over mechanisms, not a computational problem on society inputs. The theorem has no encoded class of mechanisms whose synthesis could be analyzed for complexity. Add such a class and an encoding, and the question becomes a new, more specified research problem.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 4.1 has the same gap. The rule on a finite-support distribution reads its minimum and maximum locations and returns the augmented midpoint. That is easy to compute from the support. Its substantive result is that this rule is strategyproof and has approximation ratio \(\sqrt{2}\), not a complexity guarantee. The paper’s co-located-agent argument gives real support for the proposed cohort incentive condition, so I would not reject the model as incoherent. It still asks about incentive design, rather than the complexity of a computational task.

The paper’s asymptotic lower bound in Theorem 3.1 is an even closer population analogue: its proof already takes populations to infinity and spreads a cluster over a sphere. A continuous formulation could make that limit explicit. That closeness strengthens the case that continuous populations are natural for these mechanism questions; it does not supply the missing computational question.

So the strongest negative claim is deliberately narrow: there is no worthwhile ChoCo complexity mirror here. The proponent has identified a coherent continuous mechanism-design extension, and it may be worth studying in a programme that includes mechanism synthesis. The case against is weak if “worthwhile” includes that broader agenda. It is strong only under ChoCo’s stated focus on the complexity and algorithms of computational problems.
