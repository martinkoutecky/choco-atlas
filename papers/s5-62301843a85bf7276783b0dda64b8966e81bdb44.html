The strongest adjacent mirror I can see is a new computational question about the median objective the paper defines. Let \(G=(V,E)\) be a finite median graph, and let each type \(v\in V\) represent agents with the same ideal location. The instance is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a support of \(\tau\) vertices; choose a facility location \(z\in V\) minimizing \(\sum_{v\in V}\mu_v d(z,v)\). A plausible regime is a city with many residents but relatively few ideal-location types. This keeps the outcome discrete while making the population continuous, and it closely matches the paper’s median-location objective. On an explicitly given finite graph, evaluating the objective at each vertex gives a polynomial-time solution.

That is a concrete, plausible population mirror of the paper’s underlying optimization problem, but it is not anchored in a named computational result: the paper studies axiomatic characterizations of the median function, not the complexity of computing it. Its axiom-independence results also fall outside the programme’s computational scope. My positive case therefore stops at the naturalness of this adjacent model; the lack of an eligible result is its decisive weakness.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the proponent names no computational result in the paper to mirror. Theorems 4 and 5 characterize the median function by axioms; Theorems 6–10 concern implications and independence examples; Theorem 15 is also an axiomatic result. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational problem. That is a decisive problem for an *anchored* ChoCo case.

The proposed city model is the best rescue: let residents share ideal locations, give each location a rational mass, and minimize weighted total distance. That is a coherent high-multiplicity problem, and the paper’s median objective makes it natural. But it is an adjacent problem, not a continuous counterpart of one of the paper’s results. For rational masses, choosing a common denominator turns the objective into the paper’s finite-profile objective, scaled by a positive constant. This gives a useful support-based formulation, but the paper proves no computational claim about it for continuization to refine. I cannot rule out that the formulation is worth studying on its own.

The paper’s actual results are global statements about which axioms characterize a location function, and examples showing which axioms can be independent. Recasting those as computational tasks would require a new problem and a representation of the location function; changing the population to masses does not supply either. That would be a new computational project, rather than a mirror of the paper’s contribution.

So the negative case is strong at the programme’s anchoring test: there is no named computational result here to continuize. It is weak as the broader claim that *no* worthwhile continuous question can arise in any scenario. The median-location model is plausible; I would simply not count it as a mirror of this paper.
