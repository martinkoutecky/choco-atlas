The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) candidates and a hidden unit-mass society over strict rankings \(\pi\), restricted to Plackett–Luce masses \(\mu_\pi=\prod_{r=1}^{n}\theta_{\pi(r)}/\sum_{s=r}^{n}\theta_{\pi(s)}\), where \(1/C\leq\theta_i\leq1\) and \(\max_i\theta_i=1\), an oracle accepts an \(l\)-candidate query, samples a voter independently from the society, and returns that voter’s highest-ranked queried candidate. What is the minimum worst-case number of adaptive queries needed to output, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), a permutation \(\Pi\) such that every pair with \(\Pi(i)<\Pi(j)\) satisfies \(\theta_i\geq\theta_j-\epsilon\)?

The model it lives in

A hidden continuous population of voter types, with each type a complete candidate ranking and mass given by the Plackett–Luce distribution induced by the paper’s unknown item scores. The learner adaptively chooses candidate sets, receives an independently sampled voter’s top choice in each set, and minimizes query count while producing an \(\epsilon\)-ranking with failure probability at most \(\delta\).

The objection that survived

The society is hidden behind a sampling oracle, so these theorems do not answer the canonical ChoCo task with support and rational masses supplied as input; this mirror depends on treating oracle query complexity as in scope.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the PAC total-ranking query lower bound in Theorem 3 and PDTR’s query guarantee in Theorem 5. It leaves the top-\(k\) results, experiments, and other problems outside the paper’s scope of query-based ranking untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do the matching query bounds extend to hidden ranking populations whose choice probabilities only approximately follow the multinomial logit model?
  • How do the guarantees change when queries sample without replacement from a finite electorate?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is an exact population interpretation of the paper’s query model. It covers the PAC total-ranking results, and its reach is specifically query complexity.

Take the \(n\) items as candidates shown to a large electorate. A voter type is a complete strict ranking \(\pi\) of those candidates. Give the unit-mass society the Plackett–Luce distribution induced by the paper’s item scores \(\theta\):
\[ \mu_\pi=\prod_{r=1}^{n}\frac{\theta_{\pi(r)}}{\sum_{s=r}^{n}\theta_{\pi(s)}}. \]
For each query, the learner chooses a set \(S\) of \(l\) candidates and samples a voter independently from this society; the voter returns their highest-ranked candidate in \(S\). The Plackett–Luce distribution restricted to \(S\) gives
\[ \Pr(i\text{ is returned}\mid S)=\frac{\theta_i}{\sum_{j\in S}\theta_j}, \]
exactly the paper’s MNL response probability. Thus the paper’s item scores are the population-level strengths whose ranking the learner seeks.

Continuous-population PAC Total Ranking (CPAC-TR), lead. The instance specifies \(n,l,\epsilon,\delta\), a fixed RBC constant \(C\), and sampling access to a hidden society \(\mu\) of the form above, with \(1/C\leq\theta_i\leq1\) and \(\max_i\theta_i=1\). An adaptive strategy chooses \(l\)-candidate queries and observes sampled voters’ choices. It must output a permutation \(\Pi\) such that, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), every pair with \(\Pi(i)<\Pi(j)\) satisfies \(\theta_i\geq\theta_j-\epsilon\). Its objective is to minimize the number of queries. For rational \(\theta\), the type masses are rational too.

The regime is a population of many users or voters considering a modest candidate set, with repeated ranking types. With \(n\) fixed, the possible type support is at most \(\tau=n!\); a population much larger than \(\tau\) makes the mass description natural. For example, a large electorate ranking a small field of candidates could fit this regime. The users have fixed rankings; the query randomness comes from sampling a member of the population.

Two numbered results anchor this same problem:

  • Theorem 3, “Lower bound for the total ranking problem.” For \(\epsilon\in(0,\sqrt{1/32}]\), \(\delta\in(0,1/4)\), and \(2\leq l\leq n\), the paper states that there is an instance on which every algorithm returning a correct \(\epsilon\)-ranking with error probability at most \(\delta\) needs \(\Omega\!\left(\frac{n}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)\) \(l\)-wise queries in expectation. The theorem is proved here by reduction; its proof uses a bandit lower bound cited from elsewhere. Since the population model reproduces every query’s response distribution, the same lower bound applies to CPAC-TR.
  • Theorem 5, “Theoretical performance of PDTR.” The paper proves that, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), PDTR terminates after \(O\!\left(\frac{n}{\alpha^2\epsilon^2}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)\) \(l\)-wise queries and returns a correct \(\epsilon\)-ranking if \(\alpha\leq\frac{l-1}{4(l+C-1)}\). For fixed \(C\), \(\alpha\) can be chosen as a positive constant, giving an \(O\!\left(\frac{n}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac{n}{\delta}\right)\) query bound. The theorem is proved in the paper. It gives a high-probability upper bound matching Theorem 3’s order of growth.

I expect this mirror to be tractable in query complexity: the paper’s upper bound carries over exactly, and its lower bound marks the cost of eliciting a ranking from this population. The mirror covers Theorems 3 and 5, not the paper’s top-\(k\) results or any bribery or control problem. It generates further questions: how far the bounds extend beyond Plackett–Luce societies, whether approximate MNL choice probabilities preserve them, and how finite-population sampling changes the guarantees.

The weakest point is the access model. The learner receives a sampling oracle for an unknown society, while ChoCo’s central model normally gives the society as an explicit support-and-mass input. If the full \(\mu\) were handed to the learner, these query-complexity results would no longer be the same problem. Also, these anchors establish sample complexity, not NP-hardness or polynomial-time running time. The case rests on treating adaptive PAC elicitation from a population as a computational question the authors would recognize; it is a faithful population mirror of their stated objective, with that interface caveat made explicit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The PL construction is mathematically sound, and it rules out a simple objection that a voter type is not well defined. It also gives the strongest case against this mirror: for every adaptive query, the paper’s MNL response law is exactly the law obtained by drawing a voter from the proposed PL society and asking for their top choice in the queried set. Theorem 3’s lower bound and Theorem 5’s PDTR guarantee therefore already hold for that continuous sampling model, with the same instances, transcripts, and query counts. On this interpretation, the mirror adds a population story to a problem the paper has already solved; it does not create a new continuous computational question.

The paper’s theorems concern uncertainty about latent item scores when the learner has a sampling oracle. They do not take the society’s support and masses as input or compute an intervention on those masses. If the mirror instead uses ChoCo’s explicit society input, the learner’s information is different, and Theorems 3 and 5 no longer bound the same query problem. That is a change of problem interface, not a more faithful version of the paper’s PAC guarantees. Theorems 3 and 5 thus do not provide separate surviving anchors: each either transfers unchanged to the already-solved PL sampling model or ceases to describe the explicit-mass problem.

The strongest proposed repair is to keep the society hidden and make its sampled users the query oracle. That is a plausible large-population scenario, but it preserves the exact MNL experiment rather than making population multiplicity a computational input. The paper’s parameter \(n\) counts items; no theorem varies the number of voters or the number of copies of each type. Extending the model to arbitrary hidden societies could create new questions, but these theorems would not answer them.

This is a limited negative case. If ChoCo counts oracle sample complexity as an in-scope continuous-population result, the proposed mirror is coherent and useful, and the objection weakens substantially. I do not see a convincing argument that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario; the strongest case is that the exact PL version is already covered by the paper’s own guarantees, while the explicit-mass version is a different problem.
