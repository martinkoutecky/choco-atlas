What the mirror covers

No continuous mirror is anchored; the accounting and welfare guarantees, query protocol, and experimental comparisons have no stated population-continuum counterpart.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor a mirror on. Its numbered results are Proposition 1, a portfolio-complementarity identity; Lemma 1, a report-accounting guarantee; and Proposition 2, a demand-query welfare certificate. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise classifies its complexity. Algorithm 1 and the experimental tables do not supply such a named result either.

So there are no qualifying anchors, and I can’t give the requested one-problem-per-anchor case without treating a welfare or accounting result as a complexity result. The paper does suggest a plausible continuous-population setting—a large crossing pool of accounts with shared mandates and valuation primitives—but that possibility is not supported by a qualifying named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no anchor, and the paper supplies none that meets the stated test. Proposition 1 is an algebraic identity about quadratic values; Lemma 1 is a report-accounting guarantee; Proposition 2 is a welfare bound. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Algorithm 1 and the experiments describe a procedure and measure welfare recovery, but they give no runtime, hardness, or approximation result. So there is no named computational result here to mirror.

The strongest replacement candidate is the report-based allocation problem in equations (13)–(16). But its natural population mirror would itself need substantial new modeling. A complete investor type must include the value parameters and feasible trade set that determine demand and package values. The paper’s participants have private shocks, so grouping them by broad profile alone would discard relevant differences. One could instead posit large cohorts with identical mandates and values, then aggregate their package choices into masses. That is a plausible research question, but it changes the paper’s setting: the original query budget and reports are per participant, while the cohort model must specify whether queries go to each account, once per type, or to sampled members.

That proposed study could be worthwhile. It would ask how a population of repeated investor types affects query design or the complexity of aggregate allocation. But it would be a new computational contribution built from the paper’s model, not a continuous mirror of one of its named computational results. The negative case is strongest on that narrower point. It cannot establish that no worthwhile continuous-population project could grow out of this paper; it can establish that the proponent has not identified a surviving anchor, and the paper itself offers little basis for claiming a mirror of its stated results.
