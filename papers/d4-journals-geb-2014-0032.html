What the mirror covers

The closest extension applies Proposition 2 to a distribution of anonymous pairs; it leaves the other theoretical propositions and the experimental results without a continuous computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered propositions are results about implementation, equilibrium behavior, uniqueness, and behavioral models. None classifies an algorithmic problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized; the paper gives no computational problem whose complexity it analyzes.

The closest result is Proposition 2, proved in the paper: in the two-player linear public-good game, each player chooses \(s_i\in[0,w]\), then approves or disapproves the other’s choice. Under MAM, approval by both makes each contribute their chosen amount; any disapproval makes both contribute \(\min(s_1,s_2)\). Proposition 2 says that MAM implements the symmetric Pareto-efficient outcome under BEWDS. This is a substantive anchor for a mechanism-design extension, but it is not a computational result of the kind the requested mirror must rest on.

The best positive scenario I can construct is a large population of residents who are anonymously paired to fund local public goods, with a small number of complete types—for example, shared endowment and public-good valuation. Represent the society by rational type masses \(\mu_t\). A possible extension would ask: given those masses and a distribution of pairings, compute the aggregate BEWDS outcomes when each pair uses the paper’s MAM, including the heterogeneous-type version described by the authors. Its likely direction is tractable by analyzing each type pair and aggregating. The authors might recognize this as an extension of their mechanism.

That case remains weak for ChoCo. Pairwise MAM analysis is already the paper’s two-player problem, so the population mass may add only bookkeeping. The paper’s continuity is in contribution choices; its society remains two players. A genuinely many-agent version would require additional modeling and would not inherit Proposition 2 automatically.

So the honest positive case is a plausible high-multiplicity extension, not a continuous computational mirror anchored in a named complexity result. Under the requested anchor rule, there is no qualifying anchor to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–5 concern implementation, equilibrium paths, and uniqueness of a mechanism; Propositions 6–9 characterize behavioral models. The experimental results are also not complexity results. None poses an algorithmic problem with an input whose complexity or solution method the paper studies.

Proposition 2 is the proponent’s best anchor, and there is a sensible high-multiplicity setting for it: many anonymous pairs, with finitely many types defined by endowment and public-good valuation, randomly matched to play MAM. The paper itself says its implementation argument extends to heterogeneous endowments and preferences, and discusses \(n\)-person variants. That supports the plausibility of a population regime; it is not an objection to it.

But the proposed question—compute aggregate BEWDS outcomes from the type masses—leaves each pair’s strategic problem exactly as the paper already analyzes it. Given the pair-type masses, it aggregates those pairwise outcomes. That is an extension of Proposition 2’s application, not a computational problem the paper poses. A marginal type distribution alone would not even specify correlated pairings; supplying a pairing distribution resolves that, but still leaves only aggregation.

A stronger computational formulation might ask for the least mass of agents whose preferences or behavioral types must change to reach a target provision level, or for an optimal assignment of types to pairs. Those are recognizable control or matching questions, but they add intervention costs or assignment choices absent from the paper. The MAM implementation result does not motivate a particular version or supply its computational structure. They could be worthwhile projects inspired by this mechanism; they are not continuous mirrors of a result in this paper.

The negative case is strongest against the paper as a ChoCo target, not against every conceivable related project. A single large public-good group would require an \(n\)-person approval rule; the paper’s two-player result does not define one, though its discussion makes that extension plausible. So the paper gives good evidence that high-multiplicity public-good models are sensible, but no anchored computational question. If a fresh control or assignment problem counts as a mirror, the universal “no worthwhile mirror” claim is harder to defend; under the programme’s computational-result anchor, the case against stands.
