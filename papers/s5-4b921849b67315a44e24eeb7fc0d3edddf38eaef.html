The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no qualifying computational anchor. Its numbered results are convergence and probability statements: Theorem III.6 gives the almost-sure limit of the log-linear beliefs; Lemma IV.4 gives the single-agent asymptotic uncertain likelihood ratio; Corollary IV.4.1 treats infinite prior evidence; and Theorem VI.4 establishes learning for the DeGroot rule. None classifies the complexity of a problem or proves an algorithmic tractability or hardness result. I can’t present any of them as a result that puts a problem in P, proves it hard, or otherwise charts its computational complexity.

There is a plausible population regime behind an *unanchored extension*: many sensor agents fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of types, each with the same signal model and prior evidence, and interact through connected, doubly stochastic networks. Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), prior counts \(r_{t\theta}\), and signal probabilities \(p_{tk,\theta^*}\) under the true state. Theorem III.6’s finite-population limit suggests asking for an additive-\(\varepsilon\) approximation to the log consensus score
\[ z_\theta(\mu)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\log\!\left( \frac{B(\mathbf 1)}{B(r_{t\theta}+\mathbf 1)} \prod_{k=1}^{K}p_{tk,\theta^*}^{\,r_{tk\theta}} \right). \]
The instance would give \(\theta^*\), a candidate \(\theta\), and the rational masses, counts, and probabilities; the solution would be an \(\varepsilon\)-accurate score, or a threshold decision with a promised gap. This looks tractable in the support size and input bit length, since it reduces to evaluating a finite weighted sum.

That is the strongest positive lead I can make, but it does not satisfy the required anchor condition: the paper does not study this computational problem or prove its tractability. The likely weakness is also substantive: computing the limit formula would turn the paper’s analytic convergence result into a finite-support evaluation task, rather than continue a computational problem the authors actually posed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s strongest apparent anchor is Theorem III.6, but it gives an asymptotic formula, not a computational problem. Lemma IV.4 and Corollary IV.4.1 likewise characterize limits; the DeGroot results characterize convergence. None asks for an algorithm to choose an action or solve an optimization problem, or establishes a complexity bound.

The proposed score \(z_\theta(\mu)\) is a faithful population-weighted restatement of Theorem III.6, but evaluating it is just summing the per-type limit values. It involves no combinatorial search or unresolved optimization. There is also an operational mismatch: the formula uses the true state’s signal probabilities, which the agents are trying to learn. Supplying those probabilities makes the calculation an ex-post summary; evaluating it for each possible true state gives a table of hypothetical summaries. Either way, the theorem already supplies the quantity to compute.

A stronger proposal might ask how much the type distribution must change to push a hypothesis below an acceptance threshold. In the log-linear case, the limit score is linear in the masses, so this reduces to a direct calculation or a small linear program. Adding constraints on which populations can change could make the optimization harder, but those constraints would introduce a new intervention or attack problem that this paper does not formulate. For DeGroot aggregation, the paper does not even characterize the finite-evidence limit that such a problem would need to optimize.

A high-multiplicity sensor scenario is plausible: many sensors could share the same signal model and prior evidence. That makes a continuous population a sensible description, but it does not supply the missing computational question. In a well-mixed network, the proposed aggregate score is especially simple. In a general network, preserving the interactions requires carrying a network or influence structure alongside the type masses; that is a substantial new model, not a complexity mirror of the results here.

So the case against a worthwhile ChoCo mirror is strong for this paper: its results are analytic convergence results, and the closest population-weighted computation is immediate. The weak point is the universal claim. Plausible sensor populations exist, and a new resource-allocation or robustness problem could be built around them. But that would be a research question added to the paper, not a computational mirror of one of its results.
