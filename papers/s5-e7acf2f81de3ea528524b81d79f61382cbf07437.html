The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give this paper a qualifying anchor: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a complexity result. The nearest statement is Section 4’s attribution to Pérez and De la Cruz [2]: under the paper’s voting assumptions, the Jefferson–d’Hondt allocation is the only allocation method supported by a Nash equilibrium. That is neither a numbered result of this paper nor a complexity claim. The paper’s own findings about which Madrid votes followed an equilibrium are empirical comparisons, not results about whether a problem is in P or hard.

A plausible *prospective* mirror, though, is a continuous Bureau equilibrium problem. Take \(k\) caucuses with rational masses \(\mu_j\), where \(\sum_j\mu_j=1\), and a committee with \(q\) seats. Give each caucus \(q\) equivalent candidate slots. Caucus \(j\) chooses vote masses \(x_{ji}\ge 0\) over all slots, with \(\sum_i x_{ji}=\mu_j\). Slot \(i\)’s score is \(\sum_j x_{ji}\); the \(q\) highest-scoring slots win, with ties broken uniformly. Each caucus’s payoff is its expected number of winning slots. The question is: given the masses and \(q\), find a rational pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, or determine that none exists. Here the “agents” could be many delegates in a federation or union congress, divided into a small number of caucuses with shared voting discipline; mass is each caucus’s share of delegates. Seats remain indivisible. The continuous object is the voting population.

This is a recognizable extension of the paper’s coordinated-group voting model, but it is not anchored to a named result in the paper, so it cannot supply the requested one-question-per-anchor case. Under the cited equilibrium assumptions, I would expect the single-ballot allocation question to be tractable through the d’Hondt characterization. Extending it to the whole staged appointment process raises further questions about sequential incentives, coalition formation, and the four-member group cap. The authors themselves identify dynamic equilibrium analysis as future work.

The weak point is decisive for the requested case: the paper studies a small, specific assembly and reports empirical allocations; it does not state a computational problem or prove a complexity result. The caucus scenario makes a population limit plausible, but it supports a new computational study rather than a mirror of one of this paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Its findings concern observed Madrid votes and counterfactual d’Hondt allocations, including calculations of disproportionality. It does not formulate a parameterized computational problem or establish its complexity. The d’Hondt–Nash-equilibrium characterization in Section 4 is attributed to earlier work [2]; this paper applies it empirically. Turning those findings into a complexity result would change the paper’s contribution, not continuize it.

The strongest proposed mirror is an equilibrium problem in which caucuses of masses \(\mu_j\) divide their votes among candidates. But that is a new algorithmic question, not a computational result of this paper. It also exposes a modeling mismatch: in the paper, parliamentary groups are the strategic players, and their leaders direct the members’ votes. Replacing group sizes by masses leaves those groups as the players; fractional vote allocations change their action space, rather than making the strategic population continuous. Treat individual delegates as negligible strategic voters instead, and the paper’s coordinated group game and payoff assumptions no longer specify the game. Studying sequential incentives or the group-size cap would likewise be a new extension.

A direct mass version of the paper’s allocation and disproportionality calculations is possible, but it would still be an empirical or apportionment analysis, not a mirror of a named computational result. This makes the negative case strong against treating this article as an anchor for the programme. It is weaker as a universal claim: the article could motivate a valuable new study of continuous caucus voting, but that would be a new research direction rather than a continuous mirror of this paper.
