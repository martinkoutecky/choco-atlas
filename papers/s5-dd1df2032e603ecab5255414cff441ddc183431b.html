Why no mirror

What the mirror covers

The proposed model extends the GFJ allocation objective to workload types; it does not cover the paper’s fairness axioms, survey, or other allocation properties.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the mass-weighted GFJ optimum be computed efficiently for rational type masses and resource requirements?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive signal is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It studies \(n\) users whose resource requirements are drawn from a uniform distribution and gives probability limits for the optimal values of FDS and GFJ as \(n\) grows: for FDS with \((\beta,\lambda)=(\infty,-1)\), the asymptotic dominant share is \(\frac{2m}{n(m+1)}\); for GFJ with the same parameters, the asymptotic number of jobs per user is \(\frac{2}{\nu n}\). That is unusually direct evidence that a large population described by a distribution of resource demands is a sensible regime for these authors.

A plausible mirror would take \(\tau\) workload types, each with resource requirements \(R_{ia}\), rational population mass \(\mu_a\), and per-capita resource supplies \(C_i\). Choose \(x_a\), the jobs allocated per member of type \(a\), subject to \(\sum_a \mu_a R_{ia}x_a\le C_i\). For the GFJ version, maximize the paper’s fairness function with its sums replaced by mass-weighted sums—for example, \(\operatorname{sgn}(1-\beta)\bigl(\sum_a\mu_a(x_a/X)^{1-\beta}\bigr)^{1/\beta}X^\lambda\), where \(X=\sum_a\mu_a x_a\). A plausible setting is a large cloud service with many tenants in a modest number of recurring workload classes. Mass is the share of tenants in each class; the decision is jobs per tenant. For the isoelastic GFJ parameters \(\beta>0\), \(\lambda=(1-\beta)/\beta\), I would expect a tractable convex-optimization formulation. This would cover the GFJ allocation objective, not the paper’s survey or its axiomatic claims.

But Proposition 2 is not a qualifying computational anchor under your rules: it proves asymptotic allocation values, not that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The other numbered propositions characterize allocation properties and tradeoffs; the paper gives no named computational-complexity result. So this is a plausible, author-recognizable continuous-population optimization question, but I cannot honestly present it as a mirror of a qualifying anchor. The weak point is that the paper already makes jobs and resources divisible; the mirror’s distinct move must be to make the population itself a distribution over complete resource-demand types.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the proponent’s only named anchor, and it is not a computational result. It proves convergence of optimal fairness values for randomly generated users as \(n\) grows. Propositions 1 and 3–8 characterize allocations and fairness properties. The paper states no complexity bound, algorithmic classification, or hardness result for any of these problems. Under ChoCo’s scope, there is therefore no computational result here to mirror.

The proposed workload-class model is sensible: assign each class a rational mass, scale resource supplies per capita, and choose jobs per member of each class. But that creates a new optimization question rather than continuing a computational result from this paper. The paper already optimizes over real-valued job allocations under linear resource constraints. Grouping identical users into types and rewriting the sums with masses changes the population description; it does not relax a discrete computational problem the authors studied. Its finite-type version would be a \(\tau\)-variable allocation model, not the kind of exponential-variable LP whose pricing problem drives many ChoCo mirrors.

Proposition 2 does give this proposal a nearby precedent: it already studies a large population through a random limit. That theorem is not a deterministic type-mass formulation, and it does not answer an algorithmic question, so it neither supplies the missing anchor nor establishes a collision.

The negative case is strongest as a reason to reject this paper as a *computational mirror*. It is weaker as the universal claim that no continuous-population project here could be worthwhile: the tenant-class scenario is credible, and its mass-weighted model could be useful as a separate extension. But that usefulness would not mirror a named computational result in this paper.
