The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set \(U\), a support \(T\) of coherent, complete preference lists \(\Y_t\), and positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), let \(f_t(A)\) be the first bundle on \(\Y_t\) contained in menu \(A\), and define \(F_\mu(A)_x=\sum_{t:\,x\in f_t(A)}\mu_t\). Decide whether \(F_\mu(B)_x\leq F_\mu(A)_x\) for every \(A\subseteq B\subseteq U\) and \(x\in A\); if not, return a violating \(A,B,x\).

The model it lives in

Each type is a complete chooser description \(\Y_t\); its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of departments of that type. Departments make integral bundle choices from a common menu, while the decision concerns whether aggregate choice mass for each alternative can rise when the menu expands.

The objection that survived

The aggregate condition differs from the individual-choice condition used in matching, and its institutional relevance depends on how common menus and total uptake are defined.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror extends the substitutability test to aggregate choice mass across supported types; it does not settle individual certification or the paper's single-list running-time bound for this aggregate problem.

Open questions for a prover

  • With explicit preference-list encoding, what is the complexity of deciding aggregate substitutability; is the problem coNP-hard?
  • Does fixing support size \(\tau\), or imposing a structural condition on the supported lists, yield an efficient algorithm?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a population-level extension of the paper’s substitutability test. A concrete setting is a large labor platform with many employer departments choosing bundles from the same finite menu \(U\) of standardized contract types. A type \(t\) is a department’s complete preference list \(\Y_t\) over bundles, so departments of the same type make identical choices. The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over the \(\tau\) supported lists: \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of departments with type \(t\). This is plausible when the number of departments is large and many share a list; each department still chooses an integral bundle, while total uptake of a contract type can be fractional.

My lead anchor is Corollary 1, proved in this paper. It says that the paper’s algorithm tests whether a given preference list of size \(N\) over a universe \(U\) is substitutable in \(O(|U|^2N^2)\) time; the algorithm also checks coherence and completeness, and \(N\) may be exponential in \(|U|\). The anchor is an algorithmic result, and the paper offers no named hardness result to prefer. Its relevance is direct: it studies whether expanding a menu can increase the choice of an existing alternative.

Lead mirror — Continuous Aggregate Substitutability Testing. An instance consists of a finite set \(U\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) coherent, complete preference lists \(\Y_t\) over subsets of \(U\), and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). For a menu \(A\subseteq U\), let \(f_t(A)\) be the first bundle on \(\Y_t\) contained in \(A\). Define the aggregate choice mass of alternative \(x\) by
\[ F_\mu(A)_x=\sum_{t\in T:\,x\in f_t(A)}\mu_t. \]
Decide whether, for every \(A\subseteq B\subseteq U\) and every \(x\in A\), \(F_\mu(B)_x\leq F_\mu(A)_x\). A “yes” instance has no violation. A “no” instance can be answered with menus \(A\subseteq B\) and an \(x\in A\) for which the inequality fails. Thus the alternatives and menus remain finite; the population is the continuous object.

I would provisionally expect this mirror to be Class C, with coNP-hardness a conjecture, not a claim proved by the paper. A violation has a short certificate: specify \(A,B,x\), evaluate each supported type’s choice, and compare the resulting rational masses. So the decision problem is in coNP under explicit-list encoding. The harder part is finding a violating pair: different types’ responses can offset each other, and the individual-list algorithm does not by itself resolve those cancellations. A useful next question is whether fixed \(\tau\), or a structural condition on the supported lists, makes aggregate testing tractable.

The scope is just the paper’s named substitutability-testing result, lifted to aggregate choice by a high-multiplicity population. The weakest point is that standard substitutability is usually a property of each chooser, whereas this mirror tests aggregate demand. Authors might prefer the typewise question—whether every positive-mass \(\Y_t\) is substitutable—which Corollary 1 would let one check list by list, but where the masses do no computational work. The aggregate version has a stronger population interpretation: it asks whether expanding a common menu can raise total uptake of an existing alternative across a society of heterogeneous choosers.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 1 is a real computational result, so the case against cannot rest on there being nothing to mirror. It tests whether one explicitly given preference list induces a substitutable choice function. But that object has one chooser. Making the list common to many identical choosers changes nothing: their individual choices remain integral, and the test is unchanged. Making the lists heterogeneous gives a distribution over individual tests; for example, the mass of choosers with substitutable lists is just the sum of the masses of the types that pass. Applying Corollary 1 to each supported type computes that exactly. This is a valid high-multiplicity description, but it adds no population-level computational question.

The proponent’s aggregate test is the more serious candidate. In a setting where many departments face the same menu, asking whether expanding it can increase total demand for an existing alternative is intelligible. But it changes the property being tested. The paper’s substitutability condition constrains what each chooser does; aggregate demand can rise or fall because different choosers’ responses offset one another. That aggregate condition is not the condition used to establish substitutability of employers in matching markets, where each employer’s choice function matters. Nor does the paper supply an institution in which departments’ choices combine into one collective choice function. The common-menu platform scenario makes the statistic interpretable, but it does not make aggregate substitutability the paper’s problem.

A stronger economic setting does not quite repair the mismatch. If the aim is to predict whether a larger menu increases total uptake, the aggregate test is a coherent comparative-statics question. Yet its relevance depends on how menus are assigned, whether departments’ choices interact through shared supply, and what “uptake” means for the institution. Once shared supply or coordination matters, independent applications of the paper’s choice functions no longer describe the mechanism. If choices really are independent, the exact test is a new property of a weighted mixture, not a population version of the individual axiom. It might be worth studying on its own; the paper gives little reason to prioritize it for this programme.

That leaves the negative case weaker than a proof that no mirror can be worthwhile. The aggregate problem is well-posed, and its computational complexity could be interesting. But the direct mirrors reduce to running the paper’s algorithm on each type, while the only version with nontrivial aggregate behavior changes the subject from individual choice substitutability to monotonicity of aggregate demand. On the evidence here, no continuous-population mirror of the paper’s central result is compelling enough to recommend.
