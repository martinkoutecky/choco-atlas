The continuous mirror question

Given a finite candidate set \(C\), supported voter types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\) and grade vectors \(v^t\), a committee size \(k\), and rational rank measures \(\nu^\pm=a_0^\pm\delta_0+a^\pm(q)\,dq\) with nonnegative nonincreasing step densities, choose \(S\subseteq C\), \(|S|=k\), maximizing \(G(S)=\int_{[0,1]}Q_S^+(q)\,d\nu^+(q)-\int_{[0,1]}Q_S^-(q)\,d\nu^-(q)\), where \(u_t(S)=\sum_{c\in S}v_c^t\), and \(Q_S^+\) and \(Q_S^-\) are the ascending quantile of \(\max(u_t(S),0)\) and descending quantile of \(\max(-u_t(S),0)\) under \(\mu\).

The model it lives in

Represent the society by rational masses \(\mu_t\) over complete grade-vector types. Use binary variables \(y_c\) with \(\sum_{c\in C}y_c=k\); each type’s satisfaction is \(u_t(y)=\sum_c v_c^t y_c\). Rank the positive and negative parts of these type-level satisfactions by their population mass, aggregate them with \(\nu^+\) and \(\nu^-\), and maximize the resulting biOWA score.

What the mirror covers

Covers the Section 4 biOWA multi-winner election problem and Proposition 3’s aggregation linearization; leaves the axiomatic results, robust path models, path-ranking algorithm, and numerical results outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize exact and parameterized complexity in \(m\), \(\tau\), input bit length, and \(k\), and identify rank-profile or ballot-domain restrictions that admit efficient algorithms.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a continuous biOWA multi-winner election. A voter’s type is their complete grade vector \(v^t\in\{-T,\ldots,T\}^m\) over the candidates. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over its \(\tau\) supported types, where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters of type \(t\). For a committee \(S\) of size \(k\), type \(t\)’s satisfaction remains exactly the paper’s additive utility, \(u_t(S)=\sum_{c\in S}v^t_c\).

To define the same rank-sensitive aggregation for a continuum of voters, give the instance two rank-weight profiles, \(\nu^+=a_0^+\delta_0+a^+(q)\,dq\) and \(\nu^-=a_0^-\delta_0+a^-(q)\,dq\). Each density is a rational, nonnegative, nonincreasing step function, and each profile may have an atom at rank \(0\). Let \(Q_S^+(q)\) be the ascending quantile of the positive parts of voter satisfaction under \(\mu\), and \(Q_S^-(q)\) the descending quantile of the negative parts. The committee’s score is

\[ G(S)=\int_{[0,1]} Q_S^+(q)\,d\nu^+(q) -\int_{[0,1]} Q_S^-(q)\,d\nu^-(q). \]

The C-biOWA-MWE problem is: given the supported ballot types and their rational masses, \(k\), and the two rank profiles, return a size-\(k\) committee maximizing \(G(S)\). Its decision version asks whether some size-\(k\) committee has score at least a given rational threshold. The population is the continuous object; the candidate set and committee remain discrete.

My named anchor is Proposition 3, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(x\in\mathbb{R}^n\) and nonnegative decreasing weight vectors \(\alpha,\beta\),

\[ g_{\alpha,\beta}(x) =\min_{\tau\in\Pi}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(\alpha_{\tau(i)}x_i^+-\beta_{\tau(i)}x_i^-\right), \]

where \(\Pi\) is the set of permutations of \(\{1,\ldots,n\}\). Thus one shared permutation suffices to express the biOWA value as a linear minimum. The paper computes with this result: it derives an LP for the aggregation and uses it to formulate a MIP for biOWA-MWE. This is a formulation anchor, rather than a numbered complexity theorem; the paper’s NP-hardness claim appears in prose.

The mirror preserves a direct finite-population dictionary. For \(N\) voters, take rank-bin weights \(\alpha_i=\nu^+(((i-1)/N,i/N])\) and \(\beta_i=\nu^-(((i-1)/N,i/N])\), assigning any atom at zero to the first bin. The continuous score then agrees with the finite biOWA score. Conversely, a finite nonincreasing weight vector can be represented on its \(N\) rank bins by a nonincreasing step density. So the quantile integral is a way to aggregate voter mass while keeping the paper’s ranking-based treatment of gains and losses; it does not replace biOWA with an ordinary average.

A plausible regime is a large membership organization choosing a fixed-size council, where members use a common graded ballot format and many submit the same candidate-grade vector. For example, \(N\) may be in the hundreds of thousands while only dozens or hundreds of distinct ballot types occur. In this model, the grade vector is a complete type: two members with the same vector are indistinguishable for every utility the problem uses. This is precisely the paper’s Section 4 question—choosing a committee to maximize voter satisfaction with special treatment of negative evaluations—with the voter histogram expressed in masses.

I expect the exact problem to be Class B, with hardness transferring. Take two ballot types of mass \(1/2\), nonnegative grades, \(\nu^+=\delta_0\), and \(\nu^-=0\). Then \(G(S)\) is the smaller of the two types’ total committee utilities. The finite counterpart is the valid decreasing OWA vector \(\alpha=(1,0,\ldots,0)\), which selects the minimum utility; repeating each type across an arbitrarily large electorate leaves that minimum-selection objective unchanged. The paper says its biOWA-MWE problem is NP-hard through the min-max, or minimum-selecting-items, subproblem, citing prior work that establishes hardness with two scenarios. That hardness concerns selecting candidates, not having a small electorate, so taking the population to a continuum does not remove it. The article has no numbered NP-hardness result to use as an anchor; Proposition 3 is the numbered result I rely on, while the hardness discussion informs my expected direction.

This leaves useful follow-up questions: which restricted weight profiles or ballot domains admit exact algorithms; whether parameters beyond support size, such as committee size or special candidate structure, yield tractability; and what approximation guarantees survive when the minimum-selection subcase is present. Support size alone is unlikely to help: hardness already arises with two types, barring \(P=NP\).

The weakest point is that the paper gives each finite electorate its own pair of rank-weight vectors, whereas the mirror specifies coherent continuum rank profiles. That is a real co-modification needed to define rank aggregation over voter mass. The core hardness example is less exposed to it: the atom at rank zero reproduces the paper’s permitted first-rank-only weight vector at every electorate size. The authors could reasonably recognize this as a continuous-population version of their election problem, while still questioning how broadly the chosen family of rank profiles represents their full finite model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best negative case is that Proposition 3 is a weak anchor for a computational-complexity programme. It proves a pointwise identity for a finite vector of voter utilities. Its continuous analogue is a rank-integral or coupling identity; with finitely many supported ballot types, evaluating that identity for a fixed committee amounts to sorting at most \(\tau\) utility values. The paper’s harder computation is selecting the committee, and the proposed mirror leaves that discrete decision intact. So the proposition itself gives little reason to expect a new algorithmic landscape.

That argument does not defeat the stronger mirror the proponent gives. A large membership organization with repeated graded ballots is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A grade vector is a complete voter type, and a rational distribution over those types is a sensible continuous society. Coherent rank profiles also give a recognizable mass version of the paper’s voter aggregation. The finite rank-bin correspondence makes the connection concrete.

I can’t honestly claim that this mirror is ill-posed, that the population lacks multiplicity, or that the continuum degenerates. The paper’s NP-hardness claim and discrete committee choice might make the mirror land in Class B, but that is not a reason to reject the question under the programme’s criteria. My negative case is therefore weak: Proposition 3 is not a complexity result, yet the continuous multi-winner problem remains a credible mirror of the paper’s Section 4.
