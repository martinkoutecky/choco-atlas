The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a large, repeated jury or committee with many nearly interchangeable members and only a few information-cost classes. A type records everything relevant to the decision: preferences and error losses, signal technology, reporting options, and information cost. If class \(t\) has population fraction \(\mu_t\), then \(\mu_t\) is mass, and a first-best planner chooses one information precision \(q_t\) for that whole class. The decision remains binary; the population is what becomes continuous.

One concrete candidate question is to give a prover rational fractions \(\mu_t\), a committee size \(N\), and convex piecewise-linear cost functions \(C_t\), and ask for an \(\epsilon\)-optimal first-best precision profile. In the paper’s balanced normal-signal case, let \(Q=N\sum_t\mu_tq_t\). The objective is
\[ U(Q)-\sum_t\mu_t C_t(q_t), \qquad U(Q)=-\bigl(\alpha\gamma+\beta(1-\gamma)\bigr)\Phi(-\sqrt Q/2), \]
where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF. This keeps the paper’s information-acquisition problem and its binary decision, while making the population distribution explicit. With a suitable finite encoding of the costs and numerical accuracy, I would expect this first-best optimization to be tractable by convex optimization.

There is a real foothold in Proposition 2, proved in this paper (Appendix A.3): when \(\Lambda=1\), the first-best information choice is uniquely determined by
\[ \frac{\beta(1-\gamma)\phi(s^*\sqrt Q)}{2\sqrt Q} =\frac{C'(q)}{N}. \]
That result supports the proposed optimization model, but it is not an eligible anchor under the requested standard: it characterizes an optimum and its uniqueness; it does not assert a computational complexity result or give an algorithm with a running-time bound. The paper’s heterogeneous-cost analysis in Proposition 12 is further evidence that cost classes are meaningful here, but it too is comparative statics, not a complexity result. So this paper has no named computational result that can honestly serve as an anchor, and the optimization question above is a plausible research direction rather than an anchor-compliant mirror.

The weak point is that the candidate uses the paper’s first-best benchmark, not its central strategic equilibrium. In an atomless population, an individual has no pivotal influence on the decision, which can erase the incentive to acquire information when marginal cost is positive. The authors might recognize the first-best problem as theirs, but it does not preserve the equilibrium free-riding question that motivates the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–14 characterize first-best choices, equilibria, comparative statics, and limiting decision accuracy. Proposition 2 gives a first-order condition and uniqueness result, not a complexity claim or an algorithm with a running-time bound. The proponent correctly concedes this. Under the requested standard, there is no eligible anchor here.

The proposed first-best problem is coherent: a population can be divided into cost types with masses \(\mu_t\). But it creates a new computational question around the planner benchmark rather than translating a computational result of the paper. In the balanced case, the objective depends on all type-level choices through the single aggregate \(Q\), alongside separable costs. A finite encoding of piecewise-linear costs would make that a well-defined optimization task; it would still be a separate algorithmic contribution, not a mirror anchored in this paper.

The stronger replacement would preserve the paper’s strategic information-acquisition question and give agents a small number of complete cost and signal types. That is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and Proposition 12 supports the relevance of cost heterogeneity. But in an atomless population, one member’s report has zero mass and cannot affect the collective decision. The pivotal incentive at the center of the equilibrium analysis disappears. Keeping each member’s finite influence can define a high-multiplicity finite game, but that is a new equilibrium-computation problem: the paper states no computational task or complexity result for it.

This is a strong reason to reject the paper as an anchor-compliant ChoCo mirror. It does not establish that every computational question built around its model is unworthy. The high-multiplicity scenario is sensible, and an explicitly formulated equilibrium-computation problem could prove useful. The negative case is weakest at that universal claim; what the paper supports is a lack of computational anchors, not a proof that no future mirror could be worthwhile.
