The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete firm types \(T\), a rational mass distribution \(\mu\), and each type’s demand vector and setup, holding, backlogging, and production costs, define \(c(\nu)\) for every subpopulation \(0 \leq \nu \leq \mu\) as its pooled setup-inventory minimum cost, using aggregate demand \(\sum_{t \in T}\nu_t d_t\), the cheapest available technology, binary setup decisions, and real-valued aggregate flows. Compute per-firm type cost shares \(x_t\) such that \(\sum_{t \in T}\mu_t x_t = c(\mu)\) and \(\sum_{t \in T}\nu_t x_t \leq c(\nu)\) for every subpopulation \(\nu\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution over types \(t \in T\), with mass \(\mu_t\); each type carries a demand vector and a complete cost vector. The decision is the per-firm allocation \(x_t\). Solve the grand-coalition dual for \(c(\mu)\), assign \(x_t = y \cdot d_t\) using its demand prices \(y\), and ask whether these shares satisfy budget balance and every subpopulation’s core constraint.

The objection that survived

The setup charge remains a fixed coalition-level cost as population mass grows, so the economic interpretation depends on how demand and setup costs are normalized; this bounds the intended regime but does not rule out a large pooled consortium.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2.1’s core-existence result and dual-based allocation construction. It leaves the unitary Owen point conditions and simulations, and the PI-game, nucleolus, and core-characterization results in Theorem 3.5, Theorem 4.5, and Proposition 4.7, outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Formalize the real-demand formulation and prove that the grand-coalition dual remains feasible for every subpopulation, including changes in which types have positive mass.
  • Give a running-time bound in the planning horizon \(T\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\).
  • Specify the scaling of demand and fixed setup costs in the intended large-consortium regime.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is an *unanchored candidate*: the paper has no numbered result that states a computational complexity classification. Theorem 2.1 is the closest result, but it does not say that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classify its complexity.

Theorem 2.1, proved in this paper using the integrality result of Pochet and Wolsey (1988), says that every setup-inventory game is totally balanced: every subgame has a nonempty core. Its proof also gives a core allocation from an optimal dual solution for the grand coalition. That is a useful constructive hook, but the theorem is an existence result, not a stated complexity result. Theorem 3.5 and Theorems 4.5 and Proposition 4.7 give further characterizations of core allocations and the nucleolus; they likewise do not analyze computational complexity. So under your anchor rule, I have no qualifying anchor.

If a constructive existence proof is allowed as a lead, the plausible mirror is Continuum SI Core Allocation. Imagine a large retail consortium with thousands of branches but relatively few recurring demand-and-cost profiles. A type \(t\) specifies a branch’s complete demand vector \(d_t\) and setup, holding, backlogging, and production costs \((k_t,h_t,b_t,p_t)\); the rational mass \(\mu_t\) is its share of the branches, with \(\tau\) far smaller than the number of branches.

For any subpopulation described by \(0\le\nu\le\mu\), total demand is \(\sum_t\nu_t d_t\), and its available costs are the period-by-period minima among types with \(\nu_t>0\). Let \(c(\nu)\) be the minimum setup-inventory operating cost for that coalition, retaining binary setup decisions but allowing aggregate production, inventory, and backlog to be real-valued. The question is: given \(\mu\), find a rational per-branch cost share \(x_t\) for each type such that
\[ \sum_t\mu_t x_t=c(\mu) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_t\nu_t x_t\le c(\nu)\ \text{for every }0\le\nu\le\mu. \]
The solution is the vector \(x\), a core allocation for the continuum society.

I would expect this to be tractable, in Class A, if the paper’s dual certificate extends to real aggregate demands: a grand-coalition dual price vector \(y\) would give \(x_t=\sum_j y_jd_{t,j}\), and the grand coalition’s cheaper available technologies make that certificate plausible for every subpopulation. This mirrors Theorem 2.1’s construction and preserves the paper’s core question: how to allocate pooled operating costs so no coalition pays more than its own operating cost.

The regime is credible for the authors’ model because their firms are already interchangeable when their demands and costs agree, and the coalition value already depends on summed demand and the best technology among members. The co-modification to real aggregate flows is natural for a large pooled operation, though it needs proof that the relevant LP structure survives. The mirror covers core existence and construction only; it does not claim to continuize the paper’s unitary Owen point, empirical findings, or nucleolus results.

The weak point is substantial: the paper assumes integer demands and integer aggregate ordering variables, and does not establish that the dual construction remains valid for arbitrary fractional-mass coalitions. More fundamentally, this proposed problem is not anchored in a named computational-complexity result of the paper. It is a plausible continuous version of its core-allocation question, but it cannot meet the requested anchor standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no numbered computational-complexity result here to mirror. Theorem 2.1 proves total balancedness; Theorem 3.5 characterizes when a particular allocation is in the core; Theorem 4.5 and Proposition 4.7 relate core allocations and the nucleolus to Owen points. None classifies an algorithmic problem. The best available anchor is therefore the constructive dual argument behind Theorem 2.1, rather than a stated complexity result.

The proposed branch-consortium story is plausible. Firms with the same demand and cost vectors can be grouped as a complete type, and a continuum core allocation can be stated for every subpopulation \(\nu\le\mu\). So individuality alone does not defeat the mirror. But the particular core-allocation question adds little computational substance: the paper’s dual construction already supplies the certificate. For the grand coalition, find an optimal dual solution \((y^*,\beta^*)\), and assign type \(t\) the per-unit share \(x_t=\sum_j y^*_j d_{t,j}\). For any subpopulation, aggregate demand is no greater than the grand coalition’s, while its minimum available costs are no lower. The grand-coalition dual solution therefore remains feasible, giving \(\sum_t\nu_t x_t\le c(\nu)\).

That argument also survives the natural fractional-demand version. In the paper’s facility-location formulation, each positive-demand constraint \(d_\tau\sum_t\lambda_{t\tau}=d_\tau\) reduces to \(\sum_t\lambda_{t\tau}=1\); the demand magnitude appears in the objective, not in that feasible-set condition. Thus allowing fractional aggregate demand does not create a new pricing or separation problem. Computing the allocation still amounts to solving the same finite-horizon optimization problem and assigning its dual prices to types. This is a sensible continuous formulation, but it offers little new complexity landscape beyond the paper’s existing constructive proof.

There is also a scaling tension for the paper’s distinctive setup-cost economics. If many firms each retain their own demand while the consortium’s fixed setup charges stay constant, those charges vanish per firm as the population grows. If instead total demand stays fixed as the population grows, the continuum model no longer represents that high-multiplicity regime in the usual per-firm sense. Either choice can be defensible, but the most natural large-cohort limit weakens the setup-cost sharing that motivates the unitary Owen points.

A stronger mirror might ask whether a continuum unitary Owen point is in the core, or compute a nucleolus for a type distribution. That could introduce a real optimization question over subpopulations. But it is a new computational question built from the paper’s allocation results, not a complexity result the paper establishes; and the finite type support keeps it plausible rather than ruling it out. I cannot honestly turn that possibility into a universal objection.

So the strongest negative case is narrow: the proponent’s specific continuation of Theorem 2.1 looks like a routine extension of an existing dual certificate, not a substantial new computational problem. The broader claim that *no* worthwhile continuous mirror exists is weak. A richer allocation-focused mirror may still be worth studying, especially if the authors accept it as their problem.
