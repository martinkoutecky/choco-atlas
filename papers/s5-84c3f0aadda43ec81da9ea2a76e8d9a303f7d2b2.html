The continuous mirror question

For a finite or countable family \(\mathcal H\) of measurable positive-mass associations, give each agent the paper's unit baseline value, direct benefit equal to the mass directly connected, discounted indirect benefits by association-path distance, and per-capita association cost \(\sum_{A\ni x}(c+c_0/\nu(A))\). The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly, this paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results says that a problem is in \(P\), hard, or parameterized tractable. So there is no anchor that meets the brief exactly. The strongest positive bridge is Proposition 1, proved in this paper’s appendix. It says that under linear association costs \(C(A)=c_0+c|A|\), the efficient hypergraph is the grand hypergraph \(H_n=\{N\}\) when \(\tilde c(n)=c+c_0/n\le n-1\), and the empty hypergraph \(H_0\) when \(\tilde c(n)>n-1\). This is a structural welfare result, not a stated complexity result; its threshold characterization nevertheless gives an immediate algorithm for the paper’s homogeneous welfare-maximization problem.

A continuous mirror of that result would be Continuous Hypergraph Welfare Maximization. Its instance is an atomless population \(X=[0,M]\) with Lebesgue mass \(\nu(X)=M\), one complete type, and binary-rational parameters \(c_0,c>0\) and \(0<\delta<1\). The type specifies exactly the paper’s common preferences over connections: each agent has unit value, values directly connected mass at one per unit, discounts indirect connections by distance with factor \(\delta\), and shares the same association costs. A feasible decision is a finite or countable family \(\mathcal H\) of measurable, positive-mass associations, with finite total cost for almost every agent. For \(x\in X\), let \(D_{\mathcal H}(x)\) be the mass directly connected to \(x\), and let \(\operatorname{dist}_{\mathcal H}(x,y)\) be the shortest association-path distance, with distance infinity for disconnected agents. Define

\[ B_{\mathcal H}(x) =1+\nu(D_{\mathcal H}(x)) +\int_{X\setminus D_{\mathcal H}(x)} \delta^{\operatorname{dist}_{\mathcal H}(x,y)}\,d\nu(y), \]

where \(\delta^\infty=0\), and

\[ C_{\mathcal H}(x) =\sum_{\substack{A\in\mathcal H\\x\in A}} \frac{c_0+c\,\nu(A)}{\nu(A)}. \]

The problem is to return a feasible \(\mathcal H\) maximizing
\(\int_X(B_{\mathcal H}(x)-C_{\mathcal H}(x))\,d\nu(x)\). The output can be a finite description of the chosen structure and its welfare value.

The regime is a large cohort whose members have the same unit value for contacts and the same association costs—for example, members of a standardized information-sharing community. There are \(M\) agents’ worth of mass but only one type, so \(\tau=1\). That is a narrow scenario, but it matches the paper’s own homogeneous-agent model rather than discarding its central assumptions. The population is the object being made continuous; associations remain the decisions.

The expected outcome is tractable by the same structural route as Proposition 1. In the continuous model, an association of mass \(M\) gives each member benefit \(1+M\) and cost \(c+c_0/M\). Thus the grand association’s gain over the empty structure changes sign at \(c+c_0/M=M-1\). The mirror asks whether the finite structural characterization extends to measurable hypergraphs; if it does, the optimization reduces to an exact rational threshold comparison. At equality, the usual weak definition of welfare maximization makes both empty and grand structures optimal. Proposition 1’s printed uniqueness claim at that boundary may need clarification, since the empty structure is feasible and has the same welfare there.

This mirror covers only Proposition 1’s centralized efficiency result for linear costs. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s stability results or its convex-cost discussion. Natural follow-up questions are whether the threshold characterization extends to a finite distribution of complete types, and whether the paper’s strong-stability characterization has a meaningful measurable-population counterpart.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper never asks a computational question, and the continuous model requires choices about how headcounts scale into benefits and how measurable overlapping associations are represented. Those choices make this a proposed analogue of the paper’s welfare problem, not a complexity result already present in it. It is a plausible positive mirror because it retains the authors’ objective and association structure, but it cannot be presented as a qualifying computational anchor under the brief’s strict rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is the one the prompt identifies as decisive: this paper has no computational-complexity result to mirror. Proposition 1 characterizes which hypergraph maximizes welfare; Propositions 2–5 characterize stability. None defines an algorithmic problem and analyzes its complexity, approximation, or parameterized tractability. One can wrap “find a welfare-maximizing hypergraph” around Proposition 1, but that adds a computational framing the paper does not study. That is the main reason this is a poor fit for ChoCo’s complexity programme.

The proposed continuous welfare problem is nevertheless a plausible economic analogue, and its normalization can be repaired. Under the proponent’s definition, a grand association of mass \(M\) gives each member direct benefit \(M\), compared with zero direct benefit in the empty structure. Its per-member cost is \(c+c_0/M\), so the threshold is \(c+c_0/M\le M\), rather than \(M-1\). The discrepancy is fixable; it does not defeat the mirror. The deeper limitation is that this formulation asks for a continuum welfare characterization, not a missing computational result from the paper.

Adding type heterogeneity could create a more substantial algorithmic problem, but the paper supplies no such model: every player has the same value, discount factor, and cost parameters. A finite-type version would need new assumptions about how types affect benefits, costs, and deviations. It could be worthwhile research, but it would extend the paper rather than mirror one of its results.

The stability results offer a stronger-looking alternative, though they bring a serious modelling obstacle. They rely on individual exits and structures such as stars with singleton centers. In an atomless population, those roles have zero mass. Requiring every association and overlap to have positive mass removes those singleton structures; allowing null overlaps can preserve their connecting role, but then population masses alone do not encode the incidence pattern that determines distances and payoffs. A continuous stability problem can still be designed, but its representation choices would supply much of the problem rather than follow from the paper.

So the honest negative case is strongest against a worthwhile continuous computational mirror, not against every continuous analogue. Proposition 1 has a natural mass version, and the paper’s welfare and stability questions can certainly be continued. What the paper lacks is the computational question that ChoCo’s programme is meant to chart.
