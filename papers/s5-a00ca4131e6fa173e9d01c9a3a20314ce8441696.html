Why no mirror

The proposed atomless, type-mass popular-matching existence problem is coherent and close to the paper’s matching notion. But Corollary 1 is an existence characterization, while Theorems 2 and 3 give random-instance existence probabilities; none states a computation, runtime, hardness result, or computational formulation. The mirror therefore fails the required named-result gate.

The proposed mirror extends Corollary 1’s popular-matching existence question. It leaves aside Theorems 2 and 3’s random-instance thresholds and the paper’s asymptotic random-profile analysis.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named NP-hardness or explicit \(P\)-membership theorem. Its strongest computational anchor is Corollary 1, a structural characterization of existence. It states that a strict two-weighted instance with \(w_1\ge 2w_2\) has a two-weighted popular matching exactly when its fs-relation graph contains none of the three forbidden subgraphs \(G_1,G_2,G_3\). The graph has an edge from \(f_1(x)\) to \(s_1(x)\) for each first-category applicant and from \(f_2(y)\) to \(s_2(y)\) for each second-category applicant. The forbidden patterns are: the specified path with vertices in \(S_1\cap F_2\) near both ends and adjacent \(E_1\) edges; a cycle with an attached path containing such an overlap vertex and an \(E_1\) edge; and a connected component containing two cycles. The paper proves the underlying well-formed-matching characterization in Theorem 1; Corollary 1 also uses Proposition 1, cited from Mestre. So this is a genuine existence characterization, though not an explicit complexity-class result. If only results explicitly asserting a class such as \(P\) or NP-hardness count as anchors, there is no qualifying anchor here.

A plausible mirror of Corollary 1 is Mass-Weighted Popular Matching Existence. Take a finite item set \(I\), two weights \(w_1\ge 2w_2>0\), and a finite support of types \(t=(h,\pi)\), where \(h\in\{1,2\}\) is the priority category and \(\pi\) is a complete strict ranking of the items. The society is given by rational type proportions \(\mu_t\) summing to one and a population scale \(N\); type \(t\) has mass \(N\mu_t\). Each item has capacity one person-equivalent. A feasible assignment is a matrix \(x_{ti}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_i x_{ti}=N\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{ti}\le 1\).

Define popularity by comparing assignments of the *same* atomless population. For any feasible alternative assignment \(y\), let \(z_{tij}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) agents assigned item \(i\) under \(x\) and item \(j\) under \(y\). Its row and column sums must be \(x_{ti}\) and \(y_{tj}\). Then \(x\) is popular if every such feasible comparison has nonpositive weighted margin
\(\sum_{t=(h,\pi)} w_h\sum_{i,j}z_{tij}\bigl(\mathbf{1}[j\succ_t i]-\mathbf{1}[i\succ_t j]\bigr)\).
Atomlessness matters: every coupling \(z\) with those margins can be realized by reallocating agents within a type. The problem is to decide whether a popular \(x\) exists and return one if it does.

The regime I find plausible is a large, centrally run placement batch with standardized rankings and two priority tiers: many applicants, but a moderate number \(\tau\) of exact category-and-ranking types, so \(N\gg\tau\). For example, this could arise where applicants choose from a common menu and cohort members share standardized preference lists. Item capacity one and total supply \(m\) are retained, with supply scaling as needed for the population. This is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s underlying matching problem, not its uniform-random-profile regime.

I would expect the mirror to be tractable if a mass-valued version of the paper’s well-formed characterization leads to a flow or LP formulation. That is a conjecture for the prover, not a result of this paper. The paper’s Theorems 2 and 3 establish random-instance thresholds—popular matching exists with probability \(o(1)\) when \(m/n^{4/3}=o(1)\), and with probability \(1-o(1)\) when \(n^{4/3}/m=o(1)\). They do not anchor this mirror: those are probabilistic existence results, not complexity classifications, and their random rankings typically give little repeated-type structure. Further questions are whether the well-formed characterization survives for mass assignments, what dependence an algorithm has on \(m,\tau\), and input bit length, and whether discrete popular matchings admit useful rounding guarantees.

The soft spot is that Corollary 1’s unweighted graph obstruction does not automatically extend to fractional type mass: capacities and split assignments may change which matchings are popular. Popularity also requires preserving pairwise comparisons across the same agents, which is why the coupling is part of the model. This is a credible but modest positive case for the existence problem; it does not mirror the paper’s main random-threshold results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper’s main results do not ask the kind of computational question ChoCo is set up to continuize. Theorems 2 and 3 bound the probability that a popular matching exists under a particular finite sampling process: applicants independently draw complete strict rankings, and small graph motifs determine the threshold. A continuous society supplied as a distribution replaces those realized samples with masses. Adding randomness back would make the task one about random societies, rather than computation on a continuous society.

Corollary 1 is the best candidate for a mirror: it characterizes existence through forbidden subgraphs in a graph of unit applicant edges. But the proposed mass version needs a new model of assignment and comparison. A type’s mass can split between items, capacities must scale with the population, and popularity must account for couplings between two assignments of the same atomless population. Those choices are coherent, but the corollary itself does not establish that its forbidden-subgraph criterion, or the paper’s underlying computational contribution, extends to that model. The mirror would be a new fractional matching problem motivated by the paper, rather than a continuization of one of its main results.

The standardized-cohort scenario is plausible, but it departs from the paper’s random-ranking regime. With independent uniform rankings over a growing item set, full ranking types are almost surely distinct, so the support is essentially the number of applicants. A moderate-support society would need a different preference-generation story; the paper’s random threshold results would no longer motivate that regime.

That is the strongest negative case I can make, and it is limited. The atomless matching model is well-defined, and large batches with repeated preference types are credible. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror in every scenario. The case against is that the paper offers little computational result for such a mirror to inherit; it does not show that the proposed mass problem is unworthy in its own right.
