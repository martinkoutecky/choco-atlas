The continuous mirror question

Given \(k\ge2\) and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over rational locations, draw an anchor \(p\) from \(\mu\), then choose \(k-1\) distinct support locations \(S\) excluding \(p\) with probability proportional to \(\Delta_p(S)\prod_{y\in S}\mu_y\), where \(\Delta_p(S)\) is the product of consecutive gaps among \(p\) and the locations in \(S\). If the support has fewer than \(k\) locations, open at every support location. Does this mass-weighted RAV lottery always satisfy \(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SC}_\mu(F)]\le 4(k-1)\mathrm{OPT}_k(\mu)\)?

The model it lives in

Types are rational ideal locations \(x\) with masses \(\mu_x\); decisions are lotteries over facility sets drawn by the mass-weighted RAV rule, and the objective is expected per-capita distance \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(F)=\sum_x\mu_x\min_{f\in F}|x-f|\).

The objection that survived

The stated any-lottery task permits an optimal \(k\)-median solution and so is vacuous; specifying the mass-weighted RAV lottery repairs this, while the loss of individual strategyproofness only limits coverage.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s \(4(k-1)\) welfare guarantee for every \(k\ge2\). It leaves the strategyproofness boundary in Theorem 1 aside, since individual deviations do not change a nonatomic population distribution.

Open questions for a prover

  • When \(k\) is part of the input, can the mass-weighted RAV lottery or its exact expected cost be computed more efficiently than by enumerating support tuples?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive foothold is Theorem 2 (Social-cost approximation), proved in §6. It says that for every \(k\ge2\) and every location profile \(\mathbf{x}\), \(\mathbb E[\mathrm{SC}(\mathbf{x},\mathrm{RAV}_k(\mathbf{x}))]\le 4(k-1)\mathrm{OPT}_k(\mathbf{x})\). Thus \(\mathrm{RAV}_k\) is a \(4(k-1)\)-approximation; in particular, the paper notes that \(\mathrm{RAV}_3\), which it proves strategyproof in expectation, achieves factor \(8\). This is an algorithmic-performance anchor, not a hardness or complexity-class result.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a region with many residents whose ideal clinic locations fall at a relatively small number \(\tau\) of neighborhood or transit hubs. Let a type be an ideal location \(x\), and let \(\mu_x\) be the fraction of residents of that type. The support locations and their rational masses describe the society; the random choices below are the mechanism’s own lottery, not uncertainty about which society exists.

Here is a precise continuous-population mirror, Continuous RAV Welfare Approximation. The input is \(k\ge2\) and a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over rational locations, with binary-encoded masses summing to \(1\). For a facility set \(F\), define \(\mathrm{SC}_\mu(F)=\sum_x\mu_x\min_{f\in F}|x-f|\), and let \(\mathrm{OPT}_k(\mu)\) be the minimum of this quantity over nonempty \(F\) with \(|F|\le k\). The task is to return a lottery over such facility sets with expected cost at most \(4(k-1)\mathrm{OPT}_k(\mu)\).

The mirrored algorithm is the mass version of \(\mathrm{RAV}_k\): draw an anchor \(p\) from \(\mu\), then draw an ordered tuple \((y_1,\ldots,y_{k-1})\) with weight \(\Delta_p(y_1,\ldots,y_{k-1})\prod_j\mu_{y_j}\), where \(\Delta_p\) is the product of the consecutive gaps among the anchor and the tuple’s locations. Open facilities at those locations. If the normalizing weight is zero, open at every support location. For rational \(\mu\), this is exactly the finite mechanism’s lottery after replicating each type in proportion to its mass: repeated locations have zero volume, and the remaining tuple weights agree up to a common factor. Theorem 2 therefore gives the stated bound directly.

For fixed \(k\), enumerating the support tuples computes the lottery and expected cost in polynomial time in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. The expected direction is tractable for fixed \(k\). This mirror covers Theorem 2’s welfare guarantee; it does not claim to transfer the paper’s individual strategyproofness result.

The weak point is substantial: in a nonatomic population, one individual’s report does not change \(\mu\), and that individual has zero chance of being sampled. Ordinary individual strategyproofness would therefore be vacuous. Asking for incentives against a positive-mass cohort would be a different property, and the paper’s named results do not establish it. The paper has no named hardness or standard complexity-class result to anchor; this case rests on its approximation guarantee, and the underlying one-dimensional \(k\)-median problem is already tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is the proponent’s only anchor, and it is a welfare guarantee for a specified mechanism rather than a result about computational complexity. The paper says the underlying one-dimensional \(k\)-median problem is tractable; the approximation loss is the price of incentives. So the proposed task—return *any* lottery with approximation factor \(4(k-1)\)—drops the feature that makes the theorem interesting. An optimal \(k\)-median solution is already a deterministic lottery with factor \(1\).

Keeping the RAV rule makes the mirror more faithful, but also shows its limit. Every rational distribution in the proposed input can be represented by a finite electorate with those type proportions. Under that replication, the mass-weighted sampler has exactly the RAV lottery’s law: choosing identities of distinct locations contributes the corresponding product of their multiplicities, while repeated locations have zero volume. Theorem 2 therefore transfers directly. That is a clean high-multiplicity dictionary, but it adds no algorithmic question of the kind ChoCo is charting: the paper gives no running-time or hardness result for this mechanism, and the continuous version is just its welfare bound on replicated profiles.

A more ambitious mirror would preserve incentives as well as welfare. Here the population limit is awkward: one person has zero mass, so changing that person’s report leaves the distribution unchanged and strategyproofness becomes vacuous. Requiring a positive-mass cohort to misreport instead asks about coalition manipulation, which Theorem 1 does not address. Keeping a one-person mass quantum returns to the finite replicated model; taking that quantum to zero erases the deviation.

I would not use “no multiplicity” against this paper: many residents sharing a small set of ideal service locations is plausible, and welfare depends only on their aggregate masses. That makes the continuous welfare statement coherent. The negative case is narrower: it is not a worthwhile *computational-complexity* mirror for this programme, because the only proposed anchor is an incentive-driven distortion guarantee whose mass version follows exactly from finite replication. That case is honest, but not airtight; the high-multiplicity dictionary could still be useful to mechanism-design work outside ChoCo’s complexity focus.
