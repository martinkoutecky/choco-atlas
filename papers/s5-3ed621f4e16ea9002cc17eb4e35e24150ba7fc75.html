The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive seed is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s allocation problem. Imagine a federation with many local jurisdictions, grouped into a small number \(\tau\) of types sharing the same labor base \(L_t\), investment cost \(c_t\), benefit function \(H_t\), and political attributes. A type has mass \(\mu_t\), the fraction of jurisdictions of that type; \(g_t\) is investment per jurisdiction. The aggregate budget and welfare could then be written as integrals over the type distribution, with a fixed-share rule constraining how the budget is distributed. This is a plausible regime for a federation with many similar local authorities.

But that is only a candidate reformulation of the allocation model. The paper has no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. It therefore supplies no qualifying anchor for the continuous computational question the assignment requires.

The nearest named results are economic propositions, not computational ones. Proposition 1, proved in the paper, says that under unanimity voting the expected regional shares equal the legislators’ proposer probabilities \(p_i\), for any discount factor \(\delta\in(0,1)\). Proposition 2, attributed to Zaporozhets (2006), gives the stationary-equilibrium expected payoffs under majority voting: with proposal probabilities sufficiently close to uniform, all players receive \(1/n\); otherwise, under the stated conditions on a set of \(k\) lower-probability players, payoffs follow equations (14)–(15). Neither proposition classifies the computational complexity of a problem.

The main weakness is also the reason I would not present the candidate reformulation as a valid mirror: the paper’s results depend on a finite legislature, proposer selection, voting quotas, and minimal winning coalitions. A continuum of jurisdiction types does not directly preserve those mechanisms; defining a continuum bargaining game would add a new model whose results are not anchored by these propositions. The paper’s continuous investment variables and finite regional populations do not themselves continuize the population in the programme’s sense. So, despite a plausible high-multiplicity scenario for its allocation setting, this paper offers no honest named computational anchor and no qualifying continuous problem per anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 characterize expected payoffs in finite legislative bargaining; they do not classify an algorithmic problem’s complexity or present an algorithm. Proposition 1 gives \(y_i=p_i\) under unanimity, while Proposition 2 gives equilibrium payoff formulas for finite \(n\), quota \(q\), and proposer probabilities \(p_i\). Neither specifies a computational problem with an input, output, and complexity question. Under ChoCo’s stated criterion, that is decisive.

The best proposed anchor is Proposition 1: extend the legislature to many jurisdictions grouped by bargaining type, then ask whether each type’s aggregate share tracks its aggregate proposal power. But this turns the proposition into an analytic question about a continuum bargaining game. Proposition 2 has the same problem: its result depends on finite-player voting quotas and minimal winning coalitions. In an atomless version, individual proposal probabilities and pivotality need new definitions. One could supply them, but that would define a new bargaining model rather than continuize a computational problem in the paper.

The allocation model offers a more plausible high-multiplicity setting: many jurisdictions sharing labor supplies, costs, benefit functions, and political attributes. Even in that stronger formulation, the paper’s named results do not anchor a computational question. Its fixed-share analysis chooses a scalar total budget \(G\), with regional investments determined by the shares and costs; the type-based version would replace sums with weighted sums or integrals. That may be a sensible continuous optimization model, but it is a new model of welfare allocation, not a computational mirror of one of the paper’s results. And if it also retains legislative bargaining, it inherits the problem that the proposer and coalition rules must be rebuilt for the continuum.

So I would reject this paper as a ChoCo computational anchor, while keeping the claim narrow. It is possible to imagine a continuum legislature that its authors might recognize as a related research question. The case against that being a worthwhile computational mirror of this paper rests on the absence of a computational result to mirror and on the need to reconstruct the bargaining mechanism, not on a claim that continuous bargaining or allocation research is uninteresting.
