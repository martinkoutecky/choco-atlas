The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest anchor-based case for a continuous computational mirror of this paper. It contains no named computational result—no theorem establishing that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity.

Theorem 1 characterizes rules satisfying independence of irrelevant opinions and non-degeneracy as one-vote rules. Theorem 2 adds symmetry and shows that, for at least three people, the liberal rule is the unique such rule. Both are axiomatic characterization results, not computational results. Turning either into a continuous axiomatic question would also fall outside the programme’s stated scope.

A continuous population model of group identification might be possible, but this paper provides no qualifying anchor for the computational question it would pose. To claim otherwise would stretch the requested standard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative verdict is unusually strong here: there is no computational result in the paper to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes rules satisfying independence of irrelevant opinions and non-degeneracy; Theorem 2 adds symmetry and characterizes the liberal rule. The other named results are also axiomatic. None establishes the complexity of an algorithmic problem. A continuous version of these characterizations might be interesting, but it would not chart the computational landscape ChoCo is meant to study.

The most plausible rescue is to treat people’s opinions as types and ask how to classify the resulting continuous society. But each opinion in this paper is a judgment about every named person, so a type is an opinion vector whose dimension grows with the population. Aggregating those vectors into masses loses which person receives which classification; retaining that information preserves the identity-indexed problem rather than giving the programme’s fixed type space. Restricting attention to cohorts could make the population model manageable, but would change the paper’s individual-level question.

One could add a computational task—such as optimizing interventions to change the classifications—but that task would be new. Computing the liberal rule itself just applies each person’s self-reported membership. So the paper supplies no computational anchor, and making one would mean designing a different research problem.
