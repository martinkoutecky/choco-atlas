The continuous mirror question

Given item categories \(J\) with per-capita supplies \(\sigma_j\), a ranking-mass vector \(\bar\mu\), and complete hidden types \(t=(r_t,v_t)\) inducing \(\bar\mu_r=\sum_{t:r_t=r}\mu_t\), compute the best rank-only aggregate matching \(x_{tj}=\mu_t y_{r_tj}\), subject to \(\sum_j y_{rj}=1\) and \(\sum_t x_{tj}\le\sigma_j\), minimizing the worst-case ratio \(\max_t u_t(x^*)/u_t(x)\) over complete populations consistent with \(\bar\mu\), where \(x^*\) maximizes \(\sum_t\mu_t\log(u_t(x)-o_t)\), \(u_t(x)=\mu_t^{-1}\sum_jv_{tj}x_{tj}\), and \(o_t=\sum_j\sigma_jv_{tj}\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic one-sided matching market with complete valuation types carrying mass \(\mu_t\), item-category supplies \(\sigma_j\), aggregate assignment variables \(x_{tj}\), and a mechanism restricted to observe only the ranking projection. The benchmark is the weighted Nash-bargaining maximizer, while the mechanism minimizes worst-case per-type approximation loss over cardinal populations sharing the observed ranking distribution.

The objection that survived

The proposed mirror incorrectly treats rankings as complete types; a valid version needs complete types \(t=(r,v)\) and an explicit rank-only information constraint, making it a robust mechanism-design formulation rather than the proponent's literal statement.

fatal: False

The opportunity-structure bounds in Theorems 6.1 and 6.2 remain structural observations, while the RPI mechanism, its truthfulness proof, and the other mechanism results are not covered.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified one. The paper contains no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, FPT, or W[1]-hardness theorem. Its best anchors are named approximation and mechanism results. If those count as computational anchors, the following is a genuine continuous-population mirror; under a stricter complexity-only rule, the paper fails the anchor requirement.

The mirror I would use is a large housing or course-assignment market. There are many agents, but only finitely many complete cardinal valuation types \(T\): two agents have the same type exactly when they assign the same value to every item category. The population is a distribution \(\mu\), where \(\mu_t\) is the mass of type \(t\). Because one-sided matching has one item per agent, the item side must scale with the market: there are finitely many item categories \(J\), with per-capita supplies \(\sigma_j\), \(\sum_j\sigma_j=1\). This is a co-modification forced by the matching problem, not a replacement of population continuity by outcome continuity.

The decision variable is an aggregate matching \(x_{tj}\), where \(x_{tj}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving item category \(j\):

\[ \sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{tj}\le \sigma_j,\qquad x_{tj}\ge0. \]

Every individual still receives an integral item; \(x\) is the population-level coupling or lottery induced by those assignments. The utility of type \(t\) is

\[ u_t(x)=\frac{1}{\mu_t}\sum_j v_{tj}x_{tj}. \]

For rational \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), multiplying by a common denominator \(N\) produces exactly the finite market with \(N\mu_t\) copies of type \(t\) and \(N\sigma_j\) copies of item \(j\). Moreover,

\[ \sum_t N\mu_t\log u_t(x) \]

is precisely the logarithm of the finite Nash social welfare objective. Thus this is a high-multiplicity relaxation with a two-way dictionary, not merely a fractional restatement.

My lead anchor is Theorem 6.2, proved in this paper. It states that there is a family of one-sided matching instances for which the worst-case population non-monotonicity parameter satisfies

\[ \rho^*=\Omega\!\left(2^{\sqrt{\log n}/2}\right). \]

Here \(\rho\) is the largest factor by which some remaining agent’s utility can decrease when other agents are removed and the Nash-social-welfare maximizing assignment is recomputed; \(\rho^*\) is the worst case over instances and removed subsets. The appendix proves the theorem by explicitly constructing repeated bidder and item sizes and checking Nash-optimality through KKT conditions.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous Opportunity-Structure Evaluation. Given \((T,J,\mu,\sigma,v)\) and a surviving population \(\nu\le\mu\), compute the two weighted Nash-welfare maximizers

\[ x^\mu\in\arg\max_{x\in X(\mu,\sigma)} \sum_t\mu_t\log u_t(x), \]

and

\[ x^\nu\in\arg\max_{x\in X(\nu,\sigma)} \sum_t\nu_t\log u_t(x), \]

where unused item supply is allowed after population removal. The required output is

\[ \rho(\mu,\nu)= \max_{t:\nu_t>0} \frac{u_t(x^\mu)}{u_t(x^\nu)}. \]

This is a precise computational question about a continuous society. For a fixed instance and fixed deletion \(\nu\), I expect Class A behaviour: both optima are weighted concave programs over a transportation polytope, so arbitrary-precision approximation should be tractable by convex optimization. But Theorem 6.2 says that tractability does not mean monotonicity becomes benign. Its repeated-size construction can be normalized directly into masses, with exactly the same utilities and the same value of \(\rho\). The bad phenomenon is therefore caused by valuation and item-type structure, not by having individually named agents.

The paper’s companion upper bound, Theorem 6.1, also proved here, says that every finite instance satisfies

\[ \rho=O\!\left(2^{2\sqrt{\log n}}\right) \subseteq o(n^\varepsilon) \]

for every constant \(\varepsilon>0\). Its continuous counterpart would be Continuous Opportunity-Structure Envelope: given a typed society and \(B\), decide whether every subpopulation \(\nu\le\mu\) satisfies \(\rho(\mu,\nu)\le B\), and, if not, produce a violating deletion. The interesting new question is whether the finite bound can be expressed in terms of the number of valuation types, item types, and input bit length rather than the denominator-expanded population size \(n\). I would regard that sharp envelope as a possible Class C boundary problem: the fixed-instance optimization is tractable, but the universal optimization over all deleted masses is genuinely new.

A second, independent anchor is Theorem 4.1, also proved here. It states that the worst-case approximation ratio of every ordinal mechanism relative to the Nash bargaining benchmark is at least \(n-1\). The theorem’s construction exploits agents with the same top choices but different cardinal intensities, showing that ordinal information cannot guarantee a good per-agent approximation.

Its continuous mirror is Continuous Ordinal Matching Gap. The input is a ranking-type distribution \((r_t,\mu_t)\), item supplies \(\sigma\), and a feasible aggregate matching \(x\) chosen using only the rankings. Cardinal values \(v_{tj}\) may vary arbitrarily subject to the rankings \(r_t\). Let \(x^*(v)\) maximize the weighted Nash-bargaining objective

\[ \sum_t\mu_t\log\bigl(u_t(x)-o_t\bigr), \qquad o_t=\sum_j\sigma_jv_{tj}. \]

The problem is to choose an ordinal \(x\) minimizing

\[ \sup_{v\text{ consistent with }r} \max_{t:\mu_t>0} \frac{u_t(x^*(v))}{u_t(x)}. \]

With \(q\) item categories and equal masses, the paper’s construction transfers to a population containing many repeated copies of each ranking type and gives a lower bound of \(q-1\) in the supremum, by taking the same \(\varepsilon\to0\) limit. This is best viewed as a Class B-like obstruction: increasing population multiplicity does not dissolve the difficulty because the combinatorics live in the item alternatives and cardinal intensities.

I would not anchor on Theorem 5.2 despite its appeal. It proves, here, that the randomized partial improvement mechanism is a \(4e\rho\)-approximation to the Nash bargaining solution, while Theorem 5.1 proves feasibility and individual truthfulness. In an atomless population, however, one individual has zero influence on the aggregate distribution, so individual dominant-strategy truthfulness becomes vacuous. A meaningful continuum version would need positive-mass coalition truthfulness, which is a stronger new problem rather than a direct mirror.

The weakest point is therefore clear. The paper supplies an excellent high-multiplicity subject matter match—especially because its lower-bound construction already uses repeated bidder and item sizes—and its population-monotonicity results survive normalization exactly. But it does not supply a named computational-complexity classification. A strict referee could accept the two continuous problems as natural mirrors while still rejecting the paper as evidence for ChoCo’s complexity-landscape claim until new results classify the resulting mass problems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is against the two opportunity-structure anchors. Their continuous translations do not preserve the phenomenon in the genuinely population-level sense.

For Theorem 6.2, the appendix’s asymptotic growth is supplied by repeated bulk gadgets, but the agent witnessing the bad ratio is essentially a singleton. The construction explicitly has \(k_s=1\), and the terminal loser \(e_s\) has size one, while \(n\) grows through the much larger lower-level markets. After normalization, the witness type has mass about \(1/n\), hence vanishes along the construction.

The proposed continuous \(\rho(\mu,\nu)\) still detects it only because it takes a maximum over every positive-mass type. Thus an arbitrarily small atom can dominate the statistic. That is a discrete exceptional-agent criterion written in mass notation, not a genuinely aggregate population effect. If one uses a mass-weighted welfare-loss measure, the theorem disappears; if one insists that the losing type have non-negligible mass, the appendix no longer establishes the claimed bound. Repeating the whole construction does not help: it preserves the loser’s fraction at roughly \(1/n\).

Theorem 6.1 has the same problem in reverse. Its parameter is the number \(n\) of individually present agents. In a high-multiplicity encoding, the only faithful continuous substitute is essentially \(1/\mu_{\min}\), or the denominator of the rational masses. That merely renames the finite population size. If arbitrary real deletions \(\nu\le\mu\) are permitted, a surviving type may be given arbitrarily small positive mass, and the finite \(n\)-bound supplies no corresponding uniform envelope. The proposed “Continuous Opportunity-Structure Envelope” is therefore either a re-encoding of the discrete theorem with a minimum-mass cutoff, or a new weighted-sensitivity problem not actually supported by the paper’s result.

The proposed translation of Theorem 4.1 has a more basic defect. It declares \(t\) to be a ranking type while allowing \(v_{tj}\) to vary arbitrarily within that type. Under ChoCo’s definition, those agents are not the same type: cardinal values are part of the complete description used by the benchmark. With ranking types alone, \(o_t\) and the Nash benchmark are not determined by the society; the formulation is instead a minimax problem over hidden cardinal populations.

The repair is clear: use complete types \(t=(r,v)\), let the mechanism observe only the ranking projection, and evaluate it against the full cardinal population. But that repaired problem is no longer the proponent’s stated mirror; it is a new robust mechanism-design problem with an information structure layered over the population distribution. The paper’s ordinary dominant-strategy result has an additional obstruction: in an atomless population, one agent’s report has zero effect on the aggregate, so individual truthfulness is vacuous. Positive-mass deviations would be coalitional strategyproofness, which the paper does not study.

There is, however, an important limit to this negative case. The repaired version of Theorem 4.1 can be made mathematically sensible: replicate each complete cardinal type and each item category many times, and let the mechanism observe only aggregate rankings. The bad type can then have positive mass, and the ordinal lower bound can plausibly transfer exactly. Since the objection cannot be that the continuous answer is unchanged, I cannot honestly dismiss that construction merely as a restatement.

So the honest verdict is that the universal negative claim is weak. The opportunity-structure anchors are poor evidence for ChoCo’s computational programme, and the stated ordinal mirror is malformed, but a corrected high-multiplicity version of Theorem 4.1 remains a worthwhile continuous-population question. I would not recommend claiming that no continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
