What the mirror covers

Propositions 1 and 2 characterize convexity and strict convexity; the paper also reports example computations of cores, Shapley values, and nucleoli, but gives no named computational guarantee.

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is limited. The paper’s data are already naturally aggregated: its USA example contains 232,571 publications, summarized by seven collaboration categories. That looks like a large population with small support. But those units are research outputs, not the agents in the game. The game still has exactly three players—university, industry, and government—and its payoff shares are already percentages. Treating those output shares as a continuous population would continuize the value data, not the society.

The paper has no named computational result of the kind needed for an anchor: no numbered theorem, proposition, or other result establishes that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its named results are Proposition 1, proved in the paper: a Triple Helix game is convex if and only if there is output within the system; and Proposition 2, also proved there: it is strictly convex if and only if all three bilateral collaborations and the trilateral collaboration have output. These are structural characterizations, not complexity results. The core-existence equivalence is attributed to prior game-theory work. Computing the core, Shapley values, and nucleoli for examples is reported in tables, but no named computational result is given.

The closest possible continuous problem would take an instance to be rational masses \(\mu_R\) summing to \(1\), one for each nonempty collaboration category \(R\in\{u,i,g,ui,ug,ig,uig\}\). Define \(v_\mu(S)=\sum_{\emptyset\ne R\subseteq S}\mu_R\), and ask for the core allocations \(x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^3\) satisfying \(\sum_{j\in N}x_j=1\) and \(\sum_{j\in S}x_j\ge v_\mu(S)\) for every coalition \(S\subseteq N\). A solution is the resulting allocation polytope. This is a sensible way to compute the paper’s underlying core, and it should be tractable: there are only three players and seven nonempty coalitions. But it is a reformulation of the paper’s existing payoff-share model, not a continuous-population mirror, and neither proposition is an anchor for that computational claim.

To get a genuine population mirror, one might instead treat many universities, firms, or public institutions as agents and group identical institutions into types. That would change the player set and require a new coalition-value model; the paper’s propositions would not carry over as stated. My weakest point, then, is decisive: the paper offers an aggregate suitable for a distributional representation, but no named computational result about a continuous population of agents. There is no honest anchored continuous problem to hand to a prover for this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s two named propositions are structural characterizations, not computational results. Proposition 1 says the three-player game is convex when there is output; Proposition 2 says it is strictly convex when the four collaboration categories have positive output. Neither states a complexity bound or analyzes an algorithmic problem over a population of agents.

The strongest distributional restatement would assign mass \(\mu_R\) to each nonempty collaboration category \(R\subseteq\{u,i,g\}\), then define
\[ v_\mu(S)=\sum_{\emptyset\ne R\subseteq S}\mu_R. \]
That represents the paper’s seven output shares. It does not make the society continuous: the masses describe publications or other outputs, which generate payoffs, while the game’s players remain the three helix actors. The paper already records those outputs as percentages, and its core already consists of real-valued allocations. Treating the categories as a population therefore changes the encoding of the output data, not the population of the game.

A better candidate might make universities, firms, or public institutions the players and group identical institutions into types. But the paper’s seven aggregates do not specify the value of every coalition of institutions. Preserving institution-level differences would require new coalition values and a new model; imposing enough symmetry to recover the paper’s aggregates collapses the game back to its three actors. That extension could be a separate research project, but its central question would not be the question these propositions answer.

The large publication count in the USA example supports efficient aggregation of output data. It does not establish high multiplicity of agents in the cooperative game. The negative case is strongest for a mirror of this paper: its central results have no population axis to continuize. It cannot rule out a worthwhile continuous-player theory of innovation cooperation, but that theory would need substantially new primitives and would not be this paper’s continuous mirror.
