The continuous mirror question

Given \(C\), \(k\), and a hidden rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over approval ballots \(B\subseteq C\), a learner proposes committees \(W\in\binom{C}{k}\). A rejection returns only an omitted candidate \(c\); it is valid if some \(\ell\in[k]\) has mass at least \(\ell/k\) on ballots approving \(c\) and fewer than \(\ell\) members of \(W\). What is the minimum worst-case number of rejected proposals a deterministic learner needs before acceptance, over candidate sets, hidden distributions, and truthful adaptive oracles?

The model it lives in

Types are approval ballots \(B\subseteq C\), with rational masses \(\mu_B\) summing to \(1\). The learner's decision is an adaptive sequence of size-\(k\) committees; the objective is to minimize the worst-case number of rejected proposals before acceptance under candidate-only EJR+ feedback.

The objection that survived

In candidate-only feedback, available responses depend only on the mass of eligible ballot types; every rational distribution can be expanded into a finite profile, so with the population scale known the query games coincide. The paper does not discuss this exact quotient, and the continuous formulation removes the known finite population size.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I believe is a continuous-population version of the paper’s deterministic, candidate-only EJR+ learning problem. The society is a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over approval ballots \(A\subseteq C\): \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of members with ballot \(A\). A plausible regime is a large association electing a committee from a fixed candidate menu, where many members share one of relatively few approval patterns and privacy rules disclose only the name of a candidate raised by an objection.

Here is the mirror problem. The learner knows \(C\), \(k\), and the target axiom, but not the fixed distribution \(\mu\). It proposes committees \(W\in\binom{C}{k}\). The oracle accepts exactly when \(W\) satisfies continuous EJR+, and otherwise returns only an omitted candidate \(c\). A valid hidden witness to that rejection consists of \(\ell\in[k]\) and a submeasure \(\nu\le\mu\) with total mass at least \(\ell/k\), supported on ballots that approve \(c\) and each approve fewer than \(\ell\) members of \(W\). The oracle may choose any valid witness, adaptively, while revealing only \(c\). The question, named Continuous Candidate-Only EJR+ Learning, is to determine the minimum worst-case number of rejected proposals a deterministic learner needs before acceptance, taking the worst case over \(m\), rational finite-support \(\mu\), and truthful oracle strategies. The learner’s solution is a proposal strategy; the measured resource is rejected queries.

My lead anchor is the paper’s Appendix Corollary labelled cor:asymptotic-greedy-level-sequence. The supplied text omits its rendered numeral, so I identify it by its exact label rather than guess. It is proved in this paper. It states that, as \(k\to\infty\), the deterministic rejection complexity for EJR+ is at least \((\gamma-o(1))k^{3/2}\), both with full-witness and candidate-only feedback, where \(\gamma=\int_0^\infty h(y)\,dy\approx1.1496\) and \(h\) is defined in the appendix. This is a lower bound on the number of rejected committee proposals, uniformly over electorate and candidate counts.

I expect this lower bound to transfer to the continuous question. The paper’s construction uses \(n=k\), so each voter becomes an atom of mass \(1/k\); aggregating identical ballots gives a rational distribution with at most \(k\) types. Cloning each voter preserves that distribution and gives an arbitrarily large population relative to its type support. Candidate-only feedback reveals no witness identities, so aggregating voters into ballot types does not remove information the learner was using. The reduction’s combinatorics remain in committee size and candidate choice. This is a hardness-transfer claim for rejection complexity, rather than a running-time classification: the paper puts no computational restriction on learners.

The continuous formulation is recognizable as the authors’ problem because it preserves their fixed hidden profile, committee proposals, truthful adversarial objections, and rejection-count objective. It replaces voter counts and quotas by population mass and the corresponding \(\ell/k\) quota. Ballots are complete types; committees remain discrete. The mass is part of the representation predicate and witness condition, not a fractional committee or a probabilistic voting rule.

This mirror covers the deterministic candidate-only EJR+ lower bound alone. It leaves the paper’s PJR+ results, randomized bounds, full-witness PAV algorithm, and candidate-only upper bounds outside scope. The follow-up question is whether the continuous candidate-only complexity has a polynomial upper bound or whether the gap above the transferred \(\Omega(k^{3/2})\) lower bound remains substantial; fractional witness masses could also yield a stronger lower bound.

My weakest point is that the anchor’s obstruction is driven by \(k\) and candidate search, not by population multiplicity: its finite construction already suffices, and the mass formulation may be viewed as a high-multiplicity version of the same interaction rather than a new optimization method. The mirror still asks a coherent question about a large society of ballot types, and its lower bound has a plausible large-population interpretation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would anchor the negative case on the appendix corollary cor:asymptotic-greedy-level-sequence, but it is a weak case. It is a named computational result, and its \((\gamma-o(1))k^{3/2}\) rejection lower bound transfers to the proposed distribution model: the construction’s \(n=k\) voters give rational ballot masses, and cloning them preserves those masses. A large association with repeated ballot types is a credible high-multiplicity scenario. There is no useful objection based on the anchor failing to survive continuization.

The strongest objection is that candidate-only EJR+ learning is already, operationally, a problem about the empirical distribution of approval types. For a committee \(W\), level \(\ell\), and omitted candidate \(c\), a continuous witness exists exactly when the mass of ballots \(B\) satisfying \(c\in B\) and \(|B\cap W|<\ell\) is at least \(\ell/k\). In the finite model, that is exactly the condition that enough voters have those ballots. Since the oracle reveals only \(c\), voter identities and the choice of submeasure are never exposed; only this threshold determines which responses are available. Giving the learner the same public population scale as in the paper makes the two query games identical after grouping voters by ballot. Every rational finite-support distribution can be expanded into a finite profile using a common denominator.

That makes the mirror look like a re-expression of the paper’s candidate-only game, rather than a new continuous computational object. The distinction is especially thin here because the resource is rejected queries, with no bound on the learner’s computation: no mass vector is ever presented to the learner for a continuous optimization method to act on. A version that withholds the population scale, as the proponent’s does, changes the learner’s information; that may be worth studying, but the added uncertainty is about the normalization being hidden, not a new population operation.

This does not establish that the mirror is unworthy. The programme explicitly treats high-multiplicity formulations as supporting evidence, and a well-posed query-complexity question can matter even when it has a simple dictionary to the finite model. I find no stronger ground: the anchor survives, and the universal negative is honestly weak. If the reader accepts the quotient-and-reparameterization objection as a genuine collision, it is the best one available; otherwise this anchor should stand.
