The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous population object here, but the paper gives no named computational result to anchor it. Its named propositions establish voting-rule properties, its theorem proves an axiomatic impossibility, and its manipulation findings are experimental. None asserts a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. The theorem in §5 says that for four or more alternatives no voting procedure can satisfy both the social disappointment criterion and the Condorcet winner criterion; it is proved in the paper, but it is not a computational result. In the supplied text, that theorem also has no number printed.

A plausible population regime would be a large election with many voters but a relatively small support of distinct complete rankings. A type would be a ranking \(\pi\), and \(\mu_\pi\) would be its fraction of the electorate. The paper’s LUR rule has a direct mass version: at each round, compute each remaining candidate’s mass of last-place rankings, retain those with the fewest, and repeat.

One possible continuous problem, motivated by the paper’s self-manipulation experiments, is: given a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, a target candidate \(q\), and the option for voters to swap their top two choices, find the minimum mass of voters who must do so to make \(q\) a LUR winner. Formally, choose \(x_\pi\in[0,\mu_\pi]\), where \(x_\pi\) is the manipulated mass of type \(\pi\), induce \(\mu'\) by moving that mass from \(\pi\) to the ranking \(\pi^{12}\) with its top two positions swapped, and minimize \(\sum_\pi x_\pi\) subject to \(q\in\mathrm{LUR}(\mu')\).

That is a concrete, potentially useful ChoCo question, and the paper’s discussion of manipulation gives it some connection to the authors’ concerns. But it is a new optimization problem, not a problem classified by one of the paper’s named results. The theorem’s continuous population counterpart would also be an axiomatic impossibility question, outside the computational focus. So the honest positive case is limited: the paper suggests a credible continuous-population research target, but supplies no eligible anchor for claiming that its computational landscape is being mirrored.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Its propositions and theorem concern properties of voting rules: whether they satisfy SDC, monotonicity, or the Condorcet winner condition. Its single-peakedness result is also a rule characterization. The manipulation section reports outcomes from experiments; it gives no algorithmic or complexity classification. So there is no named computational result here whose continuous counterpart would extend the paper’s contribution.

That does not make the population model implausible. A large electorate with relatively few ranking types is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. In that model, SDC can be stated using masses, and the impossibility claim can be asked again over distributions. But those are continuous versions of axiomatic questions, which ChoCo excludes as its goal. The faithful continuization preserves the kind of result while changing the population representation; it does not create a computational result to mirror.

The proposed LUR bribery problem is well-defined, and a stronger version could restrict manipulation to supporters of \(p\) who rank \(q\) second, then minimize the mass switching \(p\) and \(q\) to make \(q\) an LUR winner. That fits a large electorate with repeated rankings. But it adds an optimization objective the paper never studies. The paper’s self-manipulation experiment applies a prescribed ballot change and records whether the winner changes; it neither minimizes the amount of manipulation nor classifies the resulting problem. Such a question might be worthwhile for ChoCo, but it is a new problem inspired by the experiment, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

A mass robustness radius—minimum mass that must be reassigned to change the LUR outcome—would be another natural candidate, but it has the same gap. The experiments use several different interventions, including random ballot replacement, candidate deletion, and social influence. Turning them into one adversarial mass-transfer problem requires choosing a new manipulation model and objective. Preserving the experiments’ randomness in a continuum instead tends to replace random selection of voters with deterministic changes to type masses, changing what their reported frequencies measure.

So the negative case is strong as a screening judgment: this paper supplies no computational anchor, and its most plausible computational continuations are newly formulated problems rather than mirrors of its results. I cannot honestly establish the broader claim that *no* useful continuous question could be built from the paper. The high-multiplicity regime is credible, and mass-based LUR manipulation or robustness could be valuable research. That is the weak point in the case against.
