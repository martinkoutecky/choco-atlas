The continuous mirror question

Given four candidates \(C\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over their strict rankings, decide whether there are candidate locations \(p_c\in\mathbb R^2\) and, for every ranking type \(t\) with \(\mu_t>0\), a voter location \(q_t\in\mathbb R^2\) such that \(\|q_t-p_c\|_2<\|q_t-p_{c'}\|_2\) exactly when \(t\) ranks \(c\) above \(c'\).

The model it lives in

Types are strict rankings of four candidates, with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\). The decision variables are shared candidate locations \(p_c\in\mathbb R^2\) and one witness voter location \(q_t\in\mathbb R^2\) per supported type; the objective is feasibility. The masses matter only through whether \(\mu_t>0\).

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 2’s four-candidate \(\ell_2\) recognition criterion over positive-mass ranking types; it leaves the \(\ell_1\) maximum-support results, last-place bounds, and geometric lemmas aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is a continuous-population version of four-candidate \(\ell_2\)-Euclidean recognition. The paper’s best anchor is Theorem 2, proved here: a profile on four candidates is \(\ell_2\)-Euclidean exactly when, after possibly renaming candidates, its set of rankings is a subprofile of one of three specified 18-ranking profiles, \(\mathcal P^*_1,\mathcal P^*_2,\mathcal P^*_3\). The paper notes that this characterization was already proved by Kamiya et al.; its contribution is a simpler geometric proof. The theorem is not stated as a complexity-class result, but the paper says it makes recognition easy, and its finite characterization gives a direct recognition procedure.

Call the mirror Four-Candidate Continuous Euclidean Recognition. An instance consists of four candidates \(C\), a finite support \(S\) of strict rankings of \(C\), and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) for \(t\in S\), summing to \(1\). Ask whether there are candidate locations \(p_c\in\mathbb R^2\) and, for each \(t\in S\), a voter location \(q_t\in\mathbb R^2\) such that the candidates are strictly ordered by increasing \(\ell_2\)-distance from \(q_t\) exactly as ranking \(t\) specifies. A solution is a yes/no answer; in the yes case, the locations witness it.

A plausible regime is a very large electorate—say, users comparing four candidates in a voting-advice application—with many people sharing each of the at most \(24\) possible rankings. The type is the complete ranking used by this recognition problem; mass is the fraction of users with that ranking. This is precisely the paper’s object: it defines Euclidean realizability by the set of preferences and explicitly says duplicate rankings can be removed without loss. Theorem 2 therefore predicts tractability: check whether the support, up to candidate renaming, is contained in one of the three named profiles. In particular, any yes-instance has support size at most \(18\).

This is a legitimate but narrow positive case. The weakness is that the masses do no work: only whether \(\mu_t>0\) matters, so the mirror is effectively recognition of the same finite support the paper already studies. Also, Theorem 2 is a structural characterization for fixed \(m=4\), not a named P result, and the paper’s numbered results do not provide a hardness anchor. Its cited NP-hardness result for recognition in dimensions \(d>1\) is prior work, not a numbered result proved here. The useful follow-up is whether a weighted support formulation yields anything beyond the paper’s support-only recognition, and how the mirror behaves when the number of candidates varies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 gives a structural characterization, not a numbered complexity result. It does imply a recognizer for four candidates: check whether the ranking support, after renaming candidates, is contained in one of \(\mathcal P^*_1,\mathcal P^*_2,\mathcal P^*_3\). But that implication exposes the weakness of the proposed continuous mirror. For this question, the paper explicitly removes duplicate rankings without loss and measures profile size by the number of distinct preferences. Its input is already the set of ranking types.

Thus the proposed distribution \(\mu\) contributes only its support. For four candidates, the mirror’s condition is exactly that \(\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\) be contained in one of those three profiles, up to renaming. Any positive masses on the same support give the same answer; their binary encodings, population scale, and proportions play no role. Since there are at most \(24\) ranking types, this fixed-candidate version adds no high-multiplicity computation or optimization question to the paper’s support-only recognition criterion. Calling the input a continuous society changes its notation, not what the theorem decides.

The other prominent results do not supply a mass-sensitive anchor either. Theorem 1’s bound of \(19\), Theorem 3’s \(\Theta(m^4)\) bound, and Proposition 2’s limits on which candidates can be ranked last all count distinct rankings or ask whether a ranking occurs. Under a distribution, “occurs” becomes “has positive mass,” so each statement transfers as a support condition. A population may put nearly all its mass on one ranking or distribute it evenly across the support without changing any of these conclusions.

A better mass-sensitive proposal would be to find the largest share of a society whose rankings can be jointly explained by some Euclidean representation, or to measure the minimum mass that must change before the support is Euclidean. That is a plausible spatial-model fitting problem, but it is not a continuous version of Theorem 2’s recognition result. The paper’s geometry says which ranking regions are nonempty; it supplies no distribution of voters within those regions and no constraint linking a region’s size to its population mass. The weighted fitting objective would therefore need its own model and motivation. It may be worth studying, but it cannot rescue the proposed mirror by being treated as an implication of this theorem.

So the fixed-four recognition mirror should not be sold as a new computational question for ChoCo: the paper’s result already operates on the very support that determines the continuous answer. Still, I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. Recognition with a variable number of candidates remains a coherent population-support question, and a separately motivated weighted fitting problem may be useful. The negative case is strongest against this paper’s named results as anchors; it is weak as a proof that every continuous-population question suggested by Euclidean preferences is unworthy.
