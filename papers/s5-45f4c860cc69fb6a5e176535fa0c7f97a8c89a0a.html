What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the electorate, not the computational result. A large referendum with only two strict preference types has a natural continuous description: \(\mu_a\) of the population prefers \(a\), \(\mu_b\) prefers \(b\), and the support size is \(\tau=2\), even when the number of voters is enormous. That is a convincing high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s subject.

The nearest named result is Proposition 2, proved in the paper. For an odd electorate of \(n=2p+1\) voters, the Majority with Random Confirmations mechanism asks everyone to vote; it then randomly selects and orders \(p+1\) voters to confirm the vote-stage winner. The first \(Y\) ends the game with that winner; if all \(p+1\) say \(N\), the outcome is a lottery weighted by the vote shares. Proposition 2 says this mechanism subgame-perfect implements majority rule: every subgame-perfect equilibrium selects the majority-preferred option, and that option is attained in equilibrium.

A precise, if modest, continuous question suggested by Proposition 2 is Limit-RC Correctness. Its input is a rational distribution \(\mu=(\mu_a,\mu_b)\) with \(\mu_a\ne\mu_b\). Consider every sequence of odd finite electorates whose type proportions converge to \(\mu\), and apply the paper’s RC mechanism to each electorate. Does every sequence of subgame-perfect equilibrium outcomes eventually select the option preferred by the type with greater mass? A solution is a proof for all such approximating sequences or a counterexample sequence. The answer is yes: the strict majority margin is eventually preserved, and Proposition 2 gives the correct outcome in every equilibrium of each sufficiently large electorate. This is an immediate Class A limit result, and authors could recognize it as a high-multiplicity version of their implementation claim.

But there is no qualifying computational anchor in the paper. Proposition 2 is an implementation theorem, not a complexity or algorithmic result; the paper gives no named result classifying a problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized. Limit-RC Correctness therefore cannot satisfy the requested computational-anchor requirement, however natural its population interpretation. It does not yield the complexity question ChoCo is meant to chart.

The main weakness is also a modeling one. The RC mechanism’s incentives rely on individual pivotality and a sequential sample of \(p+1\) particular voters. In an atomless population, an individual’s vote has zero mass, and a continuum version of the random sequential confirmations needs new semantics. Keeping the finite electorate and taking a limit avoids those questions, but then the result is just the direct limit of Proposition 2. So this is a plausible population-continuous extension of the paper’s implementation result, but not a strong continuous *computational* mirror under the programme’s stated gate.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is a real implementation theorem, but it gives no computational result to continuize. The proposed “Limit-RC Correctness” is a consequence of that finite theorem: once \(\mu_a\ne\mu_b\), every sufficiently large approximating electorate has the same strict majority, and Proposition 2 settles every equilibrium in each such electorate. For the two-type input, the calculation is just comparing \(\mu_a\) and \(\mu_b\). There is no algorithmic problem or complexity landscape here.

A direct atomless version exposes a deeper problem with this anchor. RC’s incentives depend on an individual ballot changing the vote shares or the vote-stage winner. In a nonatomic society, one voter changes neither. Under the natural limit of the paper’s confirmation draw, which samples about half the electorate, every preference type with positive mass appears in the sample with probability approaching one. That does not restore ballot-level incentives: if everyone votes for \(b\), for example, a single voter’s deviation leaves the aggregate vote for \(b\) unchanged. The wrong outcome can therefore persist in a nonatomic equilibrium. The finite theorem’s implementation guarantee does not carry over.

One could preserve individual pivotality by keeping voters as atoms, but then the support grows with the electorate and the “continuous” model is a sequence of finite games. Or one could let whole preference types deviate together, but that replaces individual Nash equilibrium with coalition behavior and reduces the two-type case to a different, very small game. Neither is a compelling computational mirror of Proposition 2. The paper’s other named propositions are also implementation claims; none supplies a missing complexity result. Continuous large-population implementation may be a worthwhile analytic question, but this paper offers little to chart in ChoCo’s computational programme.
