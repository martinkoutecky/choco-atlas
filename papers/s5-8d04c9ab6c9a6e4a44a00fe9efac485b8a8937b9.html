What the mirror covers

Corollary 4 has a plausible population-level axiomatic analogue; Theorem 3 and Corollary 5 rely on effective dictatorship by a named individual. None of these results yields a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest foothold is the preference-aggregation result. In the paper’s Corollary 5, proved here, for the agenda representing strict preferences over at least three alternatives, every unbiased social welfare function with universal domain is effectively dictatorial: one individual determines each pairwise comparison, either directly or with their preference reversed. Its general source, Theorem 3, also proved here, says that on any non-separable, minimally connected agenda, every unbiased aggregation function \(F:U^n\to U\) is effectively dictatorial. These are genuine impossibility results, but they are axiomatic: neither theorem states a computational problem or a complexity bound.

There is a plausible continuous population model behind them. In a large public consultation, let a type be a complete, rational judgment set—such as a strict ranking of the alternatives—and let \(\mu_A\) be the fraction of the population with type \(A\). With a fixed agenda, the number of distinct types can remain bounded while the population grows. A natural question is whether a rule on these distributions can always produce a rational collective judgment while treating each proposition and its negation without bias. Since the distribution forgets individual identities, such a rule also builds anonymity into the model. The paper’s Corollary 4 says that, in the finite setting, no anonymous unbiased rule exists when the agenda has a non-separable, minimally connected subagenda. That is relevant evidence for this continuous analogue, not a collision or a complexity result.

The weakness is decisive for the requested case: the paper gives no named computational result to anchor a continuous computational problem. The natural continuation asks about the existence or properties of an aggregation rule over a whole space of population distributions; turning it into an algorithmic task would require adding a representation and computational objective that the paper does not provide. So the population mirror is plausible, but an honest case cannot supply the required per-anchor computational questions or predict their complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that none of the three anchors states a computational result. Corollary 5, Theorem 3, and Corollary 4 classify whether an aggregation function \(F:U^n\to U\) can satisfy global axioms on the universal domain. They give no instance-level algorithmic task, input encoding, objective, or complexity bound. Replacing profiles by a distribution \(\mu\) over judgment types gives a plausible continuous version of the same axiomatic question, but it does not supply the computational question this programme is meant to study.

Corollary 5 is especially hard to carry across intact. Its effective dictator is a particular individual who determines each pairwise preference, sometimes in reverse. A distribution over rankings forgets which individual that is; in an atomless society, each individual has zero mass. Keeping identity labels makes the support grow with the population and defeats the high-multiplicity model. Replacing the dictator with a representative type or a randomly selected person changes the claim: a type need not represent the same person across populations, and a random representative introduces lottery-valued outcomes. One can instead define unbiasedness directly over distributions. For example, if \(\mu_p\) is the mass accepting \(p\), compare \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) when \(\mu_p=1-\nu_p\). That is a coherent population-level axiom, but it remains a condition on a rule across many distributions, not a computational problem supplied by Corollary 5.

Theorem 3 has the same issue in its more general form: effective dictatorship is still defined by one named individual. Corollary 4 is the best anchor for the proponent, since anonymity is natural when the input is a distribution. A rule \(G:\Delta(U)\to U\) could be required to be unbiased and to return rational judgments. Yet this asks whether a whole rule exists satisfying global constraints. If the task is merely to return some rational judgment for a given \(\mu\), it is immediate: any type in the support is already rational. If the task is to construct or evaluate a rule satisfying the axioms over its entire domain, its complexity depends on a representation of that rule that the paper never gives. Retaining all the global axioms leaves an axiomatic existence problem; relaxing them requires a new computational objective.

There are attractive computational questions one could build nearby. Given \(\mu\), one might find a rational judgment set minimizing weighted disagreement with the population, or a ranking maximizing agreement with pairwise majorities. Those could lead to continuous optimization problems. But they add a loss function and a selection criterion absent from this paper; they do not mirror its impossibility theorems.

I would not claim that no sensible continuous population model exists. A large consultation with a fixed agenda and many people sharing complete judgment types is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and a population-level axiomatic analogue may be worth studying. The negative case is narrower: this paper supplies no computational result to anchor a continuous computational mirror, and its strongest preference result depends on individual identity in a way the distributional model removes. The case against is therefore strong on programme fit, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile related project exists.
