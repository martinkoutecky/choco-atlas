The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly supply a qualifying anchor: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its own findings are simulation estimates. The only theorem-shaped statement is its citation of Theorem 1 in Dougherty and Heckelman (2020): as reported here, that theorem says Group 1 voting systems violate IIA for at least three alternatives and two voters. It is an externally proved property of voting rules, not a complexity result. So there is no anchor under the criterion you set.

The strongest continuous mirror I can defend is narrower: the Pairwise Majority transitivity results in Table 5. That table estimates transitivity-violation frequencies across three to six alternatives and electorates from 3 to 10,000 voters, under IC and IAC. The paper reports that under three alternatives and IAC, the frequency tends to \(6.25\%\); its 10,000-voter estimate is \(6.34\%\).

Here is a precise continuous problem suggested by those results. Let the voter types be the \(m!\) strict rankings \(\pi\in S_m\). A society is a mass vector \(\mu\) in the simplex \(\Delta(S_m)\), where \(\mu_\pi\) is the fraction of voters with ranking \(\pi\). For alternatives \(a,b\), define
\[ p_{ab}(\mu)=\sum_{\pi:\,a\succ_\pi b}\mu_\pi, \]
and let Pairwise Majority rank \(a\) at least as high as \(b\) when \(p_{ab}(\mu)\ge \tfrac12\). Let \(\mathcal B_m\) be the set of mass vectors for which this social relation is nontransitive. The Pairwise-Majority IAC Continuum Volume problem is: given \(m\), compute the normalized volume
\[ V_m=\operatorname{Vol}_{\Delta(S_m)}(\mathcal B_m), \]
or return an estimate within additive error \(\varepsilon\) with confidence at least \(1-\delta\).

This is a plausible mirror of the paper’s IAC transitivity experiment. Under IAC, each anonymous profile is an integer composition of the electorate across the \(m!\) ranking types, and those compositions are equally likely. As the electorate grows while \(m\) stays fixed, the normalized compositions fill the simplex uniformly. Thus \(V_m\) is the continuous-population limit of the frequency being estimated. A plausible regime is a large electorate with a fixed, small set of alternatives: the number of voters grows while the number of possible ranking types stays at \(m!\). The mass vector is the evaluated society; there is no intervention variable because the paper studies violation frequency, not a response action.

For additive approximation, I expect this mirror to be tractable: sample \(\mu\) uniformly from the simplex, test Pairwise Majority for transitivity, and estimate the resulting probability. Exact volume computation is a separate question. This candidate covers the Pairwise Majority transitivity result only; it does not mirror the paper’s IIA tests or its full joint-violation comparison.

Its weakest point is that even this well-matched mirror is not anchored to a numbered complexity result in the paper. Also, the IAC randomness becomes a distribution over continuous societies: the paper’s finite-electorate experiments do not themselves formulate or solve this volume problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper offers no computational-complexity result to continuize. Its findings are simulation estimates under finite IC and IAC electorates; the cited IIA theorem belongs to Dougherty and Heckelman, and it is a rule property rather than an algorithmic result. That leaves no stated complexity landscape for ChoCo to carry across the population limit.

For a supplied mass vector \(\mu\), the Pairwise Majority transitivity test is just arithmetic on pairwise masses followed by checking triples. The paper does not pose a harder per-society computational task around that predicate. Its Table 5 anchor is a frequency estimate across randomly generated societies, not a result about computing on a society given as input.

The proponent’s IAC volume is the strongest repair, and I would not dismiss it because high-multiplicity voting is implausible or because it has a continuous answer. Uniform anonymous profiles do converge to uniform volume on the ranking simplex, so \(V_m\) is a coherent limit of the paper’s IAC statistic. The problem is that its input is \(m\), and its object is a measure over *all* societies. Exact volume computation would be a new geometric counting problem; it is not an algorithmic question the paper asks about a particular high-multiplicity society. A direct IC limit is less promising: the society concentrates at uniform mass, while retaining nontrivial cycle frequencies requires modeling fluctuations around that mass.

So I would recommend against treating this paper as a strong ChoCo anchor: the surviving proposal is a legitimate adjacent project, but the paper itself supplies no computational result that makes it the programme’s continuous mirror. That is a scope argument, not a proof that \(V_m\) is unworthy. The negative case is weakest precisely there: a reader who accepts global IAC volume computation as within ChoCo’s remit can reasonably keep the mirror.
