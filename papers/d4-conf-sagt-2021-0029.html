What the mirror covers

The proposed formulation targets the general-valuation welfare results in Theorems 11 and 12; it leaves the revenue guarantees and clock-auction/OXP implementation outside the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is real but narrow: this paper gives a mirror for the welfare approximation of truthful auctions. It does not prove an NP-hardness or polynomial-time result; its useful anchors are mechanism-approximation bounds. In the paper PDF, the matching results are printed as Theorems 11 and 12, and both are proved there.[Paper PDF](https://www.cs.drexel.edu/~vg399/Clock_Auctions_for_Symmetric_Interdependent_Valuations_.pdf)

A plausible regime is a very large auction for a mineral right or infrastructure concession, with many firms drawn from a small set of standardized expertise and valuation-function archetypes. A bidder type is the full public archetype—expertise class and value function—together with its private signal \(s\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu_{\theta,s}\) over those types, with support size \(\tau\ll n\). Mass is the fraction of bidders of each type. This requires specifying how value depends on the population: use the signal-mass histogram \(\mu_{\ell,r}\) for each expertise group, while retaining the individual’s own signal separately, as \(v_\theta(s,\boldsymbol\mu)\). The good stays indivisible: the mechanism outputs a lottery over individual winners.

Lead mirror — Atomless Truthful Welfare Frontier (Theorem 11). Theorem 11, proved in the paper, says that with \(k\) signal levels and \(\ell\) expertise classes, no ex-post IC auction can achieve a welfare approximation better than \(r=\ell\binom{k}{2}+1\). It also states a corresponding revenue lower bound for universally ex-post IC-IR auctions; the proof phrases its final benchmark as optimal welfare. The welfare lower bound uses monotonicity and feasibility: a construction with \(r\) bidders and rapidly separated values forces positive allocation probability for different bidders at different signal profiles, then forces those probabilities to coexist at one profile.

The continuous question is: given \(k,\ell\), a finite list of bidder archetypes with monotone rational piecewise-linear value maps \(v_\theta(s,\boldsymbol\mu)\), and rational population masses, what is the sharp worst-case welfare factor \(\alpha^*(k,\ell,\tau)\) for an ex-post IC-IR auction over every reported mass distribution? A solution is a mechanism, specified by a selection-intensity rule \(x_{\theta,s}(\mu)\) and transfers \(p_{\theta,s}(\mu)\), plus either a guarantee or a matching impossibility proof. Feasibility is \(\sum_{\theta,s}\mu_{\theta,s}x_{\theta,s}(\mu)=1\); the winner’s type has probability law \(\nu_{\theta,s}=\mu_{\theta,s}x_{\theta,s}\). In a finite \(n\)-bidder approximation, \(x\) corresponds to per-bidder winning probability \(x/n\), with transfers scaled the same way. The welfare objective is \(\sum_{\theta,s}\mu_{\theta,s}x_{\theta,s}v_\theta(s,\boldsymbol\mu)\), compared with the highest value among types in the society’s support.

My expected direction is Class A for this explicit finite encoding, potentially with \(\alpha^*=1\). In an atomless population, one bidder’s signal report does not change the aggregate histogram. That suggests choosing a highest-value cohort, distributing the winner lottery over its mass with an allocation rule monotone in each bidder’s own signal, and charging threshold payments. The finite theorem’s \(r\)-bidder monotonicity obstruction is therefore exactly what the prover should test for dissolution. This is not a claim that the paper proves the factor becomes \(1\); it is the proposed result to settle.

Mass-Mechanism Synthesis (Theorem 12). Theorem 12, also proved in the paper, gives a universally ex-post IC-IR auction with welfare factor \(r\). Its allocation gives probability \(1/\min\{n,r\}\) to the consistently tie-broken optimal bidder at a signal profile and propagates that allocation to higher reports of that bidder, making the rule monotone; threshold payments then implement it. The mirror question is: for the mass society and value maps above, construct a measurable winner lottery and threshold-payment rule that is feasible and universally ex-post IC-IR at every mass distribution, and prove its welfare factor as a function of \(k,\ell,\tau\). The expected direction is again tractable for fixed finite support and explicit value maps: the rule should operate on cohorts and their masses, rather than enumerate named bidders or signal profiles. Further questions include whether the paper’s revenue guarantee and clock-auction/OXP implementation also have meaningful mass versions.

The authors should recognize the subject: one indivisible good, interdependent values, private signals, truthful elicitation, and welfare against the best informed allocation all remain. This mirror covers the paper’s general-valuation welfare results; it does not claim to mirror the clock-auction implementation or revenue results.

My weakest point is the atomless incentive model itself. An individual has zero mass, so her report cannot change the shared signal histogram, and her literal probability of winning in a diffuse lottery is zero. Selection intensities and equally scaled transfers preserve the finite-\(n\) incentive inequalities, but a referee might regard that normalization—or the loss of individual influence—as changing the problem too much. Also, the paper permits arbitrary monotone functions on finite signal profiles; extending those functions to mass histograms is an additional modeling choice. That finite-to-continuum dictionary is part of the proposed work, not something this mirror gets for free.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these anchors is that their difficulty lives in a single bidder’s ability to change a shared quality estimate. An atomless population removes that ability. This is a substantive change to the incentive problem, not just a technical wrinkle in defining mass allocations.

For Theorem 11, the lower bound links profiles that differ in one named bidder’s signal. Monotonicity then forces allocation probability for several bidders to coexist at one profile, where feasibility rules that out. In a society represented only by type masses, one person’s report changes no mass. With the paper’s shared-quality values \(v_i(\mathbf q)\), it therefore changes no quality either. The linked profiles—and the incentive constraints that connect them—collapse in the limit. Keeping each bidder as an atom preserves those links, but then the support must grow with the number of bidders; grouping identical bidders restores small support only by making any one member’s report invisible.

Theorem 12 inherits the same problem. Its allocation rule propagates one bidder’s winning probability across profiles obtained by raising that bidder’s signal; threshold payments use those individual report comparisons to implement truthfulness. The proposed intensity rule \(x_{\theta,s}(\mu)\) can describe a lottery over cohorts, but an individual deviation leaves \(\mu\) unchanged and gives that individual zero probability of winning in the exact atomless limit. Standard individual IC is then vacuous. Multiplying utility by \(n\) can preserve finite-\(n\) incentive inequalities, but it adds a normalized, first-order incentive concept that the limiting society does not itself specify. Different finite populations can converge to the same \(\mu\) while retaining different effects from one person’s report.

The stronger versions of the mirrors face the same choice. Giving each agent a direct \(s\)-dependent value \(v_\theta(s,\mu)\) retains an individual signal effect, but adds a private-value component absent from the paper’s shared-quality setting. Giving a cohort a collective report restores influence, but changes independent bidders into strategic groups. Scaling supply with population can yield a meaningful mass allocation, but it changes the single-good auction and still leaves an individual unable to move aggregate quality. These may be worthwhile models; they do not recover the incentive problem behind either theorem. The revenue and clock-auction extensions rely on those same individual allocation and payment comparisons.

There is also a gap between the anchors and ChoCo’s main computational target. The paper proves welfare approximation bounds for truthful mechanisms, but does not give running-time or complexity classifications for a specified input problem. A complexity study of designing mechanisms over reported mass distributions could be valuable, but it would require a new strategic model and input encoding.

This is a strong objection to these particular mirrors, not an airtight case that no worthwhile continuous model exists. A limit theory that explicitly retains finite-\(n\) incentive margins might be interesting, and many standardized bidders are conceivable in some markets. The negative case is weakest precisely there: if the authors accept normalized, price-taking incentives as the object, the collapse of individual influence could become the result to study rather than a reason to reject the project.
