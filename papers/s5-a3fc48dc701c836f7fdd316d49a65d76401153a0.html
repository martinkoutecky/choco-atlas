The continuous mirror question

Fix a finite rooted hierarchy \(H\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) over supported types \(t\) attached to leaves, additive valuations \(u_t\), positive weights \(w_i\), and per-capita supplies \(q_g\in[0,1]\) for each \(g\in G\). Each unit of type mass must receive an integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\). Choose masses \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) such that \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=q_g\). Under root-child-common valuations, does a complete allocation exist satisfying, for every ordered sibling pair \(j,k\), \(\frac{U_j}{w_j}\ge \frac{\sum_g u_j(g)b_{k,g}}{w_k}\), where \(U_j=\sum_{t\text{ below }j,B}x_{t,B}u_t(B)\) and \(b_{k,g}=\sum_{t\text{ below }k,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}\)?

The model it lives in

Represent each type’s allocation by marginals \(z_{t,g}=\sum_{B:g\in B}x_{t,B}\). Since utilities and cohort-level envy constraints are additive, feasibility reduces to a rational LP with \(0\le z_{t,g}\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t z_{t,g}=q_g\), and linear sibling-fairness constraints; feasible marginals can be realized by distributions over integral bundles.

The objection that survived

Cohort averages can pass while a positive-mass recipient envies another recipient’s bundle; the one-good slack in individual M-WEF1 does not vanish for that recipient. The proposed scaling argument applies to cohort-normalized comparisons, so the mirror must be understood as group-level fairness.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3 under root-child-common valuations. It leaves the other identical-valuation guarantees, the general-valuation results, and the experiments untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • What finite-population replication or rounding guarantee relates cohort-level WEF feasibility to the paper’s individual M-WEF1 criterion?
  • Can the marginal LP extend beyond root-child-common valuations?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a narrow tractability mirror of Theorem 3. The paper has no named NP-hardness result to anchor a “hardness dissolves” story. Theorem 3 is proved here: under root-child-common valuations and arbitrary weights, MWRR returns an M-WEF1 allocation in polynomial time. Its proof extends the weighted round-robin guarantee to the paper’s multilevel setting, drawing on [11] and [19].

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large health or food distribution system: a fixed hierarchy of regions, districts, and local service units allocates standardized supplies to many recipients. Millions of recipients may share a much smaller set of complete types—defined by their place in the hierarchy and their additive valuations. For Theorem 3’s regime, recipients within each top-level branch share a valuation vector; different branches may value supplies differently. That is restrictive, but it is the paper’s own root-child-common condition, and standardized regional priorities make it credible in some settings.

Here is a continuous problem that preserves the paper’s allocation and fairness question. Fix a finite rooted hierarchy \(H\). Let \(T\) be the supported recipient types, each attached to a leaf, with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). Let \(G\) be a set of indivisible good categories, with per-capita supplies \(q_g\in[0,1]\), and let \(u_t(g)\) be type \(t\)’s additive value for good \(g\). Each recipient still gets an integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\); only the mass assigned to each bundle can be fractional. For each type and bundle, let \(x_{t,B}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(B\). Require \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}=q_g\), so all supply is allocated.

Call the search problem Mass-MWEF feasibility: find such a complete allocation satisfying weighted envy-freeness at every sibling comparison. Write \(b_{k,g}=\sum_{t\text{ below }k,\,B:g\in B}x_{t,B}\) for child \(k\)’s aggregate supply of \(g\), and \(U_j=\sum_{t\text{ below }j,\,B}x_{t,B}u_t(B)\) for child \(j\)’s aggregate welfare. Under root-child-common valuations, let \(u_j\) be the common valuation below \(j\). The fairness constraint for every ordered pair of siblings \(j,k\) is
\[ \frac{U_j}{w_j}\ \geq\ \frac{\sum_{g\in G}u_j(g)b_{k,g}}{w_k}. \]
This is the zero-slack limit of M-WEF1: in a population of size \(N\), removing one physical good changes normalized aggregate value by at most \(u_j(g)/N\), before division by \(w_k\). The co-modification from a fixed collection of goods to supplies that scale with population is natural for the scenario. It does not give fractional goods to any individual.

I expect Mass-MWEF feasibility to be tractable. Add variables \(z_{t,g}\in[0,\mu_t]\) for the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(g\). Completeness is \(\sum_t z_{t,g}=q_g\); the welfare and envy constraints above are linear in these marginals. Because preferences are additive, any feasible marginals can be realized by a distribution over integral bundles—for example, by assigning each good independently within each type. Thus the continuous question reduces to a rational LP with size polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), the hierarchy size, and the input bit length. It asks for the same hierarchical, weighted fairness property; the aggregation makes its constraints linear.

This mirror covers Theorem 3’s root-child-common result, not the paper’s general-valuation results or its experiments. The weakest point is that the paper guarantees WEF1 while the continuous limit asks for exact WEF, and the per-capita supply model changes how inventory scales. Those are real adaptations. The case survives because the tree, additive preferences, utilitarian group welfare, local sibling comparisons, weights, and individual-level bundle integrality remain in the model; exact WEF is the natural limit as one good becomes negligible relative to the population. The further questions are whether finite instances can be rounded from a Mass-MWEF allocation with a controlled \(O(1/N)\) fairness loss, and how far the LP approach extends beyond root-child-common preferences.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is the proponent’s right anchor: it is a genuine polynomial-time guarantee. The problem is that Mass-MWEF feasibility preserves the tree and the additive values, but changes what “fair” means.

In the paper, each child is an agent with one bundle, and M-WEF1 checks the child’s welfare against the value of a sibling’s bundle, after removing one good. The proposed LP instead compares cohort averages: \(U_j\) is the total welfare of the mass below \(j\), while \(b_{k,g}\) records how much of good \(g\) the mass below \(k\) receives. Those averages can hide unfair assignments within a cohort. For example, a positive mass of identical recipients could receive nothing while another positive mass receives a valuable bundle; the cohort average may satisfy the LP constraint even though an empty recipient envies a recipient with that bundle beyond one good.

That also weakens the claimed \(O(1/N)\) link. Removing one good has \(O(1/N)\) effect on a normalized cohort total when inventory scales with the population. It need not have a small effect on an individual’s WEF1 comparison: that person’s bundle and the good removed remain integral. So exact cohort WEF is a plausible new fairness criterion, but it is not the limit of the paper’s individual-child WEF1 merely because the population grows.

A more faithful mirror would retain the bundle distribution and require fairness for the individual allocations that occur with positive mass, including comparisons between recipients of the same type when they are siblings. The marginals \(z_{t,g}\) are then insufficient: they do not reveal which goods occur together in a recipient’s bundle, and the simple LP does not establish the guarantee. Keeping duplicated recipients as separate nodes preserves the paper’s comparisons, but also preserves a tree whose size grows with the population; the proposed support-size compression no longer follows.

Scaling supplies is a natural adaptation for some settings, such as recurring food distribution. The stronger objection is therefore not that the supply model is inherently implausible. It is that the proponent’s efficient formulation gets its simplicity by asking for fairness between aggregate cohorts, while the paper asks for envy protection between child agents. A cohort-fairness problem might still be valuable—and the paper’s group structure makes it a plausible extension—but the case for it must stand on its own, not on Theorem 3’s WEF1 guarantee.

That is the strongest negative case I can support, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. If the authors would accept cohorts as the units being compared, the proposed question may survive the programme’s modelling gate. The negative case is strongest against the claimed LP mirror of Theorem 3, and weak against the universal claim.
