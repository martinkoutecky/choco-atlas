The continuous mirror question

Given two atomless role populations, each with a finite action set and a common conditional ranking map ρ_i(a_{-i}), a scoring rule ν, and an iteration count \(T\), compute the WFP action-mass distributions \(x_T^{(1)},x_T^{(2)}\) and certify the stated ε_T bound. If the guarantee is measured against latent cardinal payoffs, the instance also supplies bounds \(D_i\) on the paper’s ̅d_i^+ terms.

The model it lives in

Each role is a mass-one cohort of indistinguishable agents with the same complete conditional preference map. Opposite-role actions are anonymously matched according to the other cohort’s mass distribution; those masses induce the distribution of context rankings used by ν. WFP updates the cohort action-mass vectors \(x_i\in\Delta A_i\), and the output is the pair of distributions after \(T\) rounds.

The objection that survived

The guarantee covers one common action distribution per homogeneous role; ordinary random matching does not itself derive the paper’s counterfactual context aggregation, and the result gives no guarantee for multiple distinct types.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the WFP approximate-equilibrium guarantee and its inherited cardinal-regret bound. It leaves the paper’s existence, regularization, distortion, complexity, and experimental results outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the WFP guarantee extend to cohorts with τ>1 distinct preference-map types?
  • Can the approximation guarantee improve beyond the asymptotic bound stated in the paper?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a narrow population-game mirror of the paper’s approximate-equilibrium result.

My anchor is Corollary 1 (Theorem 1 [22]). It says that after \(T\) rounds, weakened fictitious play (WFP) produces a two-player profile \(x_T=[x_T^{(1)},x_T^{(2)}]\) that is an \(\epsilon_T\)-Nash equilibrium, with

\[ \epsilon_T \leq \frac{T+1}{2T} +\frac{T-1}{2T}\max_i \bar d^+(\nu,s(u_i)). \]

Here \(\bar d^+\) bounds how much the social-choice best response can fall short of the best response for an underlying cardinal utility \(u_i\). The paper’s Theorem 2 gives the bound \(\bar d^+(\nu,s(u_i))=\kappa_i^+ + (\min_k s_k)d^+(\nu)\), where \(\kappa_i^+=\max_k s_k-\min_k s_k\). Corollary 1 cites the WFP result as Theorem 1 of [22]; Appendix C.1 traces that argument to establish the stated bound for approximate best responses. This is an algorithmic approximation result, not a classification such as NP-hardness or membership in P.

Here is a population version I think the authors could recognize. Consider an ongoing two-sided evaluation arena with a large pool of participants in each of two roles, repeatedly matched one from each side. Within each role, participants have the same action menu and the same complete conditional preference map: for every opponent action, they share the same ranking of their own actions. Thus there are many agents but just one type per role. A type’s mass is the fraction of its role population choosing each action. If \(x_{-i}(a_{-i})\) is the fraction of opposing agents choosing \(a_{-i}\), then that much mass induces the ballot \(\rho_i(a_{-i})\) over role \(i\)’s actions. A fixed scoring rule such as Borda aggregates those ballots.

The resulting problem, Two-Cohort Continuous CO-Equilibrium Approximation, is: given the two finite action sets, the conditional rankings, a scoring rule, and an iteration count \(T\), find the action-mass distributions \(x_1,x_2\) produced by WFP and certify their \(\epsilon_T\)-equilibrium guarantee. If the target is regret against latent cardinal utilities, include promised bounds \(D_i\) on the paper’s \(\bar d^+\); for the scoring-induced game itself, the ordinal scoring responses are the relevant best responses. The solution is a pair of distributions over actions, each summing to one, with the population fraction on each action equal to its mass. There is no scalar objective to minimize: the task is to compute an approximate equilibrium.

This is a sensible large-\(N\), small-\(\tau\) regime: two large role populations, with \(\tau=1\) within each role. Under random matching and atomless populations, an individual’s deviation does not change the opposing action distribution. The population best-response condition therefore has the same form as the paper’s mixed-strategy condition, while mixed-strategy probabilities become literal population shares. For fixed \(T\), WFP can be run by evaluating the finite action scores each round, so this particular approximation task appears tractable in the explicit action-set size and \(T\). That runtime observation is an inference from the construction, not a theorem proved in the paper. Also, the stated bound approaches \(1/2+\frac12\max_i\bar d^+(\nu,s(u_i))\) as \(T\) grows; it does not promise arbitrary accuracy.

This mirror covers the paper’s WFP approximation result, not its full equilibrium theory or its four-person human election analysis. Natural next questions are whether the guarantee extends to \(\tau>1\) heterogeneous types, how runtime scales in \(m,\tau,L,T\), and what the exact or much more accurate equilibrium problem costs in this continuum population game.

The soft spot is the population interpretation itself. In the paper, \(x_i\) is a finite player’s mixed strategy; in the mirror, it is an actual mass of people choosing actions. That identification is natural for large randomly matched cohorts, but it is a modeling extension, not a population theorem in the paper. The article also discusses hardness in unnumbered complexity passages, including a known-support maximal-lottery formulation tied to a cited NP-hard QCQP. Under the requested anchor rule, I would not present that as a numbered hardness result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 1 is the right anchor: it is a named algorithmic approximation result. I would not dismiss it as merely axiomatic. The difficulty for the proposed mirror is that its “population” does no computational work. For each player \(i\), the distribution over induced ballots is already determined by the opponent’s mixed strategy:
\[ \mu_i(\rho)=\sum_{a_{-i}:\rho_i(a_{-i})=\rho}x_{-i}(a_{-i}). \]
A two-cohort population with one complete type per role gives exactly these same distributions. Its mass vectors, best responses, WFP iterates, and \(\epsilon_T\) guarantee are the paper’s mixed strategies and calculations under new names. Population size never enters the input or running time. The mirror therefore supplies no new high-multiplicity computational problem for this result to answer.

The stronger population-game version exposes the modeling choice. If people are independently matched and respond to the opponent action they actually encounter, their best response can depend on that realized context. The paper instead aggregates the rankings induced by *all possible* contexts according to a social choice rule, then returns a distribution over actions. To preserve that rule, every person in a role must act on the same aggregate of counterfactual contexts. That is a coherent model, but it clones the paper’s strategic player; it does not explain its WFP process as decentralized population behavior. If the role instead chooses one shared distribution centrally, that distribution is again the paper’s mixed strategy. With multiple types per role, the corollary supplies no corresponding guarantee.

So the best case against this anchor is a collision of computational objects: the proposed continuous calculation is already exactly the calculation in the paper, while a population model that changes the calculation is no longer the problem Corollary 1 addresses. This is not a claim that the guarantee is uninteresting or too weak.

The negative case is still limited. A homogeneous, anonymously matched population is a sensible scenario, and the authors might recognize its equilibrium question as theirs. The strongest objection is that this gives a new population interpretation of an existing simplex calculation, not a new computational landscape. That makes the case against this anchor plausible, but not an airtight argument that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
