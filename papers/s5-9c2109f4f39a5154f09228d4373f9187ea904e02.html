The continuous mirror question

Given a finite project set \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a finite set \(T\) of additive utility types with rational masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and utilities \(u_t:C\to\mathbb{N}\), find a committee \(W\subseteq C\), \(|W|\leq k\), such that for every nonempty \(Q\subseteq T\) and every \(D\subseteq C\) with \(\sum_{t\in Q}\mu_t\geq |D|/k\), \(\max_{t\in Q}u_t(W)\geq\min_{t\in Q}u_t(D)\).

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete additive utility types, with indivisible unit-cost projects and a committee \(W\) of size at most \(k\) as the decision variable; the objective is to find any committee satisfying mass-FJR.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the paper’s FJR committee-search hardness results, including Theorems 3.3 and 4.1. It leaves the PJR and EJR results, the EAR guarantee, and the sequential-rule limitations outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity boundary for mass-FJR search in the support size \(\tau\) and input bit length, especially when utilities are bounded and masses are arbitrary rationals?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a search problem for an FJR-satisfying slate in a society described by utility types and their population masses. An agent’s type is their complete additive utility function \(u_t:C\to\mathbb N\); the society is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types. Keep projects indivisible and unit-cost, and keep the decision variable as a committee \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|\leq k\). The objective is feasibility: find \(W\) satisfying FJR.

For a coalition taking mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) from each type, let \(S=\{t:x_t>0\}\). The continuous condition is: for every nonempty coalition and every project set \(D\subseteq C\), if \(\sum_t x_t\geq |D|/k\), then \(\max_{t\in S}u_t(W)\geq\min_{t\in S}u_t(D)\). Equivalently, it suffices to check type sets \(S\) with \(\mu(S)\geq |D|/k\). A violating coalition can be expanded to include all mass of each type it uses: that preserves the utility extrema and can only increase its mass. The same argument works for discrete voters with identical type utilities, so cloning a profile into many copies gives an exact discrete-to-continuous dictionary for FJR.

My lead is Two-Type Mass-FJR Committee Search. Its input is a project set \(C\), a committee size \(k\), and two utility types with masses \(\mu_1=\mu_2=1/2\) and binary-encoded nonnegative integer utilities. It asks for a committee of size at most \(k\) satisfying the mass-FJR condition above.

The anchor is Theorem 3.3, proved in this paper: unless \(P=NP\), no polynomial-time voting rule can return an FJR-satisfying committee on every unit-cost election with exactly two voters and general utilities. The proof reduces from a restricted Knapsack problem; the authors show that a Knapsack solution makes the constructed “selfish” committee violate FJR, while in the no-instance it is the unique FJR committee. The theorem is weak NP-hardness, driven by the encoded utility values. Its reduction uses two distinct utility vectors, so replacing each voter with a large homogeneous bloc gives exactly the two-type mass instance above. Thus hardness transfers even when the population is arbitrarily large and the support stays at \(\tau=2\). [The paper, Theorem 3.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02030)

That regime has a plausible participatory-budgeting interpretation: two large, equally sized districts or stakeholder blocs, with members in each bloc sharing a project-utility profile. The paper itself motivates proportional representation through districts and discusses additive utilities in budgeting and deliberation, so the authors should recognize the mass-based coalition entitlement as their problem. Here \(N=2q\) agents can grow while \(\tau=2\).

A second, stronger-in-numbers anchor is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper: unless \(P=NP\), no polynomial-time voting rule can always return an FJR committee for unit-cost committee elections even when every utility lies in \(\{0,\ldots,5\}\). The authors reduce from PartialTripleCover: voters encode left-side vertices, projects encode right-side vertices, and a partial triple cover makes the selfish committee fail FJR; absent a cover, that committee is the unique FJR committee. For its mirror, call the problem Bounded-Utility Mass-FJR Committee Search: the input has \(\tau\) types, each of mass \(1/\tau\), utilities in \(\{0,\ldots,5\}\), and unit-cost projects; the task is to find an FJR committee. The reduction makes \(k=\tau\) and each voter’s private project makes that voter a distinct type, so its discrete instance maps directly to this equal-mass society. This is strong NP-hardness, again transferred from the discrete problem. [The paper, Theorem 4.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02030)

The second regime can be pictured as many equally sized neighborhood or interest cohorts, each sharing a utility profile. There, \(N=q\tau\) can be large while \(\tau\) grows with the instance. The reduction’s graph-coded profiles are synthetic, though, so this is weaker evidence for a natural empirical setting than the two-bloc mirror.

Both questions are expected to be Class B: the discrete hardness embeds, and the reduction’s combinatorics lie in selecting projects and the utility structure, not in population granularity. This mirror covers the paper’s FJR outcome-search results only. It does not continuize project bundles, lotteries, time, or uncertainty.

The weakest point is that the lead result’s hardness depends on large, binary-encoded utilities, and the mirror does not reveal a continuous-optimization speedup; it is a faithful Class B question. The strong-hardness companion avoids large utilities but needs a growing support and a more constructed type pattern. Those limits narrow the claim, but they do not erase the case for the mirror: the two-type problem is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the strong-hardness result shows where the broader mass formulation still inherits the paper’s computational boundary. A natural next question is whether bounded utilities yield fixed-parameter algorithms in \(\tau\), or only the polynomial-time behavior available when the number of types is fixed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that both proposed anchors turn the paper’s finite voter rows into masses without changing the FJR question. For any coalition using a positive amount of each type in a set \(Q\), expanding it to all of those types preserves the utility extrema and can only increase its mass. So mass-FJR depends on \(Q\) only through whether \(\mu(Q)\geq |D|/k\); the coalition’s fractional choices within those types disappear.

That makes the two-type, equal-mass mirror exactly the paper’s two-voter FJR hardness instance, written as two blocs of mass \(1/2\). The bounded-utility mirror similarly uses one type per voter and mass \(1/\tau\), so it is the paper’s bounded-utility hardness instance written as a distribution. Neither anchor shows a new continuous effect. The second also does not show hardness with a small fixed number of types.

A stronger mirror would allow arbitrary rational masses, especially for two large districts of unequal sizes. That is coherent and could pose a new algorithmic question: the masses give compressed multiplicities, and the entitlement thresholds can differ from the equal-mass cases in the paper. But this does not rescue a strong negative case. It is precisely a high-multiplicity problem, and the paper’s type-based appendix makes that regime more credible. Rational masses can be scaled to integer voter counts, though the expanded electorate may be much larger than the encoded input. The resulting model still has a computational question about finding an FJR committee.

Likewise, cloning the types in the bounded-utility reduction gives each distinct utility profile many members while preserving the same mass distribution. The reduction’s profiles are constructed, and the number of types grows with the instance, but a cohort-per-profile story is possible. Those facts weaken the naturalness of that particular example; they do not rule out the better version.

So the negative case can say that these anchors do not establish a new continuum-specific phenomenon or a route to continuous-optimization gains. It cannot honestly say that no worthwhile mirror exists. FJR’s extrema make mass-FJR a weighted type-support problem rather than a smooth coalition-mass problem, but the programme explicitly includes computational questions over such distributions. I do not see a principled objection that defeats the arbitrary-rational-mass mirror; the universal claim is weak.
