The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses \(\mu_t\) over finitely many advertiser types \(t=(v_{t1},\ldots,v_{tJ},\lambda_t,\tau_t)\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\) of slot classes, compute market-clearing prices \(p_j\) and per-advertiser type allocations \(x_{tj}\). Each type must maximize \(\sum_j v_{tj}x_{tj}\) subject to \(\sum_j p_jx_{tj}\le\lambda_t\) and \(\sum_j v_{tj}x_{tj}\ge\tau_t\sum_j p_jx_{tj}\), with allocations clearing supply, \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=q_j\).

The model it lives in

Use the finite-support extended Eisenberg–Gale program with variables \(x_{tj},c_t,d_t\): maximize \(\sum_t\mu_t[\lambda_t\log c_t-d_t/\tau_t]\), subject to \(c_t=\sum_jv_{tj}x_{tj}+d_t\), \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le q_j\), and \(x_{tj},d_t\ge0\). Its dual prices and clearing allocations describe the market where advertisers remain price-takers.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's equilibrium characterization and Theorem 3's revenue guarantee. It leaves Theorem 2's incentive-compatibility claim and Theorems 4–5's online regret analysis outside the population mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What exact or approximation bit-complexity bounds can be proved in the support size, number of slot classes, and input bit length?
  • Does the per-capita clone dictionary preserve the paper's tight revenue example for a growing population?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible, but narrow, positive case. The paper has no named hardness result and no theorem placing a problem in a standard complexity class. Its best computational anchors are Theorem 1, an equilibrium-computation characterization that the paper uses to construct its mechanism, and Theorem 3, a named revenue-approximation result. Both are proved here. Theorem 1 is my lead; Theorem 3 gives a second, distinct question about the same market.

The natural regime is a large advertising platform with many templated campaigns: for example, local or franchise advertisers using the same campaign template, budget tier, and target RoS. Let a type \(t\) specify an advertiser’s complete value vector across a small catalogue of ad-slot classes, \(v_{tj}\), together with its budget \(\lambda_t\) and RoS target \(\tau_t\). Advertisers of that type are interchangeable for everything the model uses. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(K\) supported types, with \(n\gg K\). To keep competition and supply on the same scale as the population grows, let class \(j\) have \(q_j\) units of inventory per advertiser: a population of \(N\) advertisers has \(Nq_j\) copies of that slot class. This is the allowed per-capita supply co-modification. Each type’s allocation below is per advertiser on average; fractional values can arise from splitting identical auction opportunities or from the paper’s fractional allocation rule.

Theorem 1 (Equilibrium Characterization), proved here, is the lead anchor. It says that feasible solutions to the paper’s primal and dual programs are both optimal if and only if the allocation, prices, and supplies form a competitive equilibrium, with supply equal to allocation. It also says every equilibrium is Pareto efficient and that all equilibria have the same prices and revenue, even if allocations differ. The paper uses this characterization to obtain the market-clearing outcome. The theorem is not an \( \mathrm{P} \) result; its computational force is that it identifies the equilibrium with an optimum of a convex program the paper actually uses.

My lead mirror is Finite-Support Continuous Auto-Bidding Equilibrium. Its input is the type distribution \(\mu\), rational values \(v_{tj}\), budgets \(\lambda_t\), RoS targets \(\tau_t\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\), with the paper’s competitive-market and positive-value assumptions. It asks for market-clearing prices \(p_j\) and per-type allocations \(x_{tj}\) such that each advertiser receives a utility-maximizing allocation subject to its budget and RoS constraints, and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}=q_j\) for each slot class. Payments per advertiser are \(\sum_j p_jx_{tj}\); the constraints are \(\sum_jp_jx_{tj}\le\lambda_t\) and \(\sum_jv_{tj}x_{tj}\ge\tau_t\sum_jp_jx_{tj}\). A solution is an equilibrium price-allocation pair, including a split among indifferent types where needed. The type-level extended Eisenberg–Gale program gives a finite-dimensional way to compute and certify it: its objective is \(\sum_t\mu_t[\lambda_t\log c_t-d_t/\tau_t]\), with \(c_t=\sum_jv_{tj}x_{tj}+d_t\), \(d_t\ge0\), and \(\sum_t\mu_tx_{tj}\le q_j\). The mirror question is the underlying equilibrium computation; the program is its computational witness.

I expect this problem to be Class A. For an explicit support of size \(K\), the program has \(O(mK)\) allocation variables rather than one set per advertiser. Its pricing constraints only range over the supplied types, not every conceivable advertiser profile. Still, the paper does not give a bit-complexity bound for exact solutions, so proving polynomial-time computation at a specified precision in \(m,K,L\) is a further question, not a result I would attribute to Theorem 1.

Theorem 3 (Revenue Guarantee of the Market-Clearing Mechanism), also proved here, supplies a second anchor. It says the mechanism’s revenue is at least \(\tfrac12\) of first-best revenue, and therefore at least \(\tfrac12\) of the revenue of any feasible revenue-optimal mechanism. Here the first-best benchmark maximizes revenue subject to each type’s budget and RoS constraints and the inventory constraints.

The corresponding problem is Continuous-Market Half-Revenue Benchmarking. Given the same type distribution and inventory, compute the market-clearing mechanism’s revenue \(R^{MC}=\sum_jq_jp_j\) and the first-best per-capita revenue, obtained by maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_tr_t\) over allocations and payments with \(r_t\le\lambda_t\), \(r_t\le\frac{1}{\tau_t}\sum_jv_{tj}x_{tj}\), and \(\sum_t\mu_tx_{tj}\le q_j\). A solution consists of an equilibrium attaining \(R^{MC}\), a first-best optimum, and their revenues. The expected direction is again Class A: the benchmark is an LP and the market-clearing side has the convex characterization above. Theorem 3’s ratio itself transfers cleanly to rational clone instances: clear denominators so there are \(N\mu_t\) advertisers of each type and \(Nq_j\) auctions of each class, apply the paper’s finite-market guarantee, and normalize revenue by \(N\). Whether its tight example remains tight under a nontrivial growing type population and per-capita inventory is a useful further question.

This is a plausible mirror of the authors’ market: advertisers remain the agents, their full financial and valuation profiles define their types, and the target remains market-clearing prices and allocations. The paper’s fractional allocations and high volume of auctions do not themselves make the advertiser population continuous. The mirror does. Its scope is Theorems 1 and 3. I would not extend it to Theorem 2’s incentive-compatibility claim: when advertisers become infinitesimal, unilateral reports may no longer affect market prices, so preserving the original incentive question would require additional modeling. Nor am I treating Theorems 4–5’s repeated-auction regret bounds or the non-atomic valuation assumption as population continuization; those concern the auction sequence or value randomness.

The weakest point is that exact type repetition is demanding in advertising. Public predicted values vary across accounts, and budgets can be idiosyncratic. The case rests on a narrower but plausible regime—many templated campaigns, a compact catalogue of slot classes, and common value predictions and financial settings—not on advertisers in general. Its scientific payoff is also modest: this is a faithful high-multiplicity representation of a market the paper already computes by convex optimization, rather than an instance where discrete hardness is known to dissolve. That limits the claim, but leaves a concrete, author-recognizable continuous-population equilibrium and revenue problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the mirrors need a much narrower market than the paper’s general model. A type must include an advertiser’s complete valuation vector \( (v_{t1},\ldots,v_{tm}) \), as well as its budget and RoS target. Thus, to keep support size \(K\) small, many advertisers must have the same values for every auction in the catalogue. In the paper’s online setting, values vary across auctions and are assumed non-atomic; exact repetition of full vectors is a demanding regime. If “type” instead means a campaign template whose realized values vary by impression, the type masses no longer preserve the paper’s allocation and bidding problem without adding a model of those value distributions.

That objection does not defeat the proposed stronger version. A platform with repeated slot classes and many advertisers sharing the same complete profile is a coherent clone market, and scaling inventory per capita is a legitimate co-modification. In that setting, the type-level program preserves the budget and RoS constraints for each clone, and Theorem 1’s equilibrium characterization carries over. The paper’s non-atomic assumption does not rule this out for Theorem 1: it concerns the online first-price implementation, while the equilibrium characterization allows the seller to coordinate ties.

There is a narrower computational objection. Theorem 1 characterizes equilibrium through an explicit convex program; it does not give a complexity theorem, and the proposed finite-support program has only \(O(Km)\) allocation variables. Theorem 3 is a revenue guarantee, not a computational result, and its ratio follows directly on rational clone instances by scaling the finite market and normalizing revenue. So these mirrors seem to offer little new complexity landscape: one asks to solve the program already supplied, and the other largely restates a guarantee that transfers by normalization. That is a modest-payoff argument, though not a decisive one: bit complexity and a formal high-multiplicity dictionary could still be useful, and a dull answer does not make a well-posed question unworthy.

I cannot honestly defeat both anchors in every plausible scenario. The realistic-value objection weakens the mirror for heterogeneous, impression-specific advertising, but the standardized clone-market version survives and is one the authors could recognize. Theorems 4–5 do not rescue or undermine that case: they concern the auction sequence and regret, not a continuous population. The negative case is therefore limited; the proponent’s narrow regime remains credible.
