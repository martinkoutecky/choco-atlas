What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying named computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, says that a responsibility measure satisfies anonymity, monotonicity, additivity, tail-convergence, recursivity, and consistency if and only if it has the discounted upstream/downstream form in Equation (3), with \(\gamma,\delta\in[0,1)\) and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\). That is a mathematical characterization, not a result that a computational problem is in P or hard. Propositions 2 and 3 establish identities with consumption-based and income-based accounting. The paper computes responsibility allocations in its empirical section, but gives no named complexity result. So there is no honest anchor to restate as a computational result.

The strongest positive case I can make is therefore a follow-on computational question suggested by Theorem 1, not a mirror anchored in a qualifying result. Consider many production facilities in recurring process classes. A type specifies a facility’s emissions rate and its normalized upstream and downstream shares with each class; facilities within a type share those attributes. Suppose there are \(N\) facilities but only \(\tau\ll N\) types. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for standardized facilities or suppliers, though it is not the country-sector network studied in the paper.

Define Type-Level Responsibility Evaluation as follows. An instance gives rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), per-facility emissions \(f_t\), upstream and downstream type-level transition matrices \(U,D\) with nonnegative entries and columns summing to \(1\), and rational parameters \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), \(\gamma,\delta\in[0,1)\). The total direct emissions of type \(t\) are \(E_t=\mu_t f_t\). The task is to return the responsibility allocation
\[ R=\lambda(1-\gamma)(I-\gamma U)^{-1}E+ (1-\lambda)(1-\delta)(I-\delta D)^{-1}E. \]
The assigned responsibility per facility of type \(t\) is \(R_t/\mu_t\). This is an allocation-computation problem, not an optimization problem; its output is the responsibility vector. The stochastic normalization gives \(\sum_t R_t=\sum_t E_t\), matching the paper’s no-double-counting condition.

This looks like a sensible population mirror of the paper’s responsibility-allocation model: population mass replaces counts of individual facilities, while type-level network shares retain the upstream and downstream relationships that drive responsibility. For this explicitly listed finite-support input, I would expect Class A: rational matrix inversion computes the allocation in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length. This covers computation of the responsibility measure, not the paper’s axiomatization; continuizing that axiomatic question would be outside the programme’s scope.

The weakness is substantial: the paper does not prove this algorithmic classification, and the finite matrix formula makes it fairly immediate. More seriously, facilities must share their relevant network behavior as well as their emissions profile for aggregation by type to preserve the question. If actual supply links vary idiosyncratically, the type set may grow until the high-multiplicity regime disappears. Thus this is a credible, narrow mirror of the underlying allocation problem, but it does not turn the paper’s results into a computational-complexity contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic characterization; Propositions 2 and 3 identify accounting measures with special cases of the formula. The empirical section computes allocations, but it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. Under the programme’s stated scope, those are not computational anchors.

The proposed Type-Level Responsibility Evaluation is coherent, but it is a new task rather than a mirror of one of those results. Its formula is the paper’s existing responsibility formula with \(E_t=\mu_t f_t\). The matrices \(U,D\) stay fixed as \(\mu\) varies, so the mass vector only changes the emission input to a finite network calculation. Moreover, the paper already allows real-valued emissions and gives the resolvent formula. Calling network nodes “types” does not by itself create a new computational question about a continuous population. This is not a rejection merely because exact evaluation is easy; the problem is that the paper already supplies the calculation, while proving an axiomatic characterization is outside the programme’s computational scope.

A stronger version could model many facilities with identical emissions and identical upstream and downstream profiles, or introduce decisions about abatement, taxes, or network links. The first gives an exact type-level network, but still reduces to the paper’s finite-network formula. The second could yield a substantial optimization problem, but the paper supplies no decision variables, cost functions, or constraints for it; those would define a new research problem rather than mirror a result here. Aggregating less uniform facilities also needs care: their differing network positions affect the walk sequences used to assign responsibility, so class averages need not preserve the individual allocations.

I cannot rule out every worthwhile follow-on study of continuous facility populations. The negative case is strongest and clearest against the proposed anchor and against treating this paper’s axiomatic and empirical results as computational contributions: neither establishes the kind of computational question the programme is meant to mirror.
