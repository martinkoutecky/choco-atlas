The continuous mirror question

Given recurring indivisible good categories \(G\), valuation types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational per-capita supplies \(0\le\rho_g\le1\), and monotone XOS valuations \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb{R}_+\) accessed through per-type demand and XOS oracles, find a feasible mass allocation \(x_{t,S}\ge0\) satisfying \(\sum_{S\subseteq G}x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S:g\in S}x_{t,S}\le\rho_g\), with \(\operatorname{NSW}_{\infty}(x)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S)\right)\) under the usual zero convention; determine whether achieving \(\operatorname{NSW}_{\infty}(x)\ge\left(1-\frac1e+\varepsilon\right)\max_y\operatorname{NSW}_{\infty}(y)\) requires exponentially many per-type oracle queries, independently of the finite realization size \(N\).

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity recurring-goods fair-division model in which \(N\) clones realize masses \(\mu_t\) and \(N\rho_g\) indivisible category copies; \(x_{t,S}\) assigns type mass to integral bundles, and the objective is the geometric mean of individual values, with query complexity charged per type.

The objection that survived

Allowing one \(v_t\)-cohort to split across different integral bundles can destroy the paper's single partition-index equicovering argument, and no replacement lower-bound gadget is supplied.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the main NSW approximation and query-complexity results, while leaving the exact named-agent matching and moving-knife execution and the transfer of the discrete lower-bound proof unresolved.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does allowing \(x_{t,S}\) to split a type across integral bundles eliminate the \(\left(1-\frac1e\right)\) query barrier, or can a new equicovering gadget restore it?
  • Can the configuration-pricing problem \(\max_{S\subseteq G}\{\log v_t(S)-\sum_{g\in S}p_g\}\) be solved or approximated using ordinary demand and XOS oracles?
  • What finite-\(N\) rounding guarantees relate continuous mass allocations to cloned discrete instances?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s central NSW problem, with Theorem 1 as the lead anchor and Theorem 2 as a harder boundary case.

The regime is a large population of households receiving indivisible goods from a finite catalogue of standardized good types. A household type \(t\) is a complete monotone XOS valuation \(v_t:2^G\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\), together with its oracle access. The population has finitely many types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and each good category \(g\in G\) has a rational per-capita supply \(\rho_g\). In a finite realization with \(N\) households, there are \(N\mu_t\) households of type \(t\) and \(N\rho_g\) indivisible copies of good \(g\). Thus \(N\) may be millions while \(|T|=\tau\) is modest: repeated household preference and need profiles are exactly the high-multiplicity regime.

The continuous allocation is not a fractional bundle. Let \(x_{t,S}\) be the mass of type \(t\) receiving the integral bundle \(S\subseteq G\). The constraints are

\[ \sum_{S\subseteq G}x_{t,S}=\mu_t \]

for every \(t\), and

\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\ni g}x_{t,S}\le \rho_g \]

for every good \(g\). The continuous Nash welfare is the geometric mean across individual agents,

\[ \operatorname{NSW}_{\infty}(x) = \exp\left( \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{S\subseteq G} x_{t,S}\log v_t(S) \right), \]

with value \(0\) if a positive mass receives value \(0\). This is deliberately not the geometric mean of type-average utilities: every individual still receives an integral bundle, while \(x\) records how a large cohort is distributed among such bundles.

This gives an exact high-multiplicity dictionary. If \(\mu_t\), \(\rho_g\), and \(x_{t,S}\) are rational, clearing denominators produces a finite market with cloned agents and indivisible good copies, and its ordinary NSW is exactly \(\operatorname{NSW}_{\infty}(x)\). Conversely, any such finite allocation gives an \(x\). The only population-level change is replacing named-agent multiplicities by masses and scaling inventories per capita, which is precisely the co-modification required to make indivisible fair division meaningful for a continuum.

The lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states:

Given a fair-division instance \(\langle[n],[m],\{v_i\}_{i\in[n]}\rangle\) with monotone XOS valuations and access to demand and XOS oracles, Algorithm 1 computes, with high probability, an \(O(n^{53/54})\)-approximation to the optimal Nash social welfare.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Continuous XOS-NSW Approximation. Given \(G,T,\mu,\rho\), and demand/XOS oracle access to the valuation \(v_t\) of each type, output a finitely supported feasible mass allocation \(x\) satisfying

\[ \operatorname{NSW}_{\infty}(x) \ge \frac{1}{\alpha} \max_{y\text{ feasible}} \operatorname{NSW}_{\infty}(y). \]

The primary question is whether one can obtain an approximation guarantee depending on \((m,\tau,L)\), rather than on the enormous realized population \(N\), and in particular whether a sublinear guarantee analogous to \(O(n^{53/54})\) can be obtained in terms of the number \(\tau\) of valuation types.

The authors should recognize this as their problem. It retains XOS valuations, demand and XOS access, indivisible bundles, and exactly the Nash geometric-mean objective. The repeated matchings, moving-knife step, and capped-welfare bootstrapping are not being replaced by arithmetic welfare or fractional goods; they would have to be compressed from individual agents to type masses.

The natural formulation is a configuration LP with exponentially many bundle variables:

\[ \max \sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S). \]

Its dual pricing problem is

\[ \max_{S\subseteq G} \left\{ \log v_t(S)-\sum_{g\in S}p_g \right\}. \]

That is exactly the sort of continuous-optimization bottleneck the programme is designed to expose. With a suitable logarithmic-demand or separation oracle, the problem is Class A by column generation and GLS-style separation. With only the paper’s ordinary demand oracle, which optimizes \(v_t(S)-\sum_g p_g\), the equivalence is not immediate. My expectation is therefore a split: additive, binary, and structured XOS subclasses should be tractable, while unrestricted XOS is a plausible Class C frontier whose difficulty is genuinely caused by the continuum’s configuration pricing problem. This is an open computational question, not a restatement of Theorem 1.

The paper’s strongest hardness anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. It states:

For fair-division instances with XOS valuations and any fixed constant \(\varepsilon\in(0,1]\), exponentially many demand and XOS queries are necessary to find an allocation whose NSW is at least \(\left(1-\frac1e+\varepsilon\right)\) times the optimum.

The corresponding problem is:

Continuous XOS-NSW Query Barrier. For fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), determine the minimum number of per-type demand and XOS queries required by a randomized algorithm that, on every continuous instance \((G,T,\mu,\rho,\{v_t\})\), outputs a feasible mass allocation \(x\) with

\[ \operatorname{NSW}_{\infty}(x) \ge \left(1-\frac1e+\varepsilon\right) \operatorname{OPT}_{\infty}. \]

This is a particularly useful mirror because the continuous answer is not obvious. Theorem 2’s reduction hides information in exponentially many additive clauses of the XOS valuations, not merely in the identities of individual agents. Hence a large population containing many clones of each valuation type is entirely plausible. But the continuum also permits different members of the same type to receive different integral bundles. In the paper’s equicovering construction, that may destroy the lower-bound gap: clones of one type can use different partition indices, potentially recovering high value while respecting aggregate per-capita supplies.

That makes the continuous query problem a genuine Class C boundary problem. The paper’s exact \(\left(1-\frac1e\right)\) lower bound should not be claimed to transfer automatically; it may dissolve because of population-level mixing. A surviving lower bound would require a new gadget compatible with type splitting, while an algorithm exploiting that splitting would be a substantive continuum-specific result. Either outcome is valuable and directly tests where the discrete query obstruction comes from.

The further questions are concrete: whether the configuration LP has polynomial-size optimal support; whether ordinary demand/XOS oracles can implement logarithmic pricing; whether additive or binary XOS valuations yield exact continuous algorithms; how finite-\(N\) rounding errors depend on denominators; and whether Theorem 2’s communication lower bound can be rebuilt after allowing type mass to split across bundles.

The weakest point is that this mirror must scale the goods as well as the population and must allow a type’s mass to receive several different bundles. The original paper has one-off goods and named agents, so a referee could regard the supply-scaled model as an extension rather than a literal reformulation. More seriously, Theorem 1 does not itself establish the required compressed algorithm, and Theorem 2’s lower bound may fail after type mixing. But those are precisely the computational questions the mirror exposes. The model remains recognizably the paper’s NSW problem, and its unresolved pricing and query barriers give a credible continuous research programme rather than a cosmetic replacement of \(n\) by a population measure.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper does not offer the easy negative ground: Theorems 1 and 2 are genuine computational results, and repeated household valuation types with recurring goods are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The strongest negative case is therefore structural, but it does not support the universal claim.

For the literal fair-division instance, population continuization degenerates. There are \(m\) one-off goods, while every normalized valuation gives value \(0\) to the empty bundle. Once \(n>m\), at least one agent receives no good, so NSW is \(0\). A nontrivial population limit therefore requires recurring good categories and per-capita supplies. That is defensible, but it is a substantial new primitive: the paper has valuations on a set of individually identified goods, whereas the mirror needs valuations over repeated copies or categories, together with a convention about substitutability between copies.

This matters especially for Theorem 1. Its algorithm is built around individual-agent structure: matchings between named agents and goods, reserving \(n\log n\) goods, agent-specific thresholds, random concentration over \(n\) bundles, and a sequential moving-knife procedure. Under a genuine continuum, identical types may split their mass among many integral bundles. Those individual matching and reassignment arguments no longer compress mechanically.

The proponent’s configuration formulation is mathematically sound:

\[ \max_x \sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}\log v_t(S), \]

subject to type-mass and supply constraints. But this is no longer a continuization of Algorithm 1. It is a new high-multiplicity configuration-allocation problem whose pricing oracle is

\[ \max_{S\subseteq G}\left\{\log v_t(S)-\sum_{g\in S}p_g\right\}. \]

The paper supplies ordinary demand oracles for

\[ \max_S\left\{v_t(S)-\sum_{g\in S}p_g\right\}, \]

and there is no immediate equivalence between these two problems for XOS valuations. Thus the proposed difficulty is real, but it comes from a new logarithmic configuration-pricing problem, not from any computational statement proved by Theorem 1. Conversely, the advertised question of whether an optimum has polynomial support is not itself open: an extreme-point optimum uses at most \(m+\tau\) positive configurations. The hard issue is finding that support, not its existence.

The alternatives do not repair the mismatch. Forcing each type to receive one common bundle preserves more of the paper’s structure but imposes an artificial indivisibility on a cohort; allowing type mass to split gives the configuration problem above; retaining one-off item identities prevents any large-population regime. The first is not the natural atomless model, the second is a new fair-division problem, and the third collapses to zero welfare.

Theorem 2 is an even weaker population anchor. Its lower bound is driven by exponentially many hidden clauses in \(n\) agent-specific XOS valuations \(B_i\), together with the fact that each of those agents receives one indivisible allocation. If the \(B_i\) are represented as distinct types, then the continuous instance has \(\tau=n\) and is essentially just the original instance with normalized masses. Population compression contributes nothing. If the agents are genuinely repeated types, the construction loses the private \(B_i\) information that powers the reduction.

The obvious repair—give each \(B_i\)-type positive mass and let it split across bundles—also breaks the equicovering argument. The original proof bounds one simultaneous choice of partition index for each agent. A continuum can mix different indices across different members of the same type, and the resulting aggregate allocation is not controlled by that single union bound. To prevent this, one must impose indivisible cohort assignments or encode the cohort identity into the type. The former abandons the atomless model; the latter restores \(\tau=n\) and removes the claimed high-multiplicity compression.

The query lower bound is therefore not intrinsically a population lower bound. Its difficulty resides in the private, exponentially described valuation oracles. The same information bottleneck exists with one representative of each type. A continuous version could certainly ask whether a new gadget survives type splitting, but that would be a new communication/configuration theorem rather than a mirror of Theorem 2.

This is the strongest case against the proposal: every faithful choice either keeps the paper’s one-off, identity-dependent instance and has no meaningful continuum, or scales supplies and permits mass splitting, in which case both anchors become new high-multiplicity fair-division questions whose central structure is not supplied by the paper.

But the universal negative still fails. The supply-scaled model is exactly the kind of co-modification the programme permits, and it is sociologically credible for repeated household types receiving recurring goods. Theorem 1’s configuration problem remains a legitimate computational question, even if it is not a compressed execution of the published algorithm. The honest verdict is therefore that the proposed Theorem 2 mirror is poor, while the Theorem 1 mirror survives as a plausible—though substantially extended—continuous-population programme.
