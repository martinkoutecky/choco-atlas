The case FOR (proponent)

Under the anchor rule, this paper has no named computational-complexity result: none of its numbered results classifies a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The strongest nearby result is Theorem 5.7, proved here. It says that, under assumptions (A1)–(A3), bounded stake, the securities conditions, safety, and cold-start run feasibility, the cold-start game has an objection-robust, coalition-undominated equilibrium; in every such equilibrium, authorization occurs exactly when \(\Phi^+\ge H_0\) and \(\Phi^+-\Phi^-\ge\kappa_{\text{start}}\), and an authorized run receives at least \(h_0\) compute. For continuation, \(\kappa_{\text{start}}\) is replaced by \(\kappa_{\text{halt}}\), with the corresponding feasibility condition. Here “efficient” describes the equilibrium refinement, not polynomial running time. So Theorem 5.7 is a promising mirror target, but it is not a qualifying complexity anchor.

The strongest plausible population regime is a municipal or cooperative grid service governed by an AI resource-allocation agent: many residents, but relatively few complete stakeholder types, such as tariff or service classes. A type would include the attributes the mechanism uses—valuation, belief, endowment, and arrival class. That makes the high-multiplicity version sensible: the residents are numerous, while the support size \(\tau\) is modest.

A concrete follow-up mirror of Theorem 5.7 would take a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over those types. A type-\(t\) resident contributes \(x_t\) per capita to authorize or \(z_t\) to halt, within the type’s endowment. Define normalized effective supports by
\[ \bar S^+(x)=\left(\sum_t\mu_t\sqrt{x_t}\right)^2, \qquad \bar S^-(z)=\left(\sum_t\mu_t\sqrt{z_t}\right)^2. \]
These are the finite-population QF supports divided by \(n^2\) when \(n\mu_t\) residents share type \(t\); the quorum and margin must be expressed in the same normalized units. The utilities, securities, and coalition-based equilibrium refinement remain those of the paper. The problem would be: given the type distribution and mechanism parameters, determine whether every objection-robust, coalition-undominated cold-start equilibrium authorizes, return the authorization decision, and compute the released budget. A solution includes the equilibrium characterization and the resulting decision and budget. If the theorem’s characterization carries over, the decision is governed by the corresponding mass-level reservation capacities, \(\bar\Phi^+\ge\bar H_0\) and \(\bar\Phi^+-\bar\Phi^-\ge\bar\kappa_{\text{start}}\).

My expectation for this follow-up is tractability with finite support: the intended characterization reduces the decision to sums over \(\tau\) types. That is a research conjecture, not a result of this paper; exact bit-complexity for the radical sums also needs a specified input and arithmetic model. The soft spot is the continuum game itself. With atomless individuals, a single resident cannot affect the gate, and the paper’s coalition-based equilibrium argument does not automatically transfer. The mass formulation is a credible question the authors could recognize, but the stated paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor for it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a category mismatch: this paper proves no named computational result. Theorem 5.7 is an equilibrium characterization. “Efficient” describes the equilibrium refinement, not running time. The theorem assumes, among other things, a selected securities equilibrium and bounded stakes, then characterizes authorization by two inequalities. It gives no algorithmic problem, input encoding, or complexity bound. The proposed mirror therefore starts from a result about strategic incentives, not a computational result whose complexity the programme can chart.

The proponent’s mass formula is a sound accounting translation under a specific symmetry assumption. If \(N\mu_t\) residents of type \(t\) each contribute \(x_t\), then dividing the finite QF support by \(N^2\) gives \(\bigl(\sum_t\mu_t\sqrt{x_t}\bigr)^2\); scaling the thresholds accordingly is a reasonable co-modification. And a municipality could plausibly have many residents in a modest number of complete types. So “there is no population regime here” would be a poor objection.

But the theorem’s substantive claim depends on the finite game, not just that formula. A single finite stakeholder can affect the gate; an individual in an atomless population has measure zero and cannot. At an authorized outcome, changing one person’s contribution leaves the gate unchanged while saving that person the contribution cost. The finite game’s mobilization incentives therefore do not carry over to individual residents. Treating all residents of a type as one coordinated strategic bloc could restore influence, but that changes the model’s agents and requires a new account of coordination and coalition stability. It is a possible governance model, but it is not the theorem’s high-multiplicity translation.

Nor does a type’s average contribution generally capture its QF support: because of the square roots, the support can depend on how contributions are distributed within the type. A symmetric per-capita action assumption supplies the proponent’s compact formula, but the paper does not establish that assumption for a continuum game. Preserving individual choice instead requires a new game over distributions of actions, arrivals, and histories, together with a continuum version of the paper’s coalition refinement. That could be interesting work, but it is a new equilibrium analysis, not a computation of Theorem 5.7’s result.

A more promising direction might be a population version of the paper’s manipulation-robustness target: how much persuasion would it take to flip authorization? Yet the paper leaves that target open and specifies neither a persuasion process nor its costs. Adding those, along with stakeholder susceptibility types, could yield a worthwhile continuous campaigning problem. It would be a new model built from the paper’s motivation, rather than a computational mirror of a proved result.

So I would not treat Theorem 5.7 as a strong ChoCo anchor: its direct mass translation omits the strategic feature that gives the theorem content, while restoring that feature requires substantial new modelling. The broader negative claim is weaker. The resident-cohort scenario is plausible, and manipulation of a mass electorate is a natural computational question. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous mirror; the case against is strongest for the proponent’s chosen anchor, not universal.
