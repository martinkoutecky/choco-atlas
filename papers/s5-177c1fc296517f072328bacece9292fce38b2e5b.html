The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible named computational result in this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity classification. Algorithm 1 is a heuristic, and the paper’s claims of computational tractability are supported by simulations, including comparisons for 200 households—not by a named complexity result. Reference [21] is prior work cited by the paper, not an eligible result proved here. So I can’t honestly give a continuous problem *per anchor* under your rule.

The strongest positive lead, if the unnumbered redistribution contribution is enough to motivate a follow-up question, is a continuous version of the paper’s marginal-contribution benefit allocation. A plausible regime is a large energy cooperative or housing portfolio whose households fall into a modest number \(\tau\) of complete types: each type specifies its half-hourly demand and PV profiles, network connection, and relevant tariffs and costs. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of households of type \(t\). Shared assets, feeder capacity, and network limits would need to scale consistently with population.

Call the candidate Continuous Marginal-Savings Allocation. Given the rational type distribution, feeder and tariff data, and shared-asset configuration, apply the paper’s network-constrained battery control and bill calculation to obtain total savings. For each type \(t\), define its limiting per-household contribution as the large-population limit of the change in savings when one household of that type is removed. Allocate savings to households in proportion to those contributions, with payouts summing to total savings; the solution is the per-type payout vector, exactly or within a specified error. This mirrors the paper’s leave-one-household-out method in Section III-E, whose equations define marginal contributions and use them to redistribute savings. I would tentatively expect this fixed-model calculation to be tractable in \(\tau\) and the time horizon, but that is a research conjecture, not a result of this paper. Questions include whether the limit exists under network constraints and battery degradation, and how accurately type-level calculations reproduce finite-community allocations.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper tests 200 households with real time-series profiles, and its leave-one-out method depends on how removing a household changes the network-constrained community calculation. A few types may be plausible in some large housing portfolios, but that compression—and the limiting contribution itself—needs justification. The proposal is therefore a credible population-mirror candidate, not a case anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper gives no named computational result to mirror. It has no theorem, lemma, or corollary establishing a complexity bound or algorithmic classification. Algorithm 1 is a heuristic, and the claims about tractability and scalability rest on simulations. The marginal-contribution equations define a redistribution method, but the paper does not analyze its complexity formally. The proposed anchor therefore does not meet the stated requirement.

The continuous allocation proposal is the best rescue, but it introduces a new asymptotic problem. In the paper, \(\Theta_i(T)\) compares savings for one fixed, 200-household system with savings after removing household \(i\). A continuum version needs a family of systems indexed by population size, including a rule for how the feeder, shared assets, and battery model scale. Its “per-type contribution” would then be a limiting sensitivity of savings along a chosen removal path. That may be a useful question, but it is not a continuization of a computational result established by this paper.

That negative case has a clear limit: a large portfolio with repeated household profiles and consistently scaled infrastructure is plausible, and the resulting type-level allocation could be worth studying. So I would not claim to have ruled out every worthwhile continuous project in this subject. The case against is strongest on the narrower point that this paper itself supplies no eligible computational result to anchor one.
