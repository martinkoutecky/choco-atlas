What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow, and it fails the strict anchor rule. The paper has no named computational-complexity result: nothing classifies a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved here. Under Assumptions 1–3, both HSL-NEB and HSL-BNE satisfy, for every false state \(\theta\),
\[ \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\frac{\mu_{i,t}(\theta)}{\mu_{i,t}(\theta^\star)} =-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\pi_j K_j(\theta^\star,\theta), \]
and every agent’s belief in \(\theta^\star\) tends to \(1\), \(P^\star\)-almost surely. This is a convergence and rate theorem, not a result that a problem is in a computational complexity class. So it cannot be a qualifying anchor under your instructions.

The best adjacent mirror I can make is a continuous-population version of that convergence question. Consider a sensor deployment with many agents but a modest set \(T\) of complete sensor types: each type specifies its signal likelihoods, initial belief, and role in the higher-order communication structure. Let \(\mu_t\) be the rational fraction of the population of type \(t\). Let \(E\) be a finite set of hyperedge roles. For each edge role \(e\), give a rational density \(a_{e,t}\) for the mass of type \(t\) contributing to that edge, normalized by \(\sum_t\mu_t a_{e,t}=1\). Give each type rational edge weights \(b_{t,e}\), with \(\sum_e b_{t,e}=1\). The resulting type-level averaging operator is
\[ P_{t,t'}=\sum_{e\in E} b_{t,e}\mu_{t'}a_{e,t'}. \]

Each type has a finite signal alphabet and positive rational likelihoods under each hypothesis. Agents have individual signal streams drawn from their type’s likelihood model. At each round, edge beliefs are formed by integrating log beliefs over the mass in that edge, those beliefs are aggregated back to agents by their type’s edge weights, and each agent performs the Bayesian update using its own signal. Thus mass means population share; it does not erase the paper’s two-stage hypergraph interaction.

The problem would be: given this instance and a true hypothesis \(\theta^\star\), decide whether the HSL-NEB dynamics drive the beliefs of almost every agent to \(\theta^\star\), and, if they do, return the asymptotic log-odds exponent for every false \(\theta\). Under the paper’s corresponding positivity, identifiability, and irreducibility assumptions, the expected answer is that learning succeeds, with exponent
\[ -\sum_{t\in T}\pi_t\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\ell_t(\cdot\mid\theta^\star)\,\middle\|\,\ell_t(\cdot\mid\theta)\right), \]
where \(\pi\) is the stationary distribution of \(P\). I would expect this promised version to be tractable: compute the stationary distribution of a finite rational matrix and return the KL expressions. Further questions include which interaction structures maximize the slowest learning rate, and how that rate changes when a positive mass of sensors loses its observations.

A plausible regime is a large sensor deployment built from repeated hardware and communication roles: the population size is much greater than the number of signal and hyperedge types. The authors would likely recognize this as a continuous-population extension of their learning dynamics. The mirror covers their convergence and rate analysis, not their communication-efficiency experiments or robustness scenarios.

The weak point is substantial: this is distributed statistical learning, without voters’ preferences, strategic changes, or a conventional social-choice rule. It is a plausible population-continuous computational question, but its fit to computational social choice is uncertain, and the paper’s theorem itself is analytic. Under the required anchor standard, the conclusion remains that this paper supplies no qualifying named anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor does not pass the stated gate. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 establish almost-sure learning and an asymptotic convergence rate; neither asserts the complexity of a computational problem. That is not an objection that their answer would stay the same or be easy. It is that the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror.

The strongest version of the proponent’s model is coherent: a large sensor deployment could have finitely many complete types, including signal models and hypergraph roles, with rational population masses. Its type-level operator \(P\) could determine a continuous-population learning rate. I would not reject it on the grounds that sensors are inherently individuated or that the types are artificial. But this gives ChoCo a continuous distributed-learning model, not a computational social-choice problem. The agents learn a true state from observations; they do not express preferences, select an outcome by a social-choice rule, or face a computational intervention such as bribery or control. Computing the rate from \(P\) and the KL divergences recasts the paper’s analytic theorem as an algorithmic task; it does not supply a computational question from the paper.

A stronger proposed extension would optimize the hypergraph to maximize the slowest learning rate under a communication budget. The paper’s discussion of topology design makes that a plausible network-design problem, but it adds an optimization objective the paper does not analyze. It remains a sensor-network problem, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational social-choice result. Turning it into one would require adding a voting or preference-aggregation problem, which changes the subject rather than continuizing the paper’s contribution.

So no anchor raised by the proponent survives under ChoCo’s criterion. The universal claim is less secure outside that criterion: a continuous-population version of distributed learning, or a network-design problem built around it, could be worthwhile research elsewhere. The negative case is strongest as a scope and anchor argument, not as a claim that no sensible continuum model can be made.
