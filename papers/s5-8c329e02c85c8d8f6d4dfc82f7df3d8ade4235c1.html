The continuous mirror question

Given hospitals \(H\), types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), acceptable pairs, strict type preferences over hospitals, strict hospital rankings over types, and rational share quotas \([\ell_h,u_h]\), choose assignment masses \(x_{t,h}\) and unmatched masses \(x_{t,\bot}\) with \(\sum_{h\in H\cup\{\bot\}}x_{t,h}=\mu_t\) and \(\ell_h\le\sum_t x_{t,h}\le u_h\). Minimize \(\sum_{t\in T,\,h_0\in H\cup\{\bot\}}x_{t,h_0}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\exists h\in H:\ h\succ_t h_0\ \land\ \sum_{s:\,t\succ_h s}x_{s,h}>0\right]\).

The model it lives in

A distributional HRLQ instance has rational mass on complete preference and priority types, with hospital quotas expressed as shares. Assignment variables allocate each type’s mass among acceptable hospitals or the unmatched option; the objective is the total mass assigned to outcomes from which residents have at least one justified envy witness at a preferred hospital.

The objection that survived

A vanishingly small mass of a lower-ranked type can trigger envy for a large mass, so normalized envy in finite markets need not converge to the proposed support-based objective.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 10’s Min-ER hardness and Theorem 5’s 0-1 Min-EP hardness; it does not cover Theorem 9’s exact algorithm.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does minimizing envious-resident mass remain hard when assignments are divisible, or can fractional allocation yield an efficient algorithm?
  • How does the positive-mass witness objective relate to normalized envy in finite high-multiplicity instances as individual masses shrink?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a large residency or professional-placement market in which many residents share the same complete list of acceptable programs, and each program ranks residents by a small set of profile classes. Think hundreds of thousands of residents but only tens or hundreds of distinct full profiles. A type \(t\) records both the resident’s ranked program list and the class by which every program ranks them. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the cohort with that profile. Programs have lower and upper quotas stated as shares of the cohort. The decision is how to assign each type’s mass across programs, possibly leaving some unmatched, while meeting those quotas and minimizing envy.

Here is a precise common model for both mirrors. The instance gives a finite set of types \(T\), programs \(H\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), acceptable type–program pairs, strict rankings of programs for each type, strict rankings of types for each program, and rational quotas \(0\le \ell_h\le u_h\). Types are indistinguishable in every respect the matching problem uses; consequently, a program ranks types, rather than distinguishing people within a type. A solution is a rational assignment \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_h x_{t,h}\le\mu_t\) and \(\ell_h\le\sum_t x_{t,h}\le u_h\). Write \(x_{t,\bot}=\mu_t-\sum_h x_{t,h}\) for unmatched mass.

A mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(h_0\) envies program \(h\) when \(h\succ_t h_0\) and \(h\) holds positive mass of some type \(s\) that it ranks below \(t\). Thus the envy-trigger condition is
\[ h\succ_t h_0 \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{s:\,t\succ_h s}x_{s,h}>0. \]
For the pair objective, count each unit of resident mass once for each program it envies:
\[ \mathrm{EP}(x)= \sum_{t,h_0}x_{t,h_0} \left|\left\{h:\ h\succ_t h_0,\ \sum_{s:\,t\succ_h s}x_{s,h}>0\right\}\right|. \]
For the resident objective, count a unit of mass once if it envies any program:
\[ \mathrm{ER}(x)= \sum_{t,h_0}x_{t,h_0}\, \mathbf 1\!\left[ \exists h\succ_t h_0: \sum_{s:\,t\succ_h s}x_{s,h}>0 \right]. \]
These are mass versions of the paper’s two definitions: an occupant’s presence at a program can witness envy, but several lower-ranked occupants there still create only one resident–program pair for a given resident. Each question asks for a feasible assignment minimizing its stated objective, with the instance and output encoded by binary rationals and rankings.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper by a reduction from Vertex Cover: 0-1 Min-EP HRLQ is NP-hard. In the paper’s problem, a feasible matching must satisfy all quotas, each of which is either \([0,1]\) or \([1,1]\), and the goal is to minimize the number of envy-pairs. The continuous mirror is the \(\mathrm{EP}\) minimization problem above, restricted to program quotas \([0,\kappa]\) or \([\kappa,\kappa]\) for a common rational share \(\kappa\), such as one position’s share of a large cohort. I expect this mirror to be continuum-specifically hard (Class C): the allocation variables are divisible, but the objective still depends on whether a positive mass of a lower-ranked type occupies a program. That creates choices about which envy witnesses exist, even when no individual residents are being selected. This is a prediction, not an extension of the theorem: the paper’s Vertex Cover reduction does not establish hardness for the divisible assignment model. Further questions include whether that support-choice structure yields hardness, whether column generation or another continuous method can exploit it, and how the answer depends on the number of types and programs.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Theorem 10, also proved here, by a reduction from CLIQUE: Min-ER HRLQ is NP-hard. Its problem is to find a feasible HRLQ matching minimizing the number of distinct envy-residents, with no 0-1 restriction on quotas. Its mirror is the same type-and-mass model minimizing \(\mathrm{ER}(x)\), with arbitrary rational share quotas. I again expect Class C: the number of residents who experience envy is a mass objective, but whether they experience any envy can depend on a small set of positive-mass placements. The open question is whether the CLIQUE-style selection encoded by the paper’s reduction survives fractional allocation, or whether divisibility admits a tractable relaxation. One should also ask whether minimizing envy-resident mass behaves differently from minimizing envy-pair mass, and whether the continuous optimum agrees with the discrete optimum on societies represented by rational proportions.

The scenario is plausible because the paper’s central objects remain intact: residents rank programs, programs rank resident profiles, quotas constrain assignments, and envy is witnessed by a preferred program holding someone it ranks lower. The co-modifications—share quotas and type-level rankings—are the natural way to make a large population and indistinguishable cohorts part of the model. They do not make the paper’s hardness claims carry over automatically. In particular, programs that rank people by idiosyncratic scores would require those scores in the type, potentially making the support large; and type-level rankings treat residents of the same type as indistinguishable to a program, unlike the paper’s strict rankings of named residents. That is the weakest point: the mirrors are recognizable HRLQ questions, but whether authors would regard this priority coarsening as their high-multiplicity regime is the key plausibility test. The case covers the two named hardness results, not the paper’s Theorem 9 algorithm.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection to both anchors is that the paper’s envy relation depends on residents’ individual positions in hospitals’ strict rankings. In the original model, those positions are part of what distinguishes one resident from another. Keeping them in the type makes many residents distinct, so the support can grow with the population. Collapsing residents into priority classes gives a small support, but changes the model: people in a class are tied from a hospital’s point of view, and envy is measured between classes rather than among the individually ranked residents used in Theorem 5’s Vertex Cover construction and Theorem 10’s Clique construction. Those reductions encode choices through residents’ placements and priority relationships, so they do not themselves establish a high-multiplicity version.

The proposed mass objectives expose a second difficulty. Envy is triggered by *any* positive mass of a lower-ranked type at a hospital. If that mass is \(\epsilon\), it can make a large cohort envious; when \(\epsilon\) vanishes, the envy disappears altogether. Thus normalized discrete envy need not converge with the population distribution: one exceptional resident of mass \(1/N\) can trigger envy among a constant fraction of the population, even as that resident disappears from the limiting distribution. The proposed definition is still mathematically precise, but its support-based trigger makes the continuous objective a discontinuous redesign of the discrete one, not a straightforward limit.

Capacity scaling adds another choice. Keeping the paper’s unit quotas while growing the population makes assigned mass vanish in a normalized society. Scaling quotas with the population avoids that, but changes the market regime as well as the population. The brief permits that co-modification, so this is a fidelity concern rather than a decisive objection.

These points make the mirror less direct than the proponent suggests, but they do not defeat the best version. Large markets with repeated preference profiles and priority bands are plausible; rational share quotas and positive-mass envy witnesses define coherent questions for both envy-pair mass and envy-resident mass. The authors might recognize those as their problems. And a prediction of continuum-specific hardness is not a reason to reject them: the programme explicitly values finding where continuization stops helping.

So the honest negative case is weak. I would use the ranking and limit-sensitivity issues to question how faithfully the mirrors represent these particular theorems, but I cannot make a convincing case that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. Theorem 5 and Theorem 10 both leave plausible continuous questions standing.
