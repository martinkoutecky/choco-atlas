The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its named results are Lemma 1, Corollary 1, and Propositions 1 and 2, all proved here. They establish inferences and normative properties; none gives a complexity result, an algorithm, or a computational formulation that the paper solves.

The closest anchor is Proposition 2. It says that for groups \(I,J\ne N\), options \(x,y\), and conditional probabilities satisfying ANO and IND, if the induced relation \(\succeq\) satisfies PBR and is binary, transitive, and reflexive on the observed choices, then \(|I|>|J|\) implies \(\omega_I^x\succeq\omega_J^x\). The authors prove it here. This is a normative comparative result: a larger group’s agreement gives its choice at least as much normative force. It does not analyze the computational cost of finding that ranking.

There is a plausible population-scale setting for extending that idea: many people responding to a public consultation or contributing to a poverty assessment, with relatively few complete types. A type could include the person’s two motive rankings and observed choice behavior; its mass would be its share of the population. Given two groups that reach consensus on \(x\) over \(y\), a continuous version could ask whether the larger-mass group’s choice ranks at least as highly. That is a natural population mirror of Proposition 2’s subject matter.

But the paper does not supply the continuous probability model needed to make that a faithful, standalone question. IND makes the probability of joint idiosyncratic agreement a product over individuals. Replacing group cardinality by mass does not specify what that product becomes for a continuum of agents. Defining it as a function of mass would add a new assumption; the resulting comparison might be easy, but it would not be a computational result established by this paper.

So the positive case is that normative assessment of large, type-concentrated populations is a credible setting for a continuous extension. The weak point is decisive for this assignment: the extension would be a new problem inspired by Proposition 2, not a mirror anchored in one of the paper’s named computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 2 is the strongest anchor, but the proposed consultation or poverty-assessment setting supplies a population scale, not a computational problem. The proposition proves a normative implication: under ANO and IND, larger consenting groups receive at least as much normative weight under PBR. It gives no task of finding a group, an outcome, or a ranking from an encoded instance, and no algorithm or complexity claim. The paper’s Bayesian language does not fill that gap: it assumes properties of conditional probabilities but gives no generative model or prior from which to infer them.

The strongest faithful mirror would represent a large population by a distribution over complete types, including each person’s motive rankings and observed choices, and represent consultation groups by their membership. For fixed groups and options, if the relevant probabilities are supplied, computing the PBR comparison means comparing those probabilities; under Proposition 2’s assumptions, comparing group sizes already gives the weak ranking. If the task is to find a largest group that agrees on an option, a finite-support representation lets one sum the masses of compatible types. Neither version offers a substantial computational question. Adding constraints on who may join a group could make the search harder, but those constraints would supply the computational structure the paper lacks.

The continuum does not create a nontrivial probability calculation on its own. With independent individual events, the probability that every member of a group agrees is a product for finite groups; for an atomless group, the all-agree event collapses to probability zero unless individual agreement is certain. One could rescale individual probabilities with population size to obtain a nonzero mass-based limit, but that adds a model assumption. Under the proposition’s monotonicity result, the resulting comparison still ranks groups by mass. Alternatively, trying to infer the probabilities from observed choices runs into the paper’s own limits: it leaves the mechanism linking latent motives to choices unspecified and offers assumptions for normative inference, not an identifiable statistical model.

A richer project on learning motives, modeling coalition formation, or computing rankings under added membership constraints could be worthwhile. But its computational core would come from those additions, not from a continuous mirror of Proposition 2. Large populations make the setting imaginable; they do not turn this paper’s axiomatic result into the kind of computational question ChoCo is designed to chart.
