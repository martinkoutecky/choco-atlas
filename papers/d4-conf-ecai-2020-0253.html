The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), a supplied partition of \(C\) into three blocks, supported complete rankings whose restrictions to each block are single-peaked, registered masses \(\mu_t\), a target \(p\), a budget \(b\), and an add or delete mode (with available masses \(\nu_t\) in add mode), does there exist an action vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\nu_t\) for adding or \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\) for deleting, and \(\sum_t x_t\le b\), such that the resulting society makes \(p\) beat every \(c\ne p\)? The resulting mass is \(\rho_t=\mu_t+x_t\) in add mode and \(\rho_t=\mu_t-x_t\) in delete mode; require \(\sum_t\rho_t(\mathbf 1[p\succ_t c]-\mathbf 1[c\succ_t p])>0\) for each opponent.

The model it lives in

A linear program over mass actions \(x_t\) for complete ranking types \(t\), with add or delete capacity bounds, total cost \(\sum_t x_t\), and one strict pairwise-margin condition per opponent. Maximize the minimum margin to test strict victory exactly.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 3.3, 3.5, and 4.3; it leaves the paper's other control, recognition, and structural results unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible continuous mirror here. The lead is Theorem 4.3, because Condorcet control in the paper is determined entirely by pairwise comparisons with the target, which become linear constraints on voter mass.

Use a type for each complete ranking together with everything that affects control eligibility. Let \(\mu_t\) be the registered mass of type \(t\), and \(\nu_t\) the available unregistered mass. These are binary rationals, measured against a reference population; take \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The number of people \(N\) can be much larger than the number of supported types \(\tau\). For example, a large consultation on a fixed candidate slate could have many thousands of voters but only a few dozen recurring rankings. Some ballot types could be available to recruit, while others are already registered.

For a chosen type, the controller changes a mass \(x_t\): in an adding problem, \(0\le x_t\le\nu_t\); in a deleting problem, \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\). The cost is \(\sum_t x_t\), and the decision question is whether the target can become the unique winner with cost at most a given budget \(b\). This makes “control at most \(b\%\) of the electorate” the mass version of the paper’s “control at most \(\ell\) voters.” The fractional \(x_t\) represents a proportion of a continuum cohort, while each actual ballot remains a complete ranking.

Using the full-version theorem numbering, the three anchors are:

Theorem 4.3, proved in this paper: CCAV and CCDV for Condorcet in \(3\)-CP elections are \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. The paper reduces RX3C to both variants. [Full version, Theorem 4.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.03539#page=11)

Its mirror is 3-CP Condorcet Mass Control. The instance gives a candidate set, a \(3\)-partition of it, registered and available masses over complete rankings, a target \(p\), a budget \(b\), and an add/delete mode. Every supported ranking must be single-peaked when restricted to each block. The question is whether there is an allowed mass vector \(x\) of cost at most \(b\) that makes \(p\) the Condorcet winner. For each opponent \(c\), define the pairwise margin \(D_\rho(p,c)=\sum_t\rho_t\bigl(\mathbf 1[p\succ_t c]-\mathbf 1[c\succ_t p]\bigr)\). In add mode require \(D_{\mu+x}(p,c)>0\) for every \(c\ne p\); in delete mode require \(D_{\mu-x}(p,c)>0\). This mirrors the paper’s unique-winner condition: under its Condorcet correspondence, \(p\) is the unique winner exactly when it beats every opponent.

Theorem 3.3, proved in this paper: for every fixed \(r\ge4\), CCAV for \(r\)-approval in \(3\)-CP elections is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard. The proof reduces RX3C. [Full version, Theorem 3.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.03539#page=5)

Its mirror, 3-CP \(r\)-Approval Add-Mass, has the same registered and available masses, target, budget, and \(3\)-candidate-block structure. Write \(A_\rho(c)=\sum_t\rho_t\mathbf 1[c\text{ is in the top }r\text{ of }t]\). A solution is a vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\nu_t\), \(\sum_t x_t\le b\), and \(A_{\mu+x}(p)>A_{\mu+x}(c)\) for every other candidate \(c\).

Theorem 3.5, proved in this paper: for every fixed \(r\ge3\), CCDV for \(r\)-approval is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hard even when the election is both \(2\)-axes single-peaked and \(2\)-CP single-peaked. Its proof also uses RX3C. [Full version, Theorem 3.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.03539#page=7)

Its mirror, 2-Axes-and-2-CP \(r\)-Approval Delete-Mass, gives the two axes and the \(2\)-partition as witnesses, along with registered masses, target, and budget. Every type must meet both domain restrictions. A solution is \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), \(\sum_t x_t\le b\), and \(A_{\mu-x}(p)>A_{\mu-x}(c)\) for every \(c\ne p\).

All three mass problems are expected to be Class A. Each has only \(\tau\) action variables and one winning constraint per opponent. The coefficients are computed from the explicitly listed rankings, so the support is not an exponentially large implicit type space. Strict victory is handled exactly by adding a slack variable \(\delta\) to each margin constraint and maximizing \(\delta\); a positive optimum is equivalent to a strict win. Thus these are linear programs of size polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the mass bit length \(L\). The nearly single-peaked conditions remain part of the instance, rather than being replaced by outcome or probability continuity.

The authors should recognize these as their control problems with the electorate measured in mass: the rule, target, eligible population, budget, and preference restriction all remain in place. The scenario is an electorate with repeated complete ballots over a fixed slate, where \(\tau\) is small relative to \(N\); \(3\)-CP captures voters who are single-peaked within each of three candidate blocks, while the other anchor captures a mixture of two single-peaked axes. The high-multiplicity-election literature supports treating such a regime as a sensible object. This is not a claim that the paper already studies a continuous society.

The finite-to-continuous bridge is explicit. A discrete population with \(n_t\) ballots of type \(t\) gives \(\mu_t=n_t/N\); a discrete control action gives a mass action on the grid of multiples of \(1/N\). The continuous problem allows off-grid actions. Conversely, a rational mass solution can be scaled to a finite electorate by clearing its denominators. What it does not settle is the fixed-\(N\) integrality gap: an LP solution may use a fraction of a type that the discrete controller cannot select. That is the main follow-up—bound the rounding error as \(N\) grows, especially when the target’s winning margin is small. A second question is whether jointly choosing an unknown axes or candidate partition changes the continuous complexity.

My weakest point is that divisible control is a substantive relaxation: the paper selects individual votes, while these mirrors allow control of part of a type’s mass. That is the intended population-level change, and the type retains every distinction the paper’s problem uses—ranking and control eligibility—but a reader who requires whole-cohort or person-by-person actions may reject it. The mirror covers only Theorems 3.3, 3.5, and 4.3; it makes no claim about the paper’s Copeland, Maximin, recognition, or other results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is that they change the controller’s action. The paper selects whole votes from finite registered or unregistered multisets; the proposed versions allow the controller to take an arbitrary fraction of each ballot type. That can erase the combinatorial choices used in the RX3C reductions. It also means a continuous optimum need not settle a fixed-size election when the winning margin is tiny.

That objection limits the transfer back to finite elections, but it does not defeat the mirrors under ChoCo’s stated scope. A large electorate with recurring ballots is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and selecting mass is meaningful when control is measured as a share of that electorate. The types can retain eligibility and any type-specific costs. High-multiplicity work supports this regime; it is not a collision. A margin bounded away from zero would also make the link to sufficiently large finite elections clearer.

The three anchors survive that objection. Theorem 4.3, on CCAV and CCDV for Condorcet in \(3\)-CP elections, has a direct mass version: choose addable or deletable mass by ranking type, and impose a strict pairwise-margin constraint against each opponent. Maximizing the minimum margin gives an exact LP test for whether a strict win is achievable. The \(3\)-CP condition restricts the supported types; it does not break the formulation.

Theorem 3.3, on \(r\)-approval CCAV in \(3\)-CP elections, likewise gives linear score constraints: added mass contributes to each candidate’s score according to whether that candidate is approved by the type. Capacity bounds and a mass budget complete the LP. Theorem 3.5, on \(r\)-approval CCDV in the intersection of \(2\)-axes and \(2\)-CP domains, has the same structure with removed mass subtracted from scores. Providing the axes and partition as witnesses preserves the paper’s domain restriction.

A more exact finite high-multiplicity version could retain integer counts and therefore retain some of the reduction’s hardness. But that is a separate variant; it does not make the continuous mass-control questions ill-posed or answer them already. Nor does the simplicity of the LP rule them out: tractability after continuization is one of the programme’s intended findings.

So the best negative case is a caution about how much these results would say about a particular finite electorate, especially near a tie. I cannot turn it into a convincing case that none of the three is a worthwhile mirror. Each has a natural population interpretation and a well-posed computational question.
