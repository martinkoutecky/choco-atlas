The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-agent coefficients \(a_t\ge 0\) with \(Z=\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t>0\), consider any admissible type maps \(g_t:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) and the atomless population aggregate \(F(x)=Z^{-1}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t\int_0^1g_t(x_t(u))\,du\). For each \(N\) with \(N\mu_t\) integral, form the finite replica with \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), each weighted \(a_t/(NZ)\). Compute the limits, as \(N\) grows through such values, of the sums of the paper's \(\phi\)- and \(\psi\)-importance indices within each type.

The model it lives in

Types carry masses \(\mu_t\); each member of type \(t\) has coefficient \(a_t\) and response map \(g_t\). The aggregate is \(F(x)=Z^{-1}\sum_t\mu_ta_t\int_0^1g_t(x_t(u))\,du\). Define each type's importance as the limit of its members' summed finite-replica indices; both vectors equal \((\mu_ta_t/Z)_{t\in T}\).

The objection that survived

The proposed instance lists arbitrary continuous maps \(g_t\) without finite encodings, even though the indices do not depend on them; the computational input should therefore be just \((\mu,a)\), with the maps treated as admissible model structure.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 6.3's separable additive class. It leaves Theorem 6.4's product formulas, the weighted-median results, and the paper's general axiomatic questions aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For weighted-median aggregates with finitely many repeated type classes, do the type-level limits of \(\phi\) and \(\psi\) admit efficient exact computation?
  • Under what conditions do type-level replication limits exist for nonseparable aggregation functions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is narrow: the paper has no numbered theorem that states an NP-hardness or complexity-class result such as membership in P. Its strongest computational anchor is the exact evaluation result in Theorem 6.3, proved in this paper. It says that if \(f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i f_i(x_i)\), where \(w_i\ge 0\), \(\sum_i w_i=1\), and each \(f_i:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) is continuous, weakly increasing, and surjective, then \(f\) is a simple aggregation function and both importance measures satisfy \(\phi_i(f)=\psi_i(f)=w_i\). That is an exact formula for both indices, though the paper does not itself classify its computation as a problem in P.

Lead mirror: Separable Type Importance Evaluation\(_\infty\). An instance has a finite set of agent types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, rational per-agent importance weights \(a_t\ge 0\) with \(Z=\sum_t\mu_ta_t>0\), and a response map \(g_t:[0,1]\to[0,1]\) for each type, continuous, weakly increasing, and surjective. The population is the atomless space \(\bigsqcup_{t\in T}\{t\}\times[0,1]\), with mass \(\mu_t\,du\) on type \(t\). A state profile \(x_t(u)\in[0,1]\) produces aggregate state
\[ F(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{t\in T}\mu_ta_t\int_0^1 g_t(x_t(u))\,du. \]
Here mass is the fraction of the population in each complete type; the state profile gives each member’s interval-valued state. The question is to compute the total Shapley-style and Penrose–Banzhaf-style importance assigned to each type.

Define those type totals by finite replication: for every \(N\) such that \(N\mu_t\) is integral, create \(N\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\), each with weight \(a_t/(NZ)\), and aggregate their individual indices within each type. A solution is the exact pair of type-importance vectors given by the limits of those totals as \(N\) grows. By Theorem 6.3, both limits exist and agree:
\[ \lambda_t^\phi=\lambda_t^\psi=\frac{\mu_ta_t}{Z}. \]
Under binary rational input this gives an exact polynomial-time computation in \(|T|\) and the input bit length. The algorithmic consequence is mine; the theorem supplies the formula and replication stability.

A plausible regime is a large sensor or grid-monitoring fleet built from a modest number of standardized hardware and software classes. Each unit reports a normalized fault or forecast state; its type records its reliability weight and response map. A fleet may have millions of units but only a small number of such types. That is a credible high-multiplicity setting, and it fits the paper’s engineering motivation. The mirror still asks the paper’s question—how much each agent matters to the aggregate state—while reporting importance for a type as the sum of its members’ importance. The finite replication definition also avoids pretending that a uniformly random ordering of an uncountable population is already defined.

I expect this problem to be tractable, Class A. Its direct answer is the normalized type weight, with computation consisting of rational sums and divisions. This mirrors Theorem 6.3 only: it does not cover the product formulas of Theorem 6.4, the weighted-median results, or the paper’s general axiomatic questions. Natural follow-up questions are whether replication limits remain tractable for nonseparable aggregators such as weighted medians, and whether the two type-level indices continue to agree there.

The weak point is substantial: in this separable class, the continuous computation is almost just regrouping weights, so it adds little computational difficulty or new optimization machinery. The paper also does not study high multiplicity or state a complexity theorem. I think the mirror remains plausible because the object being computed is still the authors’ importance measure, with the type total obtained from an exact, replication-stable interpretation of their individual indices. But the case is evidence for a useful continuous version of one result, not for a broader complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper has no theorem classifying the complexity of an explicitly encoded computational problem. It defines importance measures, proves their properties, and gives formulas for selected classes. Its remark that direct evaluation becomes infeasible as the number of agents grows is not a complexity result: the paper specifies no finite representation of an arbitrary aggregation function or input model for that evaluation.

That weakens the proposed anchor, Theorem 6.3. In the proposed replication, each agent of type \(t\) has coefficient \(a_t/(NZ)\). The theorem immediately gives that agent’s importance, and summing over the \(N\mu_t\) agents gives \(\mu_t a_t/Z\). The response maps \(g_t\) play no role in the computation. As stated, they are arbitrary continuous functions, with no finite encoding; if the type masses and weights are the finite input, the answer is just their normalized weighted sum. That is a valid arithmetic task, but it does not mirror a computational result in the paper or require evaluating its importance measures from a compact description of an aggregation function.

A stronger repair would use weighted medians rather than the separable class. Repeated weight classes give a credible high-multiplicity regime, and type-level limits of the paper’s importance measures are coherent questions. I cannot defeat that mirror with an objection about individual importance disappearing: the type total can remain meaningful. Nor does the existing finite power-index formulation dispose of it; high-multiplicity work supports the regime, and a discrete-to-continuous identity could itself be a result.

So the negative case is narrow. It defeats the proponent’s sole anchor as evidence of a worthwhile *computational* mirror: Theorem 6.3 supplies an explicit formula, not a complexity classification, and the proposed calculation adds no evaluation burden. But the paper’s weighted-median material leaves a plausible, nonseparable mass-based question. I cannot honestly rule out that stronger mirror, so the universal claim that no scenario is worth ChoCo’s time is weak.
