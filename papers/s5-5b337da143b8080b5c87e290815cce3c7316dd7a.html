The continuous mirror question

Given \(s\) seats and two voter types with masses \(\mu_A=f\) and \(\mu_B=1-f\), where each type approves every candidate on its own party's \(s\)-candidate slate and none of the other, determine how many seats party \(A\) receives under Jefferson (\(h=1\)) or Webster (\(h=\tfrac12\)); ties may be excluded as in Proposition 4.

The model it lives in

Use two approval types, one for each party, with masses \(f\) and \(1-f\). Choose \(s\) candidates from the two \(s\)-candidate slates to maximize \(\mu_A g_h(|W\cap A|)+\mu_B g_h(|W\cap B|)\), where \(g_h(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\frac{h}{h+j}\); the output is the seat count for each party.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant anchored case: the paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Propositions 1–5 concern, respectively, differences between sequential and nonsequential winners, representativeness, comparisons between Jefferson and Webster, two-party seat thresholds, and quota. None classifies an algorithm or the complexity of a problem.

The closest claim is the footnote on page 5: nonsequential methods are “computationally complex” and not implementable in polynomial time, attributed to Brill, Laslier, and Skowron (2016). It is unnumbered and attributed to prior work, so it does not qualify as an anchor under your rule. The paper’s note that some examples were found with an integer program is not a complexity result either.

There is a plausible mirror-shaped problem behind that claim. Let each type \(B\subseteq C\) be an approval ballot, with rational mass \(\mu_B\), and let \(k\) be the committee size. For \(h\in\{1,\tfrac12\}\), ask for a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{B}\mu_B\,g_h(|B\cap W|), \qquad g_h(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\frac{h}{h+j}, \quad g_h(0)=0. \]
Here \(h=1\) gives the paper’s nonsequential Jefferson score, and \(h=\tfrac12\) its nonsequential Webster score. A credible regime is a large membership association choosing a council: many members share approval ballots, so the number of members greatly exceeds the number of ballot types. The committee remains the decision variable, and the objective remains the paper’s voter satisfaction score. Since the hard combinatorics would lie in choosing candidates, hardness would be expected to transfer if an appropriate discrete hardness result were established.

That is a candidate continuous question, not an anchor-backed case for this paper. The weakest point is decisive for the requested format: the paper does not itself provide a qualifying named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper offers no qualifying computational result to mirror. Propositions 1–5 concern differences between winners, representativeness, thresholds and quota. The paper’s only complexity claim is a footnote saying the nonsequential methods are computationally complex, with the claim attributed to earlier work. The cited Brill, Laslier and Skowron paper studies how multiwinner rules induce apportionment methods; its stated results include PAV inducing D’Hondt. It does not supply a continuous-population complexity theorem for this paper to mirror. ([Brill et al., AAAI 2017](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/10600))

That leaves the proposed score-maximization problem without an anchor in a named computational result. But I cannot honestly defeat its stronger formulation as a worthwhile ChoCo question. With approval sets \(B\subseteq C\) as types and rational masses \(\mu_B\), one can ask for a size-\(k\) committee \(W\) maximizing
\[ \sum_B \mu_B\,g_h(|B\cap W|), \qquad g_h(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\frac{h}{h+j}. \]
A large membership organization choosing a council is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and this keeps the committee discrete while making the population continuous. The paper itself describes choosing a company board as a multiwinner scenario. ([Brill et al., AAAI 2017](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/10600))

The fact that rational masses can be represented by a finite electorate after scaling does not defeat the mirror: the programme explicitly treats that correspondence as part of the high-multiplicity bridge. Nor can I object that the committee remains a discrete decision; that would reject the intended population continuization. So the negative case is strong only in the narrower assignment sense: this paper supplies no named computational result to anchor the mirror. The broader claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror is weak; the proposed problem looks like a plausible programme extension.
