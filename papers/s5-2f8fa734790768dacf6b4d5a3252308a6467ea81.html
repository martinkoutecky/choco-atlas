The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), direct and cross-channel data \(d_t,g_{u,t}\), noise levels \(\nu_t\), overheads \(\epsilon_t\), and an initial finite partition \(\mathcal C=(x^1,\ldots,x^r)\) with \(\sum_j x_t^j=\mu_t\), let all links of type \(u\) in block \(j\) share a zero-forcing beam \(w_u^j\) against the receiver types represented there. Their type-level utility is \(U_{t,j}(\mathcal C)=\log_2\!\left(1+\frac{|d_t^H w_t^j|^2}{\nu_t+\sum_u\sum_{\ell\ne j}x_u^\ell|g_{u,t}^H w_u^\ell|^2}\right)\). For fixed \(q\), find a sequence of mergers of between two and \(q\) blocks, ending when no acceptable merger remains; a merger of blocks \(J\) is acceptable if, in the merged partition \(\mathcal C'\), \(U_{t,j}(\mathcal C)-\epsilon_t\le U_{t,J}(\mathcal C')\) for every \(j\in J\) and \(t\) with \(x_t^j>0\), strictly for at least one such pair. This uses the paper's comparison convention; the proponent's displayed inequality places the overhead on the opposite utility.

The model it lives in

A repeated-link mean-field model with finitely many complete channel and incentive types, mass-scaled aggregate interference, and a finite list of coalition mass blocks. The fixed-\(q\) task is to compute a merger sequence and a terminal partition, with enumeration polynomial in the block-list size for fixed \(q\).

The objection that survived

If type mass is split among arbitrarily many listed blocks, \(r\) need not be bounded by \(\tau\), so the polynomial bound does not guarantee a high-multiplicity reduction in every instance regime.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's fixed-\(q\) merger procedure and terminal stability; it leaves the \(\epsilon\)-core characterizations, Wiener-filter analysis, and simulation results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can a realistic deployment support repeated full channel profiles and the per-capita interference scaling used by the type kernel?
  • Can the initial block count \(r\) be bounded by \(\tau\) in a useful cohort regime, and how do terminal partitions compare with those in large finite networks?
  • What is the complexity when \(q\) is unrestricted?

The case FOR (proponent)

I see one defensible mirror, centered on the paper’s coalition-merging algorithm rather than its core results.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for Algorithm 2, with \(K\) links and a limit \(q\) on how many coalitions may merge in one step, the number of iterations is claimed to be \(O(K^q)\), and hence polynomial for fixed \(q\). This is an algorithmic result, not a hardness result; the paper offers no comparable named hardness anchor. There is a qualification: the proof’s displayed bound (28) sums \(D(K-i,q)\) over \(O(K)\) values of \(i\). For fixed \(q\), that sum is \(O(K^{q+1})\), not \(O(K^q)\). The printed exponent appears unsupported, but the weaker bound is still polynomial for fixed \(q\).

Lead mirror: \(q\)-Mass Coalition Formation. A type \(t\) describes a complete link class: antenna count, direct channel vector \(d_t\), cross-channel vectors \(g_{u,t}\) to receiver type \(t\), noise level, and deviation overhead \(\epsilon_t\). The society has finitely many types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). In a repeated industrial or private-wireless deployment, for example, many links could share a small set of channel and equipment profiles, so the number of links \(K\) is much larger than the number of types \(\tau=|T|\).

A coalition is a mass vector \(x^j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^{\tau}\); a coalition structure is a finite list \(\mathcal C=(x^1,\ldots,x^r)\) whose vectors sum to \(\mu\). Thus a type’s population can be divided among coalitions. A type’s members in a coalition use the same zero-forcing beam, aimed at the receiver types represented there. Use the dense-network, per-capita interference scaling: a link of type \(t\) in coalition \(j\) has utility

\[ U_{t,j}(\mathcal C)=\log_2\!\left(1+ \frac{|d_t^H w_t^j|^2} {\nu_t+\sum_{u\in T}\sum_{\ell\ne j}x_u^\ell |g_{u,t}^H w_u^\ell|^2}\right). \]

This makes interference an aggregate over population mass. The co-modification is part of the model: it keeps per-link utility finite as the population grows.

Given a starting partition, fixed \(q\), and rational channel and overhead data, the problem is to return a sequence of accepted mergers ending at a partition with no acceptable merger of at most \(q\) coalitions. A merger of blocks \(J\), where \(2\le |J|\le q\), is acceptable when every positive-mass type-and-block group in \(J\) weakly prefers the merged block after paying its overhead, and at least one strictly prefers it:

\[ U_{t,J}(\mathcal C')-\epsilon_t\ge U_{t,j}(\mathcal C) \quad\text{for every }j\in J,\ t\text{ with }x_t^j>0, \]

with strict inequality for at least one such pair. The solution is the resulting terminal partition and merger sequence; there is no sum-rate maximization objective. For rational data and fixed \(q\), enumerate the candidate mergers at each step. Each accepted merger reduces the number \(r\) of blocks, giving a polynomial-time algorithm in \(r,\tau\), channel dimensions, and bit length. The corrected bound from the paper’s counting argument is \(O(r^{q+1})\) merger checks up to polynomial arithmetic costs. With a small number of operational cohorts, \(r\) can scale with \(\tau\), rather than with the total link population.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime if each cohort really shares all the channel and incentive parameters the problem uses, and if coalition decisions are made by cohorts or their mass portions. The paper explicitly allows other initial coalition structures, though its simulations start from individual links. The mirror covers the bounded-merger formation procedure and its terminal-stability goal; it does not cover the paper’s \(\epsilon\)-core characterizations, WF analysis, or simulations.

I would expect this fixed-\(q\) mass-merger problem to be tractable. The open questions are whether a realistic channel model justifies the type-level interaction matrix and population scaling, how terminal outcomes relate to those of large finite networks, and what changes when \(q\) is unrestricted.

The weak point is the physical and strategic coarsening. The paper’s players are individual links, and its standard initialization treats them as singleton coalitions. The mirror treats mass portions as coalition members and normalizes aggregate interference; those choices make the continuum well-defined, but they need not preserve the authors’ intended coalition game. If they reject that co-modification, the tractability result may read as a mean-field wireless coalition model rather than a continuous form of their problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 1, and it is a genuine computational result. The strongest objection is that its input is an individually labelled channel network, not just a population of interchangeable link types. A link’s payoff and zero-forcing action depend on its channels to particular receivers. In a generic \(K\)-link network, the full payoff-relevant channel profiles do not repeat, so grouping links by antenna count or coarse channel class loses information that determines which mergers are acceptable.

The proposed type-kernel model repairs that problem by assuming links of a type have identical interactions with every receiver type, then scaling aggregate interference by mass. That is a permissible co-modification in principle, but it changes the network substantially. In the paper’s ZF model, a transmitter turns off when its coalition has more members than it has antennas. A positive-mass coalition therefore eventually exceeds any fixed antenna count. Keeping the individual receiver constraints makes large coalitions degenerate; replacing them with one constraint per receiver type changes the game by collapsing many distinct constraints into a few. WF avoids that particular ZF limit, but its continuous version still needs an averaged channel-covariance model and per-capita interference scaling. That is a mean-field wireless coalition game, rather than a direct high-multiplicity version of the paper’s generic full-CSI network.

There is also a mismatch in what Theorem 1 counts. Its \(q\) bounds the number of existing coalitions that may merge, not the number of players or the mass involved. If continuous coalition structures may have arbitrarily many pieces, the paper’s finite enumeration argument has no finite input-size bound. If the state is instead a finite list of mass blocks, the argument works on that list: the corrected counting bound is \(O(r^{q+1})\) checks for \(r\) blocks, and \(r\leq\tau\) only under additional restrictions on how type mass may be split. That can be a sensible restricted model, but those restrictions are doing important work.

These objections make the proposed mirror model-specific and fragile, not impossible. A repeated-cell deployment with a finite interaction kernel, WF precoding, and finitely represented mass blocks could be a coherent continuous question that the authors might recognize. The honest negative case is therefore weak against the burden as stated: it challenges whether that model is a mirror of this paper’s channel game, but it cannot rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror in every plausible scenario.
