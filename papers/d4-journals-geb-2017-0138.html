Why no mirror

The proposed type-mass efficiency problem is precise and could be a useful, authors-recognizable extension. But Theorem 7(d) is a characterization, and the paper does not use its LP proof to compute an outcome or establish a computational guarantee, so the anchor fails bit (a).

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only Theorem 7(d)'s ex-ante efficiency characterization; it leaves aside the other characterizations and implications, the no-trade representation results, and Proposition 16's price-of-no-transfers bound.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can rational mass plans be mapped exactly to lotteries over one-to-one matchings of cloned agents while preserving each agent's ex-ante Pareto comparison?
  • Does Theorem 7(d)'s dual characterization extend to arbitrary baseline mass plans when types are refined to preserve baseline payoffs?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is one narrow lead: a continuous, type-based version of ex-ante Pareto efficiency. The paper has no named NP-hardness or running-time result. Its relevant anchor is Theorem 7(d), proved here: for the identity matching, define \(R_{ij}=U(i,i)-U(i,j)\) and \(S_{ij}=V(j,j)-V(i,j)\). The matching is ex-ante Pareto efficient if and only if there are numbers \(v_i\) and positive weights \(\lambda_i,\mu_j\) such that, for every \(i,j\), \(v_j-v_i\leq \lambda_iR_{ij}+\mu_jS_{ij}\). The authors prove this using LP duality. This is a characterization rather than a complexity theorem, but it is the paper’s best anchor under the allowance for a formulation that the authors compute with.

Call the mirror Continuous-Type Ex-Ante Pareto Efficiency. An instance has finite male and female type sets \(X,Y\), positive rational masses \(p_x,q_y\) summing to one on each side, cardinal utilities \(u_{xy},v_{xy}\), and a baseline matching plan \(z^0\). A type records the agent’s complete utility vector over opposite-side types, so members of a type are indistinguishable for this problem. A matching plan \(z_{xy}\geq0\) specifies the mass paired across each type pair, with \(\sum_y z_{xy}=p_x\) and \(\sum_x z_{xy}=q_y\). Its entries describe how matching mass is allocated, not divisible partners; each unit is matched to one partner in a realized assignment.

The question is whether there is another feasible plan \(z\) that makes every type weakly better off in expected utility and makes some positive mass strictly better off. For example, the no-loss constraints are \(\sum_y z_{xy}u_{xy}\geq\sum_y z^0_{xy}u_{xy}\) for every \(x\), and \(\sum_x z_{xy}v_{xy}\geq\sum_x z^0_{xy}v_{xy}\) for every \(y\). Among plans satisfying them, maximize \(\sum_{x,y}z_{xy}(u_{xy}+v_{xy})\). A plan with value strictly above the baseline is a witness that the baseline is inefficient; otherwise it is efficient. With rational input, this is an LP with \(|X||Y|\) plan variables, so I expect the mirror to be tractable in the support sizes and input bit length.

A plausible regime is a large two-sided hiring market: many applicants and positions, but relatively few credential and job types. Within a type, applicants share the same payoff vector over job types, and positions share the same payoff vector over applicant types. The market might have hundreds of thousands of agents on each side but only dozens or hundreds of types. That is a scenario-specific high-multiplicity claim, not a claim that every labor market has this structure.

This should be recognizable to the authors as their problem: it keeps the two-sided market, cardinal payoffs, matching, and ex-ante Pareto comparison. The population is what becomes continuous. The mass plan is the needed co-modification: it records how much of each cohort is matched across type pairs, while the underlying outcomes remain one-to-one matches. The paper already defines ex-ante efficiency using lotteries over matchings, so a type-level matching plan is a natural anonymous counterpart to that object.

The mirror raises useful follow-up questions: does Theorem 7(d)’s specific dual characterization extend to arbitrary baseline mass plans; what exact discrete-to-continuous dictionary holds for rational cohort sizes; and what changes if the type space is infinite and the LP needs a pricing oracle? Its scope is Theorem 7(d)’s ex-ante efficiency result, not the paper’s broader claims about stability or the price of no transfers.

The weakest point is that this paper supplies no complexity classification, and ex-ante efficiency is already amenable to LP methods in the finite model. The mirror’s case is therefore about an authors-recognizable high-multiplicity version and an LP whose size tracks the number of types, not about showing that discrete hardness disappears. It also relies on an anonymous type-level lottery; if the authors require separate payoff accounting for named individuals even within a type, the aggregation would need more justification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 7(d) is not a computational result. It characterizes ex-ante Pareto efficiency using Afriat inequalities, and its proof uses LP duality, but the paper states no algorithmic or complexity claim. Turning that characterization into a decision problem for type masses creates a new computational question; it does not mirror a computational result in the paper. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, that is the decisive weakness.

The proposed model also compares type averages, which need not preserve the paper’s individual-by-individual Pareto test. Suppose two agents with the same utility type receive baseline utilities \(0\) and \(10\); assigning both utility \(6\) raises their average from \(5\) to \(6\), while making one worse off. Refining types by baseline matched-partner type could repair this: everyone in each refined cohort has the same baseline payoff, and rational mass plans can be related to lotteries over one-to-one clone matchings. That makes a stronger version of the question plausible, but it remains a new type-level LP extension rather than a computational result established by this paper.

So I would reject this as a green mirror of a named computational result. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists is much less secure: large markets with repeated preference and utility types are plausible, and a baseline-refined formulation could preserve the ex-ante comparison. The negative case is strongest on the missing computational anchor, not on the worth of that research question in its own right.
