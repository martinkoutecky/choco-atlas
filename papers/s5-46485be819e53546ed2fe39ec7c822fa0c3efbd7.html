The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(t\in\mathcal T\), masses \(\mu_t\), rate parameters \(a_t\) with \(q_t=\mu_ta_t\) and \(\sum_t q_t=1\), and value laws \(F_t\), find a succinct allocation kernel \(P_t(k)\) for nonzero count vectors \(k\in\mathbb N^{|\mathcal T|}\). Require \(P_t(k)\ge 0\), \(P_t(k)=0\) when \(k_t=0\), and \(\sum_{t:k_t>0}P_t(k)=1\). For each type with \(q_t>0\), if the other bidders’ counts are independent \(K_s\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(q_s)\), require \(\mathbb E[P_t(K+e_t)/(K_t+1)]=1-e^{-1}\); when exactly two bidders of different types are present, require each type to receive probability at most \(1/2\), with same-type bidders split uniformly. Can an oracle returning \(P(k)\) to additive error \(\varepsilon\) be computed in time polynomial in \(|\mathcal T|\), input bit length \(L\), and \(1/\varepsilon\)?

The model it lives in

A diffuse population has type masses \(\mu_t\), with each type’s users sharing value law \(F_t\) and per-user share scaled as \(a_t/N\) in a finite population of size \(N\). Conditional on a tagged user bidding, the other bidders converge to independent Poisson counts with rates \(q_t=\mu_ta_t\); the item remains indivisible and is assigned using the type-count kernel. Strategic behavior is evaluated for a tagged user in the resulting Poisson game, rather than as a deterministic Wardrop flow.

The objection that survived

The limit retains a tagged atomic user and integer Poisson bidder counts, so it changes the finite-agent Bayesian Nash setting into a Poisson-game best-response problem; uniform robustness against arbitrarily coordinated opponents is not established.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

It covers the type-symmetric limiting computation behind Theorem 6.3 and its equilibrium and robustness targets; it leaves the finite-\(N\) MWU algorithm, exact finite-size corrections, and the paper’s structural proofs untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove that the pairwise allocation caps give the claimed robustness against arbitrary coordinated opponent behavior in the Poissonized model.
  • Establish how the finite-\(N\) Bayesian Nash and robustness guarantees converge as \(N\) and the horizon grow, and whether the kernel oracle can avoid dependence on \(N\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest computational anchor is Theorem 6.3, proved in this paper using the multiplicative-weights framework of [5]. With \(K=T\) and the parameters from Lemma 6.2, Algorithm 6.3 computes allocation probabilities in time polynomial in \(T\), \(n\), and the reciprocal fair shares. It makes each agent’s \(\alpha_i\)-aggressive strategy an \(O\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{T}\operatorname{poly}(n,(1/\alpha_j)_j)}\right)\)-approximate equilibrium. At equilibrium, each agent gets at least \(\left(1-\prod_j(1-\alpha_j)\right)v_i^\star\) minus that error, with probability \(1-O(\operatorname{poly}(n,(1/\alpha_j)_j)/T^2)\). Against arbitrary behavior by the other agents, it also gives the paper’s robust guarantee with the same error probability. The robust bound in Theorem 6.3 is printed as \(\frac12+\frac12\alpha_i^2\) minus the error, without a factor of \(v_i^\star\); Theorem 3.6 includes that factor, so I would not rely on the apparent omission.

I would not use Theorem 5.6 as a computational-hardness anchor: its “hardness” is an upper bound on achievable robustness, not a claim of NP-hardness. Theorem 6.3 is the paper’s algorithmic anchor; the paper supplies no comparable named complexity-hardness result.

Here is a concrete population regime. Suppose a large cloud service or research facility allocates one indivisible slot per round among many users. Users fall into a fixed set \(\mathcal T\) of \(\tau\) classes. Within class \(t\), users have the same bounded per-round value distribution \(F_t\), the same fair-share parameter, and the same budget rule. Let \(\mu_t>0\) be the fraction of users in that class, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For a population of \(N\) users, give each class-\(t\) user fair share \(\alpha_{t,N}=a_t/N\), where \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t=1\). Thus total fair share remains one, while \(N\) grows and \(\tau\) stays fixed. This is a genuine high-multiplicity regime: type means the user’s value law and share, and the population—not the outcome space—is the continuous object. Each round still allocates one item to one user. Type-level allocation probabilities are averages over many interchangeable users.

My lead mirror is the Poisson-Type Robust Border Synthesis problem. Its input is \((\mathcal T,\mu,a,F)\) as above. Put \(q_t=\mu_ta_t\), so \(\sum_t q_t=1\). In the finite-\(N\) population, the number of class-\(t\) users who bid in a round has a binomial distribution with mean tending to \(q_t\); jointly, the bid counts tend to independent \(K_t\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(q_t)\).

A solution is an allocation kernel \(P_t(k)\), defined for every nonzero count vector \(k\in\mathbb N^\tau\): conditional on those counts, the rule gives the item to a bidder from class \(t\) with probability \(P_t(k)\), then selects uniformly among that class’s \(k_t\) bidders. It must satisfy
\[ P_t(k)\ge 0,\qquad P_t(k)=0\ \text{if }k_t=0,\qquad \sum_{t:k_t>0}P_t(k)=1. \]
For a tagged class-\(t\) bidder, let \(K\) be the vector of other bidders’ Poisson counts. The equilibrium interim probability must satisfy
\[ \mathbb E\!\left[\frac{P_t(K+e_t)}{K_t+1}\right]=1-e^{-1} \quad\text{for every }t\text{ with }q_t>0. \]
This is the limit of the paper’s target \(1-\prod_i(1-\alpha_i)\). For two bidders of different types, require each type to have allocation probability at most \(1/2\); within one type, split the item uniformly. More precisely, the kernel should be the limit of finite-\(N\) rules satisfying the paper’s pairwise robust bounds \(p_j^{\{i,j\}}\le(1+\alpha_i)/2\). Those bounds, together with the individual token budgets, are what let the paper’s robustness argument handle correlated and adversarial opponents. Since \(\alpha_{t,N}\to0\), the robust fraction tends to \(1/2\) of each user’s ideal utility. A computational answer would give a succinct kernel or an oracle that computes \(P(k)\) to additive error \(\varepsilon\), rather than listing an infinite table.

I expect this restricted mirror to be tractable in \((\tau,L,1/\varepsilon)\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. Under equilibrium, the total bid count is \(\operatorname{Poisson}(1)\), so its tail can be truncated; for each count vector, allocating among active types is a small linear optimization problem. The finite Border inequalities also have a natural type-level limit: for a set of types \(A\), their right-hand side tends to \(1-\exp(-\sum_{t\in A}q_t)\). These are reasons to attempt a Poissonized, type-symmetric version of the paper’s allocation-rule computation, not a proof that the algorithm exists. Theorem 5.6 also supports the expected boundary: its upper bound \(\frac12+\frac12\sum_i\alpha_i^2+o(1)\) tends to \(1/2\) in this regime.

The paper’s distributions \(F_i\) are uncertainty about values; by themselves they do not make the population continuous. The proposed mirror instead aggregates users by their full relevant characteristics. It covers the computation of the allocation rule behind Theorem 6.3 and the equilibrium and robustness targets that rule is intended to achieve. Further questions include whether the robust constraints survive the Poisson limit uniformly over correlated opponents, whether the equilibrium and population limits commute, and whether the kernel can be computed with no dependence on \(N\).

The weakest point is substantial: Theorem 6.3’s running time depends polynomially on \(1/\alpha_{\min}\), which grows with \(N\) here. Its algorithm therefore does not itself establish a population-size-independent computation. Also, each user’s ideal utility tends to zero; the meaningful limit is the guarantee as a fraction of that ideal utility. The continuous question requires a new type-compression and robustness argument. The authors could reasonably accept it as their high-multiplicity computational analogue, but it is a proposed next problem, not a result already contained in their theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 6.3 is a real computational anchor: the paper gives an algorithm for computing the allocation rule that supports its equilibrium and robustness guarantees. So the negative case cannot rest on saying there is no computational result.

The best proposed mirror is a large cohort of interchangeable users, with \(N_t\sim\mu_tN\) users of type \(t\) and each user’s share scaled as \(\alpha_{t,N}=a_t/N\). But this limit is singular for the result being mirrored. Each user’s ideal utility and budget vanish, and only \(O(1)\) users bid in a round. The allocation still goes to one discrete user. To retain the theorem’s individual utility guarantees, the model must condition on a tagged user and preserve integer bidder counts; an aggregate distribution over types alone cannot express those guarantees. Adding a tagged user and Poisson counts makes a coherent model, but the computational object is then a Poisson game with a continuous type parameter, not a mass-allocation problem over a continuum of society.

That distinction matters for the claimed algorithmic content. Under the proposed Poisson limit, choosing uniformly among bidders gives the tagged user interim probability
\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{1+\operatorname{Poisson}(1)}\right]=1-e^{-1}, \]
and splits a two-bidder contest equally. It therefore meets the limiting equilibrium target and pairwise robustness bound without the paper’s allocation-rule synthesis. The technical problem Theorem 6.3 solves is in the finite-\(N\) corrections: making exact interim guarantees and robust bounds coexist for individually different shares. Those corrections disappear in the proposed limit. Asking whether that simple kernel is the limit of finite mechanisms would be coherent, but it would be a convergence question; it would not carry over the theorem’s computational problem.

The obvious repair is to keep finite-\(N\) effects, perhaps as a family of type-symmetric mechanisms with exact constraints. But then the instance must retain \(N\) or its finite multiplicities, and the result remains a finite-agent algorithm. The paper’s running time is polynomial in \(n\) and \(1/\alpha_{\min}\), both of which grow in the proposed regime. The theorem supplies no population-size-independent computation, and the Poisson truncation used for equilibrium counts cannot certify behavior against arbitrary colluding opponents. A compressed exact algorithm could still exist, but finding one is a new finite-size algorithmic question rather than a consequence of continuizing the society.

This is a serious reason to doubt that the proposed mirror is worth ChoCo’s time: the clean continuum limit sheds the theorem’s computational content, while retaining that content requires restoring the discrete users and their vanishing shares. It is not a decisive universal objection. A tagged-user Poisson model may itself be a sensible computational object, and the authors could recognize it as their problem. The honest negative case is therefore limited: the specific Poisson synthesis anchor does not convincingly mirror Theorem 6.3’s finite computational result, but the paper’s algorithmic anchor survives, so I cannot support the stronger claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
