The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires: none of its numbered propositions or other results asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Its results are analytic characterizations. So I cannot honestly present a formal computational anchor or claim that the paper establishes an algorithmic complexity result.

The closest useful basis is Proposition 6, proved in the paper’s Appendix F. It says that when utility is logarithmic (\(g=1\)), voting yields higher utilitarian welfare than direct optimization by utilitarian committees if and only if
\[ \frac{d^*}{d_m}\ln\!\left(\frac{\hat d}{d_m}\right) < \frac{\hat d}{d_m}-1, \]
where \(d_m\) is the median member’s discount rate, \(d^*=(\vec d^{-1}\cdot\vec y)/(\vec d^{-2}\cdot\vec y)\), and \(\hat d=(\vec d^{-1}\cdot\vec y)^{-1}\). This is a welfare-comparison theorem, not a complexity result.

A plausible lead mirror, built on that proposition, is the *Continuum Discount-Committee Comparison* problem. Its instance is a large electorate represented by a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over types \(t=(d_t,a_t)\): each type has a rational discount rate \(d_t>0\), a rational utilitarian weight factor \(a_t>0\), and mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). All members share logarithmic utility over consumption from a public asset, whose stock obeys \(\dot S=rS-C\). The same type distribution recurs in each period. Let \(q_t=\mu_ta_t/\sum_s\mu_sa_s\), and assume the mass distribution has a unique median discount rate \(d_m\). The question is: does the voting equilibrium give higher \(q\)-weighted welfare than the utilitarian-committee equilibrium? A solution is the correct YES/NO answer, equivalently deciding the Proposition 6 inequality using \(q\) in place of the individual weights. The instance has \(\tau\) types, regardless of the much larger number of people represented.

A credible regime would be repeated public decisions about consumption from a sovereign wealth fund or another shared asset, made by a large, stable electorate whose members fall into a few common normative positions on discounting. Their action is the current consumption rate; their mass is their population share. This is population continuization, not merely fractionalizing the asset or its allocation. For the paper’s particular welfare weighting in Eq. (16), take \(a_t=d_t\).

The model is especially recognizable to the authors: they explicitly say in footnote 1 that their results extend to a continuum of committee members as \(N\to\infty\). That supports the mirror’s plausibility, though it also exposes its weakness: the continuous-member limit is already acknowledged in the paper, and Proposition 6 nearly gives the comparison formula outright. The new question would be its bit-complexity analysis on binary-rational type masses and discount rates. I would expect the additive-approximation version to be tractable, since the log-utility comparison reduces to sums of rational terms and a logarithm. Whether exact YES/NO comparison has a polynomial-time bound is a further question the paper does not answer.

I would therefore treat this as a modest positive case: there is a well-defined computational question over a plausible high-multiplicity version of the authors’ model, and they would likely recognize it as theirs. But it is not anchored by a computational result from the paper, and the formula may leave little complexity landscape to chart. Natural extensions are exact bit complexity, the \(g>1\) welfare comparison using Proposition 1’s equilibrium equation, and whether the results survive heterogeneous utility functions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 6 is not a computational result. It is an analytic welfare comparison with an explicit necessary-and-sufficient condition. The proposed “Continuum Discount-Committee Comparison” turns that condition into a YES/NO input-output problem; it does not uncover a computational task the paper left unresolved. Given finite support, the median and the weighted sums are calculated directly from the types. For \(g=1\), the remaining comparison is the stated logarithmic inequality. Exact bit complexity could be studied, but its main difficulty would be exact numerical comparison, not a computational structure in the social-choice model.

The best version of this mirror would broaden the task to computing the equilibrium and welfare ranking for \(g>1\), or to measuring how much the population distribution must change to reverse the ranking. But Proposition 1 already reduces the equilibrium to a unique scalar equation, and Proposition 6 supplies the comparison for the log-utility case. A distributional-robustness problem could be useful, but it adds a new perturbation or cost model that the paper does not study. A richer version with heterogeneous utility functions and production would be harder, but the paper supplies no result to anchor it; it becomes a new dynamic-equilibrium computation problem.

There is also unusually direct evidence that continuity of the committee population is already natural to these authors: footnote 1 says their results extend to a continuum of committee members as \(N\to\infty\). That is not prior computational work, and it does not by itself rule out a mirror. It does mean the proposed finite-support society is essentially the paper’s own continuum-member model. Grouping identical discount-rate types compresses the input, but does not reveal the sort of unresolved algorithmic bottleneck ChoCo targets, such as pricing over exponentially many alternatives or finding an intervention.

So I can defeat the anchor as a *strong computational mirror*: it is an analytic theorem wrapped as a decision problem, with exact arithmetic as the most plausible remaining wrinkle. I cannot honestly make the universal negative airtight. A stable population with a few shared discount-rate views is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the authors would recognize it. If ChoCo counts computing or stress-testing analytic equilibria as worthwhile computational questions, a broader mirror may survive.
