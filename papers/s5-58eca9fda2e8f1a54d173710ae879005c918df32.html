Its numbered theorems make claims about when Shapley allocations apply, not about the complexity of computing them. The \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-completeness statement on page 2 is attributed to other authors and concerns a voting game.

The best case against “no worthwhile mirror” is that the paper’s central subject still admits a plausible high-multiplicity computational question. Imagine many small providers in repeated network roles. A type would include the provider’s costs, capacity, customers, routing role, and contracts; a distribution over those types would describe the population. One could ask how to compute a type-level mass allocation based on providers’ marginal contributions, and whether a continuous optimization method can do so efficiently. That asks a computational question about a population, rather than about fractional outcomes. It also tests a claim central to this paper: whether Shapley-style allocation can be made meaningful for network access pricing.

But the model is hard to make faithful. The paper emphasizes topology, bilateral agreements, routing choices, and changing costs. If those details are part of a complete type, providers may rarely share a type; if they are omitted, the type distribution may lose information the allocation depends on. And a Shapley value for named providers does not automatically extend to a continuum: an individual provider’s mass contribution can vanish, while a type-level allocation would need a carefully justified definition.

So there is a conceivable continuous follow-up, especially for networks with many repeated provider roles. That weakens the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. It does not, however, defeat the narrower objection that the paper lacks an eligible result to mirror. The strongest case against the proponent remains speculative and depends on building a new computational model around the paper’s topic.
