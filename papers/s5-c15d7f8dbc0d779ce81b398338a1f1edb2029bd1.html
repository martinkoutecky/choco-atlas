The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) divisible goods, \(K\) complete user types with rational masses \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{K}\), \(\sum_k\mu_k=1\), type budgets \(w_k\), utility vectors \(u_k\), and per-capita capacities \(d\), compute a common price vector \(p>0\) and tie-split type bundles \(x_k\) satisfying \(x_k\in\operatorname{conv}\arg\max\{u_k^{\top}x:p^{\top}x\le w_k,\ x\ge0\}\), aggregate demand \(\sum_k\mu_kx_k\le d+\varepsilon\mathbf{1}\), and mass-weighted Eisenberg–Gale regret \(U_{\mu}^{\star}-\sum_k\mu_kw_k\log(u_k^{\top}x_k)\le\varepsilon\), in time polynomial in \(m\), \(K\), the encoding length \(L\), and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support high-multiplicity Fisher market with type masses \(\mu_k\), per-capita supplies \(d\), type parameters \((w_k,u_k)\), a common posted price \(p\), tie-split best-response bundles \(x_k\), and the mass-weighted Eisenberg–Gale objective.

What the mirror covers

The mirror directly covers the static-pricing and equilibrium portion represented by Theorem 1 and supports a fluid or cohort extension of Theorem 2. It does not directly cover the revealed-preference rates in Theorems 3 and 4, the feasible-variant corollaries, or the numerical experiments.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the mass-weighted Eisenberg–Gale optimum and supporting prices be computed to additive \(\varepsilon\) in time polynomial in \(m\), \(K\), \(L\), and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\)?
  • What finite-population concentration rate relates the discrete stochastic market to the rational-mass problem?
  • Can a cohort-mass version of Algorithm 1 obtain meaningful regret and capacity-violation guarantees under a specified arrival law?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a qualified yes. The paper has no named NP-hardness, P-versus-NP, W[1]-hardness, or FPT theorem, so this cannot be a hardness-transfer mirror. Its anchors are instead two proved performance theorems, with Theorem 1 as the lead: it exposes a finite-population obstruction that disappears when the population itself is represented by mass.

My lead problem is Continuum Static Fisher Pricing. An instance has \(m\) goods, \(K\) complete user types \(k\in[K]\), a rational mass vector \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{K}\) with \(\sum_k\mu_k=1\), type budgets \(w_k>0\), utility vectors \(u_k\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{m}\), and per-capita capacities \(d\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{m}\). Type \(k\) is the continuum of agents sharing exactly \((w_k,u_k)\); its mass is \(\mu_k\). The target is not a named-user allocation but a uniform posted price \(p>0\) and type-level bundles \(x_k\) satisfying

\[ x_k\in\arg\max_{x\ge 0}\{u_k^\top x:p^\top x\le w_k\}, \]

together with

\[ \sum_k\mu_k x_{kj}\le d_j \]

for every good \(j\). Ties may be resolved by a type-level splitting rule.

The continuum oracle is the Eisenberg–Gale problem

\[ U_\mu^\star= \max_{x_k\ge0} \sum_k\mu_k w_k\log(u_k^\top x_k) \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \sum_k\mu_kx_{kj}\le d_j. \]

The mirror asks: given \((m,K,\mu,w,u,d)\), find \(p\) and demand-feasible \(x\) minimizing the continuum regret

\[ R_\mu=U_\mu^\star- \sum_k\mu_k w_k\log(u_k^\top x_k) \]

and the capacity violation

\[ V_\mu= \left\| \left(\sum_k\mu_kx_k-d\right)^+ \right\|_2, \]

or, in decision form, determine whether \(R_\mu\le\varepsilon\) and \(V_\mu\le\varepsilon\). The input size depends on \(m,K\), and the encoding length of the type data, not on the number \(N\) of individuals represented by the masses.

This is a natural regime for large voucher or social-credit programmes: millions of recipients, but a modest catalogue of standardized budget-and-preference profiles. The relevant comparison is \(N\gg K\), with each type containing many interchangeable agents. This is precisely high multiplicity, not an assumption that individual prices mysteriously become identical.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states:

If users’ budget and utility parameters are drawn i.i.d. from a distribution \(D\), then there exists a market instance for which every static pricing algorithm has either expected regret \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\) or expected constraint violation \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\), where \(n\) is the number of arriving users.

The theorem’s lower-bound instance has \(n\) unit-budget users, two goods of capacity \(n\) each, and each user independently has utility \((1,0)\) or \((0,1)\) with probability \(1/2\). The result is proved here, not cited.

The continuous mirror gives a clean explanation of what the theorem is measuring. Take

\[ \mu_1=\mu_2=\frac12,\qquad d=(1,1),\qquad w_1=w_2=1, \]

with utilities \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\). The static price \(p=(1/2,1/2)\) induces

\[ x_1=(2,0),\qquad x_2=(0,2). \]

Aggregate demand is exactly

\[ \frac12(2,0)+\frac12(0,2)=(1,1), \]

so \(V_\mu=0\), and the continuum allocation attains \(U_\mu^\star=\log 2\), so \(R_\mu=0\). In the finite model, the number \(S\) of first-type users is binomial. The same price produces consumption \(2S\) of the first good, and \(\mathbb{E}[(2S-n)^+]=\Theta(\sqrt n)\). Thus the \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\) barrier is sampling noise caused by population multiplicity, not a structural impossibility of static Fisher pricing.

I therefore expect this mirror to be Class A. The type-compressed Eisenberg–Gale programme and its dual prices can be solved to prescribed accuracy in time polynomial in \(m\), \(K\), the input encoding length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\), rather than in \(N\). The paper itself already contains the nearby certainty-equivalent programme \(\mathrm{CE}(d)\), but it uses that as an internal tool for a finite-\(n\) stochastic model. Making \(\mu\) itself the society and asking for the high-multiplicity equilibrium is a distinct question, not merely a restatement of the paper’s main theorem.

A second, supporting anchor is Theorem 2, also proved in this paper. It concerns Algorithm 1, the adaptive expected-equilibrium pricing policy. Under a finite-support discrete distribution \(D\) satisfying Assumption 1, with prices bounded between positive constants, the algorithm maintains the average remaining capacity

\[ d_t=\frac{c-\sum_{s<t}x_s}{n-t+1}. \]

Until the first time \(\tau\) when \(d_t\) leaves the band \([d-\Delta,d+\Delta]\), it posts the dual prices of \(\mathrm{CE}(d_t)\); afterwards it posts the dual prices of \(\mathrm{CE}(d)\). If \(x_t\) is chosen from the corresponding certainty-equivalent optimum, then Theorem 2 states

\[ V_n(\pi)\le O(1) \qquad\text{and}\qquad R_n(\pi)\le O(\log n). \]

The natural continuous counterpart is Continuum Cohort Posted Pricing. The instance has the same finite type set and per-capita capacities, but the population arrives in a finite sequence of \(H\) cohorts of masses \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_H\). Cohort \(h\) contains type masses \(\nu_{hk}\); the planner posts \(p_h\) before seeing the current cohort’s private composition, observes only its aggregate consumption, and chooses \(p_{h+1}\) from the observed history. The offline benchmark knows every \(\nu_{hk}\) and solves the corresponding continuum Eisenberg–Gale allocation. A solution is an online price policy together with its induced type-level demands, evaluated by continuum regret and capacity violation.

In the deterministic fluid case \(\nu_{hk}=\lambda_h\mu_k\), the adaptive problem collapses to the exact continuum equilibrium and is again Class A. If cohort compositions remain random, the problem retains genuine online uncertainty while eliminating named-agent bookkeeping; a mass-level version of Theorem 2 becomes a worthwhile new theorem. The paper’s Algorithm 1 is an obvious starting point because its certainty-equivalent subproblem is already expressed over finitely many types.

I would not claim that Theorems 3 and 4 transfer automatically. Their revealed-preference guarantees, step-size schedules, and \(O(\sqrt n)\) or \(O(n^{2/5})\) rates are specifically about learning from a sequence of individually sampled users. A faithful continuous version needs a declared cohort or mass-arrival model. Likewise, continuous \(D\), unknown \(\mu\), and revealed-preference pricing may generate genuinely new continuum-specific questions.

The weakest point is therefore clear: the main mirror removes the sampling uncertainty that gives the paper much of its drama, and its static version is very close to the paper’s own certainty-equivalent programme. A sceptical referee could say that the mirror is an equilibrium-computation problem rather than the original online-learning problem. The positive case survives because that is exactly the high-multiplicity question the programme asks: does the discrete population obstruction persist when the society is represented by type mass? Here the answer appears to be no, and Theorem 1 supplies unusually concrete evidence that continuization changes the computational and performance landscape rather than merely changing notation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both anchors lose the very phenomenon they purport to continuize.

Theorem 1 is a stochastic fluctuation result, not a population-allocation complexity result. In its two-type instance, the obstruction is \(S\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/2)\): static prices face a realized empirical fraction \(S/n\) rather than its mean. If the population is replaced by known mass \(\mu=(1/2,1/2)\), the price \(p=(1/2,1/2)\) clears the market exactly, with \(V_\mu=0\) and \(R_\mu=0\). The finite violation satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[(2S-n)^+]=\Theta(\sqrt n)\), but after per-capita normalization it is \(\Theta(n^{-1/2})\), which vanishes in the continuum.

That is not merely a different answer. The lower-bound object has disappeared: the continuum has replaced a realized random population by its expectation. The proposed “Continuum Static Fisher Pricing” is just the deterministic certainty-equivalent Eisenberg–Gale programme already written in the paper as \(\mathrm{CE}(d)\), and it is solvable by the standard Fisher-market convex programme. This is not a prior-art objection—the question could still be well-posed—but it means Theorem 1 supplies no new computational phenomenon for ChoCo. Its content is sampling noise, and sampling noise is not the continuous society.

A stronger attempt would retain uncertainty by making the continuum population unknown, or by letting agents arrive in random batches. But then the relevant object is no longer the given mass distribution \(\mu\). It is a stochastic observation or arrival process. If agents arrive individually with infinitesimal mass, each observation has no aggregate capacity effect; if they arrive in positive-mass batches, the result depends on an additional cohort structure, batch sizes, and a law for the random mass vectors. The resulting problem may be interesting, but its difficulty comes from the added stochastic process, not from continuizing the population.

Theorem 2 has the same defect more directly. Its \(O(\log n)\) and \(O(1)\) bounds are driven by the stopping time at which empirical remaining capacities deviate from their expected values. In a deterministic continuum, those empirical deviations are absent, so the stopping-time argument and its rate have no counterpart. The adaptive prices simply track the deterministic remaining mass; there is no finite-population learning problem left.

The proponent’s cohort repair is the best possible version of this anchor, but it changes the problem materially. With deterministic cohorts satisfying \(\nu_{hk}=\lambda_h\mu\), the algorithm collapses to fluid equilibrium computation. With random \(\nu_{hk}\), one has introduced a time-indexed stochastic market whose behaviour is not determined by the continuous society \(\mu\). Different cohort processes with the same aggregate \(\mu\) produce different regret and violation guarantees. That could motivate a new online resource-allocation paper, but it is not a canonical continuous mirror of Theorem 2.

The paper does contain named algorithmic theorems, so “there is no named result” is not a decisive objection. Still, none is a complexity classification or a multiplicity-sensitive computational barrier. The proposed static mirror is an already tractable equilibrium subproblem; the proposed adaptive mirror is either vacuous in the fluid limit or requires a new stochastic arrival model.

This negative case is not airtight. Finite-support food-stamp or social-credit populations are genuinely high-multiplicity regimes, and a carefully specified mass-cohort model could be worthwhile. What can honestly be defeated is the claim that Theorems 1 and 2 themselves establish a strong continuous-population mirror. On that narrower question, both anchors continuize away their essential content.
