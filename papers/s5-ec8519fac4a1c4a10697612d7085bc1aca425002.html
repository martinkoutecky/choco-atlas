The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot supply a qualifying anchor from this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result. The closest algorithmic claim is Section 3.1’s procedure for \((\mathrm{Pref})\): alternate between an LP for the weights and OWA optimization problems that check for violated constraints; it terminates when \(X\) is finite. That is not a polynomial-time result, since it gives no polynomial iteration bound and the OWA subproblems may be hard. The experiments are empirical. So there is no named computational result here that I can honestly restate as an anchor.

The strongest mirror I can make is a proposed extension of the paper’s underlying elicitation problem, not an anchor-backed case. Call it Population-Weighted Passive OWA Elicitation. Imagine many local procurement offices applying one common risk policy across a small number of recurring decision templates. A type \(t\) specifies the office’s feasible set \(X_t\), its \(K\)-scenario cost matrix \(C_t\), and its observed choice \(x_t\). The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of offices of that type, with rational masses summing to one. The regime is many offices but few distinct templates, costs, and observed choices. A shared institutional policy makes a common preference vector plausible.

Let
\[ W'=\{w\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^K:\ \sum_k w_k=1,\ w_1\ge\cdots\ge w_K\} \]
and
\[ P_t=\{u\in W': x_t\in\arg\min_{x\in X_t}\mathrm{OWA}_u(x,C_t)\}. \]
For instances with every \(P_t\ne\varnothing\), the question is: find \(w\in W'\) and \(u_t\in P_t\) minimizing
\[ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t\lVert w-u_t\rVert_1. \]
The solution is a common risk-averse weight vector closest, on average, to the weights that could explain each type’s observed choice. This replaces the paper’s sum over observations with a population-weighted sum while preserving its passive elicitation question. Inconsistent choices with \(P_t=\varnothing\) suggest a further lexicographic version that first minimizes choice violations, as the paper does for individual observations.

The optimization has the paper’s useful LP-with-pricing shape: each \(P_t\) is described by linear inequalities in the weights, while checking those inequalities requires solving an OWA problem over \(X_t\). I would expect tractability when those per-type OWA problems have efficient algorithms; I would not predict it for arbitrary combinatorial feasible sets. Further questions include how complexity depends on \(K\), \(\tau\), and the representation of \(X_t\), and whether the weighted model supports preprocessing or approximation when many offices share a few types.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper studies one decision maker across observations, while this mirror treats many offices as sharing one institutional preference. That scenario is plausible, but it is a population extension the authors would need to accept. More decisively, this candidate cannot meet the requested anchor requirement: it is a concrete research question suggested by the paper’s model, not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no qualifying result anchor in the proponent’s case: they explicitly say the paper has no named complexity result to mirror. The paper does give an iterative method for \((\mathrm{Pref})\) and notes that it terminates when \(X\) is finite, but it gives no polynomial bound or complexity classification. So the proposed population model extends the paper’s method; it does not mirror a result the paper established.

The proposed extension is also the strongest case to examine. Let a type be the full tuple \((X_t,C_t,x_t)\), and let \(P_t\) be the weights that explain its observed choice. If many offices share that tuple, the model’s objective is just a weighted version of the paper’s sum of distances to \(P_t\). Repeating an observation changes its weight; it does not add population-dependent constraints, mass transfers, or a new optimization structure. The pricing work remains the same OWA optimization for each type. This can be useful weighted preference learning, but the continuum is functioning as a compressed data set, not as a society whose collective decision is being computed.

A more social-choice-shaped version would treat the \(K\) criteria as people and the OWA score as their welfare aggregate. That changes what the population axis represents: in the paper, the ordered values come from scenarios or objectives, while \(K\) indexes the weights. A continuous treatment of those criteria would continuize the aggregation or outcome model, not the population of the paper. Making offices into actual agents instead would require specifying how their individual choices produce a collective outcome, or how a distribution of their possibly different weights is to be elicited. Neither question is supplied by the paper’s shared-\(w\) model.

The negative case is strongest against this particular mirror, not against every conceivable reframing. A population of offices following one common policy is plausible, and the authors might recognize weighted observations as a natural extension. But that plausibility does not turn the extension into a continuous-population computational result of this paper. The case against “no worthwhile mirror in any scenario” remains weaker: the paper’s existing formulation could motivate a worthwhile adjacent research question, even though the proposed one does not establish a ChoCo-style mirror.
