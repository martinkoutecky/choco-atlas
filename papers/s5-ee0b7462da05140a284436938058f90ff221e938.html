The case FOR (proponent)

On your anchor rule, this paper has no eligible computational anchor. Its numbered results establish existence or characterize rationalizability; none asserts that a problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. The propositional formulas are used to prove results by logical compactness, not as an encoding the paper computes with.

The closest matching results are Theorem 4.1 (Gale and Shapley, cited): every finite one-to-one two-sided matching market has a stable matching; and Theorem 4.2 (Fleiner, 2003, with a proof given here): every possibly infinite one-to-one two-sided matching market has a stable matching. Both are existence claims, not algorithmic bounds. The closest population-facing result is Theorem 3.9 (proved here): for a possibly infinite set of items, a stochastic-choice dataset is rationalizable by a probability measure over total orders if and only if it satisfies the Axiom of Revealed Stochastic Preference. Its finite-item predecessor, Theorem 3.8, is attributed to McFadden and Richter. Neither gives a computational result; moreover, this paper already says that stochastic choice can represent population fractions of deterministic choices, so that population interpretation is already present in its model.

The strongest *new* mirror I can see adjacent to Theorem 4.1 is a continuous type-level stable-marriage problem. Let \(T_M,T_W\) be finite sets of types, where a type specifies a side and a strict ranking of the opposite-side types, with being unmatched ranked last. Let \(\mu_i,\nu_j\) be rational mass distributions over the two sides, each summing to \(1\). The task is to find a stable mass assignment \(x_{ij}\ge 0\), where \(x_{ij}\) is the mass of type \(i\) matched to type \(j\), and row and column sums do not exceed \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\). Stability requires, for every pair \(i,j\), that at least one of these residual masses be zero:

\[ \mu_i-\sum_{j':\,j'\succeq_i j}x_{ij'}=0 \quad\text{or}\quad \nu_j-\sum_{i':\,i'\succeq_j i}x_{i'j}=0. \]

The residuals are the masses who would strictly prefer the other type to their current assignment or being unmatched. Thus a solution is a coupling with no positive mass on both sides available to form a blocking pair. There is no objective: this mirrors the existence question in Theorem 4.1. A plausible regime is a large labour or placement market with millions of applicants and positions but relatively few qualification, occupation, and institution types. A continuous algorithmic question would be whether such an \(x\) can be found efficiently in the number of types and the bit length of the masses. I would tentatively expect tractability, given the structure of stable matching, but the paper proves no complexity claim for this model.

The soft spot is substantial: preferences over counterpart *types* abstract away distinctions among named individuals, so this is a type-level stable-matching variant, not a result already established by Theorem 4.1. It is plausible that matching theorists would recognize it as their problem, but acceptance of that co-modification would need to be argued. More decisively, it is not anchored in a named computational result of this paper. Under the requested standard, this is a candidate research question, not a valid anchored positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that there is no computational result here to continuize. Theorem 4.1 is an existence claim for a finite market; Theorem 4.2 extends existence to an infinite market. Theorem 3.9 characterizes stochastic rationalizability. None specifies a computational task or makes a complexity claim. Logical compactness is the paper’s proof method, not an algorithm or an analysis of computational resources. A search problem can be built around these models, but that would be a new algorithmic project, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The proposed stable-marriage mirror is plausible as its own model: rational masses of types with rankings over counterpart types, and a type-level blocking condition. But that model changes the object being studied. The paper’s matching theorems concern individually indexed agents who rank other named agents; its infinite-market result still retains those identities. A mass assignment over types replaces those rankings and individual blocking pairs with a new type-level formulation. That co-modification may be sensible, but it does not supply the missing computational anchor. Even the stronger version of the proposal—a carefully encoded search problem for rational mass assignments—would be an adjacent research question rather than a mirror of Theorem 4.1 or 4.2.

Theorem 3.9 is closer to a continuous population model, but that weakens the case for a *mirror*: the paper explicitly says stochastic choice can represent population fractions of deterministic choices, and its rationalizability model already uses a probability measure over complete rankings. In that interpretation, the distribution over types is already present. Studying the computational complexity of recognizing or constructing such a distribution could be worthwhile, but it would add a computational question to an already distributional model; it would not continuize the population in the theorem.

So the negative case is decisive for the requested standard: this paper offers no named computational result whose continuous complexity landscape could be mirrored. I would not claim that no useful algorithmic work can be developed from its matching or rationalizability models. The more limited conclusion is that such work would be a new project, not a continuous mirror of this paper.
