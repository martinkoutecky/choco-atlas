What the mirror covers

The proposed LP covers only the truncated-welfare allocation component associated with Theorem 1; it leaves out bidder-level externality payments and the incentive and Pareto guarantees, and it does not model Theorem 2's affordability mechanism.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result: no theorem states that a problem is in P, hard, or belongs to a parameterized complexity class. The closest anchor is Theorem 1, proved here: for single-minded bidders, truncation VCG is individually rational and Pareto-optimal, and at profiles with a unique allocation, truthful reports of valuations and budgets are optimal. Those are mechanism properties, not a complexity result. Theorem 2 is also about mechanism properties: budget VCG is individually rational, Pareto-optimal when it implements a non-null allocation, and makes truthful budget reporting a dominant strategy. So neither qualifies under the requested anchor rule.

The strongest positive lead I can offer is therefore provisional, not a valid anchored case: study the winner-determination problem underlying Theorem 1. Consider a replicated market with many bidders but few complete types. A type \(t\) specifies a desired bundle \(S_t\subseteq G\), value \(v_t\), and budget \(b_t\); \(\mu_t\) is its share of the population. Let \(q_j\) be the supply of item \(j\) per unit population, and let \(y_t\) be the mass of type \(t\) that receives its whole desired bundle. The rest receive nothing. Given these rational inputs, find feasible \(y\) maximizing

\[ \sum_{t} y_t\min\{v_t,b_t\} \quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\le y_t\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t:\,j\in S_t}y_t\le q_j\ \ \text{for each }j\in G. \]

Each recipient still gets an integral bundle; only the aggregate number of recipients of a type is fractional. This is a linear program with \(\tau\) allocation variables, so this continuous allocation problem is tractable. It mirrors the truncated-welfare allocation chosen by truncation VCG, but does not claim that the paper establishes this computational result.

The plausible regime is a replicated licensing or procurement market: many local markets with the same menu of resource types, and bidders grouped by bundle, value, and budget. There can be many bidders and relatively few distinct types. The supply must scale with the market; with a fixed stock of indivisible goods and a growing population, per-capita supply vanishes. The paper’s single auction does not itself establish that this replicated regime is one its authors would accept.

The weakest point is also the decisive one for the requested case: the proposed LP is not a named result of the paper, and the paper gives no computational anchor for it. It covers only the allocation component of Theorem 1, not its payment, incentive, or Pareto guarantees, and it does not mirror Theorem 2’s budget-affordability mechanism. Natural follow-up questions are whether finite replicated markets admit additive rounding guarantees, and whether any useful continuous analogue of the paper’s incentive and externality-payment claims survives when an individual bidder has zero mass.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 proves individual rationality, Pareto optimality, and truthful reporting under stated conditions. Theorem 2 proves individual rationality, conditional Pareto optimality, and truthful budget reporting. Neither gives a running time, a hardness result, an approximation guarantee, or another complexity classification. The proponent’s proposed winner-determination LP is therefore a new computational question, not a mirror of a named computational result.

That LP is a coherent idea for replicated markets: types could specify bundles, values, and budgets, while \(y_t\) records the mass of type \(t\) receiving its desired bundle. With supply scaled per capita, it computes aggregate truncated welfare. But it does not reproduce Theorem 1’s mechanism claim: the LP has no bidder-level allocation, externality payment, or unilateral report to which the theorem’s incentive guarantee applies. Recovering those objects requires retaining finite population granularity, or defining new type-level payments and incentive constraints. The first makes the question a finite high-multiplicity computation; the second changes the mechanism and what it means to report truthfully.

Theorem 2 invites a stronger version: define affordability using per-capita externalities in a large market. That could be a worthwhile project. It would require specifying how an individual’s counterfactual payment is represented in the mass model and what strategic unit reports. The paper does not analyze the computation of such a model; it proves a property of its finite-bidder mechanism. The continuum extension would supply the computational question as well as its model.

So the case against is strong under ChoCo’s anchoring standard: both named theorems concern mechanism properties, and the proposed LP isolates an allocation subproblem while leaving those properties behind. The universal claim is weaker. A replicated licensing or procurement market is plausible, and its allocation LP may merit study. I cannot honestly rule out every worthwhile continuous extension; I can say the case presented does not establish a continuous computational mirror of this paper’s results.
