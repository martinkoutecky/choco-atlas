What the mirror covers

No result is covered. Theorem 3.1 and its corollaries are axiomatic characterizations; the suggested continuous-preference extension changes the alternative space, and the infinite-agent extension remains axiomatic.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a plausible population setting: a large electorate choosing among a fixed finite set \(X\), with each type a complete ranking and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of voters holding it. The support could be small relative to the electorate. A nearby question would replace a finite preference profile with a distribution \(\mu\) and ask whether population versions of Strong Unanimity, Independence of Infeasible Alternatives, and Independence of Losing Alternatives characterize some priority rule. The authors might recognize an infinite-population extension as related: Section 7 mentions infinite agents as a possible extension.

But that is an axiomatic research question, and the paper supplies no computational anchor for it. Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper, says that for a finite alternative set with \(|X|\ge 3\), a finite population, and the universal weak-preference domain, every social choice correspondence satisfying those three axioms is serially dictatorial: there is a fixed permutation of individuals and a fixed tie-breaking preference that generate its choices. It is a structural characterization, with no runtime or complexity claim. The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind, so there are no eligible anchors and no per-anchor continuous computational problems to state.

The weak point in even the adjacent mirror is substantial: the theorem’s conclusion orders named individuals, while a distribution over types forgets their identities. A priority order over types would be a new model and a new conclusion; Theorem 3.1 does not establish it. So there is a natural continuous-population direction here, but this paper does not support a ChoCo computational mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest obstacle is that the paper does not state a computational problem. Theorem 3.1 and its corollaries characterize which social choice correspondences satisfy axioms; they do not give a decision, optimization, or algorithmic result. There is no input-output task here for a continuous optimization method to solve. Checking an explicitly represented rule against the axioms, or designing a rule that satisfies them, would be a new problem rather than a mirror of a result in this paper.

The best population model—an electorate represented by a distribution over rankings—also loses the information that drives the theorem. Serial dictatorship depends on a fixed order of named individuals, and the outcome can be determined by a voter whose share tends to zero. Two sequences of finite electorates can converge to the same distribution while having different first dictators and different outcomes. Keeping the order preserves the theorem’s object but no longer represents society by type masses. Ordering types or making a block of each type act together would instead introduce a new institution and new axioms. An atomless model faces the same choice: retain a distinguished individual or replace individual priority with an aggregate rule.

Nor do the masses supply the sort of computational structure that makes a continuous mirror valuable. The theorem’s axioms and conclusion do not optimize over voter counts or depend on their proportions; the proof constructs a priority order. Given a priority order, evaluating the resulting rule is straightforward. A more substantive task—finding or computing an aggregate rule from a distribution—would need a separately specified objective and representation, neither of which the paper provides.

So the proponent’s large-electorate scenario is plausible as a setting for a new axiomatic question. It does not yield a ChoCo computational mirror of this paper. The paper’s suggested extensions to continuous alternatives or randomized choices concern other objects; its infinite-agent suggestion keeps the focus on axiomatic aggregation. Neither supplies the missing computational anchor.
