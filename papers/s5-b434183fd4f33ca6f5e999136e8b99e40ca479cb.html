The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make a valid case for a continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s named results. Its “Result 1” and “Result 2” are experimental findings: single-ballot voters chose the future-oriented option slightly less often under NPV than OV, and more often under PV than NPV. They are not results about the complexity or algorithms for a computational problem, so they do not meet the requested anchor criterion.

A plausible population model is easy to sketch: types could record a voter’s preferences and behavioral parameters, such as expressive and bandwagon utility; mass would be the fraction of voters of each type; and a voting rule or explanation for the proxy ballot would be the intervention. One might ask which intervention maximizes the mass of future-oriented votes. But that would be a new policy-optimization problem, not a continuous version of a computational problem this paper studies or computes. The paper tests behavior in a small laboratory design; it gives no named computational result for such an optimization question.

So the honest positive case stops at plausibility of a continuous *behavioral* study of a larger population. It does not supply the computational anchor or a precise continuous problem that this programme requires.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I agree with the proponent that neither Result 1 nor Result 2 is a computational result. They report experimental differences in how single-ballot voters chose between two options. The paper also derives payoff comparisons and runs regressions, but it defines no computational problem whose complexity or algorithms a continuous version could investigate. That is the strongest objection under ChoCo’s scope.

There is a natural large-population setting: represent voters by a distribution over types that includes preferences, proxy eligibility, and behavioral traits, then estimate the share choosing the future-oriented option under each voting rule. Real electorates make this a plausible population model. But the resulting question is an empirical treatment-effect question: how does the population’s voting behavior change under the intervention? Once type-specific response rates are supplied, aggregating them is simply \(\sum_t \mu_t p_t\). The paper’s regression is already aimed at estimating those responses; replacing sample counts with population masses does not create a complexity question.

The stronger proposed computational mirror—choose how to assign proxy votes or explain them so as to maximize future-oriented votes—would require a new objective and a model of how each type responds to each policy. The paper studies whether single-ballot voters’ behavior is affected by the scheme and its explanation; it does not optimize proxy assignments or define the constraints and response functions such an optimization needs. If those additions produce a hard problem, the hardness comes from the newly introduced design model, not from continuizing a result in this paper. With fixed response rates and simple assignment constraints, the optimization may instead be routine.

There is also a limit to carrying over the paper’s behavioral mechanism. Its discussion of expressive voting depends on an individual’s chance of being pivotal under finite voting weights. In an atomless electorate, an individual’s weight has zero influence, so that channel changes substantially; retaining it requires finite voters or positive-mass blocs. That does not rule out studying aggregate responses to the proxy explanation, but it leaves a behavioral or empirical study rather than the computational mirror ChoCo seeks.

So the negative case is strong for the programme’s stated purpose, though not universal in the literal sense: the paper could inspire worthwhile research on large-electorate proxy voting. What it does not supply is a computational result to continuize. Making that research computational would require building a different problem around the paper’s findings.
