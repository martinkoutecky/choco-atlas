The continuous mirror question

Given a finite firm set \(F\), worker types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), explicit type-specific utility tables, integral salary intervals \([a_{tj},b_{tj}]\), and per-capita firm capacities \(\kappa_j\), find a stable mass allocation. Use variables \(y_{t,\bot}\) for unmatched mass and \(y_{t,j,z}\) for type-\(t\) mass hired by firm \(j\) at salary \(z\), with \(y_{t,\bot}+\sum_{j,z}y_{t,j,z}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}y_{t,j,z}\leq\kappa_j\). Require every positive-mass contract to be mutually acceptable; a firm's payoff is \(0\) when it has unused capacity and otherwise the minimum firm valuation among its positive-mass hires. Stability means that no positive-mass worker status and other firm have a feasible integral salary at which both strictly prefer to form a contract.

The model it lives in

A distribution over complete worker types, with type mass divisible across statuses but each worker unit receiving one firm or remaining unmatched at an integral salary. Firms retain the paper's minimum-hire valuation and have capacities scaled per unit of worker population. The task is to find a feasible allocation with no blocking worker-firm pair.

The objection that survived

An arbitrarily small mass of low-value hires can set a full firm's minimum payoff, so the paper's finite rejection process does not directly handle workers of one type split across contract statuses.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the stable-outcome algorithm and its termination and correctness guarantees; it leaves polynomial-time complexity and the paper's other model extensions unresolved.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a stable mass allocation always exist when workers of one type can be split across contract statuses?
  • Can one be found in time polynomial in the number of firms, supported types, explicit salary-table size, and input bit length?
  • When do stable allocations of these mass markets arise as limits of stable discrete markets?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best anchor is the paper’s algorithmic result, with an important limit: it proves finite termination, not polynomial time. Theorem 6.6 (proved here) says the proposed salary-adjustment algorithm halts after finitely many iterations. Its proof relies on there being only finitely many mutually acceptable pairs and on feasible salaries being bounded integers that can decrease only finitely often. The companion Theorem 6.5 (also proved here) says that whenever the algorithm terminates, its output is pairwise stable: matched pairs are mutually acceptable, and there is no feasible salary at which an unmatched worker–firm pair would both strictly prefer to contract. Together, these theorems establish that the algorithm produces a stable outcome. They are existence and termination results, not a classification in P or a hardness result; the paper itself leaves a polynomial-time algorithm as an open problem.

My lead mirror is Continuous-Mass Stable Hiring. The regime is a large labor pool with many workers but relatively few large employers: worker types might be standardized credential and preference profiles, while each employer has a large hiring capacity. Let \(T\) be the supported worker types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and let \(F\) be the firms. A worker type specifies the same salary bounds and utility schedules for every worker of that type. For each \(t\in T\) and \(j\in F\), salaries remain integral in a bounded interval \([a_{tj},b_{tj}]\), and the worker and firm utility tables are given explicitly. Firm \(j\)’s capacity is \(\kappa_j\) per unit of worker population; a discrete market with \(N\) workers corresponds to a firm quota of \(N\kappa_j\), when integral. Thus the population becomes continuous while each worker still receives one firm or remains unmatched, at an integral salary.

A solution is a mass allocation \(y_{t,\bot}\) and \(y_{t,j,z}\), where \(\bot\) denotes unmatched and \(y_{t,j,z}\) is the mass of type \(t\) hired by firm \(j\) at salary \(z\). It must satisfy
\[ y_{t,\bot}+\sum_{j\in F}\sum_{z=a_{tj}}^{b_{tj}}y_{t,j,z}=\mu_t, \qquad \sum_{t\in T}\sum_z y_{t,j,z}\leq\kappa_j. \]
Every positive-mass contract must be mutually acceptable. A worker’s payoff is the payoff for their status—unmatched or hired at a particular firm and salary—so the model does not treat a type’s average payoff as though every worker received it. A firm’s payoff is \(0\) when it has unused capacity and otherwise the minimum firm valuation among the positive-mass contracts in its workforce, matching the paper’s \(r_j\) rule. The question is to find such an allocation with no blocking pair: for every positive-mass worker status and every firm other than that worker’s current firm, every feasible salary must leave at least one side unwilling to deviate. The solution is the full mass allocation, with its derived worker and firm payoffs.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: the number of workers can be much larger than \(|T|\), because many applicants share complete utility schedules and salary bounds. The quota scaling is a deliberate co-modification of the model to keep jobs proportional to the growing population. Workers of the same type may be split across different contract statuses, but each unit of mass receives an ordinary integral contract. That preserves the paper’s matching question while making the population itself the continuous object.

I would provisionally expect this mirror to be tractable, as a Class A candidate: expanding the finite salary tables gives finitely many type–firm–salary states, and the matching steps suggest capacitated-flow methods over those states. That is a research direction, not a consequence of Theorems 6.5 or 6.6. Further questions are whether the salary-adjustment process can be compressed to type and status classes, whether its runtime is polynomial in the explicit table size, and whether stable mass allocations arise as limits of stable discrete markets.

The soft spot is that the paper’s finite-termination proof does not establish either claim. The mirror also scales capacities with the population and represents a type’s workers as a divisible mass across contract statuses. Those choices are natural for large employers and high multiplicity, but they need to be made explicit for the authors to recognize this as their problem. The mirror covers the paper’s stable-outcome algorithm and its termination/correctness guarantees; it does not turn those guarantees into a complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only anchor is real, but it is thinner than the case suggests. Theorems 6.5–6.6 establish that the algorithm returns a stable outcome and terminates; they give no running-time or complexity bound, and the paper explicitly leaves a polynomial-time algorithm open. Termination depends on bounded integer salary decreases, not on the number of workers. With binary-encoded salary bounds, that descent could take a number of steps exponential in the input length. And the paper allows arbitrary increasing real-valued functions without specifying how they are represented or queried. So the theorems do not themselves define a computational landscape that continuization can extend.

The strongest mirror is still the proposed type-mass market: workers with identical salary bounds and utility schedules, rational supplies, per-capita firm quotas, and mass split among integral-salary contracts. That is coherent, but its stability computation is a new problem. A type can occupy several contract statuses, and a firm’s payoff—the minimum valuation among its hires—can be determined by an arbitrarily small positive mass. The paper’s individual-worker rejection process does not establish how to handle that allocation or payoff structure. If the question is made computational by listing finite salary and utility tables, it becomes a finite type-and-contract problem; if the general functions are retained without an encoding, complexity is undefined. This is a reason to doubt that this particular paper yields a distinctive continuous-optimization challenge, though it does not rule out an algorithmic result for the finite-table version.

That leaves the negative case short of the requested universal claim. It weakens the claimed connection to a complexity theorem, but it does not defeat the better mirror: the authors could recognize stable mass allocation as their problem, and the paper’s lack of a polynomial bound makes its complexity an open question rather than a reason to dismiss it. I cannot honestly argue that no plausible scenario makes this mirror worthwhile.
