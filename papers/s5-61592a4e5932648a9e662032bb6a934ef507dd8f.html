The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is Binary-House Frege Allocation. I need to flag a limit up front: the paper has named theorems, but none asserts a computational-complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. So it has no anchor that qualifies under your stated rule. The result below is the closest named anchor, and the problem it suggests is a real computational question, but the anchor itself is not a complexity result.

For the mirror, take a constituency with many voters and \(m\) candidates. A voter’s type is the candidate they rank first; the mass \(\mu_i\) of type \(i\) is that candidate’s share of the vote, with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\). This fits a fixed electorate with many voters and relatively few distinct ballots. Given rational masses \((\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_m)\), a positive integer house size \(k\), and a fixed tie-breaking order, initialize scores \(s_i^1=\mu_i\). In each of \(k\) rounds, choose the candidate with the largest current score, then update by \(s_i^{t+1}=s_i^t+\mu_i-\mathbf{1}[i\text{ was chosen in round }t]\). The problem asks for the resulting allocation vector \(r\), where \(r_i\) counts how many rounds chose candidate \(i\). A valid solution is the uniquely tie-broken vector of nonnegative integers summing to \(k\). There is no optimization objective: this is the exact computation of a deterministic allocation rule.

The nearest anchor is Theorem 5, proved in this paper: *Frege’s apportionment method satisfies house monotonicity, upper quota, and quota for \(m\in\{2,3\}\), but fails lower quota for \(m\ge4\) and population monotonicity.* This is a complete restatement of the theorem’s claimed properties. It concerns the output of the allocation method, so it gives the proposed computation a clear connection to the paper. It does not, however, classify the method’s computational complexity.

For \(k\) given in unary—or when the output includes all \(k\) winners—direct simulation is polynomial in the output size. The more interesting question is whether one can compute the final allocation in time polynomial in \(m\), the support size \(\tau\), the rational input length \(L\), and \(\log k\), when \(k\) is given in binary. My tentative expectation is tractability for fixed \(m\), using the bounded score dynamics and some way to fast-forward repeated states; the paper does not establish that such a method runs in polynomial time. For unrestricted \(m\), I would leave the classification open. If hardness appears, it may come from the number of candidates rather than from population multiplicity, which would point toward hardness transferring from a discrete allocation problem. Further questions include the dependence on \(\tau\) versus \(m\), and whether the same fast-forwarding approach works when vote shares vary across rounds.

This mirror covers the modified Frege method and its apportionment interpretation, especially Theorem 5. It does not cover the original method’s convergence analysis or the paper’s other proportionality theorems. Its plausibility comes from using exactly the paper’s own objects: normalized vote shares, repeated selection, and an integer allocation of seats. The authors would likely recognize the computational question as being about their apportionment method.

The weakest point is that the paper already writes the modified method and apportionment problem in terms of normalized mass shares \(\mu_i\). The proposed question may therefore be an algorithmic reanalysis of a model the paper already uses, rather than a new continuous mirror. Combined with the absence of a qualifying complexity anchor, that makes this a modest positive case, not a strong claim that the paper supplies a new continuization result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proposed anchor fails the programme’s first test: Theorem 5 is an axiomatic classification, not a complexity result about a computational problem. It says which proportionality properties Frege’s apportionment method satisfies. It does not classify the time or space needed to compute an outcome. The proponent correctly notes this, but that leaves no qualifying result to mirror.

The proposed computation also uses the paper’s existing model. Section 4 defines the modified method directly on normalized plurality shares \(p_j^t=\pi_j^t/n_t\); Section 5 defines its apportionment version on a rational vote distribution \(p\) and house size \(k\). Taking voter types to be plurality choices and setting \(\mu_j=p_j\) reproduces that model and recurrence exactly. Every rational \(p\) can also be realized by a finite electorate using a common denominator. So this does not make the population continuous; the paper already expresses the method in population shares.

The sharper binary-\(k\) question could be worth studying, since direct simulation takes \(O(mk)\) updates while the final allocation vector can be much shorter than the sequence of winners. But its novelty would come from succinctly computing a long repeated process, not from continuizing the population. For unary \(k\), or when the output lists all winners, the recurrence already gives a direct simulation algorithm. The binary-horizon variant is a computational question about the paper’s existing share-based rule, not a continuous mirror of Theorem 5.

One could add changing mass distributions, full preference rankings, or a control objective such as shifting enough voter mass to change a winner. Those may lead to worthwhile research, but they add questions the paper does not analyze; they do not rescue the proposed anchor. The negative case is strongest for this paper’s results: its closest candidate is axiomatic, and its apportionment model already uses the continuous population description. I cannot rule out every interesting extension, but calling one of those extensions a mirror would stretch the term beyond the programme’s stated standard.
