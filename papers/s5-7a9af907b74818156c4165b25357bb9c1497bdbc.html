What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror concerns selecting a maximal lottery from pairwise utilitarianism over ranking types; it leaves the paper’s characterizations of preference domains, social welfare functions, rationalizable choice, and SSB representation outside the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is computing a maximal lottery for a large population represented by masses of preference types. Imagine a city choosing among a fixed, modest set of budget proposals. Each resident has a complete ranking of those proposals; people with the same ranking are one type. If there are \(n\) residents but only \(\tau\) observed rankings, with \(\tau\ll n\), the input is the rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over those rankings. Extend each type’s ranking to lotteries using the paper’s pairwise-comparison preference, represented by a skew-symmetric matrix \(\phi_t\).

Lead candidate: Continuous-Population Maximal Lottery. Given the alternatives, the supported types, and their binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\), output a lottery \(p\) over the alternatives that is maximal under pairwise utilitarianism. Specifically, form \(\Phi_\mu=\sum_t\mu_t\phi_t\) and return \(p\in\Delta(\mathcal U)\) satisfying \(\Phi_\mu p\leq 0\) coordinatewise. Equivalently, no lottery \(q\) is strictly preferred to \(p\) under the aggregate relation. Such a \(p\) exists by the minimax argument in the paper. The likely complexity is tractable: construct the aggregate matrix and solve the corresponding linear feasibility problem, in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). Further questions include computing the whole set of maximal lotteries, characterizing when a pure proposal suffices, and measuring how the maximal set changes as \(\mu\) changes.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: the agents are residents of one large consultation or electorate; type means their complete ranking over the fixed proposals; and mass means their fraction of the population. The paper’s aggregation rule depends on pairwise comparisons across agents, so replacing counts with type masses preserves the substance of that rule. For a finite profile with \(n_t\) agents of type \(t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) scales the aggregate matrix by \(1/n\), leaving the maximal lotteries unchanged. The paper’s result characterizing pairwise utilitarianism therefore identifies a natural rule to compute, and its discussion explicitly says that a maximal outcome can be found by linear programming. The mirror asks about selecting an outcome for a continuous population, not about encoding the SWF.

The weakness is decisive under your anchor requirement: the paper contains no named, numbered computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifying its complexity. The theorem and corollary characterizing pairwise utilitarianism are axiomatic results, not complexity results; the LP tractability claim is prose, not a named result. So this is a concrete and plausible follow-on mirror, but it has no eligible anchor and cannot honestly be presented as a qualifying positive case under the stated rules. It also gives no hardness result whose population dependence could be tested. The paper’s continuity is principally in the lottery outcome space, while its agents are finite—a nearby feature, but not itself a population continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Its propositions and numbered theorems characterize rationalizable choice, admissible preference domains, and Arrovian social welfare functions. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The paper does say that pairwise comparisons use matrix operations and a maximal outcome can be found by linear programming, but that is an implementation observation, not a numbered complexity result. Under ChoCo’s stated anchor rule, that is decisive.

The proposed maximal-lottery problem is the strongest candidate, and it has a sensible population model: a large electorate or consultation in which each type is a complete ranking of the proposals. Given rational masses \(\mu_t\), form \(\Phi_\mu=\sum_t\mu_t\phi_t\) and find \(p\) with \(\Phi_\mu p\le 0\). This is a legitimate high-multiplicity formulation: for a finite profile with \(n_t\) people of type \(t\), setting \(\mu_t=n_t/n\) gives the same maximal lotteries. There is no sound objection based on agents lacking repeated types.

But this formulation does not repair the anchor problem. It turns the paper’s characterized rule—and its prose claim about an LP—into a new mass-input algorithmic question; it does not mirror a computational result the paper proves. Generalizing further to a distribution over SSB preference types still gives a weighted aggregate relation and a choice computation, not a complexity result from this paper. Generalizing the theorem itself to a continuum population would be an axiomatic characterization, outside ChoCo’s computational remit. Adding robustness or intervention objectives would introduce further problems the paper does not formulate.

So I would reject this as a qualifying continuous mirror under the stated gate. The negative case is weaker as a universal claim: the maximal-lottery problem is coherent, and could be worth studying if the programme accepts computational questions induced by axiomatic papers. The case against rests on the anchor requirement, not on a claim that continuous computation is pointless or that the population model is unnatural.
