The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over provider types, where each type specifies a recurring workload, machine profile, and deadline profile under a fixed energy exponent \(\alpha\), let coalition mass \(\lambda_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) contribute proportionally scaled workloads and capacity. Let \(p(\lambda)=\sum_t\lambda_t p_t\), let \(C(\lambda)\) be the minimum energy to schedule that coalition, and set \(v(\lambda)=p(\lambda)-C(\lambda)\). Decide whether there are nonnegative type-symmetric payout rates \(q_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t q_t=v(\mu)\) that are stable against every positive-mass objection and its corresponding counter-objections, and compute such rates if they exist.

The model it lives in

An atomless provider population with type distribution \(\mu\); coalitions are measurable subsets and contribute mass-scaled machine capacity and workloads. The objective is to decide whether efficient, type-symmetric payout rates exist that withstand positive-mass objections and counter-objections, and to compute them.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Corollary 8's coalition-feasibility decision and imputation computation for energy scheduling, and could also cover the stated total-completion-time objective; it does not cover the paper's other game-theoretic discussion or broader future-work claims.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the pivotal-provider set in Theorem 6 be replaced by a characterization over type mass that exactly captures stability against positive-mass objections?
  • Can the resulting stability test and payout computation run in time polynomial in support size and input bit length for fixed \(\alpha\)?
  • How do the atomless payouts relate to stable imputations in finite populations with repeated provider types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Corollary 8, proved in this paper. It says that when organizations minimize either total job completion time, \(\sum_J C_J\), or total energy, \(\sum_J E_J\), Algorithm 1 decides in polynomial time whether the organizations can form a coalition and, if so, returns an imputation. The paper also extends the claim to polynomial-time scheduling objectives satisfying its monotonicity and additive-cost assumptions. The proof is given here; for the energy objective it invokes a polynomial scheduling algorithm cited as [2]. This is an algorithmic anchor, not a hardness result. The paper cites hardness results for other games in related work, but they are not named results about this choreography game.

My lead mirror is Fluid Stable-Alliance Detection for Dynamic-Speed Providers. Consider a large federation of service providers, each with a machine fleet and a recurring workload under the paper’s energy objective. A provider’s type records its complete relevant profile: machine configuration, jobs and deadlines per provider, the common speed-scaling exponent, and any other attributes used to determine costs. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of providers of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and support size \(\tau\). The plausible regime is many thousands or more providers but a much smaller number of recurring hardware and workload profiles. For example, many small regional or edge-service operators might use standardized capacity bundles and SLA workload templates.

Mass represents a share of a provider cohort; it does not mean a legal organization is split into pieces. In the continuous model, a coalition can include any mass \(\lambda_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each type. Its machines and fluid workload scale with that mass. Let \(C(\lambda)\) be the minimum energy needed to schedule that coalition’s jobs on its pooled, mass-scaled machines, with preemption and dynamic speed scaling as in the paper. Let \(p_t\) be the cost of running one unit of type \(t\)’s workload on its own resources, so \(p(\lambda)=\sum_t\lambda_t p_t\), and define its surplus as \(v(\lambda)=p(\lambda)-C(\lambda)\).

The question is whether the whole population has a stable revenue-sharing imputation, and, if so, to compute it. More precisely, represent providers by an atomless population \(\Omega\) whose type distribution is \(\mu\). A solution is a type-symmetric payout rate \(q_t\geq0\) per unit of provider mass, efficient in the sense that \(\sum_t\mu_t q_t=v(\mu)\). To define stability, a mass objection consists of a coalition \(P\), a positive-mass faction \(I\subseteq P\) that is offered strictly more than its current payout, and a positive-mass target faction \(J\subseteq\Omega\setminus P\). The offer \(y\) must give every member of \(P\) at least their current payout and satisfy \(\int_P y\leq v(\lambda(P))\). It is countered if some coalition \(Q\) containing \(J\) and excluding \(I\) can afford offers that give its members in \(P\cap Q\) at least \(y\), and its other members at least their current payout. A stable imputation is one for which every mass objection has such a counter-objection. The solver returns “no” if none exists, or “yes” and the payout rates \(q_t\) if one does.

I expect this problem to be tractable in the support size, profile-description length, and bit length of \(\mu\), for fixed \(\alpha\) (say \(\alpha=2\)). The reason for that expectation is the structure behind Corollary 8, not just the word “continuous.” The paper’s Theorem 6 characterizes its stable imputation using the set \(A=\{j:v([N])>v([N]\setminus\{j\})\}\): organizations outside \(A\) get zero, while each organization in \(A\) has a specified payout determined by its standalone price, the costs with organizations removed, and the costs of \(A\). Corollary 7 gives the corresponding existence test \(p(A)\geq\mathrm{cost}(A)\). These results suggest a route to the fluid version: characterize the relevant mass of contributors and compute the payouts using the continuous scheduling cost function. If that characterization reduces stability checking to an optimization or separation problem over the \(\tau\) type masses, the mirror fits Class A.

The scope here is specifically Corollary 8’s full-population alliance-detection result for \(\sum_J E_J\). It does not try to mirror every cost objective or the paper’s broader economic discussion. The underlying question remains the authors’: can the providers pool resources, and can the resulting savings be distributed so the alliance is stable? Replacing job and machine counts by workload and capacity mass is a natural extension of that question in a large market.

The weak point is the bargaining-set definition. The paper reasons about an individual provider objecting to another individual; in an atomless population, one provider has zero mass and cannot be represented by a positive change in coalition value. My formulation therefore treats positive-mass factions as the relevant objectors and targets. That is a genuine co-modification forced by the continuous regime, and the authors might prefer a different continuum bargaining concept. The paper’s Theorem 6 does not by itself prove that the payout characterization or polynomial algorithm survives that change. Establishing whether it does—and how its pivotal set generalizes—is the main open question for this mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 8 is the only anchor the proponent raises, and its weak point is the bargaining-set characterization behind it. Theorem 6 identifies pivotal organizations by strict leave-one-out effects: \(j\in A\) exactly when \(v([N])>v([N]\setminus\{j\})\). In an atomless population, removing one provider changes no mass, so for every individual \(i\), \(v(\Omega)=v(\Omega\setminus\{i\})\). The literal limit therefore has \(A=\varnothing\); Theorem 6 would assign everyone zero, even when the population has positive surplus. The proponent’s payout rates and positive-mass objections repair that failure, but they replace the paper’s individual objection and counter-objection test. Corollary 8 does not establish that the replacement has a stable imputation or a polynomial algorithm.

A stronger repair would start with finite populations containing repeated provider types, normalize costs by population size, and seek a limit of their stable imputations. But strict leave-one-out pivotality can disappear under that normalization even when it holds at every finite size. Recovering payout rates may require directional derivatives of the scheduling cost, and showing those rates withstand every positive-mass objection is a new stability theorem. There is no reason to assume that the finite characterization or its algorithm carries over. Atomless-economy research also uses multiple bargaining-set definitions with different properties, which underlines that the positive-mass version is a substantive modeling choice, not a uniquely dictated limit. [Graziano, Pesce, and Urbinati, “A Note on Bargaining Sets in Atomless Economies”](https://doi.org/10.3934/jdg.2024020)

That is the strongest negative case: the one computational result has no direct continuum counterpart, and its proposed repair changes the solution concept that makes it this paper’s result. But I cannot honestly claim it defeats the best mirror. Repeated provider profiles and mass coalitions are plausible here, and asking whether their pooled energy savings admit a stable type-level distribution remains recognizably the authors’ alliance question. The paper contains no continuous computational treatment that already answers it. So the objection lowers confidence that Corollary 8 transfers; it does not establish that the mass-stability problem is unworthy of the programme.
