Why no mirror

The anchored theorems analyze the welfare distortion of a specified strategyproof rule; under the computational gate, that is not a computational result to mirror. The mass formulation is precise, but its performance questions are already covered by the paper’s weighted-profile analysis and do not supply a qualifying computational anchor.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mass formulation covers CMP’s consistency and robustness guarantees in Theorems 1, 2, and 4, with endpoint guarantees in Theorem 3; it does not mirror Proposition 1’s individual-agent strategyproofness claim.

The case FOR (proponent)

The cleanest mirror is to make the distribution of preferred locations the input. Let \(\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\delta_{x^i}\) be a finite-support population in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), with positive rational masses summing to one. A type is a preferred location \(x^i\); its mass \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of residents with that preference. The planner chooses a facility location \(y\), with average social cost \(SC_\mu(y)=\sum_i\mu_i\lVert x^i-y\rVert_p\) and optimum \(OPT_\mu=\min_y SC_\mu(y)\).

For a prediction \(\widehat f\) and confidence \(c\), define mass-CMP by adding virtual mass \(c\) at \(\widehat f\), then taking the lower weighted median in each coordinate: the first coordinate value at which cumulative augmented mass reaches \((1+c)/2\). Define \(\alpha^{\mathrm{mass}}_{p,d}(c)\) as the supremum of \(SC_\mu(\mathrm{CMP}_c(\mu,\widehat f))/OPT_\mu\) over finite-support populations and optimal predictions \(\widehat f\); define \(\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{p,d}(c)\) by allowing any prediction. These are precise performance-analysis problems: determine the two suprema, with a proof for all populations and distributions that attain or approach the bounds.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for locating a public facility in a large city: many residents, but relatively few recurring neighborhood or preference types. For example, a city might have millions of residents whose reported preferred facility locations fall into a few hundred standard classes, with a planner’s forecast serving as the prediction. A rational mass vector is exactly a finite electorate with repeated identical types, after choosing a common denominator. The authors already work in this language to some extent: in the planar proof they normalize weighted profiles and prediction mass, then approximate the lower-bound constructions by ordinary finite profiles. That makes the mass formulation particularly recognizable to them.

My lead is Planar Mass-CMP Ratio, mirroring Theorem 1, proved in this paper (with the Euclidean planar case recovering prior work by Agrawal et al.). For fixed \(p\in[1,+\infty]\) and \(c\in[0,1)\), the question is to determine \(\alpha^{\mathrm{mass}}_{p,2}(c)\) and \(\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{p,2}(c)\). Theorem 1 gives the exact pair \(A_p(c),B_p(c)\). For \(1<p<+\infty\), they are \(A_p(c)=\left(1+\left(\frac{1-c}{1+c}\right)^{p/(p-1)}\right)^{(p-1)/p}\) and \(B_p(c)=\left(1+\left(\frac{1+c}{1-c}\right)^{p/(p-1)}\right)^{(p-1)/p}\). At the endpoints, \(A_1(c)=1\), \(B_1(c)=\frac{1+c}{1-c}\), \(A_\infty(c)=\frac{2}{1+c}\), and \(B_\infty(c)=\frac{2}{1-c}\). A solution consists of proving those bounds for every finite-support mass distribution and giving matching or arbitrarily close examples. This is the strongest anchor: it is an exact answer, and the proof’s weighted-profile analysis closely matches the proposed mirror.

A second question is Dimension-Uniform Mass-CMP Bounds, mirroring Theorem 2, proved here. For each \(1<p<+\infty\), \(c\in[0,1)\), and \(d\ge1\), establish \(\alpha^{\mathrm{mass}}_{p,d}(c)\le\Gamma_p((1-c)/2)\) and \(\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{p,d}(c)\le\Gamma_p((1+c)/2)\). Here \(q=p/(p-1)\), \(\Delta_p(\mu)=\max_{0\le a\le\mu}\frac{a^{1/p}+(\mu-a)/(1-\mu)}{(1-a)^{1/p}}\), and \(\Gamma_p(\mu)=(1+\Delta_p(\mu)^q)^{1/q}\). A solution is a proof of the two bounds for every \(d\) and every admissible mass distribution; the theorem’s scalar maximization supplies the stated factors. This is a natural Class A performance question: the result reduces the bound to a one-variable maximum. Computing the mechanism itself requires only finding weighted coordinate medians, which takes \(O(d\tau\log\tau)\) comparisons.

A third, related question is High-Dimension Mass-CMP Limit, mirroring Theorem 4, also proved here. For fixed \(1<p<+\infty\) and \(c\in[0,1)\), determine the limits of the two worst-case ratios as \(d\) grows. The theorem says \(\lim_{d\to\infty}\alpha^{\mathrm{mass}}_{p,d}(c)=\Gamma_p((1-c)/2)\) and \(\lim_{d\to\infty}\beta^{\mathrm{mass}}_{p,d}(c)=\Gamma_p((1+c)/2)\). A solution needs both the upper bounds and a family of mass distributions whose ratios approach them. This too has an analytic Class A answer in the paper. A natural further question is the exact value at each fixed \(d\ge3\), which the authors identify as open.

These anchors are named computational-performance results, not complexity-class results: the paper gives no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time classification, or parameterized-complexity theorem. So I would not claim that a discrete hardness result dissolves here, or assign a hardness-transfer class. The mirror asks about exact and asymptotic worst-case approximation of a specified mechanism. It does not cover every result in the paper: in particular, I am not claiming that its individual-agent strategyproofness proposition transfers intact.

That is the weakest point. In a literal nonatomic population, one person’s report has zero mass and cannot move the median, so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous. One can retain a meaningful strategic interpretation by treating each support type as a mass-carrying bloc with one report and per-capita distance preferences, but that is a bloc game rather than the paper’s individual-agent game. The case for the mirror rests on the welfare guarantees: the weighted median, prediction weight, and social-cost objective all carry over directly, and the paper’s own weighted-profile proofs make that analogue unusually close to its existing analysis.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is not that facility location lacks a plausible high-multiplicity regime. A city with many residents sharing a modest set of preferred locations is a natural example. The problem is that all three proposed anchors ask for quantities the paper already defines and analyzes.

For any finite-support distribution with rational masses \(\mu_i\), choose a common denominator \(n\) and make \(n\mu_i\) agents report location \(x^i\). Then
\[ \sum_i\mu_i\lVert x^i-y\rVert_p =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n\lVert x_j-y\rVert_p. \]
The optimal locations and cost ratios are unchanged. The virtual mass \(c\) also matches the paper’s \(cn\) virtual agents after dividing all weights by \(n\), including the median threshold. Conversely, every finite profile gives such a rational distribution. Thus the proposed Planar Mass-CMP Ratio is exactly Theorem 1’s \(\alpha_{p,2}(c)\) and \(\beta_{p,2}(c)\); the proposed Dimension-Uniform Mass-CMP Bounds are Theorem 2; and the High-Dimension Mass-CMP Limit is Theorem 4. This is a direct match to the paper’s own quantities, not an objection based on high-multiplicity work elsewhere.

The weighted-profile arguments make the match especially clear. The planar proof establishes its upper bounds for weighted profiles, then approximates the tight examples by ordinary finite profiles. The high-dimensional proof also works with weighted constructions and rationalizes them for finite profiles. The paper therefore already does the mathematical work needed for the proponent’s mass formulations. Asking for the same bounds over rational masses would restate the results; asking for exact values at fixed \(d\ge3\) would pursue the paper’s own stated open direction for \(\alpha_{p,d}(c)\) and \(\beta_{p,d}(c)\). That could be worthwhile geometry, but continuizing the population does not make it a new question.

There is a further mismatch with ChoCo’s computational remit. Theorems 1, 2, and 4 give distortion guarantees for a specified rule. They do not classify the complexity of a computational problem or give an exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithm for one. Computing CMP from a support list amounts to finding weighted coordinate medians; the mass input makes repeated reports easier to encode, but does not expose the kind of optimization bottleneck that motivates the programme’s continuous-optimization tools. Optimizing the social cost over facility locations would instead be an outcome-optimization question.

The strategyproofness proposition does not supply a missing computational anchor. In a genuinely nonatomic population, an individual has zero mass, so an individual deviation cannot move the median. Treating an entire type as a strategic bloc could retain meaningful incentives, but it changes the strategic unit. A mass-transfer problem could also be defined by adding prices or costs for changing preferred locations. Those may be interesting projects, but the paper supplies neither that intervention model nor a result to mirror.

So the negative case is strong against these three anchors: their high-multiplicity versions are already captured by the paper’s definitions and proofs, while the remaining obvious computational formulations either reduce to weighted median computation or introduce a new problem. I cannot make a convincing universal claim that no related mass-based intervention problem could ever merit study. I can say that the proposed anchors do not establish a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper for ChoCo.
