The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a boundary case: the paper gives a natural object to continuize, but it contains no named computational result—no result about polynomial time, hardness, parameterized complexity, or algorithms. Its main results characterize when certain axioms force or rule out belief aggregators. So none can serve as the computational anchor your instructions require.

The closest population-based analogue is the paper’s Theorem 2, “Agenda Condition for the Triviality Result,” proved here. It says that an agenda \(\mathcal A\) is negation-connected if and only if the only binarizing aggregators satisfying universal domain, zero preservation, certainty preservation, independence, collective deductive closure, and anonymity are the trivial rule. That rule accepts an issue exactly when every individual assigns it probability \(1\).

A plausible continuous version would model a large pool of templated forecasters or replicated forecasting systems. A type is a complete coherent credence function \(P\) over the possible worlds; a society is a finitely supported rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with \(\mu_P\) the fraction of the population of type \(P\). The number of agents \(n\) could be large while the number of distinct credence types \(\tau\) stays small. An aggregator maps \(\mu\) to collective binary beliefs \(F(\mu)\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal A}\). For an issue \(A\), let \(\nu_A\) be the distribution of credence values \(P(A)\) induced by \(\mu\); independence says the collective belief on \(A\) depends only on \(\nu_A\).

The resulting candidate question is: given a finite agenda \(\mathcal A\), determine whether every aggregator on all such societies satisfying universal domain, zero preservation, certainty preservation, independence, anonymity, and deductive closure must be
\[ F_{\mathrm{un}}(\mu)(A)=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mu\bigl(\{P:P(A)=1\}\bigr)=1. \]
This mirrors Theorem 2’s uniqueness claim; the society distribution is the domain of the aggregator, not a single profile supplied to it. I would conjecture that negation-connectedness remains the exact condition, though the paper does not establish that extension. The forecaster-template setting makes repeated types plausible; for a population of individually distinctive human experts, \(\tau\) may be close to \(n\), making the high-multiplicity regime much less convincing.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their belief-binarization question with population proportions in place of finite voter counts. But it remains an axiomatic question about which rules exist, not a computational question about solving a problem. It therefore does not fit ChoCo’s scope or its tractable / hardness-transfer / continuum-specific-hardness trichotomy. Asking for the complexity of deciding the agenda condition would be a separate computational problem, not a computational result of this paper.

That is the weak point as well as the limit of the positive case: distributional credence aggregation is a credible continuous-population extension of one theorem, but the theorem is not a qualifying computational anchor. The positive argument supports the *naturalness of a continuous formulation*; it cannot honestly claim that this paper opens a ChoCo complexity mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s decisive limitation for ChoCo is that it contains no computational result to mirror. Its main theorems characterize which aggregators can satisfy specified axioms, given logical conditions on the agenda. They give no algorithm, complexity bound, hardness result, or parameterized analysis of a problem over societies. The proponent’s own account concedes this.

Their “Agenda Condition for the Triviality Result” is still a coherent candidate for an axiomatic continuous extension. A society of repeated forecasting systems with the same credence function is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and representing it by a distribution \(\mu\) is sensible. I would not reject that mirror on the grounds that types are repeated, that credences are fractional, or that the continuous theorem might have the same answer.

But the distribution does not turn the theorem into a computational problem about a society. The theorem quantifies over an entire class of aggregation functions \(F\), each defined on every admissible profile; its conclusion classifies those functions. In the proposed version, asking whether every \(F\) on all societies must be trivial remains the same kind of global axiomatic question. A single input \(\mu\) is not what the theorem classifies.

One could pose a new computational question: given an encoded agenda, decide whether it is negation-connected or whether the axiomatic characterization applies. That would require specifying how agendas and their propositions are represented, and its computational difficulty would concern logical structure in the agenda. The population distribution would not be doing the work. Alternatively, given \(\mu\), one could compute the proposed trivial rule by checking the mass of types certain of each issue. That evaluates a rule; it does not mirror the theorem’s contribution. More ambitious optimization over collective beliefs would need an objective the paper does not study, so it would be a new problem suggested by the discussion, not a computational mirror of a result here.

So I can make a strong case against a ChoCo continuous-computational mirror of this paper: its only proposed anchor is axiomatic, and changing the population representation does not supply the missing computational question. I cannot honestly defend the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous extension exists in any scenario. The distributional belief-aggregation question may be worthwhile in its own right; it falls outside this programme’s computational scope.
