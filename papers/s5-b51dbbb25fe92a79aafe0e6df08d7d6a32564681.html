The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many chain classes with finite state sets, rational rewards and transition probabilities, a common rational discount factor, rational initial masses over class-state pairs, and a capacity κ ∈ (0,1], compute an ε-optimal allocation schedule for infinite-horizon discounted reward. At each period choose activated masses u_{j,s} with 0 ≤ u_{j,s} ≤ μ_{j,s} and ∑_{j,s} u_{j,s} ≤ κ; unactivated mass stays in place and activated mass transitions according to its class's Markov chain. Maximize the total discounted reward across the resulting mass flows.

The model it lives in

A continuous population of chain-state types (j,s) with rational masses μ_{j,s}; the decision variables are per-period activated masses u_{j,s}, constrained by the available mass and capacity κ. The objective is total discounted reward under deterministic aggregate flows. A finite-horizon version is a linear program, and truncating the discounted tail gives an ε-approximation; whether Gittins index priority solves the per-capita-capacity version is a separate question.

The objection that survived

Theorem 4 proves the Gittins rule only for one activation per period; with capacity proportional to population, index-priority allocation may fail, and the mechanism's individual incentives do not carry over directly.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4's optimal-planning core. It leaves the theorem's truthfulness, individual-rationality, and budget-balance guarantees aside, as well as the general-MDP results and the learning mechanism in Theorem 6.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does allocating capacity to the highest Gittins-index mass maximize discounted reward for this per-capita-capacity model, and under what conditions?
  • What are the exact bit-complexity bounds for an ε-optimal schedule as a function of the state-space size, input length, γ, and ε?
  • How closely does the fluid schedule approximate finite populations with capacity K_n satisfying K_n/n → κ?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is the planning problem behind Theorem 4, proved in this paper from Theorems 2 and 3. Theorem 3 invokes Gittins’s earlier result; Theorem 4 applies it to finite-state Markov chains where only one chain can be activated each period. The planner computes each chain’s state-dependent Gittins index and activates the chain with the highest index. The paper’s Sequential-Gittins-VCG mechanism elicits agents’ private states and uses sampled trajectories to set transfers. With a common discount factor, the theorem guarantees truthful reporting, system-optimal planning, ex ante individual rationality, and ex ante strong budget balance in Markov perfect equilibrium; its implementation takes time linear in the number of agents. The paper has no comparable numbered hardness result, so this algorithmic theorem is the best anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large service platform whose many clients each own a persistent workload. Workloads fall into a small catalog of classes: clients in the same class have identical reward and transition models, and clients in the same current state are indistinguishable for planning. There might be millions of clients but only a few dozen occupied class-and-state pairs. The platform provisions capacity to serve a fixed share of the workload each period. That last feature is a genuine scaling choice: the paper activates one chain per period, while a continuum population with a positive activation share corresponds to \(K_n/n\to\kappa>0\), not a fixed single server. It is the natural per-capita capacity co-modification for this regime.

I would hand a prover the following problem, which I’ll call Continuum Gittins Allocation. The input is a finite catalog of chain classes \(j\), each with a finite state set \(S_j\), rational transition probabilities \(P_j(s,s')\), rational rewards \(r_j(s)\), and a common rational discount factor \(\gamma\). It also gives rational initial masses \(\mu^0_{j,s}\) summing to one and an activation capacity \(\kappa\in(0,1]\). Mass \(\mu_{j,s}\) is the fraction of clients in class \(j\) and state \(s\). A solution is an allocation rule giving the mass \(u_{j,s}\) to activate in each period, with \(0\le u_{j,s}\le\mu_{j,s}\) and \(\sum_{j,s}u_{j,s}\le\kappa\). Nonactivated chains stay in their current state; activated mass flows according to \(P_j\), so the next distribution satisfies \(\mu'_{j,s'}=\mu_{j,s'}-u_{j,s'}+\sum_s u_{j,s}P_j(s,s')\). The objective is to maximize the per-capita discounted reward \(\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t\sum_{j,s}u^t_{j,s}r_j(s)\). The requested output is an optimal policy, represented compactly by the chain-state indices and the rule to allocate capacity to the highest-index mass, splitting mass at a tie.

This is a mirror of the paper’s underlying optimization question: how to allocate scarce activations across independent, rewarded Markov chains to maximize total discounted value. It continuizes the population itself. In the continuum, fractional activation of a type means that this fraction of otherwise identical clients is served; the aggregate state then follows deterministic mass flows. The theorem’s index structure suggests a Class A result: compute indices for each distinct class and state, then allocate capacity in index order. The expected running time should be polynomial in the explicit transition-table size and binary input length, and depend on the number of distinct types rather than the headcount. Exact bit complexity for index computation is one part the prover should establish.

My weakest point is that Theorem 4 is also an incentive and mechanism-design result, while this mirror isolates the planner’s allocation problem. Atomless agents may have no individual effect on aggregate allocation, so the theorem’s truthfulness and transfer guarantees do not automatically carry over. I would claim a mirror of the computational core of Theorem 4, not of its full MPE implementation. That scope is still faithful to the paper’s stated goal of computing the system-optimal joint policy.

The next questions are whether a finite stochastic population admits a useful additive approximation from this fluid policy, with bounds in headcount and support size; whether exact index computation is polynomial in the binary encoding; and whether a separate continuum mechanism can elicit private state masses with meaningful incentive guarantees. Restless chains or heterogeneous discount factors would test where the index-based tractability ends.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only strong anchor is Theorem 4. Its nontrivial setting is a fixed single activation each period: the planner chooses one agent’s chain, elicits that agent’s private state, and uses transfers to align individual incentives with system value. The proposed mirror preserves the allocation objective only by changing that capacity to a positive fraction of the population. That change is not forced by taking the population to a continuum. With one activation and the paper’s bandit reward convention, per-capita discounted reward is at most \(R/[n(1-\gamma)]\) for rewards bounded by \(R\), so the normalized objective vanishes as \(n\) grows. Scaling capacity to \(\kappa n\) avoids that degeneration by turning the one-server problem into a parallel-activation problem.

That larger problem is reasonable, but it is not the problem Theorem 4 solves. The theorem invokes Gittins’s one-at-a-time result; it does not establish that activating the highest-index fraction is optimal with multiple activations. The multiple-play extension needs additional conditions, and the Gittins rule can fail without them. [Pandelis and Teneketzis (1999)](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~teneket/pubs/GittinsIndexMultiplePlays-1999.pdf) Thus the proponent’s proposed algorithm is a conjecture for a changed resource regime, not a continuization of the theorem’s algorithmic guarantee. That does not make the new optimization question uninteresting; it does mean this anchor has not shown a worthwhile mirror of this paper.

The mechanism is an even poorer fit after aggregation. In an atomless population, one agent’s report changes aggregate allocation by zero, so individual truthfulness is vacuous in the direct continuum model. A meaningful mechanism would need to model reports or deviations by positive-mass groups, which changes the strategic object and no longer carries over the paper’s Markov-perfect-equilibrium guarantee. The proponent appropriately sets those guarantees aside, but that leaves only the parallel-allocation problem.

So my negative case is that preserving the paper’s defining single-action constraint degenerates the per-capita continuum, while avoiding degeneration changes the capacity regime and drops the paper’s central incentive result. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous bandit-allocation problem exists, or that the scaled model is incoherent. The case against this being a worthwhile mirror is therefore real but not airtight: the scaled model is a plausible adjacent research question, and the authors might recognize it as a useful extension.
