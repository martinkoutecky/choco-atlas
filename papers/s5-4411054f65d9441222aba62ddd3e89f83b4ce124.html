What the mirror covers

The proposed weighted-count mirror covers outcome evaluation for a fixed district plan, optionally repeated over the finite map ensemble; it does not reproduce ballot-sampling variability or establish the reported seat-share averages.

The case FOR (proponent)

Under your anchor rule, this paper has no eligible named computational results: it states no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition classifying a problem as hard, tractable, FPT, or otherwise. Its results are empirical simulation findings. For example, §4.1 reports average STV seat shares of 38.2 Democrats and 26.8 Republicans across 10,000 districting plans, compared with proportional averages of 35.7 and 29.3. Those findings are not named complexity anchors, so there is no honest anchor-based lead to name.

The strongest positive foothold is an unanchored continuous version of the election simulation. For each fixed districting plan, give each district a rational distribution \(\mu_{d,r}\) over truncated ranked ballots \(r\), with masses summing to one. Run STV on those masses, transferring surplus mass proportionally and using a specified tie-breaking rule; return the elected candidates and party seat shares. For the paper’s finite ensemble of plans, repeat this evaluation and report the seat-share distribution or mean. This makes the population itself a distribution over ballot types, and preserves the candidates, district seats, rankings, and transfer rule. The evaluation looks amenable to aggregation over the support of \(\mu\), so tractability is plausible.

The weak point is central: this reframes their simulation as weighted-ballot evaluation, but it does not mirror a named computational result in the paper. Nor does it automatically reproduce their reported figures: their results depend on sampled ballots, modeled voter behavior, and an ensemble of maps. It is a plausible continuous research question, but under the requested standard it cannot serve as an anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the missing anchor: the paper states no named computational result about tractability or hardness. Its findings in §4 are empirical seat-share comparisons from simulations, not complexity claims about a computational problem. The proposed weighted-ballot STV evaluation is coherent, but it is a new continuous computation, not a mirror of a computational result this paper established.

The best version of that proposal would give each district a rational distribution over truncated rankings, run STV on the resulting masses, and repeat across the districting plans. But that computes an outcome for an idealized electorate; it does not by itself reproduce the paper’s distribution of outcomes across sampled ballot profiles. STV is nonlinear, so in general \(\mathrm{STV}(\mathbb{E}[X]) \ne \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{STV}(X)]\). Preserving the latter target means retaining the paper’s ballot-generation and truncation uncertainty, alongside the map ensemble. That may be useful work, but it is a stochastic simulation question rather than a computational result to continuize from this paper. A mass-perturbation question—how much ballot mass must move to change a seat allocation—would be a stronger ChoCo connection, but it adds an objective and cost model the paper does not study.

This is a weak case for the universal claim. Voters with the same truncated ranking are a natural high-multiplicity type, and the paper’s STV simulations give a clear setting for weighted-ballot analysis or robustness questions. I can reject the attached case as an anchored mirror of a named result; I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
