The continuous mirror question

Given rational \(m\), \(k\), manipulator mass \(\rho\), and target \(d\), let \(T=S_m\), let \(\mu\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Delta(T))\) be the high-multiplicity limit of IAC, and let the coalition choose \(\nu\in\Delta(T)\); compute \(\nu\) maximizing \(\Pr_{\mu}[d\in W_k((1-\rho)\mu+\rho\nu)]\) under the paper's tie-breaking convention.

The model it lives in

Voter types are complete rankings \(t\in T\); the sincere population is a mass distribution \(\mu\), the coalition has mass \(\rho\) and report distribution \(\nu\), and \(k\)-approval winners are computed from aggregate type mass. Under IAC, \(\mu\) is uniformly distributed over the simplex, and the objective is the probability that \(d\) wins.

The objection that survived

The continuous IAC objective replaces finite-sample uncertainty with a prior over society mass vectors, so its complexity may concern volume or integration under that prior rather than individual-voter uncertainty; the proponent does not establish that this objective remains computationally simple.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the positional-summary action space of Lemma 3 and the symmetric \(k\)-approval result of Theorem 1, with a natural three-candidate Borda extension from Theorem 2; it leaves the general sampling MIP, sample-complexity propositions, regret bounds, empirical Mallows experiments, and the unnumbered hardness observation aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the balanced report distribution remain optimal under the uniform-simplex IAC limit, and can it be computed in time polynomial in \(m\) and the input encoding of \(\rho\)?
  • For finitely represented priors \(\Pi\) over \(\mu\), what is the complexity of maximizing \(\Pr_{\mu\sim\Pi}[d\in W_k((1-\rho)\mu+\rho\nu)]\)?
  • Does the balanced or near-balanced dichotomy from Theorem 2 persist for continuous three-candidate Borda manipulation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow but real: this paper supports a high-multiplicity mirror of Bayesian manipulation, especially through its positional-summary-matrix formulation. It is not, however, a strong formal ChoCo anchor, because its only explicit NP-hardness claim is unnumbered and inherited from cited work.

The mirror I would use is this. Let \(T=S_m\) be the rankings of \(m\) alternatives. A sincere society is a mass distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of sincere voters of ranking type \(t\). Let \(\rho\) be the manipulator fraction and let \(\nu\in\Delta(T)\) be the coalition’s reported-ranking distribution. The final society is

\[ \bar\mu=(1-\rho)\mu+\rho\nu. \]

For a scoring rule \(s\), the coalition chooses \(\nu\) to maximize the probability that its desired alternative \(d\) wins. Under Bayesian information, \(\mu\) is drawn from a prior \(\Pi\), so the objective is

\[ \max_{\nu\in\Delta(T)} \Pr_{\mu\sim\Pi}\!\left[d\in W(\bar\mu)\right]. \]

This is genuinely population continuization: every unit of mass still has a complete ranking and produces an ordinary discrete vote. Only the number of voters is replaced by type mass. A plausible regime is a national or platform-scale electorate with millions of voters but relatively few repeated ranking types, inferred from polling or demographic cohorts. The manipulators are a large coordinated bloc, not a collection of individually priced agents.

My lead anchor is Lemma 3, proved in the paper through a bipartite-matching argument. It states:

A matrix \(X\) is the positional summary matrix of some manipulation strategy \(v_c\) if and only if \(X\in\mathbb{N}_{\ge 0}^{m\times m}\) and \(X\mathbf 1=X^\top\mathbf 1=c\mathbf 1\).

The paper also explains that the corresponding manipulation votes can be recovered in polynomial time by repeatedly finding perfect matchings in a \(c\)-regular bipartite multigraph.

This gives the precise problem Continuous Positional-Mass Bayesian Manipulation. An instance consists of \(m\), a scoring rule, a target \(d\), rational sincere mass \(\mu\), and rational manipulator mass \(\rho\). A solution is a distribution \(\nu\) over rankings maximizing \(d\)’s winning status in \((1-\rho)\mu+\rho\nu\). Equivalently, the manipulator contribution can be represented by a real matrix \(X\ge0\) with

\[ X\mathbf 1=X^\top\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1. \]

By the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem, such an \(X\) is a convex combination of ordinary ranking matrices. Thus the continuous solution is not a fractional outcome: it is a mass mixture of integral reports.

For a known society \(\mu\), the scoring-rule version is expected to be Class A. The winning constraints are linear in \(X\), so the problem is an LP over the fractional positional-summary matrix. This is exactly the interesting continuization of the paper’s integer formulation: the finite coalition must choose \(c\) whole votes, whereas a high-multiplicity coalition chooses a distribution of reports. The natural follow-up is whether the general Bayesian version, with a finite-support prior \(\Pi\), becomes continuum-specifically hard because it maximizes the number of population states in which linear winning inequalities hold.

The second anchor is Theorem 1, proved by the authors, with the nontrivial proof relegated to the appendix of the longer version. It states that the balanced manipulation strategy is optimal for \(k\)-approval under both impartial culture and impartial anonymous culture. The balanced strategy places \(d\) in the approval set of every manipulator and distributes the remaining \(c(k-1)\) approval positions among the other \(m-1\) candidates as evenly as possible, with differences of at most one.

The corresponding problem is Continuous Symmetric \(k\)-Approval Manipulation. The instance consists of \(m,k,\rho,d\), and either the continuum impartial-culture society \(\mu_t=1/m!\), or the continuum analogue of IAC, namely the uniform prior over \(\Delta(T)\). The solution is a manipulator distribution \(\nu\) maximizing the probability that \(d\) wins. In the continuous version, the finite rounding disappears: one can require

\[ A_\nu(d)=1,\qquad A_\nu(a)=\frac{k-1}{m-1} \quad\text{for every }a\ne d, \]

where \(A_\nu(a)\) is the fraction of manipulator mass approving \(a\). I expect this to be Class A, by symmetry and linear optimization. It is a credible mirror because the paper’s central object is already the coalition’s aggregate report; only the integer multiplicity constraint has been removed.

The third anchor is Theorem 2, also proved by the authors. It states that for \(A=\{x,y,d\}\), even \(c\), and Borda voting, either the balanced or near-balanced strategy is optimal under both IC and IAC. The near-balanced strategy gives \(d\) total coalition score \(c\), while the scores of \(x\) and \(y\) differ by at most \(2\). The balanced strategy is optimal whenever either \(n\) is even and \(c+2\) is divisible by four, or \(n\) is odd and \(c\) is divisible by four.

Its mirror is Continuous Three-Candidate Borda Manipulation. The instance consists of three alternatives, a manipulator mass \(\rho\), a target \(d\), and the IC or IAC population model. The solution is a ranking distribution \(\nu\) maximizing the probability that \(d\) wins under the aggregate Borda scores. In the continuous setting, the natural balanced action is simply to put \(d\) first and split the remaining mass equally between

\[ d\succ x\succ y \quad\text{and}\quad d\succ y\succ x. \]

The parity conditions disappear because mass can be divided exactly. I expect this problem also to be Class A. Its further questions are whether the same collapse occurs for more alternatives, whether arbitrary priors destroy the balanced solution, and whether Borda’s finite-coalition hardness survives once the coalition’s positional matrix is fractional.

The paper’s empirical Mallows models and its use of sample profiles are not a novelty collision. They randomize a finite electorate; they do not make the electorate itself continuous. Likewise, the paper’s MIP is an encoding of the finite manipulation problem, whereas the proposed mirror asks about the underlying mass-distribution problem.

The weakest point is formal: no numbered theorem or proposition in the paper proves NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT solvability, or another standard complexity classification. Section 4 says that optimal Bayesian manipulation for scoring rules remains NP-hard, but this is an unnumbered observation obtained from cited hardness of finite Borda manipulation. I would not use it as an anchor under the stated rules. Consequently, this is a defensible continuous mirror with a plausible Class A story, but not a strong qualifying ChoCo paper unless formulation and structural optimality results are accepted as computational anchors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this is the wrong kind of paper for ChoCo’s computational-population programme. Its central uncertainty is over a finite realised profile, whereas continuization replaces precisely that random finite profile by a deterministic mass vector.

There is also no numbered complexity result to anchor. Lemma 3 is a representation lemma, and Theorems 1 and 2 give optimal strategies under highly symmetric distributions; neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Section 4’s NP-hardness statement is unnumbered and inherited from finite-information Borda manipulation. That does not prohibit formulating a new mirror, but it means the paper supplies no established complexity question whose high-multiplicity version needs charting.

The mismatch is clearest in the Bayesian model. If sincere votes are sampled independently from \(P\), their empirical society \(\widehat{\mu}_n\) converges to \(P\). With a manipulator fraction \(\rho\) and report distribution \(\nu\), the election therefore converges to the deterministic society

\[ (1-\rho)P+\rho\nu. \]

Away from a tie, the probability that \(d\) wins converges to either \(0\) or \(1\). Thus the paper’s central quantity—success probability caused by finite-sample fluctuations—disappears in the continuum.

There are only a few ways to retain nontrivial uncertainty. One can keep finite-\(n\) fluctuations, which leaves the population finite and makes the continuum merely a noise or asymptotic approximation. One can introduce a random macrostate \(\mu\sim\Pi\), as the proponent does, but then the object of uncertainty is a prior over societies, not the individual-voter uncertainty studied in the paper. Or one can introduce correlated latent types, which again amounts to specifying a second-order distribution over mass vectors. These may be worthwhile new problems, but they are additional modelling choices, not limits forced by the paper.

Lemma 3 is therefore a particularly weak anchor. The paper has already compressed the coalition’s reports into its positional summary matrix \(X\). Replacing the integer constraints

\[ X\mathbf 1=X^\top\mathbf 1=c\mathbf 1 \]

by a doubly stochastic matrix is simply the convexification of the manipulators’ action set. By Birkhoff–von Neumann, that matrix is a mixture of ordinary rankings. This is mathematically legitimate, but the continuous object is doing its work on the coalition’s reports, not on the paper’s Bayesian population uncertainty. For a fixed sincere society, the probability objective has disappeared; for a prior \(\Pi\) over societies, one has created a new chance-constrained optimization problem. Nothing in Lemma 3 establishes that this is the relevant high-multiplicity version of Bayesian manipulation.

Theorem 1 fares better, but still does not provide a strong ChoCo anchor. Under IC, the continuous limit removes the random profile and hence the event whose probability is being optimized. Under IAC, the proposed uniform distribution over the simplex is a coherent limiting prior over empirical societies. This is the strongest point for the positive case. But it is a new random-environment problem: the paper’s theorem concerns uniform finite voting situations, while the proposed problem concerns the measure of a region of the continuous simplex under a prior \(\Pi\). Its computational difficulty would principally depend on how \(\Pi\) is represented and how those regions are integrated, not on high multiplicity of voters.

Theorem 2 is weaker still as a mirror. Its distinctive content is tied to the parity of \(n\) and \(c\), and to the resulting one-unit rounding differences between balanced and near-balanced strategies. A continuum has no canonical analogue of “\(n\) is even” or “\(c+2\) is divisible by four.” Replacing those quantities by a fraction \(\rho\) produces a sensible new three-candidate Borda problem, but not a continuous limit retaining the theorem’s substantive statement. Preserving the finite parity effects would require retaining lattice-scale fluctuations, which is precisely a finite-population noise model rather than a continuous society.

The proponent is right that voting admits sensible high-multiplicity scenarios, and it would be a mistake to object that the answer might become easy or that existing high-multiplicity work counts as prior art. The real objection is narrower: this paper’s defining computational object is a common manipulation policy against uncertainty in a finite realised electorate. Continuizing the electorate removes that uncertainty; restoring it requires a new prior over population measures. Consequently, the proposed mirrors are plausible extensions, but they are not compelling continuizations of the paper’s named results.

That said, the universal negative is not airtight. The IAC-to-uniform-simplex construction is a genuine, recognisable continuous question, and a researcher could reasonably study it. The strongest defensible verdict is therefore not “no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario,” but that this paper is a poor flagship ChoCo anchor: it offers no numbered complexity result, and its most natural continuum either loses Bayesian uncertainty or replaces it with a new second-order model.
