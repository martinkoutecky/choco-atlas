The continuous mirror question

Given rational item values \(v_j>0\) and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\in[0,1]\) with \(\sum_j q_j\ge1\), let \(x_S\) be the mass receiving the nonempty subset \(S\subseteq[d]\), with \(\sum_S x_S=1\) and \(\sum_{S\ni j}x_S=q_j\). Is it NP-hard to return a feasible rational distribution with \(W(x)>\mathrm{OPT}-1\), where \(W(x)=\exp(\sum_S x_S\ln(\sum_{j\in S}v_j))\) and \(\mathrm{OPT}=\max_x W(x)\)?

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population receives integral subsets of \(d\) indivisible item categories. The decision variables are the subset masses \(x_S\), constrained by per-capita supplies \(q_j\); the objective is \(W(x)=\exp(\sum_S x_S\ln(\sum_{j\in S}v_j))\).

The objection that survived

The model restricts each worker to at most one item per category, so the partition reduction does not establish hardness for the more usual repeated-copy supply model in which a worker may receive several copies.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Proposition A.1's fixed additive-error hardness question for identical-additive NSW in the stated per-category-capacity model; it does not mirror Theorems 1.1–1.2 or the conventional PTAS result.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the additive-error hardness persist when item categories have repeatable copies and bundles are vectors in \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}^d\)?
  • Does the stated mass-allocation problem admit an additive approximation scheme with error scaled by \(v_{\max}\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is for a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s identical-additive NSW allocation problem. Its anchor is Proposition A.1, proved in this paper: *finding a feasible solution \(\pi\) for Identical Additive NSW with \(f(\pi)>\mathrm{OPT}-1\) is NP-hard.* This is hardness for a fixed absolute additive error without a scale parameter; it is not a claim that exact NSW maximization is NP-hard.

Consider a large workforce of identical employees receiving standardized benefit items. There are \(d\) item categories, each valued at \(v_j>0\) by every employee, and category \(j\) has \(q_j\) items per employee in aggregate, where \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]\) and \(\sum_j q_j\ge1\). Each employee still receives an integral subset of categories. Normalize the workforce to mass \(1\), and let \(x_S\) be the share of employees receiving subset \(S\subseteq[d]\). Feasibility means
\[ x_S\ge0,\qquad \sum_{S\subseteq[d]}x_S=1,\qquad \sum_{S\ni j}x_S=q_j\ \text{for every }j,\qquad x_\emptyset=0. \]
The continuum NSW is
\[ W(x)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{S\ne\emptyset}x_S \ln\!\left(\sum_{j\in S}v_j\right)\right). \]
The continuous problem is: given the binary rational values \(v_j,q_j\), find a feasible distribution \(x\) with \(W(x)>\mathrm{OPT}-1\), where \(\mathrm{OPT}\) is the maximum over feasible distributions. A solution can be given as a list of subsets and their rational masses; the logarithm makes maximizing \(W\) equivalent to a linear objective in those masses.

This is population continuization: \(x_S\) is the mass of employees receiving bundle \(S\), not a lottery each employee faces. Bundles remain indivisible for every employee. In the regime \(N\gg d\), the workforce has one preference type and each category’s stock scales with \(N\); rational \(q_j\) can be realized by choosing \(N\) with \(q_jN\) integral. The authors should recognize the mirror: identical agents, additive valuations, integral bundles, and the same Nash welfare objective, with per-capita supplies replacing a finite list of goods.

The partition reduction behind Proposition A.1 survives this mass splitting. Take a partition instance with positive integers \(a_j\), total \(\sum_j a_j=2\alpha\), and \(\alpha\ge2\). Set \(q_j=1/2\) and \(v_j=4\alpha a_j\). Every feasible mass distribution has mean unscaled bundle value \(\alpha\). If a partition \(J\) exists, assign mass \(1/2\) to \(J\) and \(1/2\) to its complement: everyone has utility \(4\alpha^2\), which is optimal by the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality. If there is no partition, every bundle’s unscaled value is an integer other than \(\alpha\). Concavity of \(\ln\) then bounds the NSW by \(4\alpha\sqrt{\alpha^2-1}\). The gap from the yes-case optimum is
\[ 4\alpha\bigl(\alpha-\sqrt{\alpha^2-1}\bigr) =\frac{4\alpha}{\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha^2-1}}>2. \]
Thus even arbitrary fractional masses over integral bundles do not erase the partition obstruction. A solver achieving additive error \(1\) would distinguish the cases. I expect this mirror to be Class B: the hardness lives in the combinatorics of item categories and integral bundles, not in the number of agents.

This case covers Proposition A.1 and the underlying NSW allocation problem; it does not claim a mirror for the paper’s Max \(p\)-Mean extension or establish a continuous analogue of Theorem 1.1. The weakest point is that the paper’s stated reduction uses two agents, while the high-multiplicity scenario comes from extending its identical-agent model to proportionate stocks. That extension is still a natural version of the authors’ allocation question, but it is the step a skeptical reader may challenge. Further questions include whether the continuous mass problem admits an additive PTAS with scale parameter \(v_{\max}\), and how its exponential bundle formulation behaves under pricing.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the proponent’s *specific* reduction from Proposition A.1. It treats each item category as usable at most once per employee. In the paper, goods are individual items; a high-multiplicity version that replicates a good type would ordinarily let an employee receive several copies. That changes the feasible allocations, and the partition gap has not been shown to survive that more faithful model. Also, the absolute error \(1\) in Proposition A.1 depends on the utility scale; the paper itself explains why its scale-aware guarantee uses \(v_{\max}\).

That objection does not defeat the mirror. A population with per-capita supplies and integral bundles is a plausible version of identical-additive NSW, and its continuous objective can be written as \(W(x)=\exp(\sum_S x_S\ln v(S))\). The divisible-goods results cited in the paper do not settle it: they let each person receive fractional goods, while this mirror keeps each person’s bundle integral. Nor does the fact that the reduction’s combinatorics concern item categories count against it under the programme’s stated criteria.

So I cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case. The proponent’s exact hardness argument needs a more faithful supply model to establish that *particular* result, but the paper’s NSW problem still has a worthwhile continuous-population mirror. The negative case is weak at the point that matters: it does not rule out a well-posed computational question the authors could recognize as theirs.
