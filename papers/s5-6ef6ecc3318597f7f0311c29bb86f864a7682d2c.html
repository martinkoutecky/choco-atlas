What the mirror covers

No named result has a surviving continuous mirror; Theorems 4.1–4.3 rely on finite-agent incentives, criticality, or equilibrium, and the experiments use finite networks.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive seed is a large deployment of replicated task agents: many copies share a base model, tools, role, competence vector, state, and local neighborhood pattern. A type would include all of those features, and \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of the fleet of type \(t\). Type-level delegation and diffusion could then be represented by mass flows. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s mechanism, rather than a claim that its outcomes are continuous.

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor the requested mirror. Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper, says that if agent \(n_i\) has a neighbor \(n_j\) whose diffused competence on task \(t_k\) exceeds \(n_i\)’s intrinsic competence, then delegating to \(n_j\) weakly improves \(n_i\)’s utility over self-delegation, for any joint action of the other agents; an optimal delegation therefore does not retain its own vote. Theorem 4.2 concerns the structure of critical agents, and Theorem 4.3 characterizes equilibrium actions. These are incentive and equilibrium results, not classifications such as polynomial-time solvability or NP-hardness. The experiments also do not supply a named complexity result.

There is a serious obstacle to turning the fleet scenario into an anchor-driven continuous question: in an atomless population, one agent’s delegation cannot change the winning path. The paper’s criticality and payments rely on individual counterfactuals, so preserving them would require changing the payoff model or treating cohorts as strategic players. Either move may take the proposed question away from the authors’ problem. Thus there is a plausible population regime, but no qualifying anchor here; I would not claim a continuous mirror for this programme on the basis of this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that this paper gives ChoCo no computational result to mirror. Theorems 4.1–4.3 concern incentives, critical agents, and equilibrium behavior; the experiments measure behavior and routing performance. None classifies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. One can invent a computational question about AgentSociety, but that would be a new contribution built from the mechanism, rather than a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

Theorem 4.1 is the most tempting anchor. A large fleet of cloned task agents is plausible, but even exact clones may have different histories and graph positions, which affect their options and payoffs. Grouping only agents with the same full state and neighborhood preserves the model but may leave few large types. A block or graphon model could create large groups; then an individual has measure zero and cannot change the winning path by redirecting a vote. The theorem’s comparison between that individual’s delegation choices loses its strategic force. Giving a whole block positive mass and making it act together could restore pivotality, but makes the block a coordinated player rather than a collection of autonomous agents.

Theorem 4.2 runs into the same problem: its critical chain is defined by counterfactual deviations by particular agents. In an atomless population, removing or redirecting one agent ordinarily leaves the winning path unchanged, so individual criticality disappears except at knife-edge ties. Defining critical blocks or measuring changes in aggregate routing could produce a meaningful question, but it changes the unit of strategic action and the payoff concept that the theorem characterizes.

Theorem 4.3 does not avoid that obstacle. Its equilibrium characterization relies on individual payoffs from being critical, alongside agents’ private competence signals and local neighborhoods. A continuum model could preserve those details with a richer network and state description, but then the paper’s individual criticality and payment results no longer carry over. A simpler mass-flow model could ask for the best feasible route or the equilibrium of aggregate delegation. That may be worth studying, but it omits the mechanism’s central claims about individual incentives, diffusion, and marginal payments; the paper does not pose it as a computational problem.

The honest weak point in this negative case is that a replicated fleet with mass-based routing could be a sensible new model. I cannot rule out its value. But its value would rest on designing and analyzing new aggregate strategic rules, not on continuizing the named results here. I would not count this paper as a worthwhile continuous mirror under ChoCo’s stated gate.
