What the mirror covers

No computational result is covered; the proposed mass-path analogy would address only the allocation characterizations in Theorems 2.21 and 2.22.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the kind this task requires. Its introduction mentions the difficulty of calculating values and approximation methods from earlier work, but this paper proves no complexity classification or algorithm for such a problem.

The closest foothold is Theorem 2.22, proved here: an allocation is reasonable and efficient if and only if it is a convex combination of special allocations. A special allocation gives each player their marginal contribution along a chain that adds players one at a time. Theorem 2.21, also proved here, says those special allocations are exactly the extreme points of the reasonable, efficient allocations. These are structural results, not computational ones: neither asks for an algorithm, analyzes complexity, nor computes a decomposition.

A charitable extension would imagine a very large consortium with many contributors in a small number of exact role types. Let \(\mu_t\) be the population share of role \(t\), and let coalition value depend on the mass of each role participating. One might replace finite player orderings with paths through coalition-mass space and ask whether an allocation can be represented as an average of marginal gains along those paths. That is suggestive of Theorem 2.22, but it is a new axiomatic question, not a computational problem this paper studies. It also risks losing the paper’s focus on allocations without symmetry: averaging members of a role can erase differences the allocation rule assigns to individual players.

So I cannot honestly name a lead mirror problem or predict its place in the tractable/hard trichotomy from this paper’s results. The potential path-mixture extension is the strongest positive hint, and its weakness is decisive: it continuizes a structural allocation characterization, not a computational result about a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that neither Theorem 2.22 nor Theorem 2.21 gives a computational result to continuize. Theorem 2.22 characterizes a set of allocation maps as a convex hull; Theorem 2.21 identifies that set’s extreme points. The paper specifies no algorithmic input and output, complexity question, or allocation-selection rule. Its remarks about costly computations and approximation schemes concern prior work, not results established here. The two theorems are also the same structural anchor in substance: one states the convex-hull characterization, the other identifies its vertices.

The best computational sequel would be to ask for a decomposition of a given reasonable, efficient allocation into special allocations, or to optimize a specified objective over that class. But those are new problems. Given a game alone, the paper’s characterization does not select an allocation to compute; given an allocation, finding a decomposition is a certificate task with its own input model. A continuous version would additionally need a finite representation of the game and the allocation, plus a specified computational objective. The theorem supplies none of these, so there is no complexity landscape here whose high-multiplicity behavior the paper’s results motivate.

The consortium example is the strongest proposed mirror, but it exposes the gap. To aggregate players into roles, the game’s value must depend only on how many members of each role join a coalition. That is a substantive restriction: a general characteristic function assigns values to coalitions individually. Even after imposing role anonymity, its values on integer coalition counts do not determine a unique function over fractional masses. One could choose an extension and replace chains of individual entrants with paths through mass space, but then one must define marginal-value densities and a selection or optimization problem over those paths. Preserving individual asymmetries requires tracking positions within each role; averaging them away yields a different, symmetric allocation question. These choices may support worthwhile new work, but they do not follow from either theorem.

So the negative case is strong for the proposed anchors: the paper’s own results offer no computational problem to mirror, and the more ambitious path formulation first requires specifying a new computational model. The universal claim that no worthwhile continuous version could ever be devised is weaker. A suitably specified cooperative-game model might yield a good problem; it would be a new computational theory prompted by the paper’s subject, rather than a continuous mirror of its results.
