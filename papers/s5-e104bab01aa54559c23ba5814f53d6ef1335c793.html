What the mirror covers

The candidate covers surplus-maximizing procurement and the proposed limiting leave-one-out payments; it does not computationally mirror the truthfulness, individual rationality, weak budget balance, or interpolation propositions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Under what regularity and scaling conditions does the finite-population leave-one-out payment converge to a well-defined per-supplier price?
  • For rational piecewise-quadratic concave revenue and convex cost inputs, can the allocation and limiting prices be computed efficiently?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a candidate mirror, because this paper has no qualifying named computational anchor. Its numbered results concern mechanism properties: Proposition 1 proves supply-side dominant-strategy truthfulness; Corollary 1 extends truthfulness to agents who act as both suppliers and consumers; Propositions 2, 3, and 4 establish Pareto efficiency, individual rationality, and weak budget balance under the stated assumptions. Proposition 5 says that, under its three interpolation conditions, the interpolated PVCG preserves truthfulness and Pareto efficiency, and preserves individual rationality and weak budget balance when Assumptions 1–3 also hold. These are not results that classify a computational problem as tractable or hard. RIM is an algorithmic proposal, but the paper reports an exploratory experiment, not a named complexity or algorithmic theorem. Proposition 2 is the closest anchor: it proves that PVCG combined with the Crèmer–McLean mechanism maximizes ex-post social surplus. It does not prove that computing that allocation is in P.

A plausible lead question is High-Multiplicity PVCG Procurement. Consider a large pool of federated-learning or crowdsourcing suppliers. A type records everything relevant to procurement: capacity and the supplier’s full cost curve. For example, many thousands of devices might fall into a few dozen hardware, data-quality, and cost-profile classes. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of suppliers of type \(t\), and let \(q_t\) be procurement per supplier of that type. With type capacity \(\bar q_t\), cost \(c_t\), and per-capita revenue function \(R\), the continuous allocation problem is to find

\(\displaystyle q^* \in \arg\max_{0\le q_t\le \bar q_t}\left\{R\!\left(\sum_t \mu_t q_t\right)-\sum_t \mu_t c_t(q_t)\right\}.\)

To include PVCG payments in the question, use the finite-population lift with \(N\mu_t\) suppliers of each type and total revenue \(N R(N^{-1}\sum_i x_i)\). For each supplier \(i\), let \(x^N\) maximize total surplus and let \(z^{N,i}\) maximize it when \(i\)’s procurement is zero. The type-\(t\) payment in the mirror is the limit, as \(N\) grows through values with integral type counts, of

\(\displaystyle p_i^N= N R\!\left(N^{-1}\sum_j x_j^N\right)-N R\!\left(N^{-1}\sum_j z_j^{N,i}\right)-\sum_{j\ne i}\bigl(c_{t_j}(x_j^N)-c_{t_j}(z_j^{N,i})\bigr).\)

The problem asks for the per-type allocation \(q^*\) and the limiting per-supplier PVCG payment, given rational type masses, capacities, and an explicit representation of the revenue and cost curves. A natural tractable version uses concave revenue and convex costs, with rational piecewise-quadratic input. Its optimization dimension is the number \(\tau\) of supported types, not the number \(N\) of suppliers, so I would expect this version to be tractable by convex optimization. The paper itself does not establish that classification.

This is recognizably the authors’ procurement problem: it retains supplier cost types, capacity limits, surplus maximization, and the leave-one-out basis of PVCG payments. The proposed regime is also concrete: many suppliers with repeated full cost-and-capacity types, rather than a small committee of individually distinct agents.

The weakest point is the payment limit. A zero-mass individual has no direct leave-one-out effect in a continuum, so the finite-population scaling is essential; the paper neither defines nor studies this limit. Establishing that the limit exists and captures an economically meaningful per-supplier PVCG payment is a new result, not a consequence of Proposition 2 or Proposition 5. That leaves a credible continuous question, but no named computational anchor in this paper to support it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Proposition 2 is not a computational result. It says PVCG selects an allocation maximizing social surplus; it does not classify the cost of finding that allocation or prove an algorithmic guarantee. Propositions 1, 3, 4, and 5 establish mechanism properties, while RIM is a proposed method illustrated by an experiment, without a worst-case runtime, approximation bound, or convergence theorem. So the proposed optimization problem is not a continuous version of a computational result in the paper.

The proposed supplier regime is plausible: many standardized devices or workers could share a capacity and cost type. The objection is not that high multiplicity makes no sense here. It is that the optimization problem adds assumptions that do the computational work. The paper assumes smoothness, monotonicity, and decreasing cross marginal revenue, but gives no finite input representation for its revenue and cost functions. With explicit concave revenue and convex costs, the proposed problem is a finite-dimensional convex optimization over the supported types. With only the paper’s general assumptions, there is no specified input on which to state a Turing complexity bound. Either way, Proposition 2 supplies no complexity question to transfer.

The payment limit has a deeper mismatch. In a continuum, removing one supplier changes its type mass by zero. The finite-population payment can be kept visible only by retaining an extensive scale, such as total surplus \(N R(N^{-1}\sum_i x_i)\), and taking a first-order leave-one-out limit. That limit may be a useful marginal externality price, but the continuum society itself does not define the individual removal on which PVCG payments and their truthfulness argument rely. Computing the limit requires new assumptions about scaling and regularity; under a smooth, well-behaved model it becomes a sensitivity calculation for the welfare optimum, while the paper proves no such limit result. It is a new asymptotic mechanism question, not a computational mirror of Proposition 2.

One could instead ask for the complexity of a type-aggregated RIM procedure. But that needs a finite representation of cost and revenue curves, a noise model, an accuracy target, and a convergence guarantee. None is supplied by the paper’s exploratory experiment. Specifying them could produce a worthwhile research problem, but it would be a new problem built around RIM rather than a continuous mirror of a named computational result.

That is the best negative case, and its limit is real: the large-pool procurement setting is credible, and a continuous analysis of limiting supplier prices could be valuable. What does not survive is the claimed anchor to this paper’s results. Under ChoCo’s computational remit, the candidate is an invitation to create a related problem, not evidence that the paper itself has a worthwhile continuous mirror.
