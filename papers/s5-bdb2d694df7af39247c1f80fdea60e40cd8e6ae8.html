The continuous mirror question

Given a rooted graph with nonnegative edge costs and a finite rational distribution over terminal-location and per-person-value types, what is the best worst-case social-cost approximation achievable by group-strategyproof, β-budget-balanced cost-sharing mechanisms for a nonatomic population? A mechanism chooses served mass \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) for each type, incurs the cost of connecting the root to every terminal with \(x_t>0\), and charges per-person shares; determine whether an Ω((\log \tau)^2/\beta) lower bound holds or can be beaten.

The model it lives in

A rooted Steiner-tree service problem with population mass μ over terminal and value types. The decision is served mass \(x_t\); social cost is connection cost plus the value of excluded mass. Study per-person cost shares that are cross-monotone in served masses, satisfy \(C(x)/\beta\le\sum_t x_t\chi_t(x)\le C(x)\), and induce a group-strategyproof service mechanism with explicitly defined positive-mass coalitional deviations.

The objection that survived

The theorem’s within-location ordering contributes one logarithmic factor, while allowing arbitrarily small divisible masses makes the natural integral of budget-balanced shares singular; neither the bound nor the proposed mechanism semantics therefore carries over directly.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 4.2, proved in this paper. It says that for every \(\beta\ge1\), every \(\beta\)-budget-balanced Moulin mechanism for Steiner tree, and every \(k\ge2\), there is a Steiner-tree cost function and a set \(S\) of \(k\) players for which the mechanism’s output has social cost at least \((c\log^2 k/\beta)\,C(S)\), for a constant \(c>0\). The paper concludes that constant-budget-balanced Moulin mechanisms must therefore lose a factor \(\Omega(\log^2 k)\) in efficiency. This is a named approximation lower bound, not a theorem proving NP-hardness; it is the paper’s strongest result for asking whether a population relaxation changes a discrete barrier.

A plausible setting is a large network service, such as broadband or a utility, with many customers clustered at a modest number of demand locations. Customers at the same location with the same willingness to pay are one complete type. There can be \(N\gg\tau\) customers but only \(\tau\) location/value types. That is a credible high-multiplicity regime for Steiner-tree cost sharing: the provider is the root, customers are terminals, and serving them requires building a connecting network.

Here is the mirror I would hand to a prover: Continuous Steiner-Tree Moulin Efficiency. An instance consists of a rooted graph \(G=(V,E)\), root \(r\), nonnegative edge costs, and a finite support \(T\subseteq (V\setminus\{r\})\times\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\). A type \(t=(v_t,w_t)\) has rational mass \(\mu_t>0\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); \(v_t\) is its demand location and \(w_t\) its per-person value for service. A decision is a vector of served masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\). Each unit of mass either receives service or does not. The service cost is
\[ C_G(x)=\min\left\{\sum_{e\in H}c_e:\ H\text{ connects }r\text{ to }v_t\text{ for every }t\text{ with }x_t>0\right\}, \]
and social cost is
\[ \pi(x)=C_G(x)+\sum_{t\in T}(\mu_t-x_t)w_t. \]
Because serving any positive mass at a terminal incurs the cost of connecting that terminal, while each additional served unit reduces excluded value, an optimum serves each type either fully or not at all. Thus the continuous input weights the population without replacing the underlying service-selection problem with a different fractional-outcome problem.

The mechanism class is the continuous analogue of Moulin mechanisms: a cross-monotone per-unit cost-share rule \(\chi_t(x)\), with \(\chi_t(x)\ge\chi_t(y)\) whenever \(x\le y\) and \(x_t>0\), induces service by price thresholds on individual bids. It must be group-strategyproof and \(\beta\)-budget-balanced:
\[ C_G(x)/\beta\le \sum_t x_t\chi_t(x)\le C_G(x). \]
The question is to determine the best worst-case social-cost approximation \(\alpha(\tau,\beta)\) achievable by such mechanisms over these instances. In particular: does every such mechanism have instances with approximation ratio \(\Omega((\log\tau)^2/\beta)\), or can fractional mass and aggregation beat that bound? A solution would be a matching mechanism and lower bound, or a better mechanism together with a counterexample to the proposed lower bound.

I expect hardness transfers (Class B) as the starting hypothesis: the costly combinatorics still live in the network and in choosing which terminal types to serve. But Theorem 4.2 does not establish the proposed \(\tau\)-bound. Its construction orders individual players and exploits groups of \(\sqrt{k}\) co-located players. In the mirror, those players may be one type whose mass is handled together. Whether an atomless mechanism can split that mass in a way that defeats the construction is a real open part of this question. Related questions are whether the right guarantee depends on support size, the smallest positive type mass, or both, and whether allowing within-type splitting changes the bound.

That dependence on named players is the weakest point of the case. If the original lower bound fundamentally needs individually ordered agents with different values, compressing them into a small number of complete types may erase its force. I still think the mirror is worth pursuing: it asks precisely whether the paper’s strongest approximation barrier survives in a plausible large-population regime, rather than claiming that Theorem 4.2 already answers it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.2 is the only strong anchor, and I would not dismiss it as “already known” or use the fact that its combinatorics live in the network. The question is whether its \(\Omega(\log^2 k)\) barrier for budget-balanced Moulin mechanisms has a meaningful continuous-population counterpart. The broadband example makes high multiplicity plausible, so “Steiner-tree users are always distinct” is not a good objection.

The difficulty is preserving what creates the bound. In the theorem’s construction, groups of \(\sqrt{k}\) players are co-located. The lower bound gets one logarithmic factor from ordering players within those groups and another from the network’s successive layers. If identical co-located users become one indivisible type, that within-group ordering disappears. The proposed type-level mirror might still have an interesting support-size bound, but it would be a different mechanism problem: the strategic units are cohorts, and the theorem’s main source of loss has been removed.

Allowing a type’s mass to split preserves more of the original player-level process, but creates a singular limit. Consider one location with fixed service cost \(F>0\). If an anonymous per-unit cost share for a served mass \(x\) is \(p(x)\), budget balance requires \(x p(x)\ge F/\beta\), hence \(p(x)\ge F/(\beta x)\). The paper’s approximation analysis relies on summing shares along a sequence of growing coalitions. Its natural continuous counterpart integrates along mass; for this example, that gives \(\int_0^\mu p(x)\,dx\ge (F/\beta)\int_0^\mu dx/x\), which diverges. This does not prove every continuous mechanism has poor efficiency, but it does show that the paper’s summability framework does not pass cleanly to a divisible population. Imposing a minimum mass increment removes the divergence by reintroducing a finite population scale.

So the strongest case against this mirror is a modeling fork: keep types indivisible and lose the theorem’s within-type mechanism; let mass split and the quantitative bridge used by the paper becomes singular. An allocation-only question about weighted terminal selection would avoid that fork, but it would drop the GSP and budget-balance trade-off that makes Theorem 4.2 worth mirroring.

That is a reason to rank this as a weak ChoCo target, not proof that no worthwhile mirror exists. A carefully defined model of divisible cohorts and measurable coalitions could still produce a valuable new result, and the paper’s authors might recognize it as their problem. The negative case is weakest precisely there: the proposed scenario is credible, and the unresolved modeling choice may itself be worth studying.
