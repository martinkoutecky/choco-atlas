The continuous mirror question

Given a rational finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over normalized common-slope single-peaked valuation types, and a per-capita cake endowment whose total supply scales with population, can one compute a feasible measurable assignment of integral bundles to residents—allowing each type’s mass to split across bundle patterns—such that the type-mass versions of the mechanisms in Proposition 3 retain their stated Pareto-optimality, envy-freeness, and dominance guarantees?

The model it lives in

An atomless population with type masses \(\mu_t\), each type described by its normalized single-peaked valuation; feasible assignments give each resident an integral cake bundle, while type-level averages may be fractional and total cake supply scales with population.

The objection that survived

A mass model must define how residents of the same type can receive individual bundles, how envy and Pareto improvement are evaluated across them, and how cake supply scales with population; the proponent acknowledges the bundle issue but does not settle these definitions.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population regime is land allocation among a large community whose residents fall into a modest number of preference types. A type would be a normalized single-peaked valuation, identified here by its peak and support; mass \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of residents with that valuation. The paper’s common-slope setting gives a particularly clean version. This is a sensible high-multiplicity scenario, though a population model would need to say how type-level average bundles represent allocations to individual residents.

I cannot, however, anchor a positive case to a qualifying named result in this paper. Proposition 1, proved here, bounds the Wang–Wu welfare loss by \((n-1)/n\), tightly. Proposition 2, proved here, characterizes Pareto-optimal allocations under common slopes as exactly those that are non-wasteful and peak-preserving. Proposition 3, also proved here, states that the utilitarian mechanism is Pareto optimal and maximizes total utility, leftmost leaves is Pareto optimal and Pareto dominates Wang–Wu, and modified Wang–Wu is envy-free and Pareto dominates Wang–Wu. These are welfare, structure, and mechanism-guarantee results; none asserts a complexity classification such as membership in P or NP-hardness. The paper’s mention of Wang–Wu’s \(2n\)-query mechanism is attributed to earlier work, not a numbered computational result established here.

The nearest candidate for a population mirror would be to characterize Pareto-efficient allocations for a rational distribution over finitely many common-slope valuation types. But that would extend Proposition 2’s structural question; it would not mirror a named computational result of the kind required here. The weakness is therefore decisive for this assignment: the paper offers a plausible high-multiplicity setting, but no eligible computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Proposition 1 is a welfare-loss bound; Proposition 2 characterizes Pareto-optimal allocations; Proposition 3 gives welfare and fairness guarantees for three mechanisms. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The \(2n\)-query Wang–Wu procedure is cited prior work, not a result this paper establishes. The proposed land-community setting may make a high-multiplicity model sensible, but that does not provide a computational anchor.

The best extension of Proposition 1 would ask for a worst-case welfare-loss bound over a distribution of valuation types. That is a natural continuous welfare question, but it remains a welfare theorem, not a computational one. For Proposition 2, one could characterize Pareto-optimal allocations for a population distributed over common-slope types. To preserve the paper’s individual fairness and efficiency concepts, that model must specify how cake is assigned among people within each type, or how supply scales with population. Either choice could be coherent, but it defines a new allocation problem; the proposition itself gives no algorithmic question to carry over. Proposition 3 has the same gap: its mechanisms could be reformulated to operate on type masses, but the paper makes no complexity claim about computing their outputs.

The nearest computational candidate is the paper’s open question about finding an allocation that is both envy-free and Pareto-optimal in the Robertson–Webb query model. A mass-based query-complexity problem could be worthwhile, but it would be a new problem with its own oracle and allocation definitions. The paper’s cited \(2n\)-query result does not establish it.
