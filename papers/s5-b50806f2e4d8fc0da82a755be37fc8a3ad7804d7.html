The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a qualified one: the paper admits a very natural continuous-population mirror, but it has no eligible numbered computational anchor under your rule. I therefore cannot present it as a fully compliant positive case without inventing a theorem number or overstating the paper’s contribution.

The best candidate is the paper’s unnumbered maximum-likelihood characterization of Condorcet’s ranking rule. The paper assumes \(n\) voters, \(m\) alternatives, independent pairwise judgments, and a common competence level \(p\in(1/2,1)\). If \(n_{ab}\) voters prefer \(a\) to \(b\), then a complete ranking \(R\) has likelihood determined by the number of pairwise votes agreeing with \(R\). The paper proves in prose that \(R\) is maximum likelihood exactly when it maximizes

\[ \sum_{a\succ_R b} n_{ab}, \]

which is the Kemeny ranking objective; the optimizer is independent of the particular value of \(p\). This characterization is argued in the paper, while the identification with Kemeny is attributed to earlier literature. It is not numbered, however, and it is not a complexity result.

A faithful continuous problem would be:

\[ \mathrm{Kemeny\text{-}Ranking}_{\infty}. \]

An instance consists of a finite candidate set \(A\), the ranking-type space \(T=\mathcal L(A)\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings. A type \(t\in T\) is a complete voter ranking; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the population with that ranking. Define the pairwise population support

\[ q_\mu(a,b)=\sum_{t:\,a\succ_t b}\mu_t. \]

The task is to output a complete ranking \(R\) maximizing

\[ K_\mu(R)=\sum_{a\succ_R b}q_\mu(a,b). \]

All maximizing rankings are valid solutions; if only a winner is required, output the top-ranked candidate of a maximizing \(R\).

The natural regime is a large expert, jury, or consultation population repeatedly evaluating a fixed small agenda. Many individuals may share one of a modest number of complete judgment profiles: \(N\gg \tau\), where \(N\) is the number of voters and \(\tau\) is the number of ranking types with positive mass. This is especially plausible because the paper explicitly treats voters anonymously: only aggregate pairwise counts matter, not voter identities. The mirror preserves complete rankings, integral alternatives, and the Kemeny objective; only the population is represented by rational mass.

The authors would likely recognize this as their problem. If \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), then

\[ K_\mu(R)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{a\succ_R b}n_{ab}, \]

so denominator clearing recovers the finite objective and exactly the same optimal rankings. Conversely, every rational society can be expanded into a finite electorate. This is a genuine high-multiplicity formulation, not a fractional ranking or outcome-space relaxation.

I would expect this problem to be Class B: the combinatorics lie in selecting an ordering of the alternatives, not in naming individual voters. Any hardness of finite Kemeny ranking transfers immediately by normalizing voter multiplicities. With fixed \(m\), brute force over \(m!\) rankings is possible; more interesting questions concern dependence on \(m\), the support size \(\tau\), approximation, and implicitly represented ranking distributions. A further ChoCo extension would be minimum-cost mass transport changing \(\mu\) until a target becomes the Kemeny winner, but that is new bribery/robustness work rather than a result of this paper.

This mirror covers only the maximum-likelihood/Kemeny ranking discussion. It does not cover the Borda observations, Condorcet’s criterion, or the local-stability uniqueness theorem: those are either informal computational remarks or axiomatic results. In particular, the paper says that Condorcet’s rule is the unique rule satisfying anonymity, neutrality, unanimity, reinforcement, and local stability, citing Young and Levenglick (1978), but that theorem is neither numbered here nor computational.

The decisive weakness is therefore source eligibility. No numbered Theorem, Lemma, Proposition, or Corollary in the supplied paper asserts NP-hardness, membership in P, fixed-parameter tractability, approximation, or another qualifying computational result. The continuous Kemeny problem is sensible and author-recognizable, and I would defend it as a real high-multiplicity mirror. But under the programme’s strict anchor gate, this paper cannot honestly support a compliant positive case; the strongest verdict is “natural mirror, no qualifying computational anchor.”

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is decisive only at the programme’s anchor gate. The paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, proposition, or corollary asserting a computational result. Its maximum-likelihood characterization is statistical: for a ranking \(R\), likelihood is maximized exactly when

\[ \sum_{a\succ_R b} n_{ab} \]

is maximized. The paper identifies this with Kemeny’s rule, but does not establish a complexity bound, algorithmic classification, approximation result, or parameterized result. “Considerably simpler to compute” is an informal observation, not an eligible computational theorem. The local-stability result is axiomatic, and the Borda discussion is likewise not a complexity result.

The proposed \(\mathrm{Kemeny\text{-}Ranking}_{\infty}\) is nevertheless a legitimate continuous-population problem. If \(\mu_t=n_t/N\), then

\[ K_\mu(R)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{a\succ_R b}n_{ab}, \]

so it is a genuine high-multiplicity normalization, with a perfectly sensible expert- or jury-population interpretation. It does not fail because mass is meaningless, because identities matter, or because the continuum degenerates. Those objections would be wrong.

That concession also exposes the limit of the negative case. Replacing Kemeny ranking by Kemeny bribery, robustness, or campaigning could produce valuable ChoCo problems, but those are new constructions rather than mirrors of results proved in this paper. Changing the representation to implicit distributions or richer type-level costs would likewise add a new computational model; it cannot turn the paper’s statistical characterization into a computational theorem.

Thus the paper has no qualifying computational anchor, and under the stated gate it cannot support a compliant positive case. But the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario is not defensible: the Kemeny high-multiplicity mirror is natural and author-recognizable. The honest negative verdict is therefore “no eligible computational anchor,” not “no worthwhile continuous problem.”
