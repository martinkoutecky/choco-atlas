The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The appended case raises no positive mirror anchor; it names Theorems 4.4 and 4.7 and 6.2 and 6.3 as tempting candidates, then rejects them. The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper contains no named computational result: no input encoding, complexity claim, runtime bound, or algorithmic boundary.

Theorem 4.4 lifts feasible utility targets into guaranteed utilities, and Theorem 4.7 gives a policy attaining the fair-floor targets. Theorem 5.7 similarly gives transfer-free guarantees with an explicit error bound. These are structural and constructive mechanism results. Their explicit formulas do not classify the cost of computing a solution.

The strongest candidate is Theorems 6.2 and 6.3: they bound how much every player’s utility can be improved at once. But these are universal feasibility bounds over distributions and allocation rules, proved by an extremal capacity argument. To make them computational, one would need to specify an encoded family of finite-support distributions and ask, for example, how to compute the feasible utility frontier. That could be a worthwhile project, but it would pose a new algorithmic question; the paper does not claim or answer one.

A high-multiplicity setting is plausible: many repeated-allocation participants could share the same value distribution, dynamics, weights, and utility model. I would not object to that regime. The obstacle to a direct population limit is that the paper’s guarantee is for each player. With equal weights \(1/n\), its single-good target is
\[ f^*(1/n,D)=\int_0^1 Q_D(u)u^{n-1}\,du, \]
which tends to zero for bounded valuations, while one good per period keeps total welfare bounded. Scaling supply with population could preserve meaningful per-capita competition, as the programme permits. That would yield a coherent aggregate allocation problem, but it still supplies no computational result from this paper to mirror; the playerwise collusion guarantees would also need a type-level formulation.

The prior distributions and randomized ranks in the paper are uncertainty and allocation machinery, not a continuous society, so I would not count them as a collision or as evidence that the population mirror is already done. The honest limit of the negative case is its universal wording: a scaled cohort model paired with a newly formulated frontier algorithm might be valuable. On the paper and the programme’s stated gate, though, that would be a new computational project rather than a continuous mirror of a result here.
