The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered result that qualifies as a computational complexity anchor. Its strongest nearby result is Theorem 3.3, proved here: every setup-inventory game is balanced, and therefore has a nonempty core. That is an existence and stability theorem; it gives no runtime or complexity classification. Lemma 3.1 constructs ordering plans but supplies no complexity bound. The introduction’s general NP-hardness remark concerns lot-sizing variants broadly, not a numbered result about this SI problem.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting: a large group of standardized retail outlets with repeated demand and cost profiles. A type would be the complete per-period vector of demand, setup, purchasing, inventory, and backlogging costs; \(\mu_\theta\) would be the population fraction of type \(\theta\), with \(\tau\ll n\). A direct continuous research question is: given rational type masses and profiles, find per-outlet cost rates \(x_\theta\) that are efficient and stable against every measurable subcoalition. For a subcoalition with masses \(a_\theta\in[0,\mu_\theta]\), define its cost \(c(a)\) by the paper’s SI optimization model, using aggregate demand \(D_t(a)=\sum_\theta a_\theta d_{\theta,t}\), the cheapest available costs among types present, real-valued aggregate inventory flows, and a binary setup decision for each period. The required allocation satisfies
\[ \sum_\theta \mu_\theta x_\theta=c(\mu) \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_\theta a_\theta x_\theta\le c(a) \ \text{for every }a\in[0,\mu]. \]
This preserves the paper’s shared channels and fixed setup charges while making the population continuous. It is a credible follow-on to Theorem 3.3, though it is not one of the paper’s computational results.

My tentative guess for this allocation problem is tractability, through finite-support separation and dynamic programming. The exponentially many active-type sets and fixed setup decisions are the main risks, and the paper establishes no algorithm or complexity bound to settle them. So the positive case is that the underlying high-multiplicity regime is convincing and the mass-core question is recognizable to these authors. Its weakness is decisive under your anchor rule: this would be a new computational study inspired by a structural theorem, not a mirror anchored in a named complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only named candidate is Theorem 3.3, and it is not a computational anchor: it proves that every SI-game is balanced and has a nonempty core. Lemma 3.1 gives a constructive proof ingredient but no runtime bound. Proposition 4.1 and the later results give sufficient conditions, a core allocation, or a population-monotonic allocation scheme; none classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The introduction’s NP-hardness remark concerns lot-sizing variants generally, not this SI problem. So there is no result here whose complexity landscape a continuous mirror would extend.

The strongest proposed mirror—a type-level mass game in which one computes per-type cost rates stable against every subcoalition—does have a credible high-multiplicity story. A type can include the complete demand and cost profile, so there is no identity-based objection. But this is a new computational problem prompted by an existence theorem. Its continuum-indexed stability constraints do not turn Theorem 3.3 into an algorithmic result, nor does the paper give a discrete algorithm or hardness result for the SI problem to compare against.

There is also a substantive limit-model choice. In the proposed game, any positive mass of a type makes its cheaper technology available to the whole coalition, while setup is charged once regardless of coalition size. Thus \(c(a)\) can jump when a type’s mass reaches zero: an arbitrarily small positive mass can change the costs of everyone else. Under per-outlet normalization of a population of \(n\) replicated firms, a fixed setup charge contributes \(k/n\) and vanishes; retaining it at unit mass describes a different scaling. Either choice may be defensible, but neither is supplied by the paper’s theorem, and the choice shapes the new problem.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s computational-result gate: no proposed anchor survives. It is weaker as the literal universal claim that no scenario could yield worthwhile continuous research. Standardized outlets make a plausible population model, and the mass-core question could be worth studying on its own. The honest objection is that this would be a new computational study of a continuous cost-sharing game, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.
