What the mirror covers

The proposed continuation targets the equilibrium path in Proposition 2; it does not cover Proposition 1 or the paper’s experimental comparisons of efficiency and equity.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest possible bridge is to large sequential elections. A plausible type would be a voter’s position in the order, private signal, and voting cost; mass would be the fraction of the electorate with each type. The decision would be how much of each type votes for \(A\), votes for \(B\), or abstains. That fits the paper’s discussion of mass elections, early voting, and costly participation.

But the paper contains no named computational-complexity result: no theorem or proposition states that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its computational complexity. Its experimental findings are not such results. The nearest named result is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It characterizes the unique sincere, neutral equilibrium path in the three-voter sequential game as pure strategies. Let \(c_0=p(1-p)(2p-1)v\). For \(c\le c_0\), the first voter votes sincerely; the second votes only when the first voted and their signals conflict; and the third votes only when the earlier votes conflict or both earlier voters abstained. Whenever they vote, they vote according to their signals. For \(c>c_0\), the first two abstain and the third votes sincerely. This explicit characterization makes the fixed three-voter equilibrium easy to compute, but Proposition 2 does not state a complexity result, and its fixed-size game is not a high-multiplicity result. So it is not a qualifying anchor under the requested standard.

A precise candidate continuation would be Mass Sequential Costly Voting Equilibrium: given a continuum of voters partitioned into three ordered stages, a distribution \(\mu\) over stage and signal types, signal precision \(p\), value \(v\), and cost \(c>0\), find a symmetric perfect Bayesian equilibrium strategy for each type at every observed history, along with the induced outcome. The expected answer in the atomless model is immediate: an individual voter has zero influence on the vote masses and cannot affect what later voters observe, so paying a positive cost to vote cannot help. Everyone abstains. That is tractable, but the strategic abstention and signaling behavior characterized by Proposition 2 has disappeared. Giving voters positive-sized influence, or making whole groups act as coordinated blocs, would change the strategic model.

That collapse is the weakest point, and it defeats the positive case: the paper offers a plausible large-election setting, but no named computational anchor whose continuous-population analogue preserves its question. The proposed continuum problem is therefore a useful diagnostic, not an honest mirror of the paper’s result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central problem is that the paper offers no computational-complexity result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 characterize equilibria in fixed three-voter games with two alternatives and two signals. Their inputs are a few scalar parameters, and the equilibrium paths are given directly. The paper establishes no algorithmic problem or complexity classification. Turning either proposition into an equilibrium-computation problem for arbitrary electorate size could be worthwhile, but that would add the computational question the paper never studied.

Proposition 2 is the strongest candidate because its sequential incentives depend on voting costs and information revealed by earlier votes. Yet the proposed atomless model removes both mechanisms. With types defined by stage, signal, and cost, an individual vote changes the mass of votes by zero; it also changes nothing later voters observe. For positive voting costs, the pivotal and signaling incentives disappear. That is a continuum degeneration: the paper’s central strategic object—individual influence through pivotality—has vanished. Setting costs to zero leaves voters indifferent; making each type-mass coordinate its members’ actions gives the mass a bloc’s influence and changes the game.

The strongest repair would retain a large finite electorate, encode voters by population shares, and preserve each voter’s \(1/N\) influence. But pivotality then depends on finite-\(N\) vote fluctuations and order. The share distribution alone does not capture it; retaining it requires the electorate size and finite-size stochastic structure that the atomless model erased. A limit that scales costs or adds fluctuation terms may be an interesting asymptotic voting-game project. It would ask a new question about finite-game limits, rather than mirror a computational result in this paper. Likewise, an algorithmic study for arbitrary \(N\) could be interesting, but the paper gives no such result to anchor it.

The paper does name mass elections as a plausible setting, so I would not reject the high-multiplicity regime itself. The negative case is narrower: its named results concern a fixed, finite game, and the best direct continuous version dissolves the pivotal incentives those results characterize. That makes the case against a ChoCo mirror persuasive, though not airtight against a separately motivated algorithmic study of large voting games.
