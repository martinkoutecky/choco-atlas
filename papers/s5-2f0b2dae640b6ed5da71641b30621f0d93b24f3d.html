The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of household types \(\Theta\), with population masses \(\mu_\theta\), group \(g(\theta)\), priority \(\beta_{g(\theta)}\), and integer demand \(d_\theta\), let each of \(T\) rounds produce an independent cohort-arrival vector \(A_r\) drawn from the same finite rational distribution, with \(0\le A_{r\theta}\le\mu_\theta\). The per-capita inventory is \(b\), corresponding to \(nb\) indivisible kits in a population of \(n\) households. A causal policy assigns arriving mass \(z_{r\theta k}\) to integral bundles \(k\in\{0,\ldots,d_\theta\}\), with \(\sum_k z_{r\theta k}=A_{r\theta}\) and total use at most \(b\). For group totals \(C_i\) allocated and \(D_i\) demanded, determine the optimal worst-case \(\alpha\) under \(\mathbb E[C_i]\ge\alpha\beta_i\mathbb E[D_i]\) for every group, as a function of \(\mathcal R_\beta=b^{-1}\sum_{r,\theta}\beta_{g(\theta)}d_\theta\mathbb E[A_{r\theta}]\). In particular, does the stationary frontier equal \([1-(1-\rho/T)^T]/\rho\) at load \(\mathcal R_\beta=\rho\), and can a causal policy attain it?

The model it lives in

A finite type distribution over households with integer demands and group priorities, stochastic cohort mass arriving each round, per-capita inventory \(b\), and causal allocation of mass among integral individual bundles; maximize the worst-group expected priority-weighted fill guarantee.

The objection that survived

Nondegenerate random mass waves require shared shocks because independent household arrivals concentrate as \(n\to\infty\); the case does not establish how central such shocks are in the target applications.

fatal: False

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is for the paper’s stationary fairness frontier, Theorem 5.6. A plausible regime is a large relief agency serving millions of households in recurring rounds. Households share a small number \(\tau\) of relevant types: for example, need group, integer kit demand, priority, and arrival profile. A type completely captures everything the allocation problem uses, and its mass \(\mu_\theta\) is the fraction of the eligible population with that type. Here \(n\gg\tau\). Uncertain arrivals can be cohort waves: in each round, a random mass of each type becomes eligible. That preserves meaningful online uncertainty even when each cohort contains many households.

For a concrete mirror, call the problem Continuous Stationary Fair Rationing. Normalize the population mass to one and let \(b\) be the inventory per unit population, supplied by \(nb\) indivisible kits in an \(n\)-household realization. A type \(\theta\) has group \(g(\theta)\), priority \(\beta_{g(\theta)}\), and integer demand \(d_\theta\). In round \(r\), the observed arriving mass is \(A_{r\theta}\). The online decision \(z_{r\theta k}\geq 0\) is the mass of type-\(\theta\) households given exactly \(k\) kits, for \(k=0,\ldots,d_\theta\); thus \(\sum_k z_{r\theta k}=A_{r\theta}\). Every household still receives an integer bundle, while the mass assigned to each bundle can be fractional. Cumulative use must satisfy \(\sum_{r,\theta,k} k z_{r\theta k}\leq b\).

Let \(C_i\) be the total kits allocated to group \(i\), and \(D_i=\sum_{r,\theta:g(\theta)=i}d_\theta A_{r\theta}\) its demand. The question is to find the largest \(\alpha\) for which a causal randomized policy guarantees \(\mathbb E[C_i]\geq\alpha\beta_i\mathbb E[D_i]\) for every group. The load is \(\mathcal R_\beta=b^{-1}\sum_{r,\theta}\beta_{g(\theta)}d_\theta\mathbb E[A_{r\theta}]\). For the stationary version, the law of the arriving mass vector is identical in each of the \(T\) rounds. A solution is an online policy and a matching upper-bound family of instances.

Anchor: Theorem 5.6, proved in this paper. For fixed \(\beta\) and \(0<\rho<T\), among time-invariant instances with \(\mathcal R_\beta=\rho\) and full support, no online policy can guarantee a fairness ratio strictly above \(\bigl[1-(1-\rho/T)^T\bigr]/\rho\) on every instance; this is the exact worst-case guarantee. The matching policy guarantee is Theorem 5.4, also proved here. The continuous question is whether this same finite-horizon frontier is optimal for stationary cohort arrivals and whether a policy attains it efficiently. I consider this the lead mirror: the stationarity assumption has a natural cohort interpretation, and the paper’s random cyclic allocation has a plausible population-scale version. I expect a Class A answer for the frontier, based on cyclic symmetry and the same total-capacity upper bound. A prover should still give the policy in terms of the finite type support and account for integer bundles in finite populations.

A second, worthwhile mirror is Continuous Time-Varying Fair Rationing, the same problem with independent but nonstationary arrival laws across rounds. Anchor: Theorem 4.7, proved in this paper. It states that no online policy can guarantee FE-FR-\(\beta\) strictly greater than \(1/(1+\mathcal R_\beta)\) on all general instances. The matching guarantee is attained by Algorithm 2 in Theorem 4.5, also proved here. The continuous question is to determine the universal optimum and produce an attaining policy for the mass-arrival model. The paper’s upper-bound construction—lower-priority demand early, with a possible high-priority request late—has a direct cohort interpretation, and its budget argument appears to scale with population mass. I also expect Class A for the guarantee, though efficient computation of the calibrated policy is less clear: a continuous residual-budget distribution may have many reachable states. The prover should report complexity in \(N,\tau,T,L\), and the arrival-law support, rather than in population size \(n\).

These are numbered algorithmic results about online fairness guarantees, proved in the paper; they are not NP-hardness or standard decision-complexity results. So this is a case for a continuous online-optimization mirror, not a claim that the paper supplies the usual hardness anchor for a continuization programme.

The authors could plausibly recognize both questions: they keep the groups, priority weights, online decisions, inventory constraint, and FE-FR objective. The change is that many similar households can arrive as a type-level mass, while physical kits and individual bundles remain integral. This is a reasonable large-cohort extension of their problem, but it does change their one-request-per-slot model. The weakest point is that the paper’s probabilities describe which single request arrives, whereas my mirror uses random arriving mass. If a referee regards that as changing the problem’s central uncertainty rather than scaling its population, the case weakens. It does not rest on calling the paper’s existing arrival probabilities a continuous society: the continuous object here is the household population itself.

My scope is deliberately limited to the fairness-frontier results in Theorems 5.6 and 4.7, with their attaining guarantees in Theorems 5.4 and 4.5. I would not stretch it to the all-or-nothing separation: aggregate mass can let a policy serve a fraction of a cohort all-or-nothing, potentially changing that separation. Further questions include finite-\(n\) rounding guarantees, efficient policy computation for arbitrary time-varying mass laws, and whether the stationary frontier survives correlated cohort waves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against both anchors is that the proposed limit changes what drives the problem. In the paper, \(N\) is the number of fairness groups, \(T\) is a sequence of decision times, and at most one request arrives at each time. The uncertainty is in that request sequence; there is no population of persistent agents whose multiplicities grow. The groups already aggregate outcomes for fairness, but that does not make the society itself a distribution over agent types.

For Theorem 5.6, the cohort model is the best repair: many similar households arrive in each round, with integer bundles for individuals and fractional mass at the type level. But if households arrive independently, then as their number grows, the normalized arrival mass tends to concentrate around its expectation. The rare cohort events that make the theorem’s upper bound tight disappear. To preserve those events, one needs common shocks that bring a substantial mass of households in together. That is a plausible stochastic rationing model, but the shock process—not high multiplicity—now carries the uncertainty. The frontier question may be worthwhile, yet it is no longer a direct population limit of the paper’s model.

Theorem 4.7 faces the same choice. Its hard instance balances early lower-priority requests against a possible late request from the highest-priority group. Independent arrivals across many households tend to produce predictable masses, changing that online dilemma. Correlated cohort arrivals can preserve it, but again the construction relies on an added bulk-arrival process. Calling the masses “types” does not resolve the issue: time and the arrival law remain central to the decision problem, rather than a population distribution over agents.

This objection does not establish that the cohort model is uninteresting. The authors might well recognize recurring large-cohort rationing as their problem. The difficulty is that the most natural independent-agent limit washes out the stochastic structure, while the version that retains it adds correlated mass arrivals as a new primitive. That leaves the negative case weak against the stationary mirror in particular: RCB’s symmetry has a plausible mass-level analogue. I would argue that neither theorem cleanly supplies the programme’s intended population continuization, but I would not claim that no worthwhile mirror exists.
