What the mirror covers

The candidate mirror concerns Theorem 2's characterization of agenda-achievable outcomes; it leaves the paper's other results without computational mirrors.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a compliant positive case under your anchor rule: the paper has no numbered result that classifies a computational problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or similar. Its named theorems are structural social-choice results. For example, Theorem 1 gives the Muller–Satterthwaite dictatorship characterization, Theorem 4 proves uniqueness of the equilibrium strategy in a tournament game, and Theorem 5 gives a Condorcet-winner result under single-crossing preferences. None states a computational complexity result.

The closest candidate is Theorem 2, proved in the paper and identified there as a fundamental tournament result due to Miller (1977). It says that three sets coincide: \(Y^{*(1)}\), the alternatives achievable as the sophisticated outcome of a binary agenda containing every alternative; \(Y^{*(2)}\), the alternatives reachable from every \(x\in Y\) by a chain of pairwise majority victories; and \(Y^{*(3)}\), the smallest nonempty set \(W\subseteq Y\) such that every member of \(W\) beats every alternative outside \(W\). The paper calls this common set the top cycle. That is a strong structural bridge, but it does not classify the complexity of computing any of these sets.

If that structural result were accepted as an algorithmic lead, the most plausible continuous question would be: given a finite policy set \(Y\), a support \(T\) of strict ranking types, and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), with no pairwise majority ties, can a chair choose a binary agenda containing every alternative whose sophisticated outcome is a specified target \(y\)? A yes-instance has such an agenda. This is a credible population mirror for a large electorate with many voters but relatively few distinct rankings. Theorem 2 suggests a tractable answer: compute each pairwise majority by summing the relevant type masses, then test whether \(y\) lies in the resulting tournament’s top cycle. That polynomial-time conclusion is an inference from the theorem, not a result claimed by the paper.

The weakness is decisive for the requested format: this candidate has no qualifying computational anchor, and the mirror would not expose a hardness result that might dissolve under continuization. So it is a plausible continuous-population formulation of Theorem 2, but I would not present it as a positive case satisfying your stated anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with the anchor rule: none of the paper’s numbered theorems classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 2 is the closest candidate, but it is a structural equivalence between agenda-achievable outcomes, majority paths, and the top cycle. It gives no algorithmic or complexity result to continuize. The proponent’s proposed query—whether a target can be achieved by some agenda—is therefore a new computational framing of that theorem, not a mirror of a computational result in the paper.

That distinction does not make the proposed question nonsensical. A society with many voters sharing a relatively small set of rankings is a natural high-multiplicity regime. Given rational masses on ranking types, one can calculate the pairwise-majority tournament and ask whether the target lies in its top cycle. With a suitable convention for pairwise ties, this is well-defined. The paper’s theorem then characterizes agenda achievability. I cannot honestly defeat this formulation by arguing that mass is meaningless, that the population is inherently individuated, or that the problem continuizes only outcomes. None of those objections fits.

A stronger follow-up might ask how much mass must be shifted between ranking types to make a target agenda-achievable. That is a recognizable population intervention question, but it adds a transfer cost and an ability to change preferences that the paper does not study; its agenda-setter changes the agenda. The resulting problem may be worthwhile, but its computational core comes from the added intervention model, not from a computational result in this paper. That keeps it outside the required anchor standard; it does not show the question is intrinsically uninteresting.

So the negative case is strong under the stated rule for what counts as an anchor, but weak as a universal claim. Theorem 2 has a plausible continuous-population computational reading, and the paper gives no substantive modeling objection that rules it out. I would reject the positive case as failing the computational-anchor requirement; I would not claim, on this evidence, that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous mirror.
