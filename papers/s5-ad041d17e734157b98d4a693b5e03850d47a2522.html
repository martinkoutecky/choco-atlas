The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its named results concern incentive and fairness properties, not whether a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

For example, Theorem 1, proved here, says that every neutral mechanism satisfying elementary monotonicity is OBIC under the uniform prior. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that a mechanism is locally robust OBIC with respect to a prior and satisfies elementary monotonicity if and only if it is strategy-proof. Corollary 2 says that when \(n\ge4\), no mechanism is simultaneously locally robust OBIC, ordinally efficient, and equal treatment of equals. These are meaningful results, but none is a computational result of the kind the programme requires as an anchor. Corollary 1’s claim that every simultaneous eating algorithm is uniform-prior OBIC is likewise not an algorithmic complexity result.

The best positive intuition is a large school-allocation market: let a type be a complete ranking of the schools, and let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of applicants with ranking \(t\). With total population normalized to one and \(m\) school types, scale each school’s supply to \(1/m\). A type-level allocation \(x_{t,a}\) gives each member of type \(t\) a share of school \(a\), with \(\sum_a x_{t,a}=1\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{t,a}=1/m\). This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime when many applicants share rankings, and the paper’s random-assignment setting makes that scenario recognizable.

But turning that intuition into a continuous computational problem would require new choices: a representation of a mechanism on population distributions, a computational task such as finding or checking such a mechanism, and a justification that the finite-agent incentive results survive the limit. The paper’s \(\mu\) is a prior over the preferences of finitely many agents; it is not itself the realized population distribution. In the proposed limit, an individual has zero mass, so a report may affect their own allocation without changing the aggregate—an important change to the incentive environment. The authors might recognize this as a large-market extension, but the paper does not establish it as a mirror of one of its results.

So my honest case for a continuous mirror is limited to the plausibility of the population regime. There is no named computational anchor here, and I would not claim a tractability or hardness prediction from these theorems. The main unresolved question is whether a suitable continuum assignment model is a faithful limit of the paper’s finite-agent OBIC problem; that would need to be established before it could support the programme’s complexity questions.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is a sufficient condition for OBIC under a uniform prior; Theorem 2 characterizes when local robustness and elementary monotonicity imply strategy-proofness; Corollary 2 is an incompatibility result. None classifies an algorithmic problem, gives a running time, or identifies a computational bottleneck. The paper’s cited work on computing manipulations of probabilistic serial does not change that: it is prior work on a different question, not a result of this paper.

A large school-allocation market is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, and scaling school capacities with the population is a reasonable model adjustment. But it does not supply the missing anchor. In the paper, \(\mu\) is a prior over the rankings of finitely many agents; the society itself is not that distribution. In a continuum market with a fixed aggregate population, one agent’s report does not change the aggregate. If the mechanism assigns outcomes by report, incentives become comparisons between finitely many type-and-report allocation rows at that aggregate. If outcomes depend only on the aggregate, those incentive comparisons vanish. Giving a deviation positive mass instead models coalition manipulation, which is a different question from the paper’s individual OBIC.

That distinction also defeats the strongest readings of the named results. Theorem 1’s uniform-prior argument averages over other agents’ finite profiles. Theorem 2 uses robustness across product priors to recover restrictions at each such profile. A continuum version could ask for analogous properties of a type-level allocation rule, but it would be a new incentive characterization, not a computational mirror of these theorems. Corollary 2 could likewise motivate a continuum impossibility question, but it remains an axiomatic question.

One could add a computational problem—for example, finding a type-level assignment rule subject to incentive and fairness constraints. With a fixed finite type space, that may be expressible using finitely many allocation variables and inequalities. But this paper identifies neither that task nor a complexity bottleneck for it. Introducing one would start a new programme rather than continuize one of the paper’s results.

This is a strong rejection under ChoCo’s stated screen, though not a proof that no interesting continuum incentive theory can grow from the model. The school-market regime is credible; it simply does not turn this paper’s results into computational anchors.
