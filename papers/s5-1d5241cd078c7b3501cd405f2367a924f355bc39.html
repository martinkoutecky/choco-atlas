The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), a fixed tie-breaking order, a rational society \(\mu\) over approval types \(A\subseteq C\), and a rational budget \(b\), decide whether there are nonnegative transfers \(x_{A,B}\) for \(A\) in the support of \(\mu\) and \(B\supseteq A\), with \(\sum_{B\supseteq A}x_{A,B}=\mu_A\), such that \(\sum_{A,B}x_{A,B}|B\setminus A|\le b\) and the resulting society \(\nu_D=\sum_{A\subseteq D}x_{A,D}\) has \(R(\nu,k)\ne R(\mu,k)\), for \(R\in\{\text{GreedyCC},\text{GreedyPAV},\text{Phragmén}\}\).

The model it lives in

A society is a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over approval types. An Add attack transfers mass from \(A\) to a superset \(B\), at cost \(|B\setminus A|\) per unit mass; the committee and each rule’s sequential choices remain discrete.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Add-Robustness-Radius questions in Theorems 2, 3, and 4. It leaves the one-operation robustness results, the Remove-Robustness-Radius results, and the random-noise experiments unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does fractional mass transfer preserve NP-hardness for any of the three rules, or can fractional attacks bypass the reductions’ score and timing gaps?
  • What are the parameterized complexity and the relationship between continuous and discrete radii as functions of \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s targeted Robustness-Radius problem. Its society becomes continuous, while the committee and each rule’s greedy choices remain discrete.

Take candidates \(C\), committee size \(k\), a fixed tie-breaking order, and a society \(\mu\) supported on \(\tau\) approval types \(A\subseteq C\), where \(\mu_A\) is the rational fraction of voters with approval set \(A\). For an Add attack, choose nonnegative masses \(x_{A,B}\) for \(A\subseteq B\subseteq C\), with \(\sum_{B\supseteq A}x_{A,B}=\mu_A\). The resulting mass on type \(B\) is \(\nu_B=\sum_{A\subseteq B}x_{A,B}\), and the attack costs \(\sum_{A,B}x_{A,B}|B\setminus A|\): the mass of approval incidences changed. Given a rational budget \(b\), ask whether such a transfer of cost at most \(b\) can make \(R(\nu,k)\ne R(\mu,k)\).

This gives three specific questions, one for each hardness result:

  • \(\textsc{Cont-Add-Radius}(\text{GreedyCC})\), my lead. Theorem 2, proved in the paper, says that GreedyCC-Add-Robustness-Radius is NP-complete: given a finite approval election, \(k\), and an integer budget \(B\), deciding whether at most \(B\) additions of individual approvals can change the winning committee is NP-complete. The paper reduces from RX3C. Its set candidates encode the sets in the instance, and its construction makes the greedy choices select \(p\) rather than \(d\) exactly when the chosen sets form an exact cover.
  • \(\textsc{Cont-Add-Radius}(\text{GreedyPAV})\). Theorem 3, also proved in the paper (with its proof in Appendix A), gives the same NP-completeness result for GreedyPAV-Add-Robustness-Radius. Its RX3C construction likewise makes the early greedy selections determine whether \(p\) or \(d\) is selected.
  • \(\textsc{Cont-Add-Radius}(\text{Phragmén})\). Theorem 4, proved in the paper, says that Phragmén-Add-Robustness-Radius is NP-complete. Its reduction uses timing and budget gaps so that the selected set candidates determine whether \(p\) or \(d\) enters the committee.

For GreedyCC and GreedyPAV, the weighted rules use the paper’s score calculations with voter counts replaced by mass. For Phragmén, each approval type has a per-member balance; its aggregate balance is its mass times that balance, and purchasing a candidate resets the balances of its approving types. That keeps Phragmén’s sequential decision and the committee integral. This mirror continuizes the population, not the outcome.

A plausible regime is a large membership organization electing a \(k\)-person board from a slate of \(m\) candidates. Members may fall into recurring approval blocs, so the electorate has \(N\) members but only \(\tau\) distinct ballots, with \(\tau\ll N\). A campaign that reaches a fraction of one bloc and changes some members’ approvals is naturally measured in approval mass. In this paper’s model, approval set is a complete type: voters with the same ballot are indistinguishable for the rule and the attack. The model thus retains the paper’s targeted-attack question while representing its scale in per-capita terms. The winning committee stays discrete.

The bridge to the paper’s problem is direct but not an automatic complexity transfer. In an election with \(N\) voters, a discrete budget of \(B\) approval changes maps to continuous budget \(b=B/N\). But the continuous attacker can split mass fractionally, so a discrete no-instance need not remain a no-instance. I expect these mirrors to be hard, most plausibly Class C, rather than claiming the paper’s NP-completeness results already transfer. The reason for optimism is that the reductions’ combinatorics live in the candidate set and the greedy selection path: the large repeated voter groups set score or timing gaps, while the RX3C choice is which set candidates are selected. Those groups compress naturally into binary rational masses. The prover’s task is to show that fractional splitting cannot bypass the reduction’s gaps.

The paper’s results make this a question its authors could recognize: its robustness radius already asks how much of the approval profile must change to alter a sequential rule’s output. Replacing the count of changed approvals with the mass of changed approval incidences is a direct high-multiplicity version of that attack problem. The work here would need to establish the fractional case; the paper itself proves only the finite-election results.

My weakest point is exactly that fractional case. An operation budget in the paper is integral, and its reductions reason about at most \(n\) individual changes. A continuous attacker may distribute the corresponding mass across types and approvals. The score-gap structure suggests a route to a proof, but it is not a proof. The continuous radius may also have an infimum that is not attained near ties, so the budget-feasibility formulation is the cleanest initial question.

The scope I am claiming is the three Add-Robustness-Radius hardness results, not the paper’s one-operation robustness levels, random-noise experiments, or its comparisons with other rules. The next questions are whether the fractional versions are NP-hard, what their parameterized complexity is in \(m\) and \(\tau\), and how their optimal radii relate to the discrete radii under rounding.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the continuous attack gives the adversary a new capability: it can split a bloc and change only a fraction of its members’ ballots. The paper studies integer approval changes. A normalized mass budget therefore defines a relaxed attack problem, and an election’s discrete radius need not determine its continuous radius. A convincing hardness result would have to survive fractional transfers among approval types.

That objection applies to Theorem 2 for GreedyCC, Theorem 3 for GreedyPAV, and Theorem 4 for Phragmén. In each case, the RX3C reduction controls the greedy path using integer changes, so its proof does not establish hardness for the continuous version. For Phragmén, the mass model also needs a careful definition of how type-level balances aggregate and reset. These are real proof obligations.

They do not defeat the mirrors. The paper’s rules depend on approval sets and aggregate voter counts, so an approval set is a complete type. A large membership organization with recurring ballot blocs supplies a plausible population regime; changing a fraction of a bloc is a recognizable targeted campaign. A normalized mass-transfer radius preserves the paper’s question about how much targeted change can alter the committee, while keeping the committee and sequential choices discrete. The mass formulation also works for all three rules, with a separate fractional-complexity question for each.

The honest negative case is weak. The three anchors are well-posed computational questions whose answers are unknown from the paper’s discrete results. Fractional attacks may make them easier or harder; either outcome would be informative. I cannot defend the claim that no scenario makes these mirrors worth studying.
