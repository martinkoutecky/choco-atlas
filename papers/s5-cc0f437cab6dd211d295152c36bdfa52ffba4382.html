The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), committee size \(k\), and a finite support \(\mathcal T\subseteq 2^C\) of \(\tau\) approval types with positive rational masses \(\mu_A\) summing to \(1\), compute an integral committee \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|=k\) such that, for every nonempty \(T\subseteq C\) and \(1\le \ell\le \min(k,|T|)\), \(\sum_{A\in\mathcal T:\,|A\cap T|\ge \ell>|A\cap W|}\mu_A\le |T|/(k+1)\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution over approval types \(A\), each with mass \(\mu_A\); every agent of type \(A\) approves exactly \(A\). The output remains an integral size-\(k\) committee \(W\). For each witness set \(T\) and level \(\ell\), the mass of types with \(|A\cap T|\ge\ell>|A\cap W|\) must be at most the normalized Droop quota \(|T|/(k+1)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Droop-FJR guarantee of Theorem 5.10 and, since Droop-FJR implies Hare-FJR, the Hare guarantee of Theorem 5.8. It leaves secondary welfare optimization and the paper’s other representation concepts aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a type-level version of the descending-budget algorithm with mass-weighted, capped charging construct such a committee in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the mass bit length?
  • Can the weighted historical-payment and row-column arguments be proved with polynomial bit complexity, and what is the complexity of verifying a supplied committee?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with Theorem 5.10, proved in this paper. It says that for every explicitly represented approval election with \(n\) voters and committee size \(k\), the descending-budget algorithm run at candidate price \(n/(k+1)\) terminates and returns a committee \(W\) of size exactly \(k\) satisfying Droop-FJR: for every \(S,T,\ell\), if \(|S|>n|T|/(k+1)\) and every voter in \(S\) approves at least \(\ell\) candidates in \(T\), then some voter in \(S\) approves at least \(\ell\) candidates in \(W\). Section 4 gives the algorithm’s running time as \(O(kmn)\) rational operations, with polynomial bit complexity. The paper’s coNP-completeness statement about verifying FJR is cited prior work, not a numbered result proved here, so I would not use it as an anchor.

The continuous mirror I would ask a prover to settle is Continuous Droop-FJR Committee Construction. An instance is a candidate set \(C\), a committee size \(k\), and a finite support \(\mathcal T\subseteq 2^C\) of \(\tau\) approval types, with positive rational masses \(\mu_A\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(A\) is a complete description of an agent for this problem: it specifies exactly which candidates that agent approves. The input gives the types and masses in binary. A solution is an integral committee \(W\subseteq C\) with \(|W|=k\).

For each nonempty \(T\subseteq C\) and \(1\le \ell\le \min(k,|T|)\), define
\[ \beta_{\ell,T}(W) = \sum_{\substack{A\in\mathcal T:\\ |A\cap T|\ge\ell>|A\cap W|}}\mu_A. \]
The committee is valid exactly when \(\beta_{\ell,T}(W)\le |T|/(k+1)\) for every such \(T,\ell\). This is the continuous Droop-FJR condition: the types in the sum are precisely those whose agents could belong to a violating coalition. A coalition can take any submass of those types, so a violating coalition of mass strictly greater than \(|T|/(k+1)\) exists exactly when \(\beta_{\ell,T}(W)\) exceeds that threshold. The requested output is just a fair committee; the source result does not optimize a welfare objective.

The natural regime is a large approval election with many repeated ballots: for example, a large association or federation election with millions of voters but far fewer distinct approval sets. Here \(n\gg\tau\), and all agents sharing an approval set are indistinguishable under the paper’s model. The quota is naturally stated as a population share. For rational masses, the mirror also has an exact high-multiplicity dictionary: replicate the types to a common-denominator electorate, and the continuous condition agrees with Droop-FJR on that replicated profile. That denominator can be enormous relative to the binary input, which is why running the paper’s explicit-voter algorithm after expansion does not settle the compressed-input problem.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. A credible route is to normalize the paper’s price to \(1/(k+1)\), keep one per-agent balance \(r_A\) for each approval type, and compute each active type’s offer as \(r_A/(h-|A\cap P|)\) at threshold \(h\). Candidate bids are then mass-weighted sums, \(\sum_A\mu_A r_A/(h-|A\cap P|)\). When a candidate is affordable, process active approver types in a fixed order, charging each type up to its offer, with a partial per-agent charge for the boundary type so the total mass payment is exactly \(1/(k+1)\). This keeps each type’s agents together in one budget state. The charge cap and exact-price payment are the ingredients used by the paper’s historical-payment invariant and row-column accounting proof; those arguments appear to carry over with mass-weighted sums. The resulting computation should depend on \(m,\tau\), and the mass bit length, rather than on the common-denominator electorate size. Theorem 5.10 also covers the paper’s stronger guarantee here; satisfying this Droop mirror implies the corresponding Hare mirror.

This is a population mirror, not an outcome-space or lottery mirror: the committee remains a size-\(k\) set of candidates. It covers the paper’s Droop-FJR construction and, through the implication above, its Hare-FJR guarantee. Natural next questions include the complexity of verifying a supplied committee from binary masses and whether one can optimize welfare among continuous Droop-FJR committees.

My weakest point is that Theorem 5.10 itself only establishes the guarantee for an explicitly listed electorate, and its charging procedure uses a voter order. It does not prove the compressed, type-level running time I expect. The type-ordered partial charging rule is a proposed bridge, not a theorem in the paper; its exact bit-complexity bound and weighted proof would need to be established.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I can’t make a convincing case against the proponent’s anchor. Theorem 5.10 is a named constructive result with a polynomial running time, and its natural population mirror is well posed: represent voters by approval types \(A\) with rational masses \(\mu_A\), while keeping the committee integral. For each \(T,\ell\), the mass of eligible but underrepresented voters is
\[ \beta_{\ell,T}(W)= \sum_{A:\,|A\cap T|\ge\ell>|A\cap W|}\mu_A. \]
A violating coalition exists exactly when this mass exceeds \(|T|/(k+1)\). The individual-witness requirement therefore does not become vacuous: a violating coalition must consist entirely of underrepresented voters.

The proposed type-level algorithm also looks like a sound bridge, not a modeling sleight of hand. Track one per-person balance for each type, use offers \(r_A/(h-|A\cap P|)\), and weight each offer by \(\mu_A\) in candidate bids. A type can be charged uniformly per person, including a partial charge for the boundary type. That preserves the cap and the accounting invariants while making the computation depend on \(m,\tau\), and the mass bit length rather than on an expanded electorate. Large elections with many repeated approval ballots give a plausible high-multiplicity regime.

The best negative point is that this may be a modest extension: the paper’s budget argument appears to aggregate directly, without needing the continuous-optimization machinery that motivates part of ChoCo. But that predicts an easy answer; it does not defeat a well-posed computational mirror. The voter-order dependence also does not block the mirror, since the guarantee is order-independent. I therefore don’t have an honest universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists for this paper; this anchor survives.
