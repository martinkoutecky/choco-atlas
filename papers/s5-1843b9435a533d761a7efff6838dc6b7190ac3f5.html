The continuous mirror question

Given \(d\) good classes with per-unit-population supplies \(q_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), and \(\tau\) types \(t\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), where type \(t\) has nonnegative additive values \(v_{tj}\) and divisibility set \(D_t\), define \(M_t=\sup_P \operatorname*{ess\,inf}_{b\sim P} v_t\cdot b\), over distributions \(P\) on valid type-\(t\) bundles satisfying \(\mathbb{E}_P[b_j]\le q_j\) for each \(j\). Given the exact \(M_t\), compute a finite-support allocation with rational bundle masses \(x_{t,b}\): for \(j\notin D_t\), \(b_j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\), and for \(j\in D_t\), \(b_j\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\); require \(\sum_b x_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}x_{t,b}b_j\le q_j\), and \(v_t\cdot b\ge 3M_t/5\) whenever \(x_{t,b}>0\). Is such an allocation computable in time polynomial in \(d,\tau\), and the binary input length?

The model it lives in

A unit population split across \(\tau\) complete types, with standardized goods supplied at rates \(q_j\) per unit population. Variables \(x_{t,b}\) assign type-\(t\) mass to individual bundles; coordinates for goods that type \(t\) views as indivisible are integers, while divisible-good coordinates may be fractional. Aggregate use is bounded by supply, and each assigned bundle must reach \(3/5\) of its type's continuous MMS \(M_t\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's constructive \(3/5\)-MMS guarantee. It leaves the paper's \(2/3\) discussion for small \(n\), its algorithm-specific failure example, and the computation of MMS values outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the allocation be computed in time polynomial in \(d,\tau\), and the binary input length without expanding to a common-denominator finite population?
  • Can the finite algorithm's structural steps yield a compressed algorithm, or does pricing over integer bundle coordinates give a hardness result?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead, and only named anchor, is Theorem 1 (proved in this paper): in every subjective-divisibility instance, there is an allocation giving every agent at least \(3/5\) of their MMS. The paper proves it with an allocation algorithm, described as polynomial time when the agents’ MMS values are known. The theorem statement itself is an existence guarantee; the runtime claim is in the surrounding text. I do not see a numbered hardness result to anchor on. The paper’s statement that computing MMS is NP-hard is unnumbered, so I would not present it as one.

The mirror I would ask the authors to consider is \(\textsc{Cont-SD-3/5}\). A unit mass of agents is divided among \(\tau\) types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Type \(t\) includes the full additive valuation vector \(v_t\) and the set \(D_t\) of goods it views as divisible. There are \(d\) standardized good classes, with rational supply \(q_j\) per unit of population. In a large cohort of \(N\) agents, this means \(Nq_j\) copies of class \(j\): supplies scale with the population.

An individual bundle for type \(t\) is a vector \(b\) whose coordinate \(b_j\) is a nonnegative integer when \(j\notin D_t\), and a nonnegative real when \(j\in D_t\). Thus agents still receive whole copies of goods they view as indivisible. Define that type’s continuous MMS, \(M_t\), as the greatest \(r\) for which a unit mass of hypothetical type-\(t\) agents can each receive a bundle worth at least \(r\), using at most supply \(q\) in aggregate. Equivalently, \(M_t=\sup_P \operatorname{ess\,inf}_{b\sim P}\sum_j v_{tj}b_j\), over distributions \(P\) on type-\(t\) bundles satisfying \(\mathbb E_P[b_j]\le q_j\) for every \(j\).

The problem is: given this rational instance and the exact \(M_t\) values, output a finite list of bundles and masses for each type, such that the listed masses sum to \(\mu_t\), total use of each good class is at most \(q_j\), and every bundle assigned positive mass to type \(t\) has value at least \(3M_t/5\). The mass on a listed bundle represents that fraction of agents each receiving the whole bundle. The algorithmic target is time polynomial in \(d,\tau\), and the binary input length. Supplying exact MMS values matches the paper’s stated condition for its polynomial-time algorithm; computing them from the compressed input would be a further question.

The regime is a large recurring cohort—say, a public service distributing standardized goods to many residents—with a relatively small number of complete preference-and-divisibility types. A type is not just a ranking or a valuation: it includes every feature relevant to the allocation, including which goods its members can use fractionally. The goods are repeated copies of standardized classes, and their supply grows with the cohort. This keeps the paper’s central issue—agents may disagree about whether a good can be split—while making the population, rather than an outcome space or a noise model, the continuous object.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity extension of Theorem 1. For rational type masses and supplies, finite blow-ups give the natural dictionary: choose \(N\) so the type counts \(N\mu_t\) and copy counts \(Nq_j\) are integral, then group identical agents and their bundles by type. With finite-support rational bundle distributions, the continuous MMS benchmark is also realizable in a sufficiently large such blow-up. The paper’s \(3/5\) guarantee therefore gives a strong reason to expect the same guarantee in this model. The straightforward expansion can be enormous when denominators are binary encoded; finding a compressed witness is the computational question, not something the paper’s finite-input runtime settles.

My provisional expectation is Class A, using a configuration LP whose variables represent mass assigned to acceptable bundles, with a type-level counterpart to the paper’s structural allocation procedure. But the bottleneck is real: pricing acceptable bundles with integer coordinates for indivisible goods has a knapsack-like form. A polynomial method for that step is what would make the Class A case hold; without one, the unrestricted compressed problem could land in Class C.

The mirror covers only the constructive \(3/5\)-MMS guarantee of Theorem 1. It does not try to continuize every algorithmic step, the discussion of \(2/3\), or MMS computation. Its weakest point is the supply scaling: the paper’s stated instances have a fixed finite set of goods, while this mirror repeats good classes as the population grows. That is a meaningful co-modification for a high-multiplicity allocation regime, but it changes which MMS partitions are available. The authors would need to accept that repeated-copy regime as their problem’s high-multiplicity analogue; the mirror preserves their subjective divisibility and per-agent bundle constraints once that regime is fixed.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the one serious anchor, and it is a genuine constructive result: the paper gives an algorithm for finding a \(3/5\)-MMS allocation when the MMS values are known. So “there is no computational result to mirror” does not work.

The strongest objection is that the theorem’s stated model has a fixed stock of \(m\) goods. If \(n\) grows while that stock stays fixed, the guarantee loses content: at most \(m\) MMS shares can contain a good an agent views as indivisible. The other \(n-m\) shares draw only on goods that agent views as divisible, so
\[ \mathrm{MMS}_i \le \frac{\sum_{j\in D_i}v_i(j)}{n-m}. \]
That tends to zero; with no goods divisible to the agent, the MMS is zero once \(n>m\). A literal population limit therefore makes the \(3/5\) guarantee vacuous.

But the proposed supply-scaled model repairs precisely that problem, and the programme explicitly allows such co-modifications. Repeated good classes, rational type masses, and type-level divisibility give a coherent high-multiplicity allocation problem. Suitable finite blow-ups connect it to the paper’s model. The paper’s algorithm runs on the expanded instance, however, so it does not settle whether one can find a compressed allocation in time polynomial in the binary input length. That compression question is real work, not a reason to reject the mirror. The fact that pricing may be knapsack-like could make it hard, but that would still be a worthwhile complexity result.

So the defensible negative case is conditional: without per-capita supply growth, the anchor degenerates; with it, the mirror depends on the authors accepting a recurring, replenished-resource setting. The proponent supplies a plausible such setting, and the model preserves the core issue of subjective divisibility. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror survives in any scenario.
