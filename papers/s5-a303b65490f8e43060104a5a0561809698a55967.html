The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(O\), ranking types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita capacities \(q_j\), and a feasible type-symmetric probabilistic assignment \(p_{tj}\), determine whether the target masses \(\mu_t p_{tj}\) have an exact finite lottery representation by ex-post Pareto-efficient assignments of an atomless population. If so, return such a lottery maximizing the minimum total mass assigned a school among outcomes with positive weight; otherwise, certify that no such lottery exists.

The model it lives in

An atomless applicant population is given by its supported complete ranking types \(t\) and rational masses \(\mu_t\). Schools have capacities \(q_j\) per capita, and each member of type \(t\) has target assignment probabilities \(p_{tj}\). A lottery outcome assigns each individual at most one indivisible seat, represented in aggregate by type-school masses \(y_{tj}\); each outcome respects capacities and admits no feasible reassignment that weakly improves everyone and strictly improves a positive mass. The lottery must satisfy \(\sum_r \lambda_r y^r_{tj}=\mu_t p_{tj}\) and maximize \(\min_{r:\lambda_r>0}\sum_{t,j}y^r_{tj}\).

The objection that survived

For familiar large-market targets, PS is already ordinally efficient and RSD approaches PS, so the practical demand for decomposing arbitrary type-symmetric targets remains unestablished.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3's NP-hardness result for MD-SD(X). It leaves Theorems 1, 2, 4, and 5, the column-generation algorithms, and the computational experiments outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of exact feasibility and maximin optimization as a function of \(m=|O|\), support size \(\tau=|T|\), and bit length \(L\)?
  • Can ex-post-efficient mass assignments be represented or optimized over with a tractable pricing or separation procedure?
  • Does the continuous problem remain hard for rational, type-symmetric assignment targets in a high-multiplicity school-choice regime?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make the mirror in a large school district where many applicants submit the same ranking template. For instance, a cohort of \(100{,}000\) applicants might have a few hundred distinct preference lists. A type is a complete ranking of the schools and the outside option; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the cohort with ranking \(t\). The repeated, type-identical applicants are the high-multiplicity regime. School capacities are expressed relative to cohort size. This is a scenario, not a claim that every school-choice market has few preference types.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that MD-SD(\(X\)) is NP-hard in general: given a one-sided matching instance and a feasible probabilistic assignment \(X\), find a decomposition of \(X\) into ex-post Pareto-efficient matchings that maximizes the minimum number of assigned agents across the matchings in the decomposition. The paper’s proof reduces from the cited result of Aziz et al. (2015), which establishes NP-completeness of deciding whether an arbitrary probabilistic assignment can be implemented over ex-post-efficient matchings, even with unit-capacity objects. Thus Theorem 3 is the paper’s result; the decision hardness it invokes is prior work.

Here is the continuous question I would hand to a prover, which I’ll call Continuum MD-SD. The input is a finite set of schools \(O\), a finite list \(T\) of possible rankings over \(O\cup\{\varnothing\}\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita school capacities \(q_j\). It also includes a type-symmetric probabilistic assignment \(p_{tj}\): each member of type \(t\) has probability \(p_{tj}\) of receiving school \(j\), with
\[ \sum_{j\in O}p_{tj}\leq 1 \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_{tj}\leq q_j. \]
The target expected mass assigned to school \(j\) from type \(t\) is \(\mu_t p_{tj}\).

A realized outcome is a measurable assignment of the atomless population to schools or the outside option. Write \(y_{tj}\) for the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(j\). Each actual applicant still receives at most one indivisible seat; fractional \(y_{tj}\) means that a fraction of the type’s mass receives that seat. Require each outcome to respect the school capacities and be ex-post Pareto-efficient: there must be no feasible reassignment that weakly improves every individual and strictly improves a positive mass of them.

The question is: either certify that no exact lottery over such outcomes exists, or return a finite lottery \((\lambda_r,y^r)_{r=1}^R\) satisfying
\[ \lambda_r\geq 0,\qquad \sum_r\lambda_r=1,\qquad \sum_r\lambda_r y^r_{tj}=\mu_t p_{tj}, \]
for every type and school, with every \(y^r\) ex-post efficient. Among these lotteries, maximize
\[ \min_{r:\lambda_r>0}\ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{j\in O} y^r_{tj}, \]
the worst-case mass receiving a school seat. This keeps the paper’s exact-decomposition and worst-case objective, while making the population itself the continuous object. The probabilistic assignment remains part of the input; its fractional entries are not the continuization.

I would ask first whether this is Class A, with complexity stated in \(m=|O|\), support size \(\tau=|T|\), and input bit length \(L\). The mass variables make each realized allocation a type-by-school flow, suggesting an LP or column-generation approach over efficient mass allocations. Whether those allocations admit a tractable pricing or separation procedure is the central question, not something this argument assumes. If the unrestricted problem resists that approach, continuum-specific hardness is a live possibility. Theorem 3’s discrete hardness does not by itself establish hardness transfer: in the mirror, mass within a preference type can split across schools in each realized outcome.

This case covers Theorem 3 only. Its weakest point is precisely that split: it is the natural atomless interpretation of a large population, but it permits type-level outcomes unavailable to a finite cohort of indivisible applicants. Also, the paper does not show that Theorem 3’s hard instances have many repeated types or type-symmetric probabilistic assignments. The mirror is a credible question for a high-multiplicity, anonymous school-choice regime, but the theorem’s hardness is a motivation for asking it, not evidence that the continuous version will be hard—or tractable.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3 is a genuine computational result, so “the paper has nothing to mirror” is not a viable objection. The strongest case against this anchor is that its hardness concerns a finer input than the proposed high-multiplicity model naturally preserves. In the paper, \(X\) has a separate row for every named applicant, and an exact decomposition must preserve every row. The hardness result is for arbitrary \(X\); the paper does not show that it persists when applicants with the same complete type have identical assignment probabilities.

For school choice, applicants with the same ranking can be grouped only if they are indistinguishable for the whole problem. If their rows of \(X\) differ, their assignment targets distinguish them, so preserving the paper’s exactness requires splitting the type by those targets. In the extreme, that can leave nearly one type per applicant. If instead the mirror uses the natural anonymous case—identical rankings also have identical target probabilities—the stated hardness result gives no evidence about its complexity. The proposed equations also preserve type-average marginals; the paper preserves each applicant’s marginals. One can repair that difference by randomizing which members of a type receive each assignment, but the repair does not restore the missing hardness evidence.

The better-motivated targets are outputs of familiar mechanisms, rather than arbitrary assignment matrices. For PS, the paper establishes ordinal efficiency. In an atomless population, its type-level assignment can be realized by assigning the corresponding fractions of each type to each school. Any positive-mass Pareto improvement would then be an ordinal improvement of the fractional assignment, so this realization is already ex-post efficient. The decomposition problem has a one-outcome solution. In standard large-market regimes, the paper also notes the asymptotic equivalence of RSD and PS, which points toward the same simplification for the most natural large-school-district setting.

That does not rule out a worthwhile mirror for arbitrary type-symmetric targets, or for a different school-choice regime where those targets are genuinely relevant. Such a question is well-posed, and its answer could be interesting. But it is a new computational problem whose connection to Theorem 3’s hardness is unestablished; the natural mechanism-generated cases appear to bypass the decomposition bottleneck. So the case against is plausible on relevance and input mismatch, not decisive on whether *any* continuous mirror is worth studying.
