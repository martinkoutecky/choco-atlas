The continuous mirror question

Given supported buyer types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), resource classes \(A\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_a\), and the paper's integer valuation and price-bound data, find a constrained Walrasian equilibrium. Output integer prices \(p\), rationing-state masses \(\lambda_{t,S}\) for \(S\subseteq A\), and assignment masses \(x_{t,S,a}\) for \(a\in A\cup\{o\}\). Require \(\sum_S\lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_a x_{t,S,a}=\lambda_{t,S}\), and \(x_{t,S,a}>0\) only if \(a\in D_t(p,S)=\arg\max_{b\in S\cup\{o\}}(u_t(b)-p_b)\). For each real resource \(a\), require \(\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S,a}\le q_a\), with equality whenever \(p_a>\underline p_a\). Whenever \(\lambda_{t,S}>0\) and \(a\notin S\), require \(p_a=\overline p_a\), positive total assignment mass to \(a\), and \(a\in D_t(p,S\cup\{a\})\).

The model it lives in

A typed population with rational mass \(\mu_t\) per valuation type and per-capita resource stocks \(q_a\). Shared integer prices and rationing states govern individual demand; \(\lambda_{t,S}\) splits each type across rationing states and \(x_{t,S,a}\) records aggregate assignment mass, while each infinitesimal agent still receives at most one indivisible resource.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2 and MAPR's equilibrium-allocation computation; it leaves expected profits, expected prices, Theorem 3's two-buyer strategy result, and the manipulation conjecture aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can weighted Hall conditions and capacitated flows replace the paper's cardinality arguments while preserving the price-update and rationing process?
  • Can the resulting equilibrium have a compact exact rational representation and be found with complexity polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and price span \(B\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: Theorem 2 is the paper’s best named computational anchor, and it is proved here. It says that MAPR’s output \(\langle p^T,R^T,\pi^{M^T}\rangle\) is a constrained Walrasian equilibrium: prices obey their bounds; each buyer receives an item in her constrained demand; every item priced above its lower bound is sold; any rationed item is at its upper bound and is sold; and restoring a rationed item to a buyer’s permitted set makes that item demanded.

There is a qualification on the anchor. Theorem 2 states correctness, not a complexity class. The paper’s surrounding analysis says MAPR terminates in time polynomial in the number of buyers, the number of items, and \(B=\sum_a(\overline p_a-\underline p_a)\). So the named theorem and the accompanying runtime argument together support a polynomial algorithm in those parameters, but no numbered result literally asserts that the problem is in P. Theorem 3, about truthful reporting with two buyers, is not a complexity result.

A plausible mirror is Mass-Constrained-Walrasian-Equilibrium. Let \(A\) be resource classes, with dummy item \(o\), and \(T\) the supported buyer types. Type \(t\) is the buyer’s complete valuation vector \(u_t:A\cup\{o\}\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\); buyers of that type share all problem-relevant attributes. The input gives rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, rational per-capita stocks \(q_a\) for each resource class, and the paper’s integer lower and upper price bounds. The question is to find any equilibrium, not to maximize welfare or revenue.

A solution gives prices \(p\), and divides each type’s mass among rationing states \(S\subseteq A\), with mass \(\lambda_{t,S}\). It also gives assignment masses \(x_{t,S,a}\), including \(a=o\). Define
\[ D_t(p,S)=\arg\max_{a\in S\cup\{o\}}\bigl(u_t(a)-p_a\bigr), \]
with \(o\) always available. Require \(\sum_S\lambda_{t,S}=\mu_t\); each cohort’s assignment masses sum to \(\lambda_{t,S}\); and \(x_{t,S,a}>0\) only when \(a\in D_t(p,S)\). For each real resource \(a\), total assigned mass is at most \(q_a\), and equals \(q_a\) if \(p_a>\underline p_a\). Finally, if \(\lambda_{t,S}>0\) and \(a\notin S\), require \(p_a=\overline p_a\), positive mass assigned to \(a\), and \(a\in D_t(p,S\cup\{a\})\). These conditions retain the paper’s rationing and constrained-demand logic. Each infinitesimal buyer still receives one indivisible resource; only the aggregate assignment is fractional.

The intended regime is a large housing or resource market with many applicants but relatively few valuation types: for example, \(N\) in the hundreds of thousands or millions, but \(\tau=|T|\) in the tens or hundreds. Resource classes can be standardized apartment categories, each with many identical units, and their stocks scale with population. That stock scaling is a co-modification needed for a meaningful population limit. A finite market with \(n_t\) buyers of each type and \(Q_a\) units in each class maps to \(\mu_t=n_t/N\) and \(q_a=Q_a/N\); rational continuous inputs can conversely be scaled to an integer market by clearing denominators. This is a credible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s price-rigid allocation problem, not continuity of outcomes or of lottery probabilities.

I would expect the bounded-price-span version to be Class A. For a fixed price and rationing state, the assignment question becomes a capacitated flow problem: type masses connect to demanded resource classes, which have capacities \(q_a\). Weighted Hall violations should replace the paper’s over-demanded sets, and min-cut can expose them; the paper’s repeated price updates then have a natural mass-based analogue. The target complexity would be polynomial in \(m=|A|\), \(\tau\), input bit length \(L\), and \(B\). The question for a prover is whether that flow-based replacement yields a compact exact equilibrium and an algorithm with the corresponding bound. This mirror covers Theorem 2 and MAPR’s allocation computation, not the expected-profit or strategic-manipulation discussion.

The weakest point is that the transfer is not already proved by the paper. Its runtime depends on the *numeric* price span \(B\), so binary-encoded bounds may leave only a pseudo-polynomial guarantee; a bit-polynomial algorithm would need more. The mass version also has to show that rationing and lottery outcomes admit a compact rational representation. And if the anchor rule requires a numbered theorem that explicitly states a complexity classification, this paper does not provide one: Theorem 2 is the nearest usable named result, paired with unnumbered runtime analysis.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a thinner computational anchor than the proponent’s framing suggests. Its numbered claim is that MAPR outputs a constrained Walrasian equilibrium; the runtime bound is argued in the surrounding prose, and depends on the *numeric* price span \(B=\sum_a(\overline p_a-\underline p_a)\). So the paper does not establish a bit-polynomial complexity classification that would transfer directly to rational, support-compressed inputs. That is a real limitation on the proposed Class A story.

The more serious gap is between MAPR and the suggested mass algorithm. The paper’s proof works with ordinary matchings and cardinality inequalities: its over-demand test counts buyers against items, and each lottery adds one buyer–item edge. A mass version would need weighted Hall conditions and capacitated flows. Those look suitable for checking assignments at fixed prices, but they do not by themselves show how to batch the lotteries while preserving each cohort’s changing rationing state, or prove that the resulting process terminates with a compact exact equilibrium. The paper’s expected outcomes are defined by a tree of individual lottery histories; collapsing those histories into mass flows is a new algorithmic step.

That weakens the claim that Theorem 2 already supplies a natural, nearly complete continuous algorithm. It does not defeat the best mirror, though. With many applicants sharing a small number of valuation types and standardized resource classes whose stocks scale with population, an aggregate constrained-equilibrium problem is coherent. Fractional assignment masses can represent indivisible items allocated across a large population, and the paper’s equilibrium conditions have plausible mass counterparts. The necessary co-modification is substantial, but it is one the authors could recognize as their problem.

So the strongest case against is that the paper anchors a *question*, not the proponent’s suggested answer: the named theorem is a correctness result, its runtime bound is numeric-span dependent, and the flow-based MAPR extension remains unproved. I cannot honestly turn those points into a universal case that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The proposed mass equilibrium survives them; the negative case is weak on the central question.
