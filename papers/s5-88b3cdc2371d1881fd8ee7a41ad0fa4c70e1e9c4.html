The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered computational result of the required kind. Its Proposition 1 (proved here) bounds firms’ profits in the salary competition model; Proposition 2 (proved here) says that changing output from \(f(h)\) to \(\tilde f(h)\) leaves equilibrium assignments unchanged, shifts residents’ payoffs by the output difference, and leaves program payoffs unchanged. Both are economic results, not complexity classifications. Section 6.3 describes a stable-match simulation, and footnote 29 gives its running time, but that is not a numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition. So there is no qualifying anchor to restate, and no anchor-backed continuous question.

The strongest positive lead I can offer is an unanchored continuization of the stable-match computation the paper uses. Call it *Resident-Proposing Stable Mass Matching*. Let \(\mathcal R\) and \(\mathcal P\) be finite sets of resident and program types. A resident type \(r\) has mass \(\mu_r\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) and a strict ranking of program types and being unmatched. A program type \(p\) has \(\nu_p\in\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) identical programs per unit of resident population, each with capacity \(q_p\), and a strict priority ranking of resident types. Types are complete for this matching problem: members of a type share the same preferences, priorities, and capacity. The input includes these rankings and masses; \(\sum_r\mu_r=1\). The task is to return the resident-proposing stable allocation \(x_{rp}\), where \(x_{rp}\) is the mass of residents of type \(r\) assigned to programs of type \(p\), with unmatched mass reported separately. Feasibility requires \(\sum_p x_{rp}\le\mu_r\) and \(\sum_r x_{rp}\le q_p\nu_p\). Stability means no positive mass of residents would prefer a program that has spare capacity or could replace a positive mass of lower-priority residents.

I would expect this problem to be tractable in \(\tau_R=|\mathcal R|\), \(\tau_P=|\mathcal P|\), and input bit length \(L\): a type-level deferred-acceptance procedure processes mass proposals and rejections, with each resident type proposing to each program type at most once. That is Class A, but it is not a claim that the paper proves, and discrete stable matching is already tractable. The useful further questions are whether the mass allocation agrees exactly with the normalized outcome in sufficiently large replicated finite markets, and how the allocation changes when rural program capacity is added or urban capacity is reduced.

The natural regime would be large residency or comparable labor-market cohorts in which many agents genuinely share complete preference and priority profiles, while the number of such profiles stays well below the number of agents. Agarwal reports about 3,000 family-medicine residents and 430 programs in a year, which makes a large matching market plausible. But the paper does not report the number of exact types: its resident utilities include heterogeneous tastes, and program priorities depend on a human-capital index. Grouping agents with different rankings or priorities would change the problem. The mirror therefore covers only the stable-assignment computation in Sections 3 and 6, and its high-multiplicity regime needs evidence. That type-support issue, together with the absence of a named computational anchor, is the case’s weakest point.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no named computational result here to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 establish economic claims about equilibrium payoffs and assignments. Footnote 29 gives a running time for the stable-match simulation, but the paper’s main contribution is preference estimation and its counterfactual findings, not a complexity result about matching. On the criterion you gave, that is decisive against an anchored mirror.

The proposed mass-matching task is the strongest salvage, but its finite-type version does not preserve the paper’s model. Program preferences depend on a continuous human-capital index, while residents’ rankings arise from heterogeneous tastes over particular programs. A complete type must preserve both. Under the paper’s distributions, exact types will generally have zero mass; grouping residents into finite ranking and skill bins changes the vertical priorities and can change the within-program variation that the paper uses for identification. A faithful version would instead take a measure over residents’ preferences and skill and compute a stable allocation and its policy effects. Continuum stable-matching models with deferred-acceptance analogues already provide close machinery for that task, though that prior work alone does not make a well-posed mirror unworthy under your rules. [Arnosti, *A Continuum Model of Stable Matching with Finite Capacities*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12881)

The paper’s large-market setup does make a continuum population plausible: it explicitly studies markets growing from draws from population distributions. That supports the regime rather than defeating it. But a complexity question about computing mass allocations or optimizing rural capacity changes would still need a new finite input representation and a new computational objective. The paper estimates preferences from finite matching data and evaluates selected policies; it does not optimize policy choices or classify the complexity of either task.

So the strongest negative case is that this paper offers no qualifying computational anchor, and its stable-matching routine is only an ingredient in the empirical analysis. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile ChoCo project built from a continuum version of its market. Large residency cohorts are a credible setting, and policy optimization over mass distributions could be valuable. That would be a new computational problem inspired by the paper, rather than a continuous mirror of one of its computational results.
