The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result that meets the required anchor rule. Theorem 1, proved here, says that an envy-free division exists when \(n\) is prime or \(4\), provided every player’s preferences are closed and satisfy the full division assumption. Theorem 2 and Proposition 1 are also existence results: they prove Sperner-type labeling statements, not complexity claims or algorithms. The authors say their proof does not directly give an algorithm.

There is an unnumbered computational claim in the paper: the envy-free division problem without the hungry-players assumption is PPAD-hard, by comparison with the classical problem. The paper also says membership in PPAD is not established. That is relevant context, but it is not a numbered result of the kind the prompt requires, and it concerns the finite-player problem.

The strongest positive evidence is that Theorem 1 applies even when players have different preferences, and its prime-\(n\) case allows a finite high-multiplicity regime: for example, many attendees with only a few complete preference-function types. But that gives a family of finite instances, not a continuous-population problem. A direct limit is problematic: the paper assigns each player a connected piece of one cake, while a continuum of players cannot each receive a positive-length, pairwise disjoint interval on a finite cake. Giving each type a shared piece or a fractional allocation would change the assignment or preference model.

So I cannot write a continuous problem per eligible anchor without inventing a result the paper does not contain. The positive case is limited to a plausible high-multiplicity *finite* regime around Theorem 1; it does not establish a continuous mirror of the paper’s computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about one limitation: none of the numbered results is a complexity theorem, and Theorem 1 itself is an existence result. But that does not settle whether the paper has a worthwhile computational mirror. Its “Algorithmic aspects” section explicitly identifies the search problem—given polynomial-time preference functions, find an envy-free division—and discusses its PPAD status. Theorem 1 supplies a new existence guarantee for that problem under the no-hunger relaxation. A computational question built around that guarantee is grounded in the paper, even if it is not a numbered complexity result.

A plausible mirror takes a finite-support society of preference types: each type \(t\) specifies a complete preference function, and has rational mass \(\mu_t\). In a continuum population, the literal requirement that every person receive a distinct positive-length interval cannot survive: a finite cake cannot be partitioned into uncountably many such intervals. But that is a reason to co-modify the allocation model, not to rule out the population mirror. Use per-capita cake supply and a type-level allocation or distribution over integral bundles, with feasibility weighted by \(\mu_t\). Then ask whether every type can be assigned only bundles it accepts, while no type prefers another type’s allocation; retain an analogue of the full-division condition to handle types that accept nothing in some situations.

That gives a concrete computational question: for rational type masses and succinct preference descriptions, can one find such an envy-free allocation, and what are its exact or approximate complexity bounds in the support size and input length? The paper’s proof does not answer it: the proof is not algorithmic, and the finite-player PPAD discussion does not establish the complexity of this mass-allocation problem. Nor do the cited analytic or axiomatic treatments of continuity answer it. The mirror would need a carefully chosen preference domain, and it would not preserve the literal connected-piece assignment. Still, under the programme’s stated allowance for population-forced co-modifications, this is a serious open computational question—not a case where the continuous population has nowhere meaningful to go.
