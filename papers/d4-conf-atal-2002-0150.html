What the mirror covers

The proposed LP covers fixed-recipe task allocation only; it does not cover the paper's incremental negotiation, context-dependent communication, or message-saving procedures, and it is not anchored to a named computational result.

The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying anchors in this paper: it states no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition classifying a problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or otherwise. Its named procedures are protocol steps, not complexity results. Table 3 reports an experiment: across ten scenarios, structured negotiation saved 13 messages and 67 facts or plan references relative to the comparison method. That supports the practical relevance of communication cost, but it cannot anchor a claim about what happens to discrete hardness in a continuum. So there is no paper result of the requested kind to quote and restate.

The strongest positive case I can make is a new mirror of the task-allocation core, rather than of the negotiation protocol’s computational complexity. Imagine a large fleet handling repeated, similar patrol tasks. A type \(t\) records everything relevant to the task: capabilities, existing commitments, and relevant beliefs about the site. Let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s fraction of the fleet. In a plausible high-multiplicity regime, thousands of robots share a small number \(\tau\) of such types.

For a fixed shared-plan recipe with roles \(j\), let \(x_{tj}\) be the fraction of the fleet of type \(t\) assigned to role \(j\). The input gives rational type masses \(\mu_t\), role requirements \([\ell_j,u_j]\), eligibility constraints derived from capabilities and commitments, and \(v_{tj}\), the contribution of assigning type \(t\) to role \(j\) to the team’s shared utility. The question is to find a feasible assignment maximizing \(\sum_{t,j}v_{tj}x_{tj}\), subject to \(\sum_j x_{tj}=\mu_t\), \(x_{tj}\ge 0\), and \(\ell_j\le\sum_t x_{tj}\le u_j\). A solution is the assignment \(x\) and its achieved utility. For a fixed finite recipe library, this is an LP for each recipe, followed by a comparison of the recipes, so I would expect this restricted mirror to be tractable.

This has some claim to author recognition: the paper explicitly treats negotiation as reaching consensus on task and resource apportionment, allows group activities to be divided along resource dimensions, and assumes team members share preferences. The fleet scenario also supplies the high multiplicity missing from the paper’s small-team robot examples. But it covers only the allocation core. It does not mirror the paper’s central contribution—incremental negotiation over evolving plans, beliefs, and context—or its communication-saving procedures. The paper gives no named complexity result showing that this allocation problem is what its algorithm computes.

My weakest point is therefore substantial: the proposed LP is a plausible subproblem suggested by the paper, not a continuous version of a named result in it. The paper’s experiments could motivate studying aggregate communication, but they do not establish a computational question or a complexity anchor. I would present this as a modest research lead, not as evidence that the paper already supplies a strong continuization target.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent supplies no qualifying anchor. The paper states no numbered result about the complexity of a computational problem. Its procedures describe how agents negotiate, and Table 3 reports message savings in ten scenarios; neither specifies a complexity question whose continuous version can be compared.

The proposed fleet LP is the strongest possible lead, but it models a different decision process. It fixes a recipe, role requirements, eligibility constraints, and additive per-type contributions \(v_{tj}\), then asks a central optimizer to assign mass to roles. The paper’s agents instead negotiate over evolving plans using their beliefs, commitments, and causal context. They share preferences, but the paper does not define their agreement as maximizing an additive assignment objective. A large fleet with repeated patrol work could make the LP a sensible high-multiplicity task-allocation problem, and the paper’s discussion of task and resource apportionment gives it some connection to the authors’ interests. Still, that scenario does not turn the LP into a continuous version of a computational result in this paper.

A closer attempt would model a continuum of agents negotiating over plans and exchanging context-dependent messages. But the paper’s message interpretation depends on who knows what, prior commitments, and links between particular subplans. Aggregating agents into types is plausible only if those interaction-relevant states remain compact; aggregating them away loses what the protocol is designed to handle. A model that instead optimizes aggregate communication or consensus would need to introduce its own objective and interaction structure, neither of which the paper supplies.

So the negative case is strongest against a mirror of this paper’s contribution: there is no named computational result to continuize, and the best proposed replacement is a new task-allocation model. It is weaker as a universal claim. The repeated-fleet scenario is plausible, and an analyst could develop it into a worthwhile adjacent project; the paper’s own framing gives that project some foothold. I cannot honestly rule out every recognized continuous mirror, but the material here does not establish one.
