The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of student types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one, student course rankings and eligibility classes for each type, course priority tiers over types, and rational per-capita course and laminar class quotas, assign each unit of type mass to an acceptable course or leave it unmatched. Compare two allocations on the same population: student votes follow course rankings, while each course compares the mass assigned to it by pairing opposite differences within the most refined classes in priority order, with unpaired mass contributing the enrollment difference. Return a popular allocation with maximum matched mass, or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete student types, where a type includes course rankings, eligibility, and quota-class membership. Each course ranks types in tiers, treating members of a type alike; allocations assign every individual integrally while allowing type-level mass to split. Course capacities and class quotas are rational shares of the cohort, and votes are measured as mass using the paper's classwise comparison rule.

The objection that survived

The paper's arguments rely on strict individual hospital rankings to determine correspondence and votes; the type-tiered model changes those votes, and the proponent does not show that either theorem's construction transfers.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the LCSM+ results on maximum-cardinality popular matching and popular matching among maximum-cardinality matchings. It leaves the structural characterization, the stable-matching popularity result, and the specialized PCSM+ runtimes without a separate continuous analogue.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the two-copy construction behind Theorem 3 admit a type-mass version with tiered course priorities and laminar per-capita quotas?
  • Can maximum-mass popular allocations be found, or nonexistence certified, in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the course and class input size, and the rational input bit length \(L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s large student–course allocation example: a national or large university system with millions of students, a fixed course catalog, and a modest number \(\tau\) of student types. A type records a student’s course ranking, eligibility, and membership in the relevant quota classes. Courses rank these types and impose laminar upper quotas. Each type has rational mass \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); course capacities and class quotas are rational shares of the cohort. This scaling is part of the model: a course with capacity \(q(h)\) in a cohort of \(N\) has capacity \(q(h)/N\) in normalized mass. The regime is credible when \(N\) is very large and \(\tau\), the course catalog, and the class trees are much smaller.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. The input is the finite type and course lists, acceptable pairs, laminar class trees, and binary-rational type masses and per-capita quotas. For each type \(t\), let \(I_t=[0,\mu_t)\) be its population mass. An allocation assigns each point of \(I_t\) to one acceptable course or leaves it unmatched; it must satisfy every course capacity and class quota. Each allocation has a finite description as rational-mass pieces of these intervals, so every individual receives an integral assignment while the type’s aggregate assignment may be fractional.

To compare allocations \(X\) and \(Y\), the same point \(u\in I_t\) represents the same student in both. Its vote is \(+1\), \(-1\), or \(0\) according to whether it prefers its assignment in \(Y\), its assignment in \(X\), or is indifferent. A course’s vote is the mass-scaled version of the paper’s rule: cancel students assigned to it in both allocations, then pair the remaining mass recursively within the most refined classes that have mass on both sides, in course preference order. Each paired unit votes according to the course’s preference between those student types; unpaired mass contributes the signed difference in the course’s enrollment. Preferences can be strict between types and tied among identical members of a type. The total vote margin \(\Delta(Y,X)\) is the integral of student votes plus these course votes. Allocation \(X\) is popular if \(\Delta(Y,X)\leq 0\) for every feasible \(Y\).

My lead question is Continuous Maximum-Mass Popular LCSM+: given that input, return a popular allocation \(X\) whose matched mass is largest among all popular allocations, or report that none exists. The matched mass is \(\sum_t \lambda\{u\in I_t:X(u)\ne\bot\}\). This asks the same underlying question as the paper’s maximum-cardinality popular matching, with population and capacity measured as mass.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that for an LCSM+ instance \(G\), construct \(G_2\) with two copies of every resident and one dummy hospital per resident, compute a stable matching \(M_2\) in \(G_2\), and map each resident back to its original hospital when one of its copies is assigned to a real hospital. The resulting matching is a maximum-cardinality popular matching in \(G\). The paper gives an \(O(mn)\)-time algorithm for LCSM+ and a linear-time algorithm for PCSM+, using stable-matching algorithms cited from elsewhere; the reduction and its guarantee are proved here. I expect the continuous question to be Class A: the two-level construction suggests a finite type-level mass matching with laminar constraints, potentially solvable with flow or continuous matching methods. The central proof question is whether that construction still certifies popularity for mass and type-level ties.

A second, related anchor is Theorem 4, also proved here. With \(n_1=|R|\), the paper constructs \(G_{n_1}\) with \(n_1\) copies of each resident, computes a stable matching \(M_{n_1}\), and maps it back. The result is a maximum-cardinality matching that no other maximum-cardinality feasible matching defeats under the paper’s voting rule. Its stated running time is \(O(mn^2)\) for LCSM+ and \(O(mn)\) for PCSM+. The corresponding continuous question is: among all feasible allocations of maximum matched mass, find one \(X\) such that \(\Delta(Y,X)\leq0\) for every other allocation \(Y\) of that same mass, or report that none exists. I also tentatively expect Class A, since the input has finite type support and laminar structure, but this is a weaker expectation: the discrete construction uses \(n_1\) levels, and compressing those levels when masses are given in binary would require a new idea.

The paper has no named NP-hardness or other hardness anchor, so these results cannot support a claim that discrete hardness dissolves. They are positive algorithmic anchors. The continuous computational target would be running time polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of courses and class nodes, and the rational input bit length \(L\), rather than in the expanded population size. Multiplying rational masses and quotas by a common denominator recovers a finite high-multiplicity instance, but that denominator may be enormous relative to \(L\).

The weak point is the preference model. The paper assumes strict preferences over individual residents; repeated residents with identical types are indistinguishable, so the mirror uses ties among members of the same type. That is a real extension, though a natural one when courses prioritize categories such as degree, department, or eligibility. The interval labels merely let us compare the same student across two allocations, as popularity requires. If the authors insist that every individual’s strict position in every course ranking is part of the type, the support may grow with the population and this mirror loses its high-multiplicity force. The continuous question is therefore strongest for course systems whose priorities are genuinely type-based.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that high multiplicity does not fit the paper’s stated preference model. Every hospital strictly ranks its acceptable residents, so residents sharing a course preference and quota-class profile can still differ in how a hospital compares them. A complete type has to include those comparisons. With overlapping acceptable sets, strict rankings therefore tend to make residents distinct types, leaving \(\tau\) close to \(|R|\). The paper’s large student–course example does not by itself escape this: a large population is not a high-multiplicity regime if the course rankings distinguish its members.

That weakens both anchors in their stated form. Theorem 3’s two-copy construction still starts from individually ranked residents, and its continuous version would need to preserve the information that makes those residents distinct. Theorem 4’s construction is even more tied to the finite instance: it uses \(|R|\) levels, with running time depending on that population size. Binary-rational masses have no corresponding intrinsic number of levels. Neither observation makes a continuous question worthless, but neither theorem transfers directly to a small-support problem.

The better mirror is the proponent’s type-tiered model: students with the same profile and quota-class membership share a priority tier, and matching mass is compared using a carefully defined extension of the paper’s voting rule. That is coherent and potentially useful. It does, however, change a feature central to the paper: hospitals’ strict rankings determine how residents are paired and how hospital votes are counted. Treating people in a tier as tied changes those votes; adding interval labels supplies a way to track agents through comparisons, but it does not preserve the paper’s individual priority order. Theorem 3 would then concern popular matchings under type-level priorities, and Theorem 4 would concern a new mass-based comparison problem.

This is the best negative case: the exact model offers little high-multiplicity compression, while the plausible repair changes the voting model that the results analyze. But it does not defeat every possible mirror. A category-priority mass model may be a sensible computational problem, and the missing analogue of Theorem 4’s population-dependent construction could make that question more interesting. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
