The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no numbered computational result of the required kind. Propositions 1 and 2 state monotonicity requirements for a Restake bonus: it should not increase when \(B\)’s signal rises, and should not increase when the relevance signal \(r\) falls. Neither classifies an algorithmic problem or gives an algorithm.

The closest candidate is §3.5’s statement that computing prices in combinatorial LMSR is \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-complete, attributed to reference [3]. It is presented as prior work, not as a numbered result of this paper. Its computation ranges over truth assignments in the outcome space, so using it as an anchor would continuize the wrong object.

There is a possible future population-level angle: a large DAO prediction market could have many participants grouped by identical priors, utilities, information, and budgets; type masses \(\mu_t\) could aggregate per-capita trades into market share changes. But the paper poses no computational problem about those participants, and the type assumptions would need justification. That observation cannot supply the missing anchor or a defensible per-anchor continuous question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a continuous mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 specify how a Restake bonus should vary with price changes; they do not classify a computational problem or give an algorithm. The \(\#\mathrm{P}\)-completeness claim in §3.5 is attributed to earlier work, and its hard input is the Carroll structure’s outcome space. Even treating it as a candidate, its complexity depends on the number and arrangement of propositions, not on the number of traders.

The proposed DAO scenario does not supply the missing population axis. Once trades are made, the LMSR prices depend on aggregate share quantities \(Q\), not on how many participants produced them. Grouping traders with identical complete types could summarize their simultaneous orders, but the securities’ outcome space—and the price-computation problem—would be unchanged. The shares are already divisible, so making trader counts fractional does not by itself yield a new tractability question.

A stronger proposed mirror might model a large population of traders and ask for the least costly mass of trades needed to move a price or trigger a decision. That would be a new market-manipulation or strategic-equilibrium problem, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper. To preserve the paper’s incentive concerns, types would need to capture information, budgets, histories, and strategies; trade order can matter, and the paper itself notes the special role of the last trader in self-resolving markets. Making traders nonatomic also changes the setting by removing an individual trader’s market impact, which is central to the Restake and manipulation concerns.

So the case against a worthwhile mirror is strong for this paper as written: there is no computational anchor, and aggregating traders leaves the paper’s actual computational bottleneck untouched. The honest limitation is that one could formulate a new continuous-agent market problem around these ideas. But that possibility does not make it a continuous mirror of this paper; it would be a different research question.
