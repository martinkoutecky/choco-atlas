The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly offer a ChoCo computational anchor in this paper. Its main result is printed simply as “Theorem,” without a number, and is proved in Section 5: if \(R^{CHS}\subseteq D\subseteq R\), then any strategy-proof, Pareto-optimal social choice function \(f:D^n\to X\) violates the minimum-consumption guarantee. In other words, there is no profile-independent \(\varepsilon>0\) that keeps every person’s bundle at least \(\varepsilon\) from the origin. That is an axiomatic impossibility result, not a result about an algorithm or computational complexity. The unnumbered Corollary and numbered Lemmas 1–7 do not supply such a result either.

There is still a plausible population mirror behind the theorem. Picture a very large exchange economy whose consumers fall into a small number of preference cohorts: \(N\) is large, while \(\tau\), the number of distinct preference types, is much smaller. A type could be a complete Cobb–Douglas or CES preference, including its parameters. Normalize the economy to mass one and let \(\Omega\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^m\) be its per-capita supply. Then \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of consumers of type \(t\), and an allocation assigns each type a per-capita bundle \(x_t\), subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t\leq\Omega\). This makes the society continuous while keeping exchange feasibility, truthful reporting, Pareto efficiency, and the consumption guarantee at the center.

A precise candidate is the following *nonatomic exchange guarantee problem*. Fix \(m\geq2\), \(\Omega\in\mathbb{R}_{++}^m\), and the rich report domain \(D=R^{CHS}\). For every finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over \(D\), ask whether there is a deterministic type-uniform direct rule \(x(r;\mu)\) assigning a bundle to each possible report \(r\in D\). Require feasibility, Pareto optimality among feasible type assignments, and a uniform guarantee \(\varepsilon>0\) such that \(\|x(t;\mu)\|\geq\varepsilon\) for every type in the support of every \(\mu\). Require truthful reporting as well: for a consumer of true type \(t\), \(x(t;\mu)\,R_t\,x(s;\mu)\) for every alternative report \(s\). A single consumer has zero mass, so the deviation leaves \(\mu\) unchanged; the rule still has to give that consumer no incentive to misreport. A solution would be a rule meeting these conditions, or a proof that none exists.

I would conjecture that the answer is still impossibility, which is the strongest positive case for this mirror: it preserves the paper’s exchange problem and its conflict between strategy-proofness, efficiency, and a minimum bundle. But this is not a computational question as stated, so it cannot honestly be classified as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard. Making it algorithmic would require specifying a representation of mechanisms and a finite decision task that the paper does not provide. The mirror also imposes type-uniform treatment, while the paper permits rules that treat people with identical preferences differently. Those are substantial gaps: the candidate is a plausible continuum analogue of the theorem, not a computational result anchored in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is the category mismatch the proponent identifies but cannot repair: this paper has no computational result to mirror. Its Theorem in §3 rules out a social choice function satisfying strategy-proofness, Pareto optimality, and a minimum-consumption guarantee. The Corollary and Lemmas 1–7 support that impossibility proof; none asks for an algorithm or classifies computational complexity. A continuous population could change the axiomatic question, but it cannot supply the missing computational anchor.

The proposed exchange setting is economically coherent. A large economy with a small number of preference cohorts is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and per-capita supplies and type-level allocations are natural co-modifications. The problem is what it asks: whether a mechanism exists with specified axioms. That is a continuous-population axiomatic question, not the computational landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.

A stronger attempt would make mechanism design algorithmic: encode a finite report domain, utility representations, and a mechanism, then ask whether a strategy-proof, Pareto-optimal rule with a specified guarantee exists. But those encodings and restrictions are new modeling choices. Fixing one distribution and computing a Pareto allocation would not capture strategy-proofness, which compares outcomes across reports. Keeping the paper’s full domain of classical preferences and quantifying over every finite-support society supplies no finite input representation or mechanism description whose complexity could be analyzed. Such a synthesis problem might be worthwhile in its own right; it would not be a computational mirror anchored in this paper.

There is also a modeling wrinkle in the proposed atomless version. A single person’s report leaves the population distribution unchanged, while feasibility and Pareto efficiency are aggregate conditions that ignore changes on a set of measure zero. Strategy-proofness, however, still evaluates that person’s bundle. The mechanism therefore needs a convention for pointwise allocations to individuals and for reports with zero mass. That can be resolved, but different resolutions define different incentive problems; none turns the paper’s theorem into an algorithmic result.

So the negative verdict is firm for ChoCo: there is no worthwhile *computational mirror anchored in this paper*. The broader claim that no worthwhile continuous research question exists in any scenario would go too far. The proponent has described a plausible continuum exchange question; its weakness is that it belongs to axiomatic mechanism design, outside the programme’s computational brief.
