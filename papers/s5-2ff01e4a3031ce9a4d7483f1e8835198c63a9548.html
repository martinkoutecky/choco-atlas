The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), target \(w\), and complete ranking types \(\rho\) with binary rational registered masses \(\mu_\rho\) summing to \(1\) and available masses \(\nu_\rho\), is there a recruitment vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_\rho\le\nu_\rho\) and \(\sum_\rho x_\rho\le\kappa\) such that \(w\) is the unique Bucklin winner after the type masses become \((\mu_\rho+x_\rho)/(1+\sum_\rho x_\rho)\)?

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity electoral control model with complete rankings and registration status in each type; the chair chooses recruited masses \(x_\rho\), subject to availability and a total-mass budget, to make \(w\) the unique Bucklin winner, minimizing \(\sum_\rho x_\rho\).

What the mirror covers

Covers the constructive adding-voters resistance in Lemma 3.15, with susceptibility supplied by Lemma 3.5; it leaves the paper’s other control scenarios and results outside the mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • What additive rounding guarantee, if any, converts a feasible mass plan to a successful finite-electorate plan with increments of \(1/N\), in terms of the winner margin and support size?
  • How large can the gap be between the minimum successful mass and the minimum successful recruitment in a finite pool with matching type proportions?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of constructive control by adding voters. The paper’s Lemma 3.15, proved there by a reduction from X3C, says that Bucklin voting is resistant to this control: given registered votes, a pool of unregistered votes, a designated candidate, and a limit \(k\), it is NP-hard to decide whether adding at most \(k\) pool voters can make the designated candidate the unique Bucklin winner. “Resistant” here includes susceptibility; the paper establishes that separately in Lemma 3.5. The X3C reduction is proved in this paper; the NP-completeness of X3C is cited to Garey and Johnson.

The mirror keeps the paper’s chair, target, candidate slate, complete rankings, and Bucklin winner rule. It changes the electorate from a list of people to masses of voter types. A type records a complete ranking of the slate and whether its members are already registered or are available to be added. This fits a large membership organization choosing from a moderate slate: many members may share each ranking and participation status, so the number of people \(N\) can greatly exceed the number of supported types \(\tau\). The chair’s action is to recruit some mass from each available type.

Precisely, call the problem Continuous Constructive Bucklin Control by Adding Voter Mass. An instance gives candidates \(C\), a target \(w\), rational binary masses \(\mu_t\) of registered types with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational available masses \(\nu_t\), and a rational budget \(\kappa\). The question is whether there is a rational vector \(x\) such that \(0\le x_t\le\nu_t\), \(\sum_t x_t\le\kappa\), and \(w\) is the unique Bucklin winner after adding that mass. The resulting normalized society has type masses \((\mu_t+x_t)/(1+\sum_t x_t)\). The associated optimization objective is to minimize \(\sum_t x_t\); the instance asks whether that minimum is at most \(\kappa\).

For clarity, let \(S_j(c;x)\) be the unnormalized mass ranking \(c\) in its top \(j\) positions after the addition, and let \(M(x)=1+\sum_t x_t\). With \(S_0(c;x)=0\), \(w\) is the unique winner exactly when, for some level \(\ell\), every candidate has \(S_{\ell-1}(c;x)\le M(x)/2\), while \(S_\ell(w;x)>M(x)/2\) and \(S_\ell(w;x)>S_\ell(c;x)\) for every \(c\ne w\). These conditions preserve the paper’s strict-majority threshold and its tie-breaking by level score.

I expect this mirror to be tractable. For each \(\ell\in\{1,\ldots,|C|\}\), those conditions, the mass caps, and the budget are linear inequalities in \(x\). Test feasibility for each level with an LP; strict inequalities can be handled by maximizing a common positive slack. This gives a polynomial-time algorithm in \(|C|\), the input support sizes, and the binary encoding length. The hardness reduction in Lemma 3.15 makes the question worth asking, but its X3C choices are integral; it does not by itself establish hardness after fractional mass choices are allowed.

This is a plausible analogue of the authors’ problem because the chair still changes participation to make the same candidate win under the same voting rule. The electorate, rather than the outcome space, is what becomes continuous. The mirror covers Lemma 3.15’s constructive adding-voters result only; I would not stretch it to the paper’s other control results.

The weak point is the relaxation of the chair’s action. In the paper, each available voter is either added or not; in the mirror, the chair may add a fraction of a type’s mass. A discrete instance embeds on the grid where masses are multiples of \(1/N\), but a fractional successful choice need not round to a successful discrete choice, especially near a Bucklin tie. So the continuous question is a credible new high-multiplicity version, while a two-way correspondence with the paper’s finite control problem remains open. It also raises the natural questions of whether rounding guarantees can be proved from a winner-margin bound, and how the minimum changes when recruiting different types has different costs.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a persuasive case that this mirror is not worth asking. The paper has a named computational result: constructive control by adding voters is resistant (Theorem 3.14, with the hardness reduction in Lemma 3.15). Its population can naturally have high multiplicity: in a large membership organization, many eligible and registered members can share the same ranking. Treating ranking and participation status as parts of a type preserves the relevant information.

The strongest objection is that the mirror changes the chair’s action. The paper selects whole voters from a finite pool; the continuous version selects mass, so it answers a fractional recruitment question. But that is exactly the high-multiplicity relaxation at issue. It has a credible interpretation when recruitment operates on large groups, and the discrete problem remains represented on the \(1/N\) grid. A type-specific recruitment cost or eligibility condition would not fix this objection: it belongs in the type description, and mass recruitment remains meaningful.

The stronger version of the mirror is also computationally substantive. For each Bucklin level \(\ell\), the winner conditions are linear in the recruited masses: no candidate has a strict majority before \(\ell\), and the target has a strict majority at \(\ell\) with a strictly higher level-\(\ell\) score than every rival. Testing each level gives a polynomial number of linear programs; strict inequalities can be handled by maximizing a common slack. That would turn this paper’s NP-hard control problem into an exact continuous optimization problem. The possibility that a fractional solution does not round to a successful discrete action is a real question about the bridge between the models, but it does not make the continuous question ill-posed or uninteresting.

One can argue that this is an especially clean LP consequence, rather than a demanding use of new optimization machinery, and that exact winners can be sensitive to tiny mass changes near ties. Those are weak objections here: an easy continuous algorithm and delicate boundaries are legitimate results for the programme. The honest negative case is therefore narrow. This mirror changes the action’s granularity, so it should be presented as a high-multiplicity relaxation rather than as the paper’s original operational problem. That caveat does not defeat the anchor.
