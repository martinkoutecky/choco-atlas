The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make a strong ChoCo case from this paper: it has no named computational result of the kind the brief requires as an anchor. Theorem 1, proved here, says the two-player DD′ game has a unique pure-strategy subgame-perfect equilibrium, with both players demanding and receiving \(1/2\). Theorem 2, also proved here, rules out subgame-perfect equilibria involving first-stage mixing. In the appendix, Theorem 3 says that for \(n>2\), payoff-equivalent subgame-perfect equilibria exist and all have equal demands; Theorem 4 rules out first-stage mixing in that version. These are equilibrium-characterization results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise has a complexity classification. None is an eligible anchor under the stated standard.

The nearest positive idea is a large arbitration service handling many independent DD′ disputes. One could take a rational distribution over dispute types—say, types differing only in the dollar stake—and ask for the equilibrium allocation for each type and the mass-weighted aggregate allocation. Theorem 1’s equal split scales with the stake, so this batch problem would be easy. But its population consists of disputes, not the strategic society in the paper; aggregating the answers is a direct-product calculation, not a continuous version of the authors’ implementation problem. For the paper’s \(n\)-player game, a population limit also has a concrete obstacle: the mechanism selects a proposer among individual minimum-demand players, and that selection is not defined by simply replacing the finite players with atomless mass.

So the honest positive case is only an adjacent aggregation story. I would not present it as a continuous computational mirror, and there is no per-anchor prover question to state.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no eligible computational result to mirror. Theorems 1 and 2 characterize subgame-perfect equilibria of the two-player game; Theorems 3 and 4 do so for its \(n\)-player extension. They establish implementation and rule out certain equilibrium behavior. They do not formulate an algorithmic problem or classify its complexity. One could ask an algorithm to output an equilibrium, but the paper’s fixed game and its \(n\)-player extension already give explicit equal-demand equilibria. Turning that characterization into a computational task would add a new question rather than mirror one of the paper’s results.

The strongest population-based candidate is the \(n\)-player game with many similar claimants sharing one estate. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting, but the limit strains the mechanism’s strategic structure. Claims must shrink with \(n\) to preserve a fixed total estate. Then an individual has vanishing mass, while the mechanism assigns the proposer role to a player with the minimum claim and allows that proposer to allocate the excess. In an atomless population, the rule for selecting one proposer among a positive mass of tied minimum claimants needs new structure; selecting a whole type block instead changes the game. Keeping a singled-out proposer or using identity-based tie-breaking retains an atom rather than making the society continuous. A continuous-population equilibrium model might be worth studying in its own right, but it would be a new analytic game, not a computational mirror of these theorems.

The proposed arbitration-service example also fails as a mirror. A distribution over separate disputes is a distribution over cases; aggregating their outcomes is a caseload calculation. Each dispute still has its own finite set of strategic players and its own dollar. A distribution over first-stage demands is a mixed strategy, which Theorem 2 already addresses; it makes the action space continuous, not the society.

So the negative case rests on the paper’s subject and its limit structure together: it supplies no computational target, and the most plausible large-population version requires new strategic rules at the point where the original mechanism selects an individual proposer. That does not rule out designing a computational bargaining problem for a continuum population. It leaves such a problem outside this paper’s results.
