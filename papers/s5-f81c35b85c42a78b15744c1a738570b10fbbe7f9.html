The continuous mirror question

Given \(\tau\) applicant types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational weights \(w_t\), and rankings over acceptable job categories, together with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\), decide whether a popular assignment exists and construct one. An assignment \(x\) allocates each type's mass across whole-job categories and its outside option, respecting supplies; it is popular if every feasible reassignment coupling \(z_{tjj'}\), with current-assignment marginals \(x_{tj}\) and challenger totals within the supplies, has nonpositive weighted preference margin \(\sum_{t,j,j'} w_t z_{tjj'}(\mathbf{1}[j'\succ_t j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_t j'])\). Ask whether this can be done in time polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of job categories, and the input bit length \(L\).

The model it lives in

Types are defined by complete preference rankings and weights. The decision variable \(x_{tj}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving job category \(j\); category supplies scale per capita, while each person still receives a whole job. Challenger couplings \(z_{tjj'}\) compare current and reassigned jobs using the paper's weighted popularity margin.

The objection that survived

For fixed \(x\), optimizing a challenger does not by itself solve the outer search for an \(x\) that defeats every challenger, and the paper's promotion-path pruning has no established analogue for split mass and capacities. The proponent acknowledges that the finite algorithm does not transfer automatically, but this remains the main obstacle to its Class A expectation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers existence and construction from Theorems 3 and 5 for strict and tied preferences; it leaves the paper's structural characterizations, pruning results, and maximum-cardinality objective without separate continuous questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a polynomial-time algorithm in \(\tau\), the number of job categories, and \(L\) exist for the strict-preference mass model?
  • Can the tied-preference model's criticality and pruning arguments be extended to mass assignments with per-capita supplies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is a high-multiplicity job market: a very large placement round in which applicants share standardized preference lists and the same priority weight, while positions come in a modest number of interchangeable categories. For example, there might be hundreds of thousands of applicants but only a few dozen or hundred applicant types. This is a credible regime for centralized housing or graduate placement. The type must include both the applicant’s full preference list and weight; those determine how that applicant behaves in the paper’s comparison between matchings.

Here is the continuous model. There are \(\tau\) applicant types \(t\), with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). There are \(r\) job categories \(j\), with rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\). Each type has a strict preference order or a ranking with ties over its acceptable jobs, and a positive rational weight \(w_t\). Every person receives one whole job or their own last-resort outside option \(\bot_t\). A type may be split across jobs, but this means that different people in the type receive different whole jobs; nobody receives a fraction of a job.

A proposed assignment is a mass matrix \(x\), where \(x_{tj}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to \(j\). Its rows sum to \(\mu_t\), and each job column sums to at most \(q_j\). To define popularity exactly, let \(z_{tjj'}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned job \(j\) by \(x\) and job \(j'\) by a challenger. Require \(\sum_{j'}z_{tjj'}=x_{tj}\), and require the challenger’s job totals to respect the same supplies. Atomlessness lets any such coupling be realized by reassigning the people in each type. Call \(x\) popular if every feasible \(z\) satisfies

\[ \sum_t w_t\sum_{j,j'}z_{tjj'} \left(\mathbf{1}[j'\succ_t j]-\mathbf{1}[j\succ_t j']\right)\le 0. \]

For tied rankings, equal-rank outcomes contribute zero in both directions. This is the paper’s weighted-majority comparison, with population mass replacing applicant counts. The outside option models the paper’s last-resort jobs; the per-capita supplies are the natural co-modification needed when job supply grows with the cohort.

My lead is Strict Weighted Popular Mass Matching. Its input is the strict-ranking version of this model; it asks whether a popular assignment matrix \(x\) exists and, if so, to produce one, or otherwise certify nonexistence. The anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper: with strict preference lists, one can find a weighted popular matching or determine that none exists in \(O(n+m)\) time. The result uses the paper’s pruning and well-formed-matching characterization, together with a cited linear-time subroutine for finding such a matching. The continuous question is whether the same existence-and-construction task can be solved in time polynomial in \(\tau\), \(r\), and the input bit length \(L\), without expanding the population into individual applicants and job slots.

A second, worthwhile anchor is Tied Weighted Popular Mass Matching, the same question when applicant types may have ties. It mirrors Theorem 5, also proved here: with ties, the paper finds a weighted popular matching or determines that none exists in \(O(\min(k\sqrt{n},n)m)\) time, where \(k\) is the number of distinct applicant weights. Its algorithm uses cited matching and rank-maximal-matching algorithms as subroutines. The continuous question is whether existence and construction remain tractable in \(\tau,r,L\), with \(k\) retained as a parameter.

I would put both questions provisionally in Class A. For a fixed candidate \(x\), its most damaging challenger is a maximum-cost mass-flow problem, and the paper’s strict and tied algorithms rely on alternating paths, pruning, and matching structure that have capacitated-flow counterparts. That makes a tractability result plausible. For the tied version in particular, a prover would need to define the right fractional counterpart of criticality and show that the pruning argument still works. My further questions would be whether the continuous answers agree with sufficiently large integer replications, and whether rational inputs guarantee efficiently representable popular assignments.

The paper contains no named hardness result, so these are algorithmic anchors rather than a claim that continuization dissolves known hardness. The serious weakness is that the paper’s running times use the explicit graph size \(n,m\); they do not establish a bound in support size. Nor does its finite matching algorithm automatically solve the continuous problem, where type mass can split and popularity quantifies over all feasible couplings. Thus the expected Class A result would be new work. Still, the mirror preserves the paper’s actual question—whether an assignment exists that no alternative defeats under weighted preference votes—while making the repeated population, rather than the outcome received by any individual, the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The most substantial objection is to the proponent’s tractability argument. For a *fixed* mass assignment \(x\), finding its strongest challenger is a linear optimization problem over couplings \(z\). That does not by itself solve the proposed existence problem. The search is for an \(x\) such that every feasible challenger has nonpositive margin. Dualizing the challenger LP makes its objective depend on products of \(x\) and dual variables; the paper’s pruning argument is what avoids this difficulty for integral matchings. The proponent has not shown that its strict or tied variants admit a polynomial algorithm in \(\tau,r,L\), or even that the mass version has the same structural characterization.

This gap applies to both anchors. Theorem 3’s strict-preference algorithm depends on which applicant gets each first-choice job and on promotion paths through those particular assignments. In a mass assignment, a job category can serve many applicants, and a type’s mass can be split across jobs. The corresponding path and pruning arguments would need to account for capacities and fractional flow. Theorem 5 adds another layer: its criticality tests and rank-maximal matching subroutine concern integral bipartite matchings. A capacitated mass analogue is plausible, but the paper does not establish it. The tied anchor therefore inherits the strict anchor’s unresolved outer search and requires a further extension of the criticality machinery.

That is a case against the proponent’s *Class A expectation*, not against the questions themselves. The strongest version of the proposed model remains coherent: take a large placement round, group applicants by complete preference list and weight, group genuinely interchangeable jobs into categories, and scale category supplies with the cohort. Applicants are compared by the jobs they receive under each allocation, and the coupling records which mass changes from one job to another. This gives a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Applicant identity is not needed for the paper’s vote, and positive type masses keep the vote from vanishing. Theorem 3 and Theorem 5 are named computational results. None of the permitted objections rules out this mirror.

Prior work makes the novelty boundary worth checking carefully, but it does not settle the case against. Weighted capacitated house allocation is an established finite model ([Sng and Manlove](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/25731/)), and popular mixed or fractional matchings have their own algorithmic literature ([Huang and Kavitha](https://epubs.siam.org/doi/10.1137/1.9781611974782.151)). Those are useful neighbors, not automatic collisions: neither fact alone establishes the support-parameterized computation for a society given by type masses. Treating adjacent matching work as proof that the continuous-population question is already answered would overstate what it shows.

So the best defensible negative case is narrow: the positive analyst has moved too quickly from a tractable challenger LP to an expected tractable search, and the paper’s integral structure may be exactly where the proposed algorithms stop transferring. I cannot make that into a convincing claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. Even if the mass versions turn out to require new machinery or land in a harder class, those are meaningful computational questions under the programme’s criteria. The strict and tied anchors both survive the stronger model; I would temper the prediction of tractability, rather than reject the mirrors.
