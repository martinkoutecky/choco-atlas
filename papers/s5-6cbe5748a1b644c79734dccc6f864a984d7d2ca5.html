The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no qualifying named computational result. It states no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition about complexity or algorithms. Its findings are empirical comparisons from simulations on finite networks. Equations (2)–(4) specify the HK dynamics; the figures report how the tested strategies perform. Neither can serve as the requested anchor. So there is no per-anchor continuous problem to state honestly.

The most plausible *unanchored extension* would model a large platform whose users fall into a small number of repeated community and behavior types. A type would include confidence, initial opinion, mixing profile, and intervention cost; \(\mu_t\) would be its share of the population. Given an intervention budget, choose masses \(z_t\) of each type to make stubborn and a shared opinion schedule for those agents, maximizing the population’s mean opinion at a specified horizon. This is a concrete population continuization of the influence problem, but it is a new optimization question, not a result the paper establishes or computes. Its complexity is unclear: the bounded-confidence updates are nonlinear and thresholded.

The main weakness is that the paper’s comparisons depend on individual network structure and centrality rankings. Aggregating users into a few types may erase precisely the structural differences that drive those results. Also, the fact that opinions are continuous does not itself continuize the population; the proposed mirror would need to do that explicitly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s first point is decisive for a paper-specific mirror: the paper states no theorem, lemma, corollary, or complexity result about an algorithmic problem. Its claims about which targeting strategies work are simulation findings on finite LFR networks, under a prescribed stubborn-agent fraction and opinion schedule. There is no stated computational result to translate into a population-mass problem.

The proposed extension—choose a mass of agents to target and an opinion schedule to maximize the final mean under a budget—is coherent, but it adds an optimization problem the paper neither formulates nor solves. A stronger version could use a finite-block graphon, with types for communities and a kernel for interactions. That would be a legitimate new model. But it would not preserve the paper’s centrality comparisons on sparse LFR networks: HK updates depend on the evolving opinions of an agent’s actual neighbors, while coarse type proportions lose those correlations. Retaining enough network structure to recover them risks making the types as numerous as the agents. A sparse-network limit could address that modeling problem, but it would be a substantial new choice of framework, not a mirror supplied by the paper.

The dynamic result is the most tempting anchor: a very small intervention fraction can attract most of the population. Yet the striking \(0.1\%\) case is one stubborn agent at \(N=1000\) and two at \(N=2000\). A vanishing-mass seed’s influence need not survive a population limit, while fixing a positive mass changes the regime being studied. One can still ask how positive-mass campaigns perform in a chosen continuum network model, but that remains a worthwhile neighboring question rather than a continuous version of a named result here.

So the negative case is strongest on the narrow point that this paper offers no computational result to mirror. It is weaker as the universal claim that no useful continuous opinion-influence problem could be designed: a carefully specified continuum network-control problem might be valuable, but its value would belong to that new formulation, not to a result established by this paper.
