The anchor — algorithmic

Proposition 3.7

Every anchor argued

  • Corollary 3.10 — fell: The opponent shows that the individual guarantee’s positive-share component vanishes with population size, while the proposed mass-sized guarantee is a new cohort guarantee.
  • Proposition 3.7 — survived: The opponent concedes this anchor: the paper’s max-deficit condition supports a mass-weighted aggregate equilibrium whose contributions can be split among identical agents.
  • Theorem 3.6 — fell: The opponent shows that the finite individual share shrinks away, and the proposed nontrivial threshold instead gives a pooled type its full mass to control.

The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\) of size \(\tau\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and a peak \(p_t\in\Delta^m\) and specified \(\ell_r\) utility for each type, find allocations \(x_t\in\Delta^m(\mu_t)\) with \(q=\sum_t x_t\) such that every type is best responding: \(u_t(q)\ge u_t(x'_t+\sum_{s\ne t}x_s)\) for every \(x'_t\in\Delta^m(\mu_t)\).

The model it lives in

A type-mass game in which type \(t\) controls an allocation of mass \(\mu_t\) and evaluates the aggregate outcome \(q\) using its peak and utility. The paper’s construction suggests minimizing \(\frac12\|z\|_2^2+\sum_t\mu_t\max_j(p_{t,j}-z_j)\), then recovering each \(x_t\) from its type’s maximum-deficit alternatives.

The objection that survived

Each type acts as a coordinated player controlling mass \(\mu_t\), whereas a standard atomless individual has zero influence, so this is not a Wardrop-style limit. The max-deficit condition still gives a credible extension: a type’s aggregate contribution can be split among identical agents in finite replications.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 3.7’s equilibrium existence and computation. It leaves the IFS-value and IFS–Pareto-efficiency results, the strategyproofness results, and the impossibility results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove that the weighted quadratic program characterizes equilibria for arbitrary rational masses and yields a polynomial-time algorithm in \(m,\tau\), and input bit length.
  • Give a formal correspondence between these type-mass equilibria and equilibria of finite replications with identical agents within each type.

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with the paper’s Corollary 3.10: for every finite profile with \(\ell_t\) utilities, there is a distribution satisfying \(\mathrm{IFS2}\) and Pareto efficiency, and such a distribution can be computed in polynomial time. This is proved in the paper. Two further proved-here anchors are Proposition 3.7, which says that the budget aggregation game has a pure Nash equilibrium, including one strategy profile that is an equilibrium for all \(\ell_t\) utilities simultaneously, and that this profile can be computed in polynomial time; and Theorem 3.6, which says that any agent’s \(\mathrm{IFS2}\)-value under \(\ell_t\) utilities can be computed in strongly polynomial time. Proposition 3.7 uses Rosen’s existence theorem, but its computational result is proved here. These are algorithmic anchors; the paper’s Theorem 4.3 is an impossibility about rule axioms, not a computational-hardness result. [The paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.01228)

The strongest mirror I see is a type-mass version of IFS2 with Pareto efficiency, anchored on Corollary 3.10. Consider a city choosing how to divide an annual budget among \(m\) projects. There are many residents, but the consultation offers a limited menu of budget-split preferences: perhaps \(N=100{,}000\) residents choose among \(\tau=40\) templates. A type \(t\) is the complete description relevant to this problem: its preferred distribution \(p_t\in\Delta^m\) and its utility model. For a clean first question, take all types to have \(\ell_1\) utilities, equivalently overlap utility \(o_t(q)=\sum_j\min\{p_{t,j},q_j\}\). The population is \(\mu\), with rational type masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\).

The co-modification is to give type \(t\), whose mass is \(\mu_t\), control over a \(\mu_t\)-sized contribution to the common budget. Its type-level \(\mathrm{IFS2}\) guarantee is
\[ \theta_t(\mu)= \min_{y\in\Delta^m(1-\mu_t)} \max_{x\in\Delta^m(\mu_t)} o_t(x+y). \]
Here \(y\) is the rest of society’s contribution and \(x\) is the type’s response. Define Type-Mass IFS2–PO as follows: given \(m\), the \(\tau\) rational peaks \(p_t\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\), find a distribution \(q\in\Delta^m\) maximizing \(\sum_t\mu_t o_t(q)\), subject to \(o_t(q)\geq\theta_t(\mu)\) for every type. The output \(q\) is the solution. Since every supported type has positive mass, maximizing this weighted sum over the fair distributions gives a Pareto-efficient distribution.

I expect this question to be Class A. The type-mass game gives a feasible fair distribution, and the remaining selection problem is a linear program for \(\ell_1\) overlap utilities. For rational inputs, the natural target bound is polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. A specific further question is whether the same bound and existence result hold for all \(\ell_t\) utilities and their corresponding type-mass guarantees.

The bridge to Proposition 3.7 is a second, precise problem: Type-Mass Budget-Game Equilibrium. On the same instance, find vectors \(x_t\in\Delta^m(\mu_t)\) such that, writing \(q=\sum_s x_s\), each type \(t\) prefers its chosen contribution to every alternative contribution of the same mass:
\[ o_t(q)\geq o_t\!\left(x'_t+\sum_{s\ne t}x_s\right) \quad\text{for every }x'_t\in\Delta^m(\mu_t). \]
The solution is the full profile \((x_t)_{t\in T}\), or that profile together with \(q\). I also expect Class A. The paper’s equilibrium construction suggests the mass-weighted convex quadratic program
\[ \min_{z,s}\ \frac12\|z\|_2^2+\sum_t\mu_t s_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad s_t\geq p_{t,j}-z_j\ \ \text{for all }t,j, \]
followed by recovery of the type contributions. A further question is whether equilibrium selection can also optimize Pareto welfare without losing the mass-weighted equilibrium guarantees.

The third mirror, of Theorem 3.6, is Type-Mass IFS2-Value Computation: on the same instance, compute \(\theta_t(\mu)\) for each supported type, optionally returning optimal \(x\) and \(y\). For the \(\ell_1\) version, letting \(\alpha_t=\min_j p_{t,j}\), the corresponding threshold is \(\theta_t(\mu)=\mu_t+\min\{\alpha_t,1-\mu_t\}\). When a type has mass \(1/n\), this becomes \(1/n+\alpha_t\), matching the paper’s overlap formula for the finite agent’s \(\mathrm{IFS2}\)-value. This mirror is Class A as well; in this \(\ell_1\) case the threshold has a direct formula. Its next question is how the value computation behaves for general \(\ell_t\) utilities and arbitrary rational type masses.

This covers the paper’s computational fairness results, not its strategyproofness and nonexistence results. The population is what becomes continuous; the outcome remains a distribution over projects, as it already is in the paper.

The weakest point is the interpretation of “individual.” In a literal atomless population, one resident has zero mass and thus no \(1/n\)-sized share to control. The proposed mirror gives a type’s whole mass \(\mu_t\) a pooled share; for repeated finite types, this is a coalition-strengthening of the paper’s individual guarantee, not an exact preservation of it. That is a real change, and the authors might accept it as type-level fair share while rejecting it as the literal continuum limit of individual IFS. The case for it is that the types are identical in every modeled respect, so pooled control is a coherent aggregate decision variable, and the paper already studies fair guarantees through a budget-contribution game. The mirror is strongest in the limited claim that these three named algorithmic questions extend naturally to type-mass societies—not that it continuizes the paper’s strategyproofness theorems or the whole paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the two IFS mirrors is that an atomless person has no positive share to control. For \(\ell_1\) overlap utility, the finite individual guarantee is \(\alpha_t+1/N\), where \(\alpha_t=\min_j p_{t,j}\). As \(N\) grows, it tends to \(\alpha_t\); every distribution \(q\) already gives type \(t\) overlap at least \(\alpha_t\). Indeed, some coordinate has \(q_j\ge p_{t,j}\), so its overlap contribution alone is at least \(\alpha_t\). Thus the limiting individual fairness constraint in Corollary 3.10 is automatic. Theorem 3.6’s individual value similarly loses its positive-share component.

The proposed way to avoid that collapse is to give a type of mass \(\mu_t\) control over \(\mu_t\) of the budget. That yields the nontrivial threshold \(\mu_t+\min\{\alpha_t,1-\mu_t\}\), but it changes individual fair share into type-bloc fair share: a cohort acts jointly and receives a guarantee that depends on its population mass. Identical preferences make people interchangeable; they do not, by themselves, make them a coordinated bargaining bloc. That is a legitimate new group-fairness question, but it is the main reason to doubt that the proposed IFS problems mirror the paper’s individual guarantees.

That objection does not dispatch Proposition 3.7. In an atomless game, an individual’s action has zero effect on the outcome, so the nontrivial equilibrium construction disappears. But the proposed type-mass game is a natural aggregate of the finite game: sum the contributions of equal types, and the paper’s max-deficit condition lets an aggregate contribution be split back among those agents. Its mass-weighted quadratic program is a credible high-multiplicity version of the paper’s equilibrium computation, with a bound in \(m,\tau,L\). Calling it merely a rewording is not a strong objection under ChoCo’s stated standard.

So the negative case can plausibly reject the two IFS anchors as individual-fairness mirrors, while conceding that the type-mass equilibrium anchor may survive. I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario: Proposition 3.7 supplies a credible narrow Class A candidate, even if the IFS proposals rely on changing the unit of fairness from a person to a coordinated type.
