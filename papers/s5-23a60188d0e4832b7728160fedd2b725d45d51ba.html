The continuous mirror question

Given doctor types \(I\) and hospital types \(J\), rational masses \(a_i,b_j\), acceptable type pairs \(E\subseteq I\times J\), complete weak rankings over acceptable partner types, and a closure set \(S\subseteq J\), decide whether a rational flow \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) exists with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le a_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le b_j\) that is stable. For each type pair \((i,j)\), let \(u_i=a_i-\sum_k x_{ik}\) and \(v_j=b_j-\sum_\ell x_{\ell j}\), and define \(D^w_{ij}=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succeq_i k}x_{ik}\), \(D^s_{ij}=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik}\), \(H^w_{ij}=\sum_{\ell:\,i\succeq_j\ell}x_{\ell j}+\mathbf{1}_{j\notin S}v_j\), and \(H^s_{ij}=\sum_{\ell:\,i\succ_j\ell}x_{\ell j}+\mathbf{1}_{j\notin S}v_j\). Stability requires that neither \(D^s_{ij}>0\) and \(H^w_{ij}>0\), nor \(D^w_{ij}>0\) and \(H^s_{ij}>0\). For the anchored restricted case, require at most three acceptable partner types per type and require each doctor type to rank every adjacent hospital type in \(S\) above every adjacent type outside \(S\).

The model it lives in

A population of interchangeable doctor and hospital types with rational masses; each type has a complete preference order over partner types, and each hospital type has a closure status. Feasible matchings are nonnegative type-to-type flows respecting the masses. A type pair blocks when positive mass on both sides is willing to match and at least one side has positive mass that strictly prefers it.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's stable-existence question and its degree and preference restrictions. It leaves the polynomial-time results in Theorems 2 and 3 without proposed mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the complexity of rational-mass stable-flow existence under Theorem 1's degree and preference restrictions, as a function of the type count \(\tau\) and input bit length \(L\)?
  • When does a stable mass flow correspond to stable matchings in finite replicas, and can fractional splitting create stable outcomes absent from every integral replica?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a continuous existence problem for strongly stable matching with closures. It keeps the paper’s solution concept and asks its central question—does a stable matching exist?—while making repeated preference classes the population units.

Let \(I\) be doctor types and \(J\) hospital types. A type records an agent’s side, acceptable partner types, complete weak preference order over those types, and—for hospitals—whether it is in the closure set \(S\). Thus members of a type are interchangeable for the problem; they tie among individual partners of the same type. Give each doctor type \(i\) rational mass \(a_i\) and each hospital type \(j\) rational mass \(b_j\), with acceptable pairs \(E\subseteq I\times J\). The masses may be normalized to a common cohort size, but need not sum to the same total on the two sides.

A matching is a nonnegative flow \(x_{ij}\) on \(E\), with \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le a_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}\le b_j\). The flow is fractional only at the population level: every individual receives at most one partner. Write \(u_i=a_i-\sum_jx_{ij}\) and \(v_j=b_j-\sum_ix_{ij}\) for unmatched mass. For each acceptable pair \((i,j)\), define

\[ \begin{aligned} D^w_{ij}&=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succeq_i k}x_{ik}, & D^s_{ij}&=u_i+\sum_{k:\,j\succ_i k}x_{ik},\\ H^w_{ij}&=\sum_{\ell:\,i\succeq_j\ell}x_{\ell j} +\mathbf{1}_{j\notin S}v_j, & H^s_{ij}&=\sum_{\ell:\,i\succ_j\ell}x_{\ell j} +\mathbf{1}_{j\notin S}v_j. \end{aligned} \]

These are the masses of doctors and hospitals, respectively, who weakly or strictly prefer the proposed partner type to their current assignment. Unmatched doctors are willing to take any acceptable partner; unmatched hospitals are willing only when they are not closed. Call \(x\) stable if, for every \((i,j)\in E\), neither \(D^s_{ij}>0\) and \(H^w_{ij}>0\), nor \(D^w_{ij}>0\) and \(H^s_{ij}>0\). That rules out a positive-mass blocking pair with one side strictly preferring the match and the other weakly preferring it.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that deciding whether a stable matching exists is NP-complete even when every doctor and hospital has at most three acceptable partners, and every doctor strictly prefers any adjacent hospital in \(S\) to any adjacent hospital outside \(S\). The paper proves NP membership by showing that a matching can be checked in polynomial time, and proves NP-hardness by reduction from \((3,\mathrm{B}2)\)-SAT; the cited result of Berman, Karpinski, and Scott supplies the source problem’s NP-completeness. My mirror asks the same existence question for rational masses and stable matching flows, with those same degree and preference restrictions interpreted over types. A solution is a feasible flow satisfying the stability condition above; there is no optimization objective in this anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large recurring placement market: many applicants share credential and preference profiles, while many positions share program type, priority order, and closure status. The masses are population shares; the type count \(\tau=|I|+|J|\) can be far below the number \(N\) of applicants and positions. A position class that withdraws when left empty gives the closure rule a concrete interpretation. Type-level preferences are a necessary co-modification: the agents rank position or applicant categories, rather than every named person separately. The paper allows ties, so ties between members of a category fit its preference language. The closure operation also retains its original role. In this scenario the authors could plausibly recognize the question as theirs: it is still existence of a strongly stable matching with closures, now for a society made of large, repeated types.

I would provisionally expect the general mass-flow problem to remain hard, and treat it as a candidate for Class C, pending a proof. Stability is defined by the presence or absence of positive masses, so choosing which type-to-type flows are positive creates combinatorial structure even though the flow values are continuous. The hardness construction also uses a network of bounded-degree preference relationships, not a large population count alone. But Theorem 1 does not establish hardness of this continuous problem: divisible type mass may split across partners and erase the gadget’s Boolean choices. Nor does its reduction show hardness when many agents share a small set of types. So the claimed hardness is a research conjecture, not a transfer result.

That gap suggests useful follow-up questions: does the continuous problem stay hard when each type has many copies and \(\tau\ll N\)? When does a stable mass flow correspond to stable matchings in large finite replicas, and can fractional splitting create stability where no discrete matching exists? Does the paper’s bounded-degree algorithmic structure extend to the mass-flow problem?

The scope here is deliberately one result: Theorem 1’s NP-completeness question. I am not claiming that the paper’s polynomial-time cases have continuous counterparts. The weakest point is the change from named-agent preferences to preferences over types, together with divisible mass. That change is natural for a high-multiplicity market and preserves the closure and blocking logic, but it may alter the existence question enough that the paper’s hardness construction no longer works. The mirror is still worth asking precisely because this is the point where continuization might help—or where new hardness might appear.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is that the mirror’s apparent simplicity hides a change in what the paper computes. In the strongest formulation, doctors and hospitals are grouped by complete preferences over partner types and closure status, and rational masses are matched by a flow \(x_{ij}\). A pair blocks when positive mass on both sides is willing to match, with at least one side strictly preferring it. A recurring placement market with interchangeable applicant and position categories makes that a coherent continuous-population question.

The reservation is that Theorem 1’s hardness is built from a network of individual roles: each gadget agent’s precise preference list and connections encode part of a Boolean assignment. Grouping agents preserves that construction only by keeping its roles distinct, so the reduction offers little reason to expect a gain when the number of types \(\tau\) is small. And the flow model does not turn stability into a linear program: for every type pair, stability requires that certain willingness masses be zero. Choosing which masses vanish preserves a combinatorial support-selection problem. The continuous-optimization machinery that makes some continuizations valuable may therefore have little purchase here.

That is a real concern about whether this anchor will yield a tractable algorithm or a useful new structural result. It does not defeat the mirror. The mass model still asks the paper’s central existence question in a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and discovering that fractionality either removes the hardness or leaves a continuum-specific hard problem is within the programme’s stated aims. I can’t honestly make the universal negative case: Theorem 1 survives as a worthwhile mirror.
