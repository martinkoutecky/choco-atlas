The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many complete additive valuation types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and finitely many indivisible good slots \(G\) with rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), can one compute in time polynomial in \(|G|\), \(\tau=|T|\), \(k\), and input bit length \(L\) a sparse rational allocation \(\lambda_{t,B}\)? Here \(\lambda_{t,B}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) agents receiving the integral bundle \(B\subseteq G\), with \(\sum_B\lambda_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}\lambda_{t,B}=q_g\); for every pair of positive-mass bundle classes \((t,B),(s,C)\) and every \(Y\subseteq C\) with \(|Y|=k\), require \(v_t(B)\geq \frac{k}{k+1}v_t(C\setminus Y)\).

The model it lives in

Use finitely many complete valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and indivisible good slots with rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\). The decision variables \(\lambda_{t,B}\) assign mass of each type to whole bundles \(B\); type-level averages may be fractional, but every unit of mass receives an integral bundle. Require the paper’s \(\frac{k}{k+1}\)-EFkX comparison across every bundle class in the positive support, and ask for a polynomial-time algorithm in \(|G|,\tau,k,L\) that outputs a sparse rational \(\lambda\).

The objection that survived

The EFkX constraints couple every pair of positive-mass bundle classes, so choosing a compatible support may make the compact allocation problem combinatorial rather than an immediate LP.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1’s \(k/(k+1)\)-EFkX allocation guarantee. It does not cover the stronger guarantee in Theorem 3.6, the bounded-agent \(2/3\)-EFX result in Theorem 3.7, or the graph-orientation results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can one compute a polynomial-support rational \(\lambda\) in time polynomial in \(|G|,\tau,k,L\), rather than in the expanded population and goods counts?
  • Can the paper’s Round-Robin and Envy Cycle Elimination method be compressed over valuation types while maintaining the required EFkX comparisons among all bundle classes?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best hardness anchor is Theorem 4.4, proved in this paper. As printed, it says: for \(k\leq n-1\), EFkX Orientation is NP-complete. Its input is a graph whose edges are goods, with each edge valued positively by its two endpoints; a solution assigns every edge to one endpoint so that, for every ordered pair of agents \(i,j\) and every \(k\)-good subset \(Y\subseteq X_j\), \(v_i(X_i)\geq v_i(X_j\setminus Y)\). The authors prove NP-hardness by induction on \(k\); the base case, EFX Orientation hardness, is cited from Christodoulou et al. (2023).

My lead mirror is Block-Continuous EFkX Orientation. Take a finite graph \(H=(V,E)\) with the paper’s endpoint valuations, and a continuum of isomorphic local communities indexed by \(z\in[0,1]\). Community \(z\) has agents \((z,i)\) for \(i\in V\) and goods \((z,e)\) for \(e\in E\). An agent values only goods in their own community, according to \(H\). The population’s role types are the vertices, each with mass \(1/|V|\); each edge role has one local good per community. The regime is many repeated service units or workplaces, each with the same small set of roles and the same pairwise task structure, so the number of people grows while the number of role types stays fixed.

The question is: does there exist a measurable allocation rule assigning each \((z,e)\) to one of its two endpoint agents, such that almost every community’s allocation is EFkX? A solution is that measurable assignment rule, with each person receiving an integral bundle of goods. Agents in other communities value that bundle at zero, so the fairness comparisons that matter are exactly the within-community comparisons from the paper. A measurable solution exists iff \(H\) itself has an EFkX orientation. Thus I expect Class B: the graph’s combinatorics persist under population replication. This is a precise, author-recognizable continuous version of their orientation problem, though its complexity is inherited directly from the finite template.

A second, more population-active mirror rests on Theorem 3.1, also proved here. It says that for any \(k\in[1,n]\), the paper’s \(k\)-RoundRobin followed by Envy Cycle Elimination algorithm runs in polynomial time and produces a \(\frac{k}{k+1}\)-EFkX allocation.

Call its continuous counterpart High-Multiplicity Approximate-EFkX Allocation. An instance has finitely many additive valuation types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and finitely many standardized good types \(G\), each with a rational per-capita supply \(q_g\). Types describe the full value vector over \(G\); repeated copies of a good type are represented by separate slots if an individual could receive more than one. The regime is a large population of tenants, residents, or workers with a small set of valuation profiles, and supplies that scale with the population.

The task is to compute a measurable allocation of the population into integral bundles with the paper’s \(\frac{k}{k+1}\)-EFkX guarantee. Precisely, let \(\lambda_{t,B}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) agents assigned bundle \(B\subseteq G\). It must satisfy \(\sum_B\lambda_{t,B}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,B:g\in B}\lambda_{t,B}=q_g\). For every pair of positive-mass types \(t,s\), every bundle \(B,C\) with \(\lambda_{t,B},\lambda_{s,C}>0\), and every \(Y\subseteq C\) with \(|Y|=k\), require \(v_t(B)\geq \frac{k}{k+1}v_t(C\setminus Y)\). Thus the type averages may be fractional, but each unit of agent mass receives a whole bundle and fairness is checked across the bundles people actually receive.

I expect Class A here, through a type-compressed version of the paper’s allocation method, but Theorem 3.1 does not establish that. Clearing denominators gives a finite high-multiplicity instance, so the theorem guarantees a solution; its running time may still depend polynomially on the expanded population rather than on the binary encoding of the masses. The open computational question is whether one can produce a sparse \(\lambda\) in time polynomial in \(|G|\), \(\tau=|T|\), \(k\), and the input bit length \(L\).

The orientation result is my lead because it supplies the paper’s named hardness anchor; the general allocation result is the safer author-recognizability anchor. The weakest point is that preserving Theorem 4.4’s endpoint relation requires local community structure, which the type marginals \(\mu\) alone do not record. If instead each good type is made available to two whole agent classes, the model becomes more active in population mass, but it changes “valued by two particular agents” into “valued by two types,” and type splitting may undo the source reduction. I would treat that as a separate extension, not claim the paper’s NP-hardness transfers to it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The orientation result is the stronger negative case. Theorem 4.4 concerns a finite graph whose goods are edges and whose endpoints are particular agents. To replicate it across communities, each role-\(i\) agent in community \(z\) must value that community’s goods and not the corresponding goods elsewhere. With goods named globally, those are different value vectors for different \(z\), so the community coordinate is part of the complete type. That gives uncountably many types, not the finite-support population in the proposed mirror. Erase the coordinate and the local incidence pattern disappears. Keeping it yields a continuum of separate finite gadgets: it preserves the theorem by retaining each community’s identities, rather than by aggregating a population into types.

A shared-type version is more natural: have many agents of each vertex role and many copies of each edge type, assign each copy to an agent in one of its endpoint classes, and check fairness across the bundles people actually receive. But that no longer models the theorem’s orientation problem, where each edge is assigned to one of two particular agents. It may be a worthwhile new high-multiplicity problem; Theorem 4.4 alone does not establish that it is the right mirror. This objection is about what the type model can represent, not about whether the finite hardness would carry over.

Theorem 3.1 is much harder to dismiss. Its best mirror is close to the proponent’s \(\lambda_{t,B}\) formulation: rational masses of complete valuation types, per-capita supplies of good types, integral bundles for each assigned unit of mass, and the paper’s \(\frac{k}{k+1}\)-EFkX comparison across every bundle class that actually occurs. The programme explicitly allows supplies to scale with population and type-level averages to be fractional. So neither indivisible bundles nor fractional masses are a fundamental objection.

The strongest criticism is that Theorem 3.1 does not give an algorithm for this compact input. Round Robin and envy-cycle elimination act on the expanded list of agents and goods; clearing rational denominators can make that expansion large relative to the input bit length. Nor is the \(\lambda\) model an immediate LP: two bundle classes may coexist only if their fairness comparisons are compatible, making the choice of positive support part of the problem. That shows the theorem’s algorithm does not automatically compress. It does not show that the resulting computational question is unworthy; asking whether it can be solved in terms of type support and input bit length is exactly a high-multiplicity question.

The nearby high-multiplicity fair-allocation literature is not an answer to this mirror, and under the programme’s rules it is not a collision. It does show that compact multiplicities and fair allocation form a studied regime; for example, Bredereck et al. analyze binary-encoded multiplicities and parameterized fair-allocation problems with envy-freeness variants, including EFX ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04907)). That evidence strengthens the case that the allocation mirror is sensible.

So I can make a substantial case against using Theorem 4.4 as a continuous-population anchor: its faithful replication keeps the community identities that type aggregation is meant to remove. I cannot honestly defeat the stronger mirror of Theorem 3.1. It remains a plausible, author-recognizable high-multiplicity problem, and my negative case is weak precisely there.
