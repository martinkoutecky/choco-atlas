The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper offers no eligible computational anchor. It contains no numbered result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified.

The nearest results are Propositions 1–3, all proved in the paper, but they are mathematical characterizations. Proposition 1 relates convex preferences to betweenness, with a converse for complete relations on interval convexities. Proposition 2 says that the convexity of a bounded distributive lattice is generated by a set of orderings. Proposition 3 says that a reflexive, transitive relation on such a lattice is a convex preference exactly when it is compatible with the lattice order and satisfies meet dominance: if \(x \succeq y\), then \(x \wedge y \succeq y\). None states a computational result.

One could imagine many consumers sharing preference types over a common finite lattice, with \(\mu_t\) denoting each type’s population fraction. But the paper defines no population-level decision problem, aggregate rule, action variable, or objective for that society. Supplying one would create a new problem rather than continuize a problem the paper studies. Its convexity concerns the space of alternatives; that is not continuous population.

So there is no precise per-anchor continuous problem to hand to a prover under the requested criteria. Proposition 3 might inspire a separate computational question, such as recognizing convex preferences from a finite input, but that would be a new question, not a computational result asserted or used by this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no computational result to continuize. Propositions 1–3 characterize convex preferences and lattice convexity; none classifies an algorithmic problem. The paper gives no decision problem, input encoding, objective, or complexity claim for a continuous society to mirror.

The paper’s objects also belong to the alternative space, not the population. In Proposition 2, the set of orderings generates a convexity on \(L\); those orderings are not voter types. Repeating consumers who share a preference would leave each proposition unchanged. Treating \(X\) or \(L\) as the continuum would continuize the outcome space, which is outside ChoCo’s scope.

The strongest proposed population version would put mass on preference types over a fixed lattice and ask whether an aggregate preference is convex, or how to change the society to make it so. But Proposition 3 applies to one reflexive, transitive relation. A majority relation built from the population need not be transitive, and choosing an aggregation rule and a mass-transfer objective would add the central problem the paper never specifies. An intersection of convex preferences would preserve convex upper contours, but that makes the characterization straightforward; adding an optimization task would again create a new problem.

That follow-on could be worthwhile research. It is not a continuous mirror of a result in this paper. The negative case is strongest on that boundary: a plausible population scenario can be invented, but it cannot supply the missing computational result or make the paper’s alternative-space geometry into a population model.
