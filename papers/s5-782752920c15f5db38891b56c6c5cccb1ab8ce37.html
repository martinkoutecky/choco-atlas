The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying anchors in this paper: it contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result such as polynomial time or NP-hardness. Algorithm 1 and the empirical tables are numbered, but they are not such results. The paper’s account of priceability and tractability in the Method of Equal Shares is background about earlier work, not a named computational result proved here. So I can’t honestly supply one continuous problem *per anchor*.

The strongest positive direction is the computation at the paper’s core: producing a KES allocation and its receipts. A possible mirror, though not an anchor-backed one, is Continuous KES Allocation. Its instance has a finite project set \(P\), rational normalized costs \(c_p\), a finite set \(F\) of impact fields with rational weights \(w_f\), a budget split \(r\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete voter types. A type specifies the same normalized utility for every project and any other property the rule uses. The type masses sum to one; each field has its own project-utility vector \(v_f\).

Normalize the total budget to one. Type \(t\) starts with aggregate voter budget \(b_t=(1-r)\mu_t\), and field \(f\) starts with \(b_f=rw_f\). At each step, compute project support
\[ S(p)=\sum_t b_tu_t(p)+\sum_f b_fv_f(p). \]
Among affordable projects, select the one with greatest support, using a fixed tie rule. Charge each type and field in proportion to its contribution to \(S(p)\), update their remaining budgets, and continue until no project is affordable. The requested solution is the funded set and the receipts recording each type’s and field’s payments. This is a rule-computation problem: its selection criterion is the paper’s iterative rule, not a separate global objective.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime would be a city-wide grant vote with, say, \(100{,}000\) residents choosing among a fixed slate of projects, with repeated exact point-ballot patterns yielding a support of a few thousand types. A small deliberative committee could still supply the fixed impact-field signals and weights. That preserves the paper’s two channels and gives a substantial population with comparatively few types; the paper’s own committee of 38 does not. This mirror covers the allocation and receipt computation, not the co-design process or the empirical claims about learning, fairness, and participant behaviour.

For this proposed question, I would expect tractability: the direct implementation makes at most \(|P|\) selections and scans the projects and \(\tau+|F|\) agents each round, so it uses \(O(|P|^2(\tau+|F|))\) rational-arithmetic operations, with bit complexity to be bounded in terms of input length. It also suggests follow-up questions about exact agreement with finite KES instances, sensitivity to changes in \(\mu\), and how tie-breaking affects results.

My weakest point is that this is a computational reading of the algorithm, not a mirror of any hardness or complexity result in the paper. There is also a specific formal gap: Algorithm 1 initializes impact-field budgets, but its displayed support calculation and payment loop sum over and charge only individuals. The model above follows the prose’s intended dual-channel rule; authors would need to resolve that discrepancy before it could be treated as an exact mirror of the printed algorithm.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the paper supplies no named computational result to mirror. It proves no complexity theorem for KES. Algorithm 1 describes a procedure, the empirical sections compare outcomes, and the MES complexity claims are background from earlier work. So there are no result-based anchors here.

The proposed replacement—compute a KES allocation and receipts for voter types—is the strongest possible mirror, and a city-wide grant vote is a plausible population regime. The objection is that scaling this scenario pulls apart the paper’s defining dual role. Keep deliberation with a small committee, and the large society supplies only voter ballots; let the whole society deliberate, and the scale-up must specify how group discussion produces its signals. That weakens the claim that this is the paper’s hybrid process, though it does not rule out an authors-recognizable variant.

Restricting the question to the allocation step avoids that problem: treat voter ballots and field scores as inputs, group identical ballots, and compute the funded set and type-level receipts. That is a coherent computational question, but the paper neither states it as a complexity problem nor studies its type-compressed form. There is also a mismatch between the prose and the pseudocode: Algorithm 1 initializes field budgets but calculates support and payments using individuals alone. The prose makes the intended dual-channel version easy to repair, so this is a formal gap, not a decisive objection.

I can make a case that the paper offers no theorem-backed anchor and that a large-scale deployment would alter its participatory design. I cannot honestly make the universal case that no worthwhile mirror exists. The narrow allocation mirror survives those objections; the negative case is weak precisely there.
