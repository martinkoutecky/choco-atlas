Why no mirror

Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 4.2 establish fairness properties of allocations that already maximize NSW; neither says how to compute one or how hard that computation is. The paper’s continuous fairness questions are well-posed, but its computational Max-NSW question appears only as future work, not as a numbered computational result of this paper.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the fairness guarantees of Theorems 3.1 and 4.2 for allocations maximizing NSW over mass assignments. It leaves the computation of such allocations, the paper’s tightness examples, and its other claims unaddressed.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 3.1, proved here, says every Max-NSW allocation is \(1/4\)-EF1 and Pareto optimal. Theorem 4.2, also proved here, says every Max-NSW allocation is \((1/2-5/\kappa^{1/4})\)-EF1, where \(\kappa=\min_{i,j}B_i/c_j\). Both are fairness guarantees, not results about a problem being in P, hard, or approximable by an algorithm. Section 5 explicitly says computing Max-NSW was not studied; its observation that APX-hardness follows from the unconstrained case is an unnumbered claim, not a qualifying anchor.

The strongest nearby mirror is still quite natural. Consider a large allocation programme serving cohorts of applicants who share the same additive values for standardized goods and the same capacity budget. A type \(t\) records that full value vector and budget; let its population mass be \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The regime is one where the number of applicants is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of types. Let goods come in finitely many classes \(g\), identical in cost and value to every type, with per-capita supply \(q_g\). Scaling supplies with the population is part of making the allocation model meaningful. Each recipient still gets an integral bundle; only the mass of recipients assigned each bundle is continuous.

Write \(y_{t,S}\) for the mass of type \(t\) receiving feasible integral bundle \(S\). The constraints are \(\sum_S y_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S}a_g(S)y_{t,S}\le q_g\), where \(a_g(S)\) is the number of goods of class \(g\) in \(S\), and \(\sum_g c_g a_g(S)\le B_t\). The continuous welfare objective is \(\sum_{t,S}y_{t,S}\log v_t(S)\), assuming positive utilities; it is the mass version of maximizing the product of individual utilities. This configuration model keeps individual bundles and budgets intact. Replacing them with fractional bundles or comparing only type-average utilities would change the question.

A guarantee-level mirror of Theorem 3.1 would ask: for every such instance, is every welfare-maximizing mass allocation \(1/4\)-EF1 and Pareto optimal? Here EF1 means that for every two type-bundle pairs with positive assigned mass, every subbundle \(R\) of the second bundle that fits the first type’s budget has some item \(j\in R\) for which \(v_t(S)\ge \tfrac14 v_t(R-j)\). Theorem 4.2 gives a second, stronger question for instances with large \(\kappa=\min_{t,g}B_t/c_g\): does every maximizer satisfy the stated \((1/2-5/\kappa^{1/4})\)-EF1 bound? I would lead with this large-budget mirror: the per-capita inventory model preserves the small-item-versus-budget condition that drives the paper’s refinement.

I expect the fairness guarantees to extend: the proofs compare reallocations affecting two agents, and those exchanges appear adaptable to equal infinitesimal slices of mass. That is a tractable structural theorem question. It also generates useful follow-ups: do the tight examples remain tight at continuum scale, and does the guarantee survive if the welfare-maximizing allocation is only approximate? Computing the maximizer is a separate problem. Its configuration LP has exponentially many bundle variables, and pricing involves budgeted bundle selection. The paper does not resolve that computation, so I cannot classify it from these theorems.

The weak point is decisive for the requested role: these are mirrors of the paper’s fairness results, not continuous mirrors of named computational results. The paper may support a credible theorem-level continuation, but it does not supply a qualifying computational anchor or establish a complexity landscape for that continuation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proposed anchors is that neither is a computational result. Theorem 3.1 guarantees that a Max-NSW allocation is \(1/4\)-EF1; Theorem 4.2 improves that guarantee when budgets are large. Both describe properties of an optimum, not the complexity of finding one. Their continuous versions can be stated cleanly by assigning mass to integral bundles and defining envy between positive-mass bundle assignments. But those remain fairness theorems, outside ChoCo’s computational question. The large-budget condition also survives naturally when goods come in classes with per-capita supplies, so that is not a sound objection to the model. The paper itself says that computing Max-NSW was not studied and presents it as future work. ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.03766))

That is a strong reason to reject these *two theorems* as computational anchors. It does not establish the requested universal claim. A better computational question is to maximize \(\sum_{t,S} y_{t,S}\log v_t(S)\) over masses assigned to feasible integral bundles, subject to type-mass and per-capita supply constraints. Its exponentially many bundle variables give a concrete pricing problem. A large programme with repeated cohorts of applicants who share valuations and budgets is a plausible setting. The paper’s own open computational question points toward this continuation, even though it is not a result to mirror.

High-multiplicity work on Nash social welfare reinforces that this regime is sensible; it cannot be used to dismiss the mirror as already done. Recent work studies high-multiplicity NSW directly, including hardness and approximation results for restricted type counts. ([high-multiplicity NSW paper](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3233/FAIA240881))

So I can make a strong case that Theorems 3.1 and 4.2 do not provide eligible computational anchors. I cannot honestly make the broader case that no worthwhile continuous computational question arises from this paper: the high-multiplicity Max-NSW problem is a credible follow-on, even if it is not a mirror of a result the authors proved.
