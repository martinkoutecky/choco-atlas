The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a continuum version of the paper’s link between bargaining equilibria and the Bayesian core. It is plausible for a large population, but there is a problem with the requested anchors: the paper has no numbered computational-complexity result. Its only numbered theorem is relevant, but it does not establish that a problem is tractable or hard, or give a computational formulation.

The closest anchor is Theorem 1, a result the authors claim here, stated informally; the Bayesian-core concept itself comes from their cited earlier work [2]. It says: if the Bayesian core (BC) of a Bayesian coalitional game \(G\), and the BC of each subgame of \(G\), are nonempty, then regardless of nature’s choice of proposer, the corresponding fixed-belief discounted bargaining game has a sequential equilibrium under fixed beliefs (SEFB) that produces a BC element. Conversely, an order-independent SEFB profile produces a configuration in the BC of the underlying game. The paper’s polynomial-program formulation of PBE and its discussion of the resulting complexity are unnumbered, so they cannot serve as anchors under the rule you set.

A plausible regime is a large platform forming project teams from a much larger pool of contractors: thousands or millions of people, but only a small number of recurring capability and bargaining types. A complete type \(\theta\) would include a contractor’s capabilities, reservation value, discount rate, feasible offers, and initial beliefs about partners. Thus, \(\mu_\theta\) means the fraction of the active population of type \(\theta\), and \(\tau\), the number of represented types, can remain small as the population grows. That is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario if people of the same type really are interchangeable in every feature the model uses.

I would call the candidate problem High-Multiplicity SEFB-to-Core Limit. Its input is a finite type set \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu_\theta\), a finite menu of proposals and payoff shares, reservation values, values for each allowed coalition composition, a prior over hidden partner types with a Bayesian update rule, a maximum coalition size \(q\), and a discount rate \(\rho\). The finite approximations have \(n\mu_\theta\) agents of each type, \(n\) bargaining stages, one uniformly chosen active proposer per stage, and discount factor \(\delta_n=e^{-\rho/n}\). This puts the bargaining process on a unit time horizon as \(n\) grows.

The question is whether there is a sequence of order-independent SEFB profiles whose behavior and induced allocations converge as \(n\to\infty\). A solution is the limiting type-contingent strategy and posterior system, together with an aggregate measure \(\lambda_{r,a}\) of coalitions of composition \(r\) using allocation \(a\). Feasibility requires \(\sum_{r,a}r_\theta\lambda_{r,a}\leq\mu_\theta\) for every type. The induced allocation must be a Bayesian-core element: no feasible type-contingent deviation plan can improve the interim payoff of every participating type. Agents maximize their discounted expected share, as in the paper; the problem asks for an equilibrium and core allocation, not a social-welfare optimum.

My provisional expectation is continuum-specific hardness for the general problem: even after individual identities disappear, Bayesian updating and sequential best responses remain coupled equilibrium constraints. That is a conjecture, not a result of the paper; aggregation could instead make bounded-\(q\), small-\(\tau\) cases tractable. The useful follow-up questions are whether the discrete equilibria have such limits, whether the limiting allocation is a core element, and how complexity depends on \(\tau\), \(q\), and input bit length.

This mirror covers only Theorem 1’s equilibrium/core connection. It does not claim that the paper’s heuristic algorithm or its empirical results have a continuous-population analogue. The paper’s future work on a continuous bargaining-action space is a different continuity angle: it changes the actions, not the society.

The weak point is the population limit itself. The paper forms one coalition at a time from named agents; the proposed limit needs a growing number of stages, scaled discounting, and anonymous treatment of interchangeable partners. Those are defensible co-modifications for this regime, but the paper does not establish that its SEFB result survives them. So this is a credible candidate mirror, not a fully anchored complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 does not give this paper a computational anchor. It conditionally connects nonempty Bayesian cores across subgames to the existence of an order-independent SEFB outcome, and conversely. It gives no complexity classification or algorithm. The paper’s actual computational discussion—the polynomial constraints for PBE, the decision-procedure bound, and the heuristic—is unnumbered. A continuum version of Theorem 1 could be an interesting equilibrium-limit result, but that is an analytic question, not the complexity question ChoCo is set up to chart.

The contractor-pool scenario is plausible: people with the same capabilities and bargaining attributes could be genuinely interchangeable. The objection is not that the population cannot have high multiplicity. It is that the proposed High-Multiplicity SEFB-to-Core Limit asks whether equilibria and allocations converge as the number of agents and stages grows. That is a new limit problem, not a computational result of the paper. The choice \(\delta_n=e^{-\rho/n}\) sensibly avoids discounting away the horizon, but it also makes the growing horizon part of the new model. Theorem 1 concerns fixed-belief equilibria; a limit with evolving posteriors must additionally specify and analyze belief dynamics over the growing histories. If beliefs stay fixed, the mirror drops the learning central to the paper’s bargaining process.

One could sharpen the idea into a finite-input computation problem by imposing, for example, a bounded team size, finite proposals, and type-symmetric strategies. That might be worth studying. But those restrictions define a new anonymous bargaining model; they do not make Theorem 1 a complexity result. The paper does not establish that its equilibrium/core connection survives those changes, nor supply a finite representation of the limiting strategies and posterior system.

So the strongest negative verdict is that the proponent has not established a worthwhile computational mirror anchored in this paper. I would not claim that no high-multiplicity bargaining problem could merit ChoCo’s time: the more carefully specified version above could. That makes the universal negative case weak, but it also means the proposed limit should be treated as a plausible new research direction, not as a continuous counterpart of a computational result this paper proved.
