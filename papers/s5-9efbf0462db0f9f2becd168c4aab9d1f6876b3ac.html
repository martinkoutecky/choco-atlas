The continuous mirror question

Given finite sets of applicant types \(A\) and position types \(B\), rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\), an acceptable-pair set \(E\), strict applicant rankings \(\succ_a\) over acceptable position types, and weak position rankings \(\succ_b\) over acceptable applicant types, find a mass assignment \(x_{ab}\geq 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{(a,b)\in E}x_{ab}\), subject to \(\sum_b x_{ab}\leq\mu_a\), \(\sum_a x_{ab}\leq\nu_b\), and stability. For each \((a,b)\in E\), define \(D_A(a,b)=\mu_a-\sum_{b'}x_{ab'}+\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'}\) and \(D_B(a,b)=\nu_b-\sum_{a'}x_{a'b}+\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}\); require \(D_A(a,b)=0\) or \(D_B(a,b)=0\).

The model it lives in

A typed bipartite matching market with rational applicant and position masses, type-level acceptability and preferences, mass assignment variables \(x_{ab}\), and the objective of maximizing total matched mass subject to no acceptable type pair having positive improvement mass on both sides.

What the mirror covers

Covers the maximum-cardinality stable-matching objective underlying Theorem 2; it does not transfer the paper's \(13/9\) analysis or its tightness result for the named algorithm.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the maximum stable mass assignment be computed in time polynomial in \(p,q,L\), rather than \(2^{pq}\operatorname{poly}(pq,L)\)?
  • Can the two-proposal algorithm be given a mass-based version, and what approximation guarantee would it achieve?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is a mirror of the maximum-cardinality stable matching problem, anchored by Theorem 2. It says that for a bipartite stable marriage instance with incomplete lists and ties on only one side, the two-proposal algorithm of Huang and Telikepalli returns a stable matching \(M\) with \(|M|\geq 9|\mathrm{OPT}|/13\). Bauckholt and coauthors prove this \(13/9\) approximation guarantee here, by refining the algorithm’s analysis; the algorithm itself comes from earlier work. The paper’s Introduction also mentions NP-hardness, but cites earlier work for it rather than proving it as a numbered result, so I would not use that as an anchor. [The paper’s Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/html/1805.05391)

Call the mirror Maximum Stable Matching over One-Sided-Tie Type Masses. An instance has applicant types \(A\) and position types \(B\), with rational masses \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) summing to one on each side. A type specifies the complete preferences and acceptable partners of its members. Applicants strictly rank acceptable position types; positions weakly rank acceptable applicant types, with ties allowed. A market of many applicants and positions can plausibly have far fewer preference types—for example, a large centralized placement market with standardized applicant rankings over role categories and programs that treat applicants in the same credential band alike.

A solution is a nonnegative mass assignment \(x_{ab}\), with \(x_{ab}=0\) for unacceptable pairs, \(\sum_b x_{ab}\leq\mu_a\), and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\leq\nu_b\). It represents the mass of type \(a\) matched to type \(b\); leftover mass is unmatched. The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{a,b}x_{ab}\). For an acceptable pair \((a,b)\), let
\[ D_A(a,b)=\mu_a-\sum_{b'}x_{ab'}+\sum_{b':\,b\succ_a b'}x_{ab'} \]
and
\[ D_B(a,b)=\nu_b-\sum_{a'}x_{a'b}+\sum_{a':\,a\succ_b a'}x_{a'b}. \]
These are the masses on each side that would strictly prefer the pair to their current assignment or being unmatched. Require \(D_A(a,b)=0\) or \(D_B(a,b)=0\) for every acceptable pair. This is weak stability for the atomless market: if both improving masses were positive, some positive mass could form a blocking match. The output is an optimal stable mass assignment.

I expect this typed problem to be tractable when support is modest: it has an exact algorithm taking \(2^{pq}\operatorname{poly}(pq,L)\) time, where \(p=|A|\), \(q=|B|\), and \(L\) is the input bit length. For each acceptable pair, choose which side has zero improving mass, impose that linear equality, and solve the resulting rational LP. Every stable assignment satisfies at least one such choice for each pair, so taking the best LP optimum finds the maximum. This gives fixed-parameter tractability in support size and polynomial time for fixed support. It is a Class A result for the high-multiplicity regime, while polynomial time in \(p,q,L\) remains open. The mirror covers the maximum-cardinality one-sided-ties problem behind Theorem 2; it does not claim the paper’s \(13/9\) guarantee itself carries over. That motivates a further question: can the two-proposal algorithm be given a mass-based version, and what approximation factor would it achieve?

The main weakness is the type model’s preference granularity. The paper ranks named individuals; this mirror ranks interchangeable cohort types. A convincing application must really have agents who compare categories or roles, with members of a category interchangeable for matching. If applicants must strictly distinguish every individual position, or positions distinguish every individual applicant, the support may grow with the population and the proposed advantage recedes. The mirror is strongest for large standardized markets where those category preferences are genuine.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest anchor is real: the paper proves an approximation guarantee for a named computational problem, and also proves that guarantee tight for the algorithm it analyzes. There is a natural candidate mirror: maximize matched mass subject to stability in a market with rational masses of applicants and positions. A fixed typed market can be expanded into a finite market of clones, so this is a genuine high-multiplicity version of stable matching—not just a continuous outcome space.

The best case against is that the compression needed to make this a *small-support* mirror changes the paper’s central input structure. In the paper, a type must include an agent’s acceptable partners and preferences over them. For an arbitrary bipartite graph, those relations are to named individuals; preserving them as the market grows tends to make the number of types grow too. Keeping support small instead requires cohorts whose members are interchangeable and whose interactions are determined by cohort-level preferences. That is a meaningful typed market, but a narrower, block-structured problem than the one the paper’s approximation theorem covers. The paper gives little reason to think its \(13/9\) example or the underlying algorithm’s guarantee captures that restricted regime.

The better version of the mirror would still be worth stating: define stability for divisible cohorts, maximize matched mass, and ask whether a mass version of the proposal algorithm has an approximation guarantee. But the guarantee does not carry over automatically. The algorithm tracks individual proposals, promotion states, and a graph whose degrees are at most two; an aggregate version would need new rules for how mass proposals are accepted, rejected, and tied. The theorem’s tightness result concerns the specified finite algorithm, not an inherent \(13/9\) barrier for mass markets. So this is a fresh algorithm-design question, rather than a direct continuization of the paper’s proof.

There is a practical reason to doubt its payoff relative to other mirrors. Stability gives a disjunction for each type pair: at least one side must have no mass that would form a blocking pair. Enumerating those choices yields an exact algorithm exponential in the number of type pairs. That is a legitimate parameterized result, but by itself it offers no polynomial-time method in support size and no evident column-generation or continuous-optimization advantage. It also does not defeat the mirror: the programme includes parameterized algorithms, and better methods might exist.

So the negative case is mainly about fit and likely reach: this paper’s arbitrary, identity-specific preference graphs may compress poorly, while a small-support mirror studies a narrower cohort market and the paper’s approximation analysis does not transfer directly. That is a reason to rank the mirror below stronger candidates, not a sound basis for claiming that no worthwhile scenario exists. Large placement markets with genuinely interchangeable cohorts are plausible, and the corrected mass problem survives the objections above. The universal negative case is weak.
