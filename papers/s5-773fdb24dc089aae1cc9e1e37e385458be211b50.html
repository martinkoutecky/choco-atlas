The continuous mirror question

Given rational finite-support mass tables for seller and buyer valuation types across independently matched bilateral-trade venues, compute an optimal Bayesian incentive-compatible, interim individually rational, strongly budget-balanced mechanism for each venue type pair.

The model it lives in

Types are role and valuation, with masses μ_S(s) and μ_B(b); venues pair seller and buyer types independently. Choose allocation probabilities x(s,b) and implementing payments to maximize aggregate gains from trade, subject to per-venue BIC, interim IR, and SBB constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers finite-support computation of the second-best mechanism; the half-efficiency bound and tightness family carry over as welfare consequences, but are not the computational basis. It leaves fixed-price comparisons and coupled pooled markets outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give an explicit polynomial bit-complexity bound in the support sizes and input bit length for solving the LP and recovering implementing payments.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror I see is a population of many independent bilateral trading venues. Each venue has one seller and one buyer, one unit of the good, and its own mechanism. Seller types are role and valuation; buyer types are role and valuation. A type is complete because agents with the same role and value have the same preferences and face the same mechanism. The masses \(\mu_S(s)\) and \(\mu_B(b)\) are the fractions of each role population with each valuation. Pair venues according to the product of those distributions. The plausible regime is many venues with relatively few distinct valuations, so the number of agents is large while the support sizes stay moderate.

At each venue the decision is an allocation probability \(x(s,b)\) and payments to the buyer and seller. The mechanism maximizes aggregate expected gains from trade, subject to Bayesian incentive compatibility, interim individual rationality, and strong budget balance. Per venue, the objective is

\[ \sum_{s,b}\mu_S(s)\mu_B(b)(b-s)x(s,b), \]

and the first-best benchmark replaces \(b-s\) by \((b-s)_+\). Repeating the same mechanism across venues makes these per-venue quantities the aggregate quantities too. This is a precise mass interpretation: masses are population shares across venues, not beliefs about the types of two named people.

My lead question is the sharp population efficiency guarantee: over all such mass distributions with positive first-best gains, what is the largest \(\rho\) for which every population admits a BIC, interim-IR, strongly budget-balanced mechanism with gains at least \(\rho\) times first best? A solution should give a universal guarantee and a family of populations showing it cannot be improved.

The strongest negative anchor is Theorem 6.2, proved in this paper. It states that no BIC, IR, weakly budget-balanced mechanism can guarantee a worst-case approximation ratio to first-best GFT greater than \(1/2\). In the mirror, the problem is to find populations whose optimal second-best GFT is below any proposed guarantee \(q>1/2\), with an argument that certifies optimality. The paper gives a tight family; its grid approximation makes finite valuation supports a natural form for these population instances. I expect this boundary question to be tractable by explicit analysis, as it is in the paper. It is a welfare impossibility, not a computational hardness result.

The matching positive anchor is Theorem 5.12, also proved here. It states that for every pair of independent buyer and seller distributions, the second-best GFT is at least half of first-best GFT. Its population question is constructive: given rational mass tables, return a feasible mechanism attaining at least half the first-best gains. I expect this to be tractable, since the exact optimum can be computed by the LP below.

The computational anchor is Corollary 3.2, proved here and used in the paper’s analysis. For seller values \(s_i\) with masses \(p_i\) and buyer values \(b_j\) with masses \(q_j\), it formulates second-best GFT as maximizing

\[ \sum_{i,j}p_iq_j(b_j-s_i)x(s_i,b_j) \]

over \(0\leq x(s_i,b_j)\leq1\), subject to the discrete budget-balance constraint

\[ \sum_{i,j}p_iq_jx(s_i,b_j) \left( b_j+(b_j-b_{j+1})\frac{1-F_B(b_j)}{q_j} -s_i-(s_i-s_{i-1})\frac{F_S(s_{i-1})}{p_i} \right)\geq0, \]

and monotonicity of interim trade probabilities: seller interim allocation is nonincreasing in seller value, and buyer interim allocation is nondecreasing in buyer value. The endpoint conventions are \(b_{N+1}=b_N\) and \(s_{-1}=s_0\). The corresponding population problem is Mass Second-Best Optimization: given rational masses and valuation lists, output an optimal mechanism. An allocation table is the LP solution; payments can then be recovered to implement it. With explicitly listed supports, the LP has a number of variables and constraints polynomial in the support sizes, and rational LP algorithms give a polynomial-time method in the input bit length. That is an inference from the formulation, not a complexity classification stated by the paper.

These anchors cover the second-best bilateral-trade objective and its half-efficiency boundary. They do not cover the paper’s fixed-price literature or a pooled market where buyers and sellers compete across venues. A pooled market would add allocation and market-clearing constraints absent from the authors’ problem.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper’s priors describe uncertainty about one buyer and one seller, while my mirror describes population shares across many separate venues. The per-venue mathematics is exactly theirs, so the two theorems transfer directly, but the population interpretation adds no interaction among venues and no new complexity phenomenon. Still, the prior distributions are not themselves a population of agents. A researcher could reasonably accept the repeated-venue setting as their bilateral-trade problem at high multiplicity, while judging it a modest mirror whose central result is already known.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The repeated-venue construction is coherent, and its types can properly include role and valuation. Its weakness is that every venue is an independent copy of the paper’s original two-agent problem. With \(N\) venues, expected total gains from trade and expected first-best gains are each \(N\) times their per-venue values. The incentive and budget constraints remain venue by venue, so the aggregate optimization is exactly the paper’s optimization for \(F_S\) and \(F_B\). The population shares add no new decision structure.

That defeats both welfare anchors by collision. The paper already formulates the problem for arbitrary Borel probability distributions in Proposition 3.1, proves the half guarantee in Theorem 5.12, and gives a tight family in Theorem 6.2. Theorem 3.5 also relates the continuous-distribution worst case to a discrete-support worst case. These results concern distributions of two agents’ valuations rather than a society of traders, so the population interpretation is a change in what the measures represent. But under the proposed independent-venue model, it changes no mathematics: the guarantee and tightness claim are already answered by the paper.

Corollary 3.2 is the only clear computational anchor. For finite seller and buyer supports, its allocation variables number \(\tau_S\tau_B\); its constraints are the budget inequality, allocation bounds, and interim monotonicity constraints. Given rational masses, this is a rational LP, and payments can be recovered from the allocation rule. So the proposed “Mass Second-Best Optimization” uses the paper’s own LP as its algorithm. That makes it an exact restatement of the computational object, rather than a new high-multiplicity problem whose complexity landscape remains to be charted.

A pooled market could introduce real population-level coupling—for example, shared supply or cross-subsidies across trades. That may be a worthwhile research direction, but it changes the feasibility and incentive constraints. The paper’s single-buyer, single-seller theorems would no longer be the results being mirrored; one would be studying a many-agent market extension.

The negative case is strongest for the anchors actually raised: their best population interpretation either reproduces the paper’s continuous-distribution model or changes the problem enough that the named results no longer anchor it. The universal claim is less secure: a carefully chosen coupled-market model could be both recognizable to the authors and worth studying, but this paper alone does not identify one.
