The continuous mirror question

Given finite \(G\) and \(C\), support \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational type data \((A_t,r_t,d_t,b_t)\), and per-capita supplies \(q_j\), find exact prices \(p\) and per-request bundles \(x_t\). Each \(x_t\) must minimize \(d_t\cdot x\) subject to \(A_tx\ge r_t\), \(p\cdot x\le b_t\), and \(x\ge0\); aggregate consumption must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le q_j\), with equality whenever \(p_j>0\), on instances whose scaled representative-agent market satisfies extensibility and sufficient demand.

The model it lives in

Use one representative agent per type with aggregate bundle \(y_t=\mu_t x_t\), covering right-hand sides \(\mu_t r_t\), budget \(\mu_t b_t\), and supply \(q_j\); retain the type's delays \(d_t\) and covering coefficients \(A_t\). This exactly represents the type-mass demand and clearing conditions, so Theorem 2 applies when the scaled market meets its hypotheses.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2's equilibrium-computation algorithm. It leaves the existence result, equilibrium-price geometry examples, fairness properties, and incentive-compatibility results unmodeled.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which scheduling, laminar-assignment, and network-flow application families retain extensibility and sufficient demand after mass scaling?
  • What finite-to-continuous dictionary best describes replicated discrete cohorts and rational type masses?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a type-mass version of the paper’s equilibrium-computation problem. It keeps the finite set of goods, the covering constraints, budgets, delays, and market-clearing prices. Only the agents become a population distribution. This fits a large cloud or scheduling market where many requests share a small number of exact workload templates, and capacity grows with the cohort. A type must include everything the model uses: its budget, covering matrix and requirements, delay vector, and any restrictions on eligible goods. Thus \(\mu_t\) means the fraction of requests with the same complete specification, not merely similar jobs.

Here is the problem I would hand to a prover. The input is a finite goods set \(G\), covering constraints \(C\), a support \(T\) of \(\tau\) types with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational type data \((A_t,r_t,d_t,b_t)\), and rational per-capita supplies \(q_j\). Each type gets a per-request bundle \(x_t\). At prices \(p\), \(x_t\) must minimize \(d_t\cdot x\) among bundles satisfying \(A_tx\ge r_t\), \(x\ge0\), and \(p\cdot x\le b_t\). Aggregate supply must satisfy \(\sum_t\mu_t x_{tj}\le q_j\), with equality whenever \(p_j>0\). The task is to output exact prices and type bundles meeting these conditions. Normalizing each \(q_j\) to \(1\) is optional. For a cohort of \(N\) requests, per-capita normalization means physical capacity scales as \(Nq_j\); this scaling is the co-modification needed to keep the market feasible as the population grows.

The lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for any finite market satisfying extensibility and sufficient demand, there is a polynomial-time algorithm that computes an equilibrium allocation and prices, with running time polynomial in the bit description of the market. This is the paper’s named in-P result; it is proved here, with the algorithm’s full analysis in Appendix F. The paper mentions PPAD-hardness for a broader model via Rubinstein, but that is a cited result, not a numbered computational theorem of this paper, so I do not use it as an anchor.

For the continuous problem, extensibility should mean its mass-level counterpart: take any measurable subpopulation and any supply-feasible assignment minimizing its total delay; when further mass of a type is added, an optimal assignment for the larger population can preserve the delays of the incumbents. Sufficient demand also needs a cohort-scale form. One explicit, strong version is that every zero-price delay-minimizing bundle \(z_t\) for each positive-mass type has some good \(j\) with \(\mu_t z_{tj}>q_j\). Then a type bundle with budget slack would have to be zero-price optimal, yet assigning it to the whole type cohort alone would exceed supply. This recovers the budget-tight feature used in the paper’s equilibrium algorithm. It is a stronger mass-scaled promise, not a claim that Definition 4 transfers unchanged.

I expect this problem to be Class A, tractable, under those promises. The paper’s method gives a credible route: a parameterized allocation LP and its dual, followed by a search over segments using a surplus-budget function and submodular minimization. With identical copies compressed into types, candidate segments become subpopulation masses rather than subsets of named agents. The open algorithmic question is whether that mass optimization has an exact representation that runs in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,|C|\), and the total input bit length \(L\). The result would mirror the same equilibrium question: each request still chooses a minimum-delay affordable bundle, and prices still clear the shared resources. The goods remain finite and divisible as in the paper; the continuous object is the society.

This mirror covers Theorem 2 only. It does not try to continuize the paper’s fairness claims, incentive-compatibility results, or examples of non-convex equilibrium-price sets. Further questions include whether basic scheduling, laminar restricted assignment, and the stated network-flow cases satisfy mass-extensibility; whether finite markets with \(N\) replicated requests admit a precise approximation or convergence dictionary with the type-mass problem; and whether the mass algorithm can avoid a running time polynomial in \(N\) when masses are encoded in binary.

My weakest point is that Theorem 2’s algorithm is polynomial in the finite market’s description, which includes the number of agents. Expanding a rational mass distribution into repeated agents can take time polynomial in \(N\), but \(N\) may be exponential in the bit length of the masses. The theorem therefore does not establish the type-compressed result. Also, the mass versions of extensibility and sufficient demand need justification in real application classes. Those are substantial open steps, but they leave a recognizable mirror of the authors’ computational problem: finding equilibrium prices and allocations for a high-multiplicity population of covering-constrained agents.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has one anchor, Theorem 2, and it is a genuine computational result: the paper gives a polynomial-time equilibrium algorithm under extensibility and sufficient demand. The skeptical point is that the theorem does not itself give a compressed, type-mass algorithm. Its input lists agents explicitly, and its segment search minimizes a submodular function over subsets of those agents. With rational masses, the population may represent far more agents than the input has bits; the paper proves neither that the search can be compressed to \(\tau\) types nor that its subset optimization has an exact polynomial-time mass analogue.

The proposed promises also need more work. Extensibility in the paper concerns extending an allocation for a finite subset while preserving incumbents’ delays. A mass version must say what happens when a positive fraction of a type is added, and whether total supply stays fixed during that extension. Sufficient demand has a sharper mismatch: in the finite model, a feasible allocation gives each agent at most one unit of any good, so a zero-price optimum demanding more than one unit cannot be that agent’s equilibrium bundle. In an atomless model, only aggregate consumption is bounded. A positive but small fraction of a type can choose a high-demand zero-price optimum without exceeding aggregate supply. Thus the proponent’s condition that each full-type zero-price optimum exceeds per-capita supply does not establish the budget-tight property used by the theorem’s algorithm.

That is a serious gap in the proposed route from Theorem 2 to a tractability claim. But it does not defeat the stronger mirror: directly ask for exact equilibrium prices and type-level allocations in the specified mass market, with aggregate supply constraints. This is a well-posed computational problem, and the authors could recognize it as their equilibrium-computation problem even if the answer is that their algorithm does not extend, or that a different demand and extensibility condition is needed. The finite algorithm’s failure to transfer is not evidence that the question is unworthy; it is part of what the question would investigate.

So the strongest negative case is that the proponent has not justified the claimed mass-level hypotheses or the proposed algorithmic path. I cannot honestly turn that into the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. The paper’s computational theorem remains a viable anchor, and a carefully specified type-mass equilibrium problem survives the objections.
