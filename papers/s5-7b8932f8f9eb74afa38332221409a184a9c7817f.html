The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of customer types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), and nonnegative rational connection costs \(\gamma\) on the complete graph over \(T\cup\{s\}\), let \(q\in[0,\mu]\) be a coalition mass vector and let \(C(q)\) be the minimum spanning-tree cost connecting \(s\) to \(\operatorname{supp}(q)=\{t:q_t>0\}\). Set \(\rho(q)=\min\{\sum_t q_t,1-\sum_t q_t\}\). Classify the complexity, as a function of \(|T|\) and the binary input length, of computing charges \(p_t\) and the maximum feasible \(\epsilon^\star\) subject to \(\sum_t\mu_t p_t=C(\mu)\) and \(\sum_t q_t p_t\le C(q)-\epsilon^\star\rho(q)\) for every \(q\in[0,\mu]\).

The model it lives in

An atomless population of customers is described by finitely many connection-cost types \(T\), with rational mass \(\mu_t\) per type. A coalition may contain any mass vector \(q\in[0,\mu]\); its connection cost is the MST cost \(C(q)\) on the supply node and the types in its support. A type-level charge \(p_t\) must allocate total cost efficiently, and the least-core margin \(\epsilon\) is normalized by the smaller of coalition mass and its complement, \(\rho(q)=\min\{\sum_t q_t,1-\sum_t q_t\}\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the least-core allocation task anchored by Theorem 3.3, adapted to the stated customer-mass model and smaller-side priority. It leaves the theorem’s other priority functions, the \(f\)-nucleoli, and the matching-game and sports-competition results without mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the minimum-cover reduction extend to the smaller-side priority \(\rho(q)\)?
  • Can the continuous least-core problem be reduced to a finite formulation with running time polynomial in \(|T|\) and the input bit length?
  • What exact dictionary relates finite high-multiplicity MCST games to this mass-based model?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is one theorem in Chapter 3: Theorem 3.3, proved in the thesis. It says that for every *feasible* priority function \(f\), computing an allocation in the \(f\)-least core of a minimum-cost spanning-tree game is NP-hard. Here, feasibility is defined through a reduction from minimum cover: for the constructed graph, the proposed value \(\zeta_f\) must be the optimum of the \(f\)-least-core LP, and among covers \(D\) with \(q\le |D|\le q+2\), equality \(\zeta_f=\zeta_f(D)\) must hold exactly for minimum covers. The theorem’s formula is \(\zeta_f(D)=\frac{|D|+2q-1}{|D|f^u+3qf^w+f(N\setminus\{g\})+f(D\cup W\cup\{g\})}\), with \(f^u=f(N\setminus\{u\})\) and \(f^w=f(N\setminus\{w\})\). The thesis verifies that familiar priorities, including \(f(S)=|S|\), fall within the relevant class. The reduction’s hard choices are which network terminals form a cover.

The mirror I would put to a prover is a continuous least-core problem for a population of network customers. The instance has a finite set \(T\) of customer types, rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to one, a supply node \(s\), and rational connection costs \(\gamma\) on a complete graph over \(T\cup\{s\}\). A type records everything relevant to the connection cost: for example, location and service profile. For coalition mass \(q\), where \(0\le q_t\le\mu_t\), let \(\operatorname{supp}(q)=\{t:q_t>0\}\) and let \(C(q)\) be the minimum spanning-tree cost connecting \(s\) to \(\operatorname{supp}(q)\). Thus, any positive mass of a type needs that type’s location connected; additional customers of the same type share that connection.

A solution is a rational per-unit charge \(p_t\) for each type and a largest fairness margin \(\epsilon^\star\). Efficiency requires \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t p_t=C(\mu)\). For every coalition mass \(q\), require \(\sum_t q_t p_t\le C(q)-\epsilon\rho(q)\), where \(\rho(q)=\min\{\sum_t q_t,\,1-\sum_t q_t\}\). The question is to compute an efficient charge vector \(p\) attaining the maximum feasible \(\epsilon\), together with \(\epsilon^\star\).

The two-sided mass factor is a deliberate co-modification. With an atomless population, coalition mass can approach either zero or the whole society arbitrarily closely. A direct extension of the finite per-capita margin using only \(\sum_t q_t\) would therefore collapse at the grand-coalition boundary. The smaller-side normalization keeps the least-core question meaningful for both a small coalition and a small group left outside it. Its finite counterpart weights a coalition by the size of the smaller side, rather than applying the thesis’s exact finite priority unchanged.

The regime is large groups of customers who share a modest number of relevant profiles: for example, many households served through perhaps a few dozen local connection points. Each household’s type includes its connection-cost relationships to the other types. A discrete version has many identical copies per type, with zero connection cost among copies of the same type and cost \(\gamma_{tu}\) between types \(t\) and \(u\). Any coalition of copies then has exactly the cost \(C(q)\) for its type support. As the number of households grows, the possible mass vectors become dense while the type graph remains the description of the distinct connection profiles. That is a concrete high-multiplicity instance regime, not a claim that every network-cost game is naturally continuous.

I think the authors would recognize this as a continuous form of their network cost-allocation problem: the society varies by type mass, the coalition cost is still the cost of connecting its members, and the decision is how to allocate the shared cost. The connection point is not that the thesis already studies continuous populations; it does not. It is that co-located customers with identical costs are a natural repeated-player regime for the very MCST game it studies.

For this specified problem, I expect hardness to transfer (Class B). The minimum-cover reduction depends on choices among distinct terminals, and those choices remain available in the support of a mass coalition even when each terminal represents many indistinguishable customers. The discrete reduction therefore has a plausible route into a high-multiplicity instance. This is a forecast, not a consequence of Theorem 3.3: the reduction must be adapted to the two-sided normalization, and the resulting continuous problem could behave differently. Useful next questions are whether that adaptation works, what dictionary relates the finite least cores to this mass version, and whether fixing the number of types or restricting the type graph yields efficient algorithms.

The mirror covers Theorem 3.3’s least-core hardness for MCST games, through its minimum-cover construction. It does not try to continuize the thesis’s matching-game or sports-competition results, or to mirror the entire thesis.

My weakest point is the change in priority at the atomless boundary. The thesis proves hardness for specified finite priority functions, including \(f(S)=|S|\); it does not prove hardness for \(\rho(q)\). A referee could reasonably accept the network and population model while asking whether this altered fairness measure is still the authors’ problem. I think the case survives because the alteration addresses a real degeneracy caused by divisible coalitions, while the central object—the cost of connecting a coalition and allocating that cost—remains intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.3 is a genuine computational anchor, so the negative case cannot rest on there being nothing to mirror. Its direct per-capita continuation does have a real degeneracy. Let \(C=C(\mu)>0\), use the priority \(f(q)=\sum_t q_t\), and take \(q=(1-\delta)\mu\) for small \(\delta>0\). This coalition still has every type in its support, so its cost is \(C\); efficiency makes its charge \((1-\delta)C\). The least-core inequality then gives
\[ (1-\delta)C\le C-\epsilon(1-\delta), \]
so \(\epsilon\le \delta C/(1-\delta)\). As \(\delta\to0\), the best margin is at most zero. Since an MCST game has a core, zero is feasible: the per-capita margin vanishes, leaving the ordinary core problem. That is a substantive objection to treating the theorem’s \(f(S)=|S|\) result as a direct, nondegenerate continuum mirror.

But the proposed \(\rho(q)=\min\{\sum_t q_t,1-\sum_t q_t\}\) fixes that particular collapse. Its finite counterpart is the priority \(f_n(S)=\min\{|S|,n-|S|\}/n\). Theorem 3.3 does not establish hardness for this priority, and the proponent’s hardness forecast is only a conjecture. Neither fact defeats the question: the thesis explicitly develops \(f\)-least cores for varied priorities, and determining the complexity for this one is a well-posed computational problem.

The stronger population story also survives the main objections. Many customers sharing a modest number of service locations can have identical connection-cost profiles, while the cost of serving a coalition depends on which locations it uses. Cloning customers at each location gives a discrete high-multiplicity version of the proposed support-based cost. The fixed cost creates a discontinuity when a type’s mass reaches zero, but that reflects the connection model; it does not make the population interpretation incoherent. The thesis contains no continuous-population treatment that already answers the resulting allocation problem.

So I can make a case against the *direct* per-capita mirror: its least-core margin collapses. I cannot honestly defeat the two-sided version, or the better shared-location model, on the grounds allowed here. The negative case is weak; the remaining gap is whether the authors would regard that modified priority as their problem, not whether it presents a worthwhile computational question.
