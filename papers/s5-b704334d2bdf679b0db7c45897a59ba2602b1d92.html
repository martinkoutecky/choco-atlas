The continuous mirror question

Given a candidate set \(C\), an initial distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings specified by its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and binary-rational masses, and a binary-rational budget \(\epsilon\), does there exist a transfer \(x_{t,t'} \ge 0\) with \(\sum_{t' \ne t} x_{t,t'} \le \mu_t\), total changed mass \(\sum_{t \ne t'} x_{t,t'} \le \epsilon\), and resulting distribution \(\nu\) whose Schulze winner set differs from that under \(\mu\)? The output rankings may be any ranking of \(C\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete rankings of \(C\). A transfer moves mass \(x_{t,t'}\) from an original ranking \(t\) to a ranking \(t'\); its cost is the total mass moved. The continuous margin of victory is the minimum such cost that changes the Schulze winner set, or the corresponding budget decision problem.

The objection that survived

The paper’s audit motivation is only partly preserved: a normalized radius can approach zero when only a few ballots are pivotal, while audit design depends on finite sampling and the risk of missing errors.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Schulze margin-of-victory question in Theorem 4; the proposed cup counterpart covers Theorem 5. It does not assert continuous analogues for the exact-MOV results in Theorems 6 and 7.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the minimum changed mass be computed in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\)?
  • Does the discrete NP-hardness persist for this distributional problem, or does allowing fractional mass change the complexity?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror is continuous margin of victory for Schulze elections. It keeps the paper’s problem intact: how much of the electorate’s ballots must change before the winner set changes? It replaces a count of individual ballots with a fraction of the society.

Take a large ranked election with \(m\) candidates. A type \(t\) is a complete ranking, and the society is a distribution \(\mu\) over rankings: \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of voters with ranking \(t\). The input lists its \(\tau\) positive-mass types and their binary-rational masses. For a finite election with \(N\) voters, this corresponds to \(\mu_t=n_t/N\); a budget of \(k\) changed votes corresponds to \(\epsilon=k/N\).

For either voting rule \(E\), let \(x_{t,t'}\) be the mass moved from ranking \(t\) to ranking \(t'\), with \(\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}\le\mu_t\). The resulting society has mass
\[ \nu_t=\mu_t-\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t,t'}+\sum_{t'\ne t}x_{t',t}. \]
The action is replacing ballots; its cost is the fraction changed, \(\sum_{t\ne t'}x_{t,t'}\). Define Schulze Continuous Margin of Victory as follows: given \(\mu\) and a rational budget \(\epsilon\), is there such a transfer of cost at most \(\epsilon\) for which the Schulze winner set under \(\nu\) differs from the winner set under \(\mu\)? A yes-instance has a transfer and resulting society as its solution. The objective is to minimize the changed mass; the budget question is the direct counterpart of the paper’s decision problem.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper: *for Schulze elections, MOV is NP-complete*. Here MOV asks, given an election and positive integer \(k\), whether its winner set can be changed by changing at most \(k\) votes. The paper derives hardness from the NP-hard Schulze destructive unweighted bribery result of Parkes and Xia [12], using the connection to MOV due to Xia [24]; it notes that membership in NP is straightforward. The continuous question retains the Schulze rule, the pairwise majority graph, and the winner-set change. In a large ranked election with many voters but relatively few recurring rankings, asking what percentage of ballots must be erroneous or altered before the result changes is a sensible version of the same robustness question.

A second anchor is Theorem 5, proved here: *for cup elections, MOV is NP-complete*. The paper’s reduction establishes cup destructive-bribery hardness from VERTEX COVER and uses that connection to obtain MOV hardness. Its continuous counterpart is Cup Continuous Margin of Victory: the input also includes the paper’s fixed voting tree, schedule, and tie-breaking rule; the question is whether replacing at most \(\epsilon\) mass of ballots changes the cup winner set. This keeps the agenda and every pairwise contest in the tree. Both mirrors concern a continuous population, not a fractional outcome, a noise model, or an axiomatic question.

For both problems, my expectation is Class B: hardness may transfer, because the candidate and agenda structure remains combinatorial when voters become mass. That is a prediction, not a consequence of either theorem. The continuous versions are well-posed computational questions with parameters \(m,\tau,L\) (and the voting-tree size for cup), and are natural targets for optimization and pricing methods. Useful follow-up questions include whether either has an algorithm polynomial in those parameters, whether the continuous radius approximates normalized discrete MOV as the electorate grows, and how much the answer depends on allowing fractional changes within a voter type.

The weakest point is that the paper’s cup reduction may rely on precisely the indivisibility the mirror relaxes: its Vertex Cover construction selects whole voters, while fractional mass could behave like a fractional cover. The Schulze theorem also inherits hardness from a cited bribery result rather than supplying a reduction that establishes hardness for this mass-transfer model. So these anchors support the case that the continuous questions are recognizable and worth studying; they do not establish that their discrete hardness survives. I would keep the scope to Theorems 4 and 5 rather than claim a continuous version of the paper’s exact-MOV results, where an integer optimum becoming a real-valued mass threshold needs its own careful formulation.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against these mirrors is practical and narrow. Both inherit their appeal from the paper’s margin-of-victory question, whose stated applications include ballot errors and post-election audits. A continuous radius—\(\inf_{\nu:W_E(\nu)\ne W_E(\mu)} \mathrm{TV}(\mu,\nu)\)—measures the least fraction of a population that must change its ballot. That is a coherent robustness measure, but it does not by itself answer an audit question: audit design concerns a finite set of ballots, sampling, and the risk of missing particular errors. When only a few ballots are pivotal, the normalized radius can tend to zero and lose the distinction the finite count records.

That weakens the paper’s audit motivation for both anchors, but it does not dispose of the Schulze mirror. The better formulation is exactly this distance to the boundary of the Schulze winner region, over distributions on complete rankings. Large elections with relatively few recurring rankings are a plausible setting, and the population fraction is a meaningful quantity there. The fact that Theorem 4 gets its hardness through destructive bribery rather than a new reduction does not change that: the continuous radius is a different computational question. The proponent is right that fractional mass may change the complexity, and that difference is a reason to study the mirror, not a reason to reject it.

The cup mirror has a weaker practical case. Its result depends on a fixed voting tree and schedule, so the relevant population must share both a ranking domain and the same agenda. That can make a large, low-support population harder to motivate than for Schulze. But it is easy to imagine large preference polls over a fixed bracket or agenda, and the tree does not make voters individually identifiable. The Vertex Cover reduction’s use of whole voters also cannot rule out a useful fractional version; it simply leaves open whether the mass model behaves differently.

So the best negative case is that these mirrors may be less useful for the paper’s audit applications, and the cup scenario is less compelling. It cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile scenario exists. The Schulze anchor in particular survives the stronger objection: its continuous-population version is well-posed and natural under the programme’s criteria. A confident claim against every mirror would overstate what the paper supports.
