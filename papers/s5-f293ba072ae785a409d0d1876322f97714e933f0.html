What the mirror covers

The proposed extension carries Theorem 1's bundling-incentive claim to a mass of repeated doctor–hospital pairs; the paper's results themselves concern finite markets.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a compliant ChoCo computational case from this paper: it contains no named computational result. Its relevant anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that if some doctor and hospital share at least two contractual primitives, then for *any* stable matching mechanism there are preferences over primitives and a contract language under which at least one of them prefers to unilaterally bundle contracts before matching. Thus no stable mechanism is bundling-proof. That is a result about incentives and mechanism existence, not about an algorithm, complexity, or approximation.

The strongest adjacent population scenario would be a large market made of repeated, otherwise independent doctor–hospital pairs. A type would specify an agent’s role and complete preferences over the primitive bundles relevant to that pair; mass would be the fraction of pairs of each type. Each agent’s action would be to bundle a set of available contracts, and the outcome would be the stable contract assignment. One could ask whether a stable mechanism is mass-bundling-proof: whether, for every rational distribution over pair types, no positive-measure set of agents can improve its primitive outcome by bundling. A counterexample would specify the type, the bundle, and the before-and-after stable outcomes.

This is a plausible population extension of Theorem 1, and repeating its two-agent examples across a continuum of identical pairs would give a positive mass of agents with the same incentive. But it adds a population-level question that the paper does not study, and the repeated example supplies no computational classification. The weakest point is therefore decisive for ChoCo: even the most sympathetic continuous mirror of the theorem is an economic impossibility result, not a mirror of a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only plausible anchor is Theorem 1. It proves an incentive impossibility, not a computational result: for any stable mechanism, some preferences and contract language let an agent benefit from bundling. The paper gives no algorithmic problem, complexity bound, or approximation result to continue.

The proposed repeated-pair market is the strongest population mirror. But if pairs are independent, Theorem 1’s two-agent example can simply be repeated across a positive mass of identical pairs. That establishes a mass of agents who benefit; it does not create a computational question. A more ambitious version could ask for an algorithm to detect profitable bundling in a particular population market. To make that nontrivial, however, one would need to specify how matching and stability work across types, how contracts and capacities scale with mass, and how an individual bundle changes the aggregate outcome. Those choices create a new computational model. They are not questions answered—or posed—by this paper.

There is a real mismatch of scale in that proposal: an individual’s unilateral bundle can matter to that individual while having zero effect on an aggregate population measure; making a positive-mass cohort bundle together changes the strategic action from unilateral to collective. Either choice can be modeled, but neither supplies a computational mirror of Theorem 1.

This negative case is strongest as a verdict on the paper’s results, not as proof that matching with a continuum of agents could never merit study. High-multiplicity matching is a plausible regime. The paper simply gives ChoCo no computational anchor for pursuing it.
