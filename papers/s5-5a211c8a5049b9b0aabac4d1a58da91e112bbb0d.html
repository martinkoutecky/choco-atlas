What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror would cover neither theorem as a computational result. Both concern cut counts for individual allocations; the paper leaves computation of those allocations aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive angle is that the paper studies a quantity that can be stated for a population distribution: the least number of cuts needed for a proportional allocation. But under the anchor rule in the prompt, the paper has no named computational-complexity result to support that mirror.

The closest results are Theorem 1, proved here: for every \(n\ge2\), there are \(n\) value measures and an entitlement vector for which every \(t\)-proportional division needs at least \(2n-2\) cuts; and Theorem 2, also proved here: for every \(n\), set of value measures, and entitlement vector, some \(t\)-proportional allocation uses at most \(2n\log_2\widehat n-2\widehat n+2\) cuts. These are bounds on the fragmentation of an allocation. The paper explicitly sets aside how the allocation is computed, so neither theorem establishes that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified.

A plausible population-level question would take types \(\theta=(V_\theta,q_\theta)\), where \(V_\theta\) is a valuation measure and \(q_\theta\) an entitlement rate, with type masses \(\mu_\theta\), \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta=1\), and \(\sum_\theta\mu_\theta q_\theta=1\). At population scale \(N\), type \(\theta\) represents \(N\mu_\theta\) agents, each entitled to \(q_\theta/N\) of their cake value. One could ask for the minimum cuts needed to give each agent an actual bundle meeting that threshold. This is a concrete high-multiplicity family, but it does not give a valid anchor here: Theorem 1’s construction has essentially one bespoke type per agent and one entitlement approaching \(1\), while replacing individual allocations with a type’s pooled or average bundle would change the paper’s concern with connected pieces for each person.

So the case for a mirror is a natural *question*, not a result-backed case: cake-cutting has large-cohort regimes, and cut minimization can be posed for them. Its weakest point is decisive for this task: the paper’s named theorems concern allocation geometry, not the computational complexity of that population problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that neither anchor is a computational result. Theorem 1 bounds how many cuts some allocation requires; Theorem 2 bounds how many cuts suffice. The paper explicitly sets aside how an allocation is computed. Its mention of query complexity concerns prior algorithms, not a complexity classification proved here. So these theorems can inspire a new computational question, but they do not supply a computational result for a population continuum to mirror.

A high-multiplicity version is easy to describe: take a large cohort with repeated valuation types \(\theta\), assign type \(\theta\) mass \(\mu_\theta\), and give each person in that type an entitlement proportional to \(1/N\). This is a sensible finite-\(N\) regime, and it avoids the weak objection that every person must have a unique type. But it does not give the paper’s cut measure a finite population limit. With positive entitlements and an exclusive partition, every person needs a positive-valued piece; \(N\) people therefore require at least \(N\) pieces and at least \(N-1\) cuts. The number of cuts grows without bound as the population grows.

Pooling each type’s shares could produce a finite type-level allocation, but it would answer a different question: whether a cohort collectively receives enough value, rather than whether every individual gets a connected piece meeting their entitlement. Requiring those individual pieces restores the dependence on \(N\). One could study cuts per person or another normalized fragmentation measure, but that would be a new geometric objective, not a continuous version of either theorem’s cut bound.

Theorem 1’s particular lower-bound construction makes the mismatch sharper: it uses a dominant agent with entitlement near \(1\) and \(n-1\) agents with distinct tiny entitlements and bespoke valuable intervals. That construction does not describe a fixed finite set of types with bounded per-capita entitlements. Theorem 2’s upper bound also grows with \(n\), so it supplies no finite cut bound for an uncountable population. These are reasons the anchors fail to carry over as stated, not reasons to claim what a properly defined continuous problem would answer.

I would vote against a continuous mirror of this paper. A computational problem about minimum cuts for large finite cohorts might be worthwhile, but it would need a new computational formulation and a new population-scale fragmentation objective. The honest limit of the negative case is that this does not rule out such a separate project; it does show that the paper’s named results themselves do not anchor one.
