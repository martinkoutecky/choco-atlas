The case FOR (proponent)

The honest preliminary verdict is that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor under ChoCo’s rule. Its numbered results are welfare and equilibrium bounds, not complexity classifications:

  • Theorem 1: Random Priority has pure Price of Anarchy \(O(\sqrt n)\).
  • Theorem 2: Probabilistic Serial has pure Price of Anarchy \(O(\sqrt n)\).
  • Theorem 3: every mechanism has pure Price of Anarchy \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\).
  • Theorem 4: every deterministic mechanism has pure Price of Anarchy \(\Omega(n^2)\).
  • Theorems 5–10 extend these bounds to other equilibrium concepts, normalizations, or Price of Stability.

These are proved in the paper, but none asserts NP-hardness, membership in P, FPT, approximation complexity, or a comparable computational classification. The complexity remarks in the introduction and bibliography are not results proved here. Thus this cannot be presented as a fully compliant ChoCo case with a named computational anchor.

The strongest substantive mirror is nevertheless clear, and I would lead with Theorem 2.

Theorem 2, proved in this paper, states that the pure Price of Anarchy of Probabilistic Serial is \(O(\sqrt n)\). In the paper’s model there are \(n\) agents and \(n\) items, each agent has a unit-sum valuation, agents may strategically report preferences, and the ratio compares optimal matching welfare with the welfare of the worst pure Nash equilibrium of Probabilistic Serial.

A natural continuous-population version is Continuous Probabilistic-Serial Equilibrium Welfare.

An instance consists of:

  • a finite set \(A\) of item or appointment types, with rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma_a\ge 0\) satisfying \(\sum_{a\in A}\sigma_a=1\);
  • a finite set \(T\) of complete valuation or preference types;
  • rational population masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\);
  • a unit-sum valuation vector \(u_t\) for every type \(t\);
  • a rational welfare threshold \(W\).

The population is a continuum: mass \(\mu_t\) consists of agents indistinguishable in preferences, values, and reporting possibilities. An allocation is a measurable assignment of each infinitesimal agent to one integral item; its aggregate is a matrix \(x\) satisfying

\[ \sum_{a\in A}x_{t,a}=\mu_t \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{t,a}=\sigma_a. \]

The aggregate welfare is

\[ SW(x)=\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{a\in A}x_{t,a}u_t(a). \]

For a report profile \(r=(r_t)_{t\in T}\), continuous Probabilistic Serial runs the paper’s eating process with population masses in place of agent counts: each type consumes its currently most-preferred available item at unit speed, and moves when that item’s supply is exhausted. This produces an aggregate assignment \(x^{PS}(r)\).

To preserve the paper’s strategic content, a type is allowed to deviate as a positive-mass cohort: replacing \(r_t\) by \(r'_t\) changes the report of the whole mass \(\mu_t\). A cohort-report profile is an equilibrium if, for every \(t\),

\[ \sum_a x^{PS}_{t,a}(r)u_t(a) \ge \sum_a x^{PS}_{t,a}(r'_t,r_{-t})u_t(a) \]

for every alternative report \(r'_t\). The decision problem asks whether there is such an equilibrium with welfare at most \(W\); the optimization version asks for the minimum-welfare equilibrium and its ratio to

\[ OPT(\mu,u)= \max_x \sum_{t,a}x_{t,a}u_t(a), \]

the transportation LP over type mass and item supply.

This is a genuine population continuization. The paper already has fractional consumption probabilities, but its agents remain finitely named individuals. Here \(\mu\) is the society, repeated types are the primitive input, and capacities scale per capita. A plausible regime is a national course-allocation or medical-appointment system with millions of students or patients but only hundreds or thousands of recurring preference/value types.

I would expect the fixed-report allocation problem and \(OPT(\mu,u)\) to be Class A: the PS flow can be simulated from type masses, and the optimum is a transportation LP. The full equilibrium-search problem is more uncertain and may be continuum-specifically hard. The paper’s \(O(\sqrt n)\) proof uses finite agents’ unilateral deviations; in an atomless population, an individual deviation has zero aggregate effect, which is why the cohort-deviation convention is necessary. Whether the \(O(\sqrt n)\) guarantee survives under that convention is an interesting new theorem rather than something supplied by this paper.

The paper’s Theorem 3 provides useful supporting intuition, though not a second qualifying anchor: it proves, here, that every mechanism has pure Price of Anarchy \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\). Its construction is based on groups of agents with different favoured items, so normalized masses can reproduce much of it. This suggests that the welfare obstruction may survive continuization when the number of distinct preference types grows. But the proof singles out individual agents, so the exact lower-bound transfer requires new work.

The weakest point is therefore decisive: the proposed mirror is mathematically natural, but it is not anchored in a named computational result of the paper, and its strategic equilibrium notion must be redesigned for a continuum. I would submit it as a promising continuous-population research question inspired by Theorem 2, not as a formally eligible positive ChoCo case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has conceded the decisive point: this paper contains no qualifying computational anchor. Theorems 1–10 are welfare guarantees and equilibrium inefficiency bounds. None classifies a problem as tractable, hard, approximable, or parameterized. Under ChoCo’s gate, that is not a minor defect: the proposed mirror is not a continuization of a computational result in the paper.

Theorem 2 is the strongest possible candidate, but its actual content is the analytic statement that Probabilistic Serial has pure Price of Anarchy \(O(\sqrt n)\). The proposed “continuous equilibrium welfare” problem is new: it asks for a minimum-welfare equilibrium in a redesigned game and compares it with a transportation LP. That may be an interesting problem, but it is not the computational content of Theorem 2, because Theorem 2 contains no equilibrium-computation question.

The strategic redesign also exposes a deeper obstruction. In the finite paper, a unilateral deviation changes one named agent’s report and can affect the PS eating process. In a genuine atomless population, changing one individual’s report has no effect on the aggregate trajectory. If payoffs are defined only through the type-level aggregate assignment, the deviator’s aggregate payoff does not change, so the equilibrium condition becomes vacuous. If individual consumption paths are retained, one obtains a nonatomic Wardrop-style game, not the paper’s finite-agent Nash game.

The proponent repairs this by making an entire type mass deviate together. That is mathematically possible, but it changes unilateral strategic behavior into coordinated coalition behavior. Worse, it depends on the chosen type partition. Splitting one type of mass \(\mu_t\) into ten identical subtypes changes the size and power of each strategic player; splitting it further drives the model back toward the atomless, negligible-deviation limit. There is no canonical high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s equilibrium notion here. The type distribution remains a sensible continuous allocation object, but the strategic part is either degenerate or an invented finite weighted game.

The parameter \(n\) also loses its original meaning. In the paper, \(n\) is simultaneously the number of strategic agents and the number of individually labelled items. In the proposed model, population mass is normalized to \(1\); one instead has numbers of item types and valuation types. Replacing \(n\) by \(|A|\) or \(|T|\) is a new theorem about a different model, not a continuous version of the paper’s \(O(\sqrt n)\) statement.

Theorem 3 cannot rescue the case. Its lower-bound proof selects one particular agent \(i_j\) from each group and changes precisely those agents’ valuations. In a continuum, those selected individuals have zero mass; changing them does nothing to the society. Replacing each selected individual by a positive-mass cohort changes the PS process and the deviation incentives, so the proof no longer transfers. A new type-level lower bound might well exist, but it would be a new result rather than a continuous mirror of Theorem 3—and still not a computational result.

This is not the mistaken objection that PS already has fractional outcomes, nor the claim that a continuous answer would be uninteresting if it remained hard. The population-level PS/transportation model is genuinely plausible, especially for recurring student or patient types. The negative case is narrower but decisive: preserving the paper’s result gives an analytic welfare statement; obtaining a computational question requires replacing its strategic model. Thus the paper supplies no worthwhile ChoCo mirror anchored in one of its named results. The broader research problem may be worthwhile, but that is inspiration from the paper, not continuization of it.
