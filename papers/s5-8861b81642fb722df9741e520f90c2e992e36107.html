The continuous mirror question

Given a finite support of complete \(t\)-layer ballot types \(x\), with binary-rational masses \(\mu_x\) summing to \(1\), a rule set \(R\), a distinguished candidate \(p\), a satisfaction threshold \(d\), and a rational quota \(a\), is there a shared assignment \((r_1,\ldots,r_t)\in R^t\) for which \(\sum_x \mu_x\,\mathbf{1}[\max_j \operatorname{Sat}(p,v_x^j,r_j)\ge d]\ge a\)? Theorem 7’s mirror uses \(d=1\), \(a=1\), and uniform mass on clause types.

The model it lives in

Continuous Max-MECSR: the population is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over complete tuples of layer ballots, or their satisfaction data as permitted by the paper’s formal model. The decision variables are one shared rule \(r_j\in R\) for each layer; the objective is the mass of types whose maximum per-layer satisfaction reaches \(d\).

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover the paper’s two-rule NP-hardness results for the max and sum models; they leave the min-model results and the parameterized classifications aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the weighted partial-quota problem’s complexity depend on support size \(\tau\), number of layers \(t\), and input bit length \(L\)?
  • Which hardness constructions remain realizable when satisfaction tables must come from explicit rankings and conventional voting rules?

The case FOR (proponent)

A strong mirror is continuous-population MECSR: a large electorate has recurring multi-view ballot profiles, and a chair chooses one rule for each layer, applying that choice to the whole electorate. A type \(x\) is a complete tuple of a member’s \(t\) layer ballots, or equivalently the satisfaction data those ballots induce for every rule in the paper’s formal model. Its mass \(\mu_x\) is the fraction of the electorate with that type. The support has size \(\tau\), and the listed masses are binary rationals summing to \(1\).

For a rule assignment \(\mathbf r=(r_1,\ldots,r_t)\), let \(s_x(j,r_j)\) be type \(x\)’s satisfaction on layer \(j\). In Continuous Max-MECSR, the objective is to maximize \(\sum_x\mu_x\,\mathbf 1[\max_j s_x(j,r_j)\ge d]\); the decision version asks whether some \(\mathbf r\) gives accepted mass at least a specified rational quota \(a\). In Continuous Sum-MECSR, replace the maximum condition with \(\sum_j s_x(j,r_j)\ge d\). In both problems, the solution is the shared rule assignment \(\mathbf r\). Each person’s score remains per-person; mass measures the fraction accepted. This keeps the paper’s action and acceptance tests intact while making the population a distribution over complete types.

The regime is a large electorate or membership choosing among candidates, where each person supplies a ranking for each of several views—such as education, environmental policy, or public services—and many people share the same tuple of rankings. The rule set is common to the population, and the chair picks a rule for each view. It is plausible to have \(N\) people and \(\tau\ll N\) recurring ballot tuples. The paper explicitly permits treating \(\operatorname{Sat}(p,v,r)\) as input data, so the type-level score representation is native to its formal problem.

Lead: Theorem 7. It states that MECSR with the max model is NP-hard even when there are only two rules. The authors prove this result here by reduction from 3-SAT, using binary satisfaction values: layers correspond to variables, the two rules encode true and false, and each clause is represented by a voter satisfied when one of its literals is made true. [Theorem 7 and its reduction](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.17430)

The continuous question is Continuous Max-MECSR with two rules and quota \(a=1\). Give each clause type mass \(1/q\), where \(q\) is the number of clauses; set \(d=1\). The rule assignment accepts mass \(1\) exactly when it satisfies every clause, so the 3-SAT reduction carries over. I expect hardness to transfer (Class B): the hard choices live in the layer-by-layer rule assignment, and fractional population mass does not remove them. Repeating each clause type many times gives arbitrarily large electorates without changing the instance’s type distribution. This is the strongest anchor because it gives a direct interpretation of the two rules and uses only \(0/1\) satisfaction.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 2. It states that sum-model MECSR is NP-hard even with two rules; the paper proves it here by reduction from Dominating Set. The construction uses binary satisfaction values, as noted for the paper’s results in Table 1. [Theorem 2 and Table 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.17430)

The matching continuous question is Continuous Sum-MECSR with two rules and quota \(a=1\). For a discrete instance with \(n\) voters, give each complete ballot type mass equal to its multiplicity divided by \(n\), and set \(a=\alpha/n\). A rule assignment then has accepted mass at least \(a\) exactly when it accepts at least \(\alpha\) of the original voters. Thus Theorem 2’s hardness transfers directly to the rational-mass problem. Its further question is whether binary-score hardness persists under restrictions on the number or structure of supported types; the cited reduction uses a support that can grow with its graph instance.

These mirrors cover the paper’s constructive rule-selection problem and its max- and sum-model hardness results. They do not claim to mirror the min model or the paper’s parameterized classifications. One immediate algorithmic question for both mirrors is how complexity changes with the support and number of layers: enumerating all \(\ell^t\) rule assignments gives an exact \(O(\ell^t\tau t)\)-time procedure, while the paper’s hardness results rule out expecting tractability from the population interpretation alone. For Max-MECSR, a further question is how the weighted, partial-quota case \(a<1\) behaves beyond the full-coverage instances used by Theorem 7.

The weakest point is the connection to concrete voting rules. The paper’s formal model allows satisfaction values to be supplied directly, and these mirrors preserve that model. A referee who requires each reduction’s score table to arise from explicit rankings and a fixed conventional rule family could ask for an additional realizability proof. The paper’s stated abstraction makes that a limitation on how broadly to interpret the examples, rather than a mismatch with the problem it analyzes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the proponent’s two anchors is that both use full coverage, where the proposed continuous population contributes no mass information. In Theorem 7’s max-model reduction, quota \(a=1\) means every positive-mass clause type must accept \(p\). With \(d=1\), that is exactly the original SAT condition: every clause is satisfied by the layer-by-layer rule assignment. In Theorem 2’s sum-model reduction, the quota likewise requires every voter to accept. The graph-row types’ masses do no work in the reduction. These are sound finite constraint problems, but the cited results do not themselves show a population-level effect.

There is a precise reason: for rational masses and a rational quota, a common denominator \(D\) turns each type’s mass into an integer count \(D\mu_t\), and the quota into an integer acceptance count \(Da\). The continuous instance is then exactly a finite MECSR instance with repeated voters. This is a valid high-multiplicity bridge, but the full-coverage versions of these anchors are particularly thin: duplicating or reweighting types cannot change their answer.

The stronger versions deserve separate consideration. Partial quotas make mass matter, and maximizing accepted mass gives a coherent weighted problem. Yet the assignment still chooses one of \(\ell^t\) discrete rule vectors; the population supplies weights for evaluating those choices. The resulting model is a weighted constraint satisfaction problem on the layers. It has no fractional population decision for continuous optimization to act on. Moreover, the reductions use unrestricted satisfaction tables, so interpreting their hard instances as recurring tuples of rankings under conventional voting rules would require a realizability argument.

That is the best negative case: the two cited theorems establish hard rule-selection problems, while their stated continuous versions add little beyond binary-weighted high-multiplicity inputs. It is a case against prioritizing these particular mirrors as examples of ChoCo’s continuous-optimization promise. It does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The partial-quota model is coherent, and the high-multiplicity formulation can make complexity in support size and bit length worth studying. Since the programme explicitly counts such questions as in scope, the universal negative claim remains weak.
