The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper’s results have no computational problem to lift. Theorem 2 characterizes which choice functions are set-rationalizable; Theorem 3 characterizes which are self-stable. Both take a choice function \(S\) as given and state properties of its behavior across feasible sets. Neither takes a voter population as input or asks for an output, an algorithm, or a complexity bound. Replacing a finite electorate with a distribution over rankings does not turn either characterization into a computational result.

The suggested rescue—given a distribution \(\mu\), compute a self-stable choice set—needs extra choices before it becomes a problem. Theorem 3 characterizes a class of functions; it does not select one to compute. One could choose a named example such as the minimal covering set and ask for its output on an agenda. That is a plausible high-multiplicity winner-determination problem, but it is a computational companion to the paper’s example, not a mirror of Theorem 3. Similarly, testing \(b\alpha\) or \(b\gamma\) for a profile-induced rule would require specifying the rule and asking a new verification question; minimizing mass changes to alter its output would add a campaign or robustness objective the paper never studies. These may be worthwhile projects, but the paper does not supply the computational question they would continuize.

The case is strongest against calling any of these a *mirror*, and weaker against calling them worthwhile follow-up work. A reader could reasonably think that computing the output of a self-stable correspondence is close enough to the paper’s subject. But that possibility depends on broadening “mirror” to include computational questions newly attached to an axiomatic result.
