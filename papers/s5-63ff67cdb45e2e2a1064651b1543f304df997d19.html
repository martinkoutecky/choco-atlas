The continuous mirror question

For each fixed \(d\ge3\), given rational planar sites \(P\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^2\) with rational masses \(\mu_p\ge0\) summing to \(1\), decide whether there are masses \(x_A\ge0\) on all size-\(d\) multisets \(A\) of sites such that \(\sum_A k_p(A)x_A=\mu_p\) for every \(p\in P\), and, for every proposed group profile \(B\), some \(p\) occurring in \(B\) has \(\sum_{A:c_p(A)>c_p(B)}k_p(A)x_A=0\), where \(k_p(A)\) counts occurrences of \(p\) and \(c_p(A)\) is the sum of distances from \(p\) to the other members of \(A\).

The model it lives in

Use locations as complete preference types with masses \(\mu_p\); let \(x_A\) be the mass assigned to each size-\(d\) multiset of locations. Require exact mass balance at every location and rule out a blocking profile whenever each participating type has positive mass assigned to groups it strictly likes less.

The objection that survived

The formulation sits close to fractional-core and stable-hypergraph models, which have their own existence and search-complexity results; the boundary between those models and this full-cover, Euclidean, fixed-size version needs sharper positioning [Biró and Fleiner](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/seminars/seminars/conferences/ctn/bf12dp.pdf), [Ishizuka and Kamiyama](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2018.11).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s stable-partition existence question for fixed \(d\ge3\); it leaves the \(d=2\) algorithm and the paper’s questions about other distance rules and one-dimensional geometry aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed \(d\ge3\), is this feasibility problem polynomial-time solvable or NP-complete in the support size and input bit length?
  • Does requiring all mass to belong to size-\(d\) groups separate this problem from existing fractional stable-matching frameworks, or can those results decide it?
  • Can discrete hardness be compressed to a regime with many agents but modest location support?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s central decision problem. Its Theorem 1, proved in this paper, says that for every fixed \(d\ge 3\), deciding whether a set of \(dn\) points in \(\mathbb R^2\) has a stable partition into \(n\) groups of size \(d\) is NP-complete. Each agent ranks groups by the sum of Euclidean distances to their other members, and a group blocks if every member strictly prefers it to their assigned group. The paper proves hardness by reduction from planar cubic Exact Cover by 3-Sets, alongside membership in NP.

I would mirror it with Continuous Euclidean \(d\)-Stable Roommates. Fix \(d\ge 3\). An instance is a finite set of locations \(P\subseteq\mathbb Q^2\) and positive rational masses \(\mu_p\), summing to \(1\). A type is a location \(p\): all agents there have the same preferences under the paper’s distance rule. A group profile \(A\) is an unordered multiset of \(d\) types, allowing repeated locations. Let \(x_A\ge 0\) be the fraction of the population assigned to groups of profile \(A\). The mass constraints are
\[ \sum_A k_p(A)x_A=\mu_p \qquad (p\in P), \]
where \(k_p(A)\) is the number of occurrences of \(p\) in \(A\).

For a type \(p\) in a profile \(A\), write \(c_p(A)\) for its sum of distances to the other members of that group. The mass of type-\(p\) agents currently assigned to groups they strictly dislike relative to a proposed profile \(B\) is
\[ D_p(B;x)=\sum_A k_p(A)x_A\,\mathbf 1[c_p(A)>c_p(B)]. \]
Call \(x\) stable if, for every size-\(d\) profile \(B\), at least one type \(p\) occurring in \(B\) has \(D_p(B;x)=0\). Otherwise each type in \(B\) supplies a positive mass of agents who strictly prefer \(B\), and a positive mass of such groups can form. The question is: does a stable mass matching \(x\) exist? A solution is the vector of group masses \(x_A\); because the population is nonatomic, feasible mass assignments can be split among group profiles.

This keeps the paper’s locations, group size, distance-based preferences and strict blocking rule. The continuous change is in the population: agents of one type form a divisible mass, and a block must be formable by positive mass. The allocation records the distribution of agents’ actual group costs, rather than replacing them with a type’s average cost. That makes this a plausible mass version of the authors’ fixed-size stable-core problem, with mass-valued grouping as the necessary co-modification.

A credible regime is a large intake of residents or workers drawn from a modest number of sites—large apartment buildings, campuses or recruitment hubs—who are grouped in fixed-size teams by how close their home bases are. There may be millions of people but only hundreds of location types, with many agents at each site. The same-coordinate agents are genuinely indistinguishable for the preferences used in the paper. The case is weaker if actual preferences depend on personal traits beyond location; those would have to be included in the type.

This question has a real computational shape: for fixed \(d\), there are \(O(\tau^d)\) group profiles when the support has size \(\tau\), but stability imposes a disjunction for each proposed blocking profile—some type in that profile must have zero mass of strictly improvable agents. Thus the continuum yields a finite, structured feasibility problem, but not automatically an LP. A useful initial observation is that the problem is in NP for fixed \(d\): a certificate can select, for every \(B\), a type required to have zero improving mass, after which feasibility is rational linear programming. Exact comparisons of the fixed-size sums of Euclidean distances determine which terms count as improvements.

My weak prior is Class C, continuum-specifically hard, because those coupled disjunctions preserve combinatorial choices even after population mass is divisible. That is a research hypothesis, not a hardness claim. The paper’s reduction does not establish hardness transfer: its exact-cover construction uses \(\Theta(n)\) individually placed agents, so its support grows with the population, and fractional grouping may change whether a stable solution exists. The resulting classification is therefore open. Further questions include whether the problem is in P or NP-complete for fixed \(d\), what changes when \(d\) is input, and whether stable mass matchings correspond to stable matchings in finite high-multiplicity profiles. The mirror covers Theorem 1 only; it does not extend the paper’s \(d=2\) algorithm or its concluding questions about other distance rules and one-dimensional geometry.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s main theorem is a real computational anchor: for every fixed \(d\ge 3\), it proves NP-completeness of deciding whether a stable partition exists. I do not see a sound objection that removes that result from consideration.

The strongest negative point is that the proposed mass model is already close to established fractional stability frameworks. Its variables \(x_A\) describe how much population takes each group profile; the constraints balance the mass of each type, and the no-blocking condition says some participating type has no mass assigned to profiles it likes less. Those are the basic ingredients of fractional-core and stable fractional hypergraph matching models. Scarf-based work covers stable allocations with capacities and contributions, and stable fractional hypergraph matching has its own complexity literature ([Biró and Fleiner](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/seminars/seminars/conferences/ctn/bf12dp.pdf), [Ishizuka and Kamiyama](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ISAAC.2018.11)). So the proponent’s “NP certificate” is not yet a compelling account of what is new or difficult.

That is a warning about the formulation, though, not a defeat of the mirror. The existing general frameworks do not settle the Euclidean restriction: here, a type is a location, every group profile is available, and preferences come from sums of geometric distances. Studying whether that structure makes finding a stable mass assignment easier or harder remains a meaningful computational question. The cited fractional-matching results are relevant prior art, but they do not show that this paper’s model is already a continuous-population model or that the Euclidean question has been answered.

The scenario is also credible: many residents or workers can share one of a modest number of sites, with their preferences determined by distances between sites. Location then completely describes the preference type, while \(x_A\) preserves the distribution of the different groups those agents join. The paper’s gadgets use many individually placed points, so its hardness proof does not establish hardness for that regime; under ChoCo’s rules, that is not an objection to asking the continuous question.

My negative case is therefore weak. The best criticism is that the proposed anchor needs sharper positioning against fractional-core and stable-allocation work, and that the existence formulation may not be the most informative computational target. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The theorem anchor survives the objections available here.
