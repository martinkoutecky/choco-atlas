The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a mean-field campaign-design problem based on Section 4. Its population mirror is natural: the paper varies a stubborn group’s size and opinion to maximize followers or social influence, and explicitly interprets group size as campaign intensity. Replacing counts by mass asks what campaign works for a large population with recurring opinion types.

A plausible regime is a large public campaign around a contested issue. Survey respondents fall into \(\tau\) opinion segments; each segment contains many people with the same initial opinion and the paper’s common confidence level \(\varepsilon\). The input is a rational distribution \(\rho_0=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}p_j\delta_{x_j}\), where \(p_j\) is the fraction of normal agents in segment \(j\). The two stubborn groups have fixed opinions \(r\) and \(q\), with \(r>1/2>q\), and masses \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), measured relative to the normal population. Here \(n\gg\tau\): the point is that many people share each modeled type, not that the paper’s 200 simulated agents are themselves a limit.

Call the resulting problem Mean-Field HK Campaign Design. At time \(t\), let the normal population’s opinion distribution be \(\rho_t\), and let \(\lambda_t=\rho_t+\alpha\delta_r+\beta\delta_q\) be the total interaction mass. For an opinion \(x\), define
\[ M_t(x)= \frac{\int_{[x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon]} y\,d\lambda_t(y)} {\lambda_t([x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon])}, \qquad \rho_{t+1}=(M_t)_\#\rho_t. \]
The stubborn masses stay fixed; the normal agents move according to the HK update. Given the opposing campaign \((\beta,q)\), a permitted mass range \([0,A]\), and a permitted opinion range \([r_{\min},1]\) with \(r_{\min}>1/2\), choose \((\alpha,r)\) to maximize the eventual fraction of normal mass within \(10^{-3}\) of \(r\). For a fully defined objective even when trajectories do not converge, count segment \(j\) as an eventual follower when \(\limsup_{t\to\infty}|x_j(t)-r|\le 10^{-3}\), where \(x_j(t)\) is that segment’s opinion. The objective is then the sum of the corresponding \(p_j\). A solver could be asked for the supremum and a strategy within a specified rational additive tolerance.

The paper gives good evidence that this is *their* question in a new regime. In Section 4, Fig. 6, for example, the reported follower-maximizing strategy at \(\varepsilon=0.2\) and opponent opinion \(R_2=0.1\) uses a small first group and a nearly central opinion, whether the opponent group has size 40 or 160. When \(R_2=0.4\) and the opponent group has size 40, the reported optimum instead uses a large first group and a more moderate opinion. Those are simulation findings, not qualifying anchors. There is no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition in this paper asserting a computational complexity result. The article’s numbered equations, figures, and empirical strategy comparisons do not meet the anchor requirement, so I cannot honestly present this as a mirror of a named theorem.

If this model were pursued, I would initially expect a Class A route: with moderate \(\tau\), the dynamics aggregate exactly by opinion type, so the state does not grow with population size \(n\). The open algorithmic issue is whether one can handle the changing confidence neighborhoods and optimize over campaign mass and position efficiently as \(\tau\) and the input bit length grow. If tractability fails, the source may be the dynamics’ own threshold geometry; the paper provides no basis for claiming hardness transfers from a discrete result. Further questions include optimizing the difference in follower mass between campaigns, optimizing the paper’s RMSD-based social influence objective, and locating parameter regions where outcomes switch sharply.

The weakest point is the missing named computational anchor. The positive case rests on the paper’s simulation objectives and findings, not a numbered result that the requested rubric allows me to mirror. There is also a modeling choice to defend: the paper samples initial opinions randomly, whereas this finite-support mirror represents a society with \(\tau\) repeated opinion segments. That is a plausible high-multiplicity scenario, but it is not the paper’s specific simulation regime.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The central problem for the proponent is that this paper states no computational result to mirror. Section 4 reports simulation-based strategy comparisons: for selected parameter settings, the authors estimate follower counts and RMSD over repeated runs, then summarize the apparent optima. It gives no algorithm, complexity bound, hardness result, or formally established optimum. The proponent acknowledges this, so its proposed mean-field campaign problem has no qualifying named result as an anchor.

The strongest version of that proposal is coherent: represent recurring opinion segments by finitely many masses, add two stubborn-group masses, and optimize a campaign’s mass and opinion. But that is a newly specified computational problem. Its input ranges, tolerance, and infinite-horizon objective—including the proposed \(\limsup\) follower criterion—are choices needed to turn the paper’s numerical study into an algorithmic problem. The paper’s findings about follower maximization and social influence motivate those choices; they do not establish a computational question or result.

There is also little population-complexity structure in this particular model. With common confidence and deterministic updates, agents sharing an initial opinion continue to share one. A society with \(\tau\) initial opinion types therefore has at most \(\tau\) normal-agent opinion atoms at each step, plus the two stubborn opinions. Each update can be computed from those weighted atoms without iterating over the headcount. The interesting remaining difficulty would concern convergence, changing confidence neighborhoods, and optimizing campaign parameters. That could be a worthwhile problem in computational opinion dynamics, but it is not a computational result of this paper made tractable or hard by continuizing its population.

I cannot make the broader claim that no plausible high-multiplicity scenario exists: large campaigns with recurring opinion segments are natural, and the authors explicitly discuss campaign intensity. Nor does prior analytic work on continuous HK dynamics settle the computational question. The negative case is therefore strongest at the level of the assigned mirror test: the paper offers a promising simulation objective, but no named computational result for a continuous mirror to preserve or classify. If the programme also welcomes new computational questions motivated by simulations, this objection is substantially weaker.
