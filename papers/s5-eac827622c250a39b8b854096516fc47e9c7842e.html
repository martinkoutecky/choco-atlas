The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a compliant named computational anchor from this paper. Theorems 1–4 and Corollaries 1–4 characterize implementable and coalition-proof social choice functions; none states a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or another complexity class. In particular, Theorem 2 and Corollary 2 show that certain provision rules depend only on the share favoring provision and can be implemented by voting. That is a structural characterization, not a computational result. Calling it an algorithmic anchor would overstate what the paper proves.

The strongest positive direction I see is a computational follow-up to Section 6’s second-best mechanism design. Imagine a large jurisdiction with many residents but only \(\tau\) valuation types. A type is a resident’s valuation and any other characteristics the design problem uses; \(\mu_t\) is that type’s population share. Given a per-capita cost \(k\), a finite rational prior over possible population distributions, and rational valuations, ask for an anonymous mechanism maximizing expected aggregate surplus. The mechanism chooses equal per-capita payments \(P^0\) when the good is not provided and \(P^1\) when it is, plus a provision rule. Require \(P^0\ge 0\), \(P^1\ge k\), robust incentive compatibility, and immunity to robust blocking. Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 suggest representing the rule by a threshold on the share \(\mu(\{v:v>P^1-P^0\})\); the solution is the welfare-maximizing payments and threshold.

That looks like a sensible problem for the authors to recognize: it computes the second-best design problem they discuss, while keeping the society continuous and its support small relative to the population. For this finite-input version, I would expect tractability by enumerating the valuation intervals for \(P^1-P^0\) and the possible thresholds across the listed states, then optimizing payments within each case. That expectation is a proposed result, not one established in the paper.

The weak point is substantial: the paper itself already studies a continuum population, and it gives no numbered computational result to anchor the proposed mirror. Its value here is as evidence that the population regime is natural and that second-best mechanism design is a plausible computational target. It does not, by itself, furnish the required anchored case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has no named computational anchor. Theorem 2 and Corollary 2 characterize which provision rules can survive the paper’s assumptions; Theorem 3 supplies a converse. None analyzes an algorithm or proves a complexity bound. That is decisive against calling those results computational mirrors.

The Section 6 proposal is the strongest repair: encode finitely many valuation types and possible population states, then optimize expected surplus over payments and a provision rule. But that is a new computational problem inside the paper’s existing continuum-population model. The paper already treats the society as a distribution over types; making the states and support finite and rational gives the problem an algorithmic input, but does not continuize a discrete population model. So it is a plausible computational follow-up, not a mirror of a named result.

A stronger version—optimize over every robustly implementable, blocking-immune rule for that finite input—still looks like a sensible question. The paper’s characterizations may help formulate it, and I cannot dismiss it as ill-posed or unrecognizable to the authors. That leaves the negative case weak: I can reject the proponent’s claim to an anchored mirror, but I cannot honestly conclude that no worthwhile computational project grows from the paper.
