What the mirror covers

The proposed extension addresses the core concept behind Theorem 6; it leaves Theorems 28 and 37 without a continuous computational question.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the kind your anchor rule requires. Its results characterize game-theoretic solution concepts; none classifies an algorithmic problem as polynomial-time, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar.

The closest hooks are *Theorem 6*, proved here, which characterizes the core: each player’s wealth must be \(0\), \(1/2\), or the reciprocal of the number of regions with positive wealth. *Theorem 28*, also proved here, says no stable set exists when \(I=4,N=2\), or when \(I\ge4,N\ge3\). *Theorem 37*, proved here, says \(D\) is the unique farsighted core of consistent expectations when \(1\le I\le3\) or \(N=1\). These are structural results, not computational ones, so I can’t honestly present any of them as an eligible anchor.

There is a plausible population regime behind a future mirror: large regional populations whose members share institutional traits and initial endowment classes, with a small number of regions. A state could be a distribution \(\nu\) over current region and wealth, with unit population mass and normalized average wealth. A pillage would move winning mass from one source region to a destination region and transfer wealth from losing mass there; the winning mass’s total wealth must exceed the losing mass’s. This models the population itself continuously, rather than relying on the paper’s already continuous wealth-allocation simplex.

An unanchored candidate is Atomless Spatial Core Membership: given finite \(R\) and a rational finite-support \(\nu\) over \(R\times\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}\), decide whether any such measurable winning and losing masses can make a legal pillage. The literal atomless version appears to collapse: within any positive-wealth cohort in one region, split off a larger mass as winners and a smaller mass as losers; the winners have greater aggregate wealth and can pillage the losers. So every state is dominated, leaving no core. That is a clear continuum-specific change, but it makes this particular membership question trivial rather than a promising complexity result.

The positive case is therefore limited: the population scenario is credible, and it yields a precise potential model. Its weakest point is substantial: atomless coalition formation erases the finite-player granularity on which the paper’s core and stability results depend. No prover question can be tied to a qualifying computational anchor in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case starts with the paper’s actual results: Theorems 6, 28, and 37 are characterizations and existence claims about the core, stable sets, and farsighted core. None states the complexity of a computational problem. A ChoCo question could be invented from them, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper.

The population story itself is plausible. Large regional populations with shared endowments are a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and I would not object that prices or costs become properties of types. The problem is what happens when coalitions can choose arbitrary fractions of those types. Consider the proposed atomless model with a finite-support distribution over region and per-capita wealth. Any positive-wealth cohort in a region can be split into disjoint winning and losing masses, with the winning mass larger than the losing mass. Since the two groups have the same per-capita wealth, the winners have strictly greater aggregate wealth and can pillage the losers. Thus every state is dominated, and the core is empty. The finite-player characterization in Theorem 6 relies on wealth being concentrated among identifiable individuals; its dyadic and regional-share structure has no nondegenerate atomless counterpart. This is a collapse of the solution object, not merely a different answer.

Theorem 28’s stable-set nonexistence result is also not a computational anchor. A continuous stable set would be a global subset of a space of population measures, with stability tested against every feasible fractional coalition. Finite-support input does not keep that problem finite: partial participation and transfers produce new wealth levels and potentially new types. One could impose whole-type coalitions or a minimum organizing mass to restore granularity. Those are possible models, but they supply a coalition-formation restriction absent from this paper’s costless pillage rule. The resulting question would be about that added institution, not a high-multiplicity version of the stated game.

Theorem 37 has an even less direct computational route. Its consistent expectations are arbitrary maps on the state space, and the farsighted-core claims quantify over such maps. The paper provides no finite encoding of an expectation for an algorithm to take as input. Restricting expectations to a computable class could produce a new question, but the choice of class would become a central modeling assumption. In the one-region branch, the finite-player answer is the dyadic set; in an atomless version, fractional coalitions again remove the player-level granularity on which that set is defined.

So I find the case against a worthwhile *computational mirror of this paper* fairly strong: there is no computational theorem to anchor one, the natural atomless core question degenerates, and the other solution concepts lack a clear finite representation. The universal claim is weaker. Costly coalition formation or institutionally indivisible blocs might support an interesting continuous model, but that would need its own motivation and computational result; this paper alone does not make it a compelling ChoCo target.
