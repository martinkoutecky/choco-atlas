The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying named anchor. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity classification or algorithmic result. Observation 1 proves monotonicity when adding alternatives; it is not a computational result. The substantive computation is unnumbered: for \(k=3\), the SAT search finds a profile with 6 agents and 6 alternatives of Condorcet dimension 3, and rules out profiles with fewer agents and at most 6 alternatives, or fewer alternatives and at most 6 agents, having dimension 3. That is relevant evidence, but it cannot serve as an anchor under your rule.

The most credible proto-mirror, therefore, is not anchored as requested. Call it *Continuous Condorcet-Dimension Existence*. Given \(m\) and \(k\), let \(A\) be \(m\) alternatives and \(\mathcal L(A)\) their strict rankings. Does there exist a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal L(A)\) with Condorcet dimension at least \(k\)? For \(X\subseteq A\) and \(y\notin X\), define
\[ q_\mu(X,y)=\sum_{\substack{R\in\mathcal L(A):\\ \exists x\in X,\ xRy}}\mu_R. \]
Here \(X\) Condorcet-covers \(y\) when \(q_\mu(X,y)>1/2\); a Condorcet-winning set covers every alternative outside it. A solution is a list of rankings and rational masses summing to 1 whose smallest winning set has size at least \(k\).

A natural regime is a large electorate with a small agenda: millions of voters, but relatively few distinct complete rankings, each shared by a substantial bloc. The type is the complete ranking, and its mass is the fraction of voters with that ranking. That fits the paper’s profile model closely. Any rational witness can be scaled by a common denominator into a finite profile, and every finite profile gives such a distribution. So this question exactly matches the discrete question of whether *some* electorate size admits the dimension—not the paper’s fixed-\(n\) question.

I would expect the continuous and unbounded-size discrete versions to have the same complexity: any hardness transfers through that correspondence; this paper does not establish such a classification. The continuous formulation also invites further questions about the minimum support size and the smallest denominator needed for a witness. Its weakness is that it discards the paper’s specific result about the *minimum number of agents*: the denominator may be arbitrarily large, and this mirror may yield no algorithmic gain. So there is a plausible population-continuous problem here, but the paper supplies no eligible named anchor for the case you asked me to make.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

On the requested standard, there is no named computational result here to anchor a mirror. Observation 1 is a structural fact, not a complexity or algorithmic result. The SAT section reports finding a profile of dimension \(3\) and establishing its minimality, but it states those computational findings outside a numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition. That is a strong reason not to call this a mirror of a named result; it does not by itself show that no worthwhile continuous question exists.

The proponent’s existence question is the best candidate, but it has a limitation: it asks whether *some* electorate size works, rather than continuing the paper’s fixed-\((n,m)\) problem or its minimum-electorate finding. For \(\dim_C(\mu)\ge k\), each set \(S\) of size \(k-1\) must have some \(y\notin S\) with \(q_\mu(S,y)\le 1/2\). Fixing a choice of such a \(y\) for every \(S\) gives a system of rational linear inequalities in the masses. There are finitely many choices, and any feasible system has a rational solution. Clearing denominators turns that solution into a finite electorate. Thus the proposed continuous question is exactly whether a profile exists for *any* finite electorate size.

That equivalence makes the continuous existence question coherent, but it loses the paper’s sharpest result: the smallest number of agents needed. Headcount becomes a denominator question—minimize the common denominator of a feasible mass vector—not a continuous objective. Asking instead for minimum support would count distinct rankings, not voters; it is a possible new question, but it does not continue the paper’s minimality result. Given a fixed distribution, computing its dimension would also be a different task from synthesizing a profile and proving the smallest electorate that supports one. Cost-based mass-transfer or robustness questions could be designed, but the paper supplies no intervention or cost model to anchor them.

So the strongest case against is that every close continuation either collapses to the unbounded finite-profile existence question or changes the paper’s target. But this case is limited: the paper’s object is already an aggregate over ranking types, and a large electorate with repeated rankings is a natural high-multiplicity regime. I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile new continuous question. I would withhold a green on the basis of this paper’s stated computational results, while conceding that the negative case against all possible mirrors is weak.
