The continuous mirror question

Given finite recipient types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\), rational positive weight densities \(q_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t=1\), good classes \(G\) with rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma_g\), and bivalued valuations \(v_t(g)\in\{1,k\}\), compute a distribution \(x_{t,B}\) over integral bundles \(B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{G}\). Require \(\sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}B_g=\sigma_g\), and, for every positively represented \(B\) of type \(t\), \(B'\) of type \(s\), and \(g\) with \(B'_g>0\), \(\frac{v_t(B)}{q_t}\ge\frac{v_t(B')-v_t(g)}{q_s}\); the resulting population allocation must also be fractionally Pareto optimal.

The model it lives in

A continuum of recipients represented by type masses, with per-capita supplies of good classes and a distribution over integral individual bundles. The allocation must satisfy the paper’s WEFX or WEQX comparisons across all represented type-bundle pairs and be fPO under divisible reassignment; equilibrium prices may serve as an fPO certificate.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s WEFX–fPO result and Theorem 4’s WEQX–fPO result; it does not cover the cited algorithm’s nontermination example or the paper’s future directions on personalized values and chores.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s weighted bivalued-goods problem. Picture a large course-allocation system or public distribution program: many students or recipients, a much smaller set of preference-and-entitlement types, and standardized goods whose supply grows with the population. A type records the recipient’s value for every good class and their fairness weight. Two recipients belong to the same type only when those attributes agree. If there are \(N\) recipients, the number of types \(\tau\) can be far smaller than \(N\).

Here is a precise continuous model. Let \(T\) be a finite set of recipient types and \(G\) a finite set of good classes, with copies in a class sharing the same valuation profile across \(T\). Type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), and a positive fairness-weight density \(q_t\), normalized so \(\sum_t\mu_tq_t=1\). Its additive valuation satisfies \(v_t(g)\in\{1,k\}\) for a common \(k>1\). Good class \(g\) has rational per-capita supply \(\sigma_g\).

A bundle \(B\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{G}\) gives each recipient an integral number of copies of each good class. Let \(x_{t,B}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) recipients who receive \(B\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_B x_{t,B}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}B_g=\sigma_g\quad\text{for every }g. \]
Thus an average bundle within a type may be fractional, while every recipient receives an integral bundle.

Cont-WEFX–fPO asks for such an assignment in which every pair of positively represented bundles \(B\) for type \(t\) and \(B'\) for type \(s\), and every \(g\) with \(B'_g>0\), satisfy
\[ \frac{v_t(B)}{q_t}\ge \frac{v_t(B')-v_t(g)}{q_s}. \]
The allocation must also be fractionally Pareto optimal: there is no feasible reassignment of divisible goods that weakly improves almost every recipient and strictly improves a positive mass of recipients. A solution can include positive prices \(p_g\) such that every good in every assigned bundle maximizes \(v_t(g)/p_g\) for its recipient type; the paper’s Fisher-market lemma makes this an fPO certificate.

The lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It states that for \(n\) agents with positive normalized weights and additive bivalued goods valued in \(\{1,k\}\), the proposed algorithm returns a WEFX and fPO allocation in \(O(\min\{k,m\}n^2m^2)\) time. Cont-WEFX–fPO mirrors its exact fairness and efficiency objectives while replacing named recipients with masses of complete types.

A second, independently useful anchor is Theorem 4, also proved here. Under the same bivalued-goods and weighted-agent assumptions, its algorithm returns a WEQX and fPO allocation in \(O(\min\{k,m\}n^2m^2)\) time. Its mirror, Cont-WEQX–fPO, uses the same instance and supply constraints, but replaces the WEFX inequality with
\[ \frac{v_t(B)}{q_t}\ge \frac{v_s(B')-v_s(g)}{q_s}. \]
That is the paper’s equitable comparison: the recipient’s own valuation of their bundle appears on the right.

These are algorithmic anchors, not hardness anchors; the paper gives no named hardness result. Its theorem about the earlier Garg–Murhekar algorithm’s nontermination is proved here, but it does not classify the allocation problem’s complexity.

The type model matches the paper’s weighted formulation. In a finite expansion with \(N\mu_t\) recipients of type \(t\), assign each recipient weight \(q_t/N\). The weights sum to one, and dividing the paper’s fairness inequalities by \(N\) gives exactly the continuous inequalities above. Choose \(N\) to clear the denominators of the masses and supplies, so there are \(N\sigma_g\) copies of class \(g\). Applying Theorem 3 or 4 then proves that every rational instance of the corresponding continuous problem has a solution. Fractional Pareto efficiency lifts as well: any continuous fractional improvement can be averaged over the finite recipients’ mass cells to produce a fractional improvement in the expanded finite instance.

I would expect both mirrors to be tractable, in the sense that the paper’s transfer and market structure should admit a direct mass-based algorithm when \(\tau\) is moderate. That is a conjecture, not a consequence of the theorem’s stated running time. Clearing denominators and running the finite algorithm proves existence, but the resulting \(N\) and number of goods can be exponential in their binary input length. The concrete algorithmic question is whether one can find a sparse rational allocation and price certificate in time polynomial in \(|T|\), \(|G|\), and the input bit length, without expanding the population.

These mirrors cover the paper’s main WEFX–fPO and WEQX–fPO results. They leave out the specific failure of the cited algorithm and make no claim about personalized bivalued values or bivalued chores. They also keep the goods indivisible for each recipient: the continuous object is the population, with per-capita supplies that scale with it, not a fractional bundle handed to an individual.

The weakest point is the algorithmic compression. The paper’s procedures identify individual least and big spenders and transfer individual goods. A mass-based algorithm must preserve those fairness guarantees while splitting recipient mass among different integral bundles; the finite expansion establishes that a solution exists, but not that it can be found efficiently from succinct mass data. That is the main open question this mirror would put to the authors.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot honestly defeat either anchor. The paper has named computational results: Theorem 3 gives a polynomial-time WEFX–fPO algorithm, and Theorem 4 gives a polynomial-time WEQX–fPO algorithm. Neither is merely axiomatic or analytic, so the decisive “no computational result to mirror” objection does not apply.

The strongest case against Theorem 3 is that its running time is polynomial in the expanded numbers of agents and goods, not in the bit length of a high-multiplicity input. Clearing the denominators of the type masses and supplies transfers existence to the continuous model, but may create an exponentially large finite instance. The proposed mirror therefore asks a genuinely new question: can one find a compressed allocation over integral bundles efficiently? Theorem 4 has the same gap. In both cases, fairness depends on which bundles have positive mass, so the paper’s individual transfer steps do not immediately become a linear program over type masses.

That is a real limitation of the positive case, but it does not defeat the better mirror. A large course-allocation or public-distribution system with repeated valuation-and-weight types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Keeping individual bundles integral, scaling supplies per capita, and requiring fairness between every pair of represented bundles gives a coherent continuous-population question. The possibility that its support constraints make the problem hard is not a reason to dismiss it; the programme explicitly treats continuum-specific hardness as a worthwhile result.

The nontermination theorem concerns one earlier algorithm, rather than the complexity of the allocation problem, so it supplies no stronger objection. My negative case is therefore weak: the continuous algorithms are not already supplied by this paper, and the compressed questions raised by both main theorems appear worth asking.
