The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest candidate is a continuous Kemeny median problem. There is a qualification up front: this paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result. It does say in prose that the median linear-order problem was proved NP-hard by Hudry (1989), but that is a citation to prior work, not a numbered result proved here. Its unnumbered “Proposition” concerns a distance property in transitive tournaments, not a complexity classification. So the Kemeny result is the best substantive anchor available, but it does not meet your required anchor format.

For the mirror, let \(X\) be the candidates and let each type \(\sigma\) be a complete ranking of \(X\). The instance is a support of \(\tau\) rankings with rational masses \(\mu_\sigma\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). Define \(T_\mu(x,y)=\sum_{\sigma:\,x\prec_\sigma y}\mu_\sigma\) and \(w_\mu(x,y)=\max\{T_\mu(x,y)-T_\mu(y,x),0\}\). The Continuous Kemeny Median problem asks for a total order \(\pi=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) minimizing \(\sum_{i<j}w_\mu(x_j,x_i)\). A solution is a minimizing order and its exact value; if desired, the task can ask for all minimizing orders, as the paper’s enumeration section does. For strict rankings this objective differs from average Kendall disagreement only by an order-independent constant, so it preserves the paper’s underlying consensus question.

A plausible regime is a large membership organization or public consultation with many voters ranking a fixed candidate list, where ballots recur in a moderate number of full-ranking types. Then the population may number in the millions while \(\tau\) is in the hundreds or thousands. Mass is the fraction of voters with each ballot. This matches the paper’s weighted-majority tournament construction directly, and the authors should recognize the objective as their median-order problem, rather than a softened substitute.

I would expect hardness to transfer. A finite profile with \(N\) voters maps to masses \(\mu_\sigma=n_\sigma/N\), preserving the optimal orders and scaling the objective by \(1/N\). Thus the paper’s cited NP-hardness, if it applies to the ranking-profile formulation described around that citation, carries over to this continuous version. The natural follow-up is whether useful algorithms exist under restrictions on candidate count, support size \(\tau\), or rational bit length \(L\), and whether enumerating all optimal orders admits an output-sensitive bound.

This mirror covers the paper’s median-order problem in Section III and the cited hardness claim. It does not cover the heuristic comparisons or the fixed-size selection problem in Section IV; the paper gives no comparable named complexity result for those. The weakest point is the missing qualifying anchor, compounded by the scenario assumption: exact full rankings may have a large support in some real populations. The mirror itself is a close one, but the paper does not formally state the computational result needed to anchor the case as requested.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is a paper-level one: the proponent has no qualifying result to anchor the mirror to. The paper’s NP-hardness statement for Kemeny is in prose and attributed to Hudry (1989); it is not a complexity result established here. The paper presents heuristics, simulations, and an enumeration procedure, but no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary giving a complexity classification. Under the criterion for this task, that is decisive against treating Continuous Kemeny Median as a mirror of a computational result *in this paper*.

That objection does not defeat the better version of the mirror as a research question. A large association or public consultation could have many voters but a moderate number of recurring full rankings. Representing those rankings by rational masses gives a sensible population model, and the continuous Kemeny objective remains meaningful: the masses induce pairwise weights, and the objective measures the resulting order’s disagreement. Neither identity dependence nor degeneration gives a sound objection.

So the negative case is weak in the broader sense the prompt asks for. The anchor fails the paper-result requirement, but its continuous-population counterpart is plausible and recognizably the authors’ problem. I cannot honestly conclude from this paper that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario; I can only conclude that this particular case does not establish one by mirroring a computational result proved here.
