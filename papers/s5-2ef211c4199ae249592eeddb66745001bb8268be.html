The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of complete household types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a coalition-size cap \(K\), and a succinct nonnegative monotone cost evaluator \(c(q)\), where \(q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{T}\) and \(1\le\lVert q\rVert_1\le K\), consider a cost-sharing rule with type utilities \(u_t(q)\). A coalition-density vector \(y_q\ge0\) is feasible when \(\sum_q q_t y_q=\mu_t\) for every \(t\); define \(\mathrm{OPT}_\mu=\min_y\sum_q c(q)y_q\) over feasible \(y\). Call \(y\) mass-stable if no pattern \(q\) can be assembled at some positive density \(\varepsilon\) from available coalitions \(r\), using only type-\(t\) mass for which \(u_t(q)>u_t(r)\), with at most \(r_t y_r\) mass drawn from each pattern \(r\). Among polynomial-time cost-sharing mechanisms that always output a mass-stable feasible \(y\), is every mechanism’s worst-case ratio \(\sum_q c(q)y_q/\mathrm{OPT}_\mu\) at least \(\Omega(\log K)\), or can some mechanism achieve \(o(\log K)\)?

The model it lives in

Households with identical demand and generation histories, batteries, tariffs, and other cost-relevant features form types \(t\) with masses \(\mu_t\). Integer patterns \(q\) specify coalition compositions; densities \(y_q\) fractionally partition the population. The objective is total cost \(\sum_q c(q)y_q\), compared with its minimum over feasible fractional partitions, and stability excludes positive-density groups whose members all gain by switching to \(q\).

The objection that survived

The set-cover reduction’s element-specific incidence may require a distinct complete type for each element, so it gives no evidence that the lower bound survives with moderate support or a fractional benchmark.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s SPoA lower-bound frontier; it leaves Theorem 5’s decentralized algorithm and the empirical results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the Ω(log K) SPoA lower bound persist when coalition structures are fractional partitions over type masses?
  • Which mass-stability definition best captures repeatable blocking deviations while preserving the paper’s strong-equilibrium interpretation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case is in the paper’s general cost-sharing model, not in its 31-household energy trial. Imagine a large community energy-sharing programme with many households but relatively few complete profiles: households sharing a type have identical demand and generation histories, batteries, tariffs, standalone costs, and every other feature that affects coalition costs or payments. The population is a distribution \(\mu\) over those types, with far more households than types. Coalitions still contain at most \(K\) households. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for repeated household profiles; it does not claim that the paper’s particular field trial was already such a regime.

My anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that, assuming \(P\ne NP\), every cost-sharing mechanism for which a stable coalition structure can be found in polynomial time has worst-case SPoA at least \(\Omega(\log K)\). The proof reduces set cover to coalition formation and invokes the standard set-cover approximation hardness. This is the paper’s strongest hardness anchor. The result concerns the approximation guarantee of stable outcomes, rather than an NP-hard decision problem, so I would not restate it as an NP-hardness theorem.

Here is the continuous question I would ask. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete household types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to \(1\). A coalition pattern is a vector \(q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{T}\) with \(1\le\lVert q\rVert_1\le K\), specifying how many households of each type it contains. Let \(c(q)\) be its nonnegative, monotone cost, given by a succinct cost evaluator; its description and evaluation time are part of the input size. For P2P energy sharing, \(c(q)\) can be the minimum operating cost from the paper’s energy-management optimization applied to that mix of household profiles. A cost-sharing rule specifies each type-\(t\) member’s payment \(p_t(q)\); for a budget-balanced rule, \(\sum_t q_t p_t(q)=c(q)\), and utility is \(u_t(q)=c(e_t)-p_t(q)\), where \(e_t\) is the singleton pattern.

A continuous coalition structure is a vector of coalition densities \(y_q\ge0\), where \(y_q\) is the number of coalitions of pattern \(q\) per representative household. It must account for all population mass:
\[ \sum_q q_t y_q=\mu_t\qquad\text{for each }t\in T. \]
Its social cost is \(\sum_q c(q)y_q\); the social optimum minimizes this quantity subject to the mass constraints.

For this mirror, call \(y\) mass-stable if it has no positive-density blocking coalition. More precisely, a pattern \(q\) blocks if there are \(\varepsilon>0\) and source amounts \(d_{t,r}\) such that \(0\le d_{t,r}\le r_t y_r\), \(d_{t,r}=0\) unless \(u_t(q)>u_t(r)\), and \(\sum_r d_{t,r}=\varepsilon q_t\) for every type \(t\) with \(q_t>0\). Those agents can then form \(\varepsilon\) coalitions of pattern \(q\), and any remaining members of disrupted coalitions can stand alone. Thus the solution to the named problem, Mass-Stable SPoA, is a mass-stable vector \(y\). The research question is whether every polynomial-time cost-sharing mechanism that outputs such a \(y\) must have worst-case ratio \(\sum_q c(q)y_q/\mathrm{OPT}_\mu=\Omega(\log K)\), or whether allowing fractional coalition densities permits a better guarantee.

I expect this to be continuum-specifically hard for variable \(K\) and succinct costs: the optimum is an LP over coalition patterns, but finding or certifying stability adds a search over those patterns, whose number can grow rapidly with \(\tau=|T|\) and \(K\). For fixed \(K\), there are only polynomially many patterns in \(\tau\), making that regime a natural tractability target. The theorem’s set-cover reduction suggests a route to hardness, but does not establish the continuous lower bound: making each universe element a type encodes the incidence structure, while fractional coalition densities may erase the integral gap that drives set-cover hardness. Whether the \(\Omega(\log K)\) barrier survives is itself the right question.

This is a recognizable analogue of Theorem 3 because it preserves the paper’s coalition costs, individual utilities, stable outcomes, coalition-size cap, and comparison with the social optimum; it replaces the named population with type masses. The mirror covers only Theorem 3’s lower-bound frontier, not Theorem 5’s \(O(n^K)\) deferred-acceptance algorithm or the empirical results. I would not use Theorem 5 as another anchor: its runtime is in the number of named participants, and a support-based continuous bound would require a separate argument.

The weakest point is the stability notion. In an atomless population, a finite coalition of at most \(K\) named households has zero mass. Positive-density blocking is a defensible high-multiplicity limit, but it is a modeling choice, and the authors might prefer a different way to define deviations in the limit. The set-cover hardness also cannot simply be carried over. Those gaps weaken the claim that the theorem’s lower bound persists; they do not make the continuous question a restatement of the finite one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor raised is Theorem 3, the \(\Omega(\log K)\) lower bound for polynomial-time mechanisms that find stable coalition structures. The strongest case against its mirror is that the theorem’s hardness reduction depends on named identities: each set-cover element is a participant, and coalition costs encode which named elements belong to which sets. Under the programme’s complete-type definition, that incidence information is part of a participant’s type. In a typical set-cover instance, preserving it gives one type per element, so \(\tau\) grows with the population and the reduction supplies no high-multiplicity regime. Merging elements into a shared type would make them interchangeable and would no longer preserve the reduction.

The proposed mass model repairs that mismatch by giving each type a coalition-pattern cost and allowing fractional coalition densities. But that changes the benchmark as well as the population representation: the paper compares integral coalition partitions, while the proposed social optimum is a fractional partition. The set-cover proof therefore does not establish the same lower bound against this benchmark. The mass-stability definition is also a modelling choice. With coalitions capped at \(K\), individual deviations have zero mass in the limit; requiring a positive density of deviators captures repeatable deviations, but it is not forced by the paper’s finite definition.

Those are real limits on treating Theorem 3 as a direct continuization. They do not defeat the better mirror, though. Asking whether mass-stable outcomes can beat the logarithmic barrier relative to the fractional optimum is a well-posed new computational question, and the answer is not an objection at this stage. Cost-sharing also admits plausible large populations with repeated complete household profiles, especially under standardized devices and tariffs. The negative case is therefore weak: the paper’s reduction does not carry over automatically, but the proposed mirror is precisely a way to investigate what replaces it. I can’t honestly conclude that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
