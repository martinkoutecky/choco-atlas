What the mirror covers

The proposed typed evaluation extends the uniform reallocation rule characterized in Theorem 5.2 to a society with type masses; it does not computationally mirror that theorem’s axiomatic uniqueness claim or the paper’s other characterizations.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a plausible population mirror of the paper’s allocation model, though it has no qualifying theorem anchor. Imagine a large institution distributing a fixed workload among many staff. Staff in the same type share a complete preference and the same initial allotment; each type’s mass is its fraction of the workforce. The total workload scales with the population, so the model uses per-capita allotments.

A candidate computational problem is Typed Uniform Reallocation. The input is a finite support \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational initial allotments \(e_t\) and preference peaks \(p_t\) for each type. The output is the uniform reallocation \(x_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t x_t=\sum_t\mu_t e_t\). Under excess demand, \(x_t=\min\{p_t,e_t+\lambda\}\); under excess supply, \(x_t=\max\{p_t,e_t-\lambda\}\); in the balanced case, \(x_t=p_t\). Choose \(\lambda\ge0\) to satisfy feasibility. A solution is the exact allotment vector. This is a recognizable population version of the paper’s rule, and its computation is tractable by sorting the type thresholds and solving the resulting weighted piecewise-linear equation. This is my best positive candidate, but it is a simple rule-evaluation problem, not a complexity result established by the paper.

The closest named result is Theorem 5.2, proved in this paper: for problems with at least three agents, the uniform reallocation rule is the only individually rational and bilaterally consistent rule. That is an axiomatic characterization, not a result that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise has a stated complexity. The other numbered results are also characterizations of rules or properties. So the paper contains no named computational result of the required kind, and there is no honest way to supply one continuous problem per qualifying anchor.

The weakness is that the finite-agent paper already defines the uniform rule directly, so computing its typed-population version may be too routine to motivate a substantial computational study. Also, its commodity is infinitely divisible while its population remains finite: the proposed mirror genuinely continuizes the population, but the paper’s named results do not establish that this is a computational contribution.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The staff workload example is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. Types can include both preference peaks and initial allotments, and masses can represent workforce shares. The fact that the good is already divisible does not weaken that population mirror.

The obstacle is the paper’s results. Theorem 5.2—the closest candidate—characterizes the uniform reallocation rule through individual rationality and bilateral consistency. The other named results are also axiomatic characterizations, not complexity results about a computational problem. A continuous-population version of Theorem 5.2 could be a worthwhile axiomatic question, but it would not fill ChoCo’s computational gap.

The proponent’s Typed Uniform Reallocation problem is well-defined, but its computation is direct: sort the type thresholds and solve the weighted, piecewise-linear feasibility equation for \(\lambda\). With \(\tau\) supported types, that takes \(O(\tau\log\tau)\) sorting operations plus rational arithmetic, with polynomial bit complexity. It offers no hard optimization problem, exponentially large formulation, or pricing problem. Checking the resulting allocation’s properties is similarly not a complexity question established by this paper.

A richer algorithmic problem could add costs for changing preferences, uncertainty about types, or a robustness objective. But those additions would supply the computational problem themselves; the paper neither poses nor analyzes them. So the negative case is strong under the programme’s anchor criterion: there is no computational result here to mirror, and the obvious rule-evaluation task is too routine to make a substantial complexity study. The broader claim that no worthwhile project could grow from this natural population model is less secure; the paper simply does not provide the computational anchor for one.
