The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a research direction, not an eligible anchor. The paper has no named computational-complexity result: it does not classify a problem as hard, tractable, or parameterized. Its numbered Theorems 1 and 2, both proved here, give structural conditions instead. Theorem 1 says that if a coalition satisfies E1, none of its members is a veto holder and at least two are not dummies. Theorem 2 says that if it satisfies E2, it must have one of three stated compositions involving veto holders, dummies, and members who are neither. The paper’s enumeration of possible House coalitions is a case study, not a named complexity result.

A plausible follow-on would ask whether those coalition-effectiveness criteria have a meaningful high-multiplicity limit: take many members sharing types that specify voting weight and other relevant attributes, let coalition membership be a mass per type, and evaluate E1 and E2 in the limit. But that is not yet a precise mirror of a result in this paper. Banzhaf power is defined through discrete voting subsets, and the paper supplies no continuum model for that probability space or limit. Without such a construction, I cannot give a defensible continuous problem *per anchor* or claim the authors would recognize it as their computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has raised no surviving anchor: they concede that the paper states no computational result and cannot formulate a continuous problem per result. The numbered theorems characterize when coalitions meet \(E1\) and \(E2\); the House table enumerates coalitions for an eight-party example. Neither asserts a complexity classification or algorithm. A computational question about finding effective mass coalitions would be a new project, not a mirror of a named result here.

The strongest version of that new project is plausible: take a large cooperative or shareholder body with many members sharing the same voting weight and rights, let a coalition bind a positive mass of them to vote together, and ask which mass coalitions satisfy continuum versions of \(E1\) and \(E2\). That scenario passes the high-multiplicity test. The obstacle is that the paper’s Banzhaf criteria use counts over finite voting subsets, while a distribution of type masses alone does not specify how to sample coalitions.

The natural finite approximation shows the trouble. With equal weights, a simple-majority quota, and independent fair inclusion in a random subset, a bloc of any fixed positive mass becomes pivotal with probability tending to \(1\): the other voters’ included mass concentrates near half of their total. Every proper subbloc of positive mass does the same, so the strict comparison required by \(E1\) disappears. An individual voter’s pivotal probability tends to \(0\), removing the scale on which \(E2\) compares individual members. Other quota choices produce threshold behavior and boundary cases sensitive to finite-size details. Rescaling the indices or adding noise could retain more structure, but the paper does not select such a model, and those choices would define a new voting-power question.

That is a strong case against this as a ChoCo mirror, not proof that continuum voting-power research could never be worthwhile. The honest weakness is that a researcher could develop a meaningful new limit theory for mass coalitions. The objection is that this paper supplies neither a computational result to mirror nor a nondegenerate continuum version of its defining Banzhaf comparisons.
