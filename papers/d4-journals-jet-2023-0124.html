The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires. It studies whether social choice sets can be implemented by mechanisms in behavioral equilibria; it does not classify a computational problem as tractable or hard. I would not treat a constructive mechanism theorem as an algorithmic result.

The closest hook is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: when there are at least three individuals and the choice-incompatible-pair property holds, any social choice set \(F\) with a profile of interim-consistent sets of IAA acts is fully implementable in behavioral interim equilibrium. Theorem 1, also proved here, gives the necessity direction: implementation in BIE implies the existence of such an interim-consistent profile. These are results about the existence of mechanisms and equilibrium outcomes, not about the complexity of deciding or computing them. They therefore cannot honestly anchor a ChoCo complexity mirror.

The most plausible future direction would be high-multiplicity behavioral implementation. One could group agents by complete behavioral type—including their private-information structure and their choice correspondence—and represent a large population by rational masses over finitely many such types. A mechanism would map aggregate reports to an outcome, and the question would be whether some mechanism fully implements a target social choice set in the corresponding continuum equilibrium. A large public allocation problem with many households sharing a signal environment and behavioral type is a conceivable regime. This is recognizably related to the authors’ implementation question, but it is a proposed extension, not a result of this paper.

Its central weakness is substantial: Theorem 2’s construction relies on named individuals, including an odd-man-out and a modulo game. In an anonymous continuum, one agent’s deviation may leave aggregate reports unchanged, so that implementation logic may fail. The paper also supplies no computational representation for the proposed continuum problem. I therefore cannot give a valid lead anchor or classify its mirror as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent did not actually name a continuous question to anchor. Their nearest candidates are Theorems 1 and 2, and neither supplies a computational problem with a defined input or complexity measure.

Theorem 1’s best possible mirror would ask whether a target social choice set admits an interim-consistent profile of acts when behavioral types occur with rational masses. But each choice correspondence \(C_i^{\theta_i}\) is arbitrary and is defined over acts \(A_i=X^{\Theta_{-i}}\). Even for finite state spaces, representing that choice correspondence requires specifying choices over an enormous space of acts and their subsets. As the population changes, the act space changes too. A finite behavioral-type model could fix this, but it would need substantial new restrictions and an encoding; the theorem itself gives no canonical version to analyze.

Theorem 2’s best mirror would study full implementation through anonymous mechanisms that respond to reported type masses. Its equilibrium condition is about individual deviations. In a nonatomic population, one person’s report changes no aggregate mass, so the mechanism cannot use that deviation to discipline the reported aggregate. Treating a whole positive-mass type group as the deviator could restore that leverage, but it changes the strategic unit to a coordinated bloc and the equilibrium concept along with it. Retaining named individuals preserves the paper’s incentive structure, while the mass distribution no longer describes enough of the problem. The theorem’s odd-man-out and modulo construction makes this mismatch especially visible.

Proposition 6 offers the strongest alternative: many households could share a behavioral type, and endowments and supplies could scale per capita. That is a sensible continuous-population extension. Yet the paper proves an implementation result for behavioral CREE, not a computational result about finding an equilibrium or mechanism. To pose a computational question, one would still need to restrict and encode the behavioral rules, equilibrium objects, and mechanisms. That could be worthwhile new work, but it would be a new computational model rather than a mirror of a named result here.

So the negative case is strong against the paper itself providing a continuous computational anchor: it gives existence and characterization results over very general, unencoded objects, and its implementation concept depends on individual deviations that population aggregation removes. It does not establish the universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile mirror. A structured model of repeated households with a finite set of behavioral rules could be sensible; high-multiplicity work would support that regime. The honest conclusion is narrower: the proponent has identified a possible research direction, not a continuous computational mirror anchored in this paper.
