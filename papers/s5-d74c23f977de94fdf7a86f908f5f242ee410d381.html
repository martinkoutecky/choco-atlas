The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible anchor in the required form. Section 6.1 says that computing the efficient decision for a single-minded auction with arbitrary requested item subsets is NP-hard, citing LCS02. That claim is unnumbered and reported from elsewhere, not proved here. The paper’s “Groves Theorem” is also unnumbered, and concerns strategy-proofness rather than computational complexity. So I cannot quote a numbered result as requested.

If that Section 6.1 claim is allowed as an explicitly unanchored lead, the strongest candidate is a continuous version of its welfare-maximization problem. Let \(J=[m]\) be item types, and let each supported voter type \(t\) specify a requested subset \(S_t\subseteq J\) and value \(v_t\ge 0\). The input gives rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita item supplies \(q_j\). Choose served masses \(x_t\) to maximize
\[ \sum_t v_t x_t \quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\le x_t\le\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t:j\in S_t}x_t\le q_j\quad(j\in J). \]
Each unit of served mass receives its type’s entire requested bundle; \(x_t\) is the fraction of type \(t\) served. A natural regime is a large allocation market with \(N\gg\tau\) agents but only \(\tau\) repeated bundle-and-value types, and inventory growing proportionally with \(N\), so item \(j\) has about \(Nq_j\) copies.

This mirrors the paper’s efficient allocation step for single-minded bidders, and the authors could plausibly recognize it as that step’s high-multiplicity fluid version. Its likely classification is tractable: it is a rational linear program with \(\tau\) allocation variables and \(m+\tau\) constraints. The scope is only the allocation decision. The paper’s distributed network, taxes, and incentive guarantees are not part of this mirror; in particular, an individual agent has vanishing mass in the limit, so extending the VCG tax result would need separate modeling. Further questions include how the continuous optimum approximates finite markets and how large the discrete integrality gap can be.

The weakness is substantial: this requires scaling item supplies with population and allowing a submass of identical agents to be served, and the resulting LP is immediate. It is a plausible narrow research direction, but it does not repair the missing numbered computational anchor or support a compliant anchor-based positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against starts at the anchor requirement: this paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, or corollary asserting a computational result. The “Groves Theorem” concerns strategy-proofness, not complexity. The NP-hardness of allocation with arbitrary item subsets appears only as an unnumbered claim attributed to another paper; the paper’s own auction implementations and algorithms are not complexity results. On the stated standard, there is no eligible result to mirror.

The proposed LP is the strongest counterexample to that negative case, and it deserves a fair hearing. It models a plausible large market with repeated bundle-and-value types and per-capita supplies. That fits the programme’s high-multiplicity idea, including its allowance for supplies to scale with population and fractional type-level averages. Recasting it with interval bundles to match the implementation more closely would not remove the objection below.

The objection is that this is an unanchored, very narrow mirror of one decision step, not a continuous computational result of the paper. The paper’s contribution is a distributed platform for implementing mechanisms; the proposed LP leaves aside reports, taxes, incentives, and distributed execution. And the LP itself has one variable per supported type, so its tractability follows directly from a standard formulation. That makes it a weak use of this programme’s time, though ease alone does not disqualify a mirror.

I would not claim the universal negative is airtight. If the authors regard bulk, multi-copy single-minded allocation as their problem, this is a coherent high-multiplicity question. The strongest case against is therefore that it does not satisfy the requested anchor standard and adds little computational depth—not that no worthwhile continuous mirror could exist under any recognized formulation.
