What the mirror covers

The learning-rate formulas and scoring-rule comparisons already use \(F\); the Mallows analyses and empirical design results apply those same distributional rates. No separate high-multiplicity result remains.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is rate-optimal scoring design for a large participatory-budgeting electorate. Let a type be a complete ranking \(\sigma\in S_M\), and let \(\mu_\sigma\) be the fraction of residents with that ranking. In a large election, \(N\) could be much greater than the number \(\tau\) of ranking types represented. The organizer chooses a scoring rule \(\beta\), and the objective is to make the desired winner tiers easier to recover from sampled ballots.

A precise candidate problem is: given rational masses \(\mu\) on \(\tau\) rankings, a target tier partition \(G=(C_1,\ldots,C_T)\), and an accuracy \(\epsilon>0\), find a nonincreasing scoring rule \(\beta\), normalized by \(\beta(1)=1,\ \beta(M)=0\), whose outcome learning rate is within \(\epsilon\) of optimal. That rate is

\[ r_\mu(\beta)= \min_{s<t}\min_{\substack{i\in C_s\\j\in C_t}} \left[ -\inf_{z\in\mathbb R} \log\left( \sum_{\sigma}\mu_\sigma e^{z(\beta(\sigma(i))-\beta(\sigma(j)))} \right) \right]. \]

This preserves the paper’s central design question and its goal of identifying winner tiers; it does not simplify the task to a different outcome. A large poll or citywide budgeting election with many residents but relatively few shared ballot types is a plausible high-multiplicity setting.

The obstacle is that this cannot honestly be attached to a qualifying anchor. The paper’s numbered results concern learning-rate formulas and whether randomization between scoring rules helps. None classifies the complexity of an underlying problem as tractable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. The dynamic program mentioned for Mallows probabilities is also not stated as a numbered result with a complexity bound. So there is no named computational result here to mirror under the requested rule.

The weakest point is deeper than missing complexity theorems: the paper already represents voter preferences by a distribution \(F\) over rankings. Reading \(F\) as a continuous population makes the candidate problem above look natural, but risks merely restating the paper’s own model. Its learning rates concern finite samples drawn from \(F\); replacing the sampled electorate with exact population masses removes that sampling error. I therefore see a plausible continuous-design question, but not an honest anchored case that this paper supplies a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the point the proponent concedes: this paper supplies no named complexity result to mirror. Its pairwise and overall learning-rate propositions give formulas for error exponents; its randomization theorem compares those exponents across mechanisms. None classifies the complexity of an algorithmic problem. The Mallows dynamic program is mentioned as a computational aid, but the paper does not state a complexity theorem for it. So the paper offers no qualifying computational anchor.

The proposed “choose a rate-optimal scoring rule” problem is the best possible attempt to supply one, but it inherits almost all its substance from the paper’s existing model. The input distribution \(F\) is already a distribution over complete ranking types, and the paper defines rate-optimal designs using learning rates calculated from \(F\). Replacing \(F\) by rational population masses \(\mu\) makes that input explicit and finite; it does not create a new population-level design problem. If the continuous society is treated as exact, sampling error disappears and there is no learning rate to optimize. If voters are still sampled from \(\mu\), then the learning-rate question is the paper’s original one, now written with mass notation.

The stronger versions of the other apparent anchors run into the same boundary. The randomization result already quantifies over arbitrary voter distributions and compares randomized with static scoring rules; a continuous-population reading supplies no new algorithmic claim. And the Mallows computation concerns probabilities under a sampling model. Treating those probabilities as population masses either preserves that model or removes the sampling question the computation serves. A new complexity analysis of optimizing the exponent over \(\beta\) might be interesting, but it would be a new problem suggested by the paper, not a continuous mirror of a result the paper established.

This is not an airtight universal objection. Large participatory-budgeting electorates with repeated ballot types are a credible high-multiplicity regime, and an algorithmic study of rate-optimal design could be worthwhile on its own. The negative case is therefore weak if the standard is “could this motivate a new computational question?” It is stronger under the requested standard of finding a continuous mirror of a named computational result: there is no such result here, and the most plausible replacement either repeats the paper’s distributional learning model or removes the sampling phenomenon that gives its rates meaning.
