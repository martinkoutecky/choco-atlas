The case FOR (proponent)

There are no eligible computational anchors in this paper: no numbered result asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The numbered results concern stability and nonemptiness. I won’t recast those as complexity results.

The strongest structural result for a continuous follow-on is Proposition 4.3, proved in the paper. It says: for \(H(n)\le k\le n\), \(0<\delta\le1\), and a partition \(P\), if \(\psi\) satisfies D, ET-, and EF2-, then \(\Omega_n(\delta)<k\le n\) and a party \(S\in P\) with \(|S|\ge k\) imply \(P\notin C_{\mathrm{proj}}(\varphi_{\psi,(n,k),\delta})\), where
\[ \Omega_n(\delta)=\frac{2^{1/\delta}}{1+2^{1/\delta}}(n+1). \]
That is a structural stability theorem, not a computational anchor.

A plausible mass version keeps the voters and their one-person, one-vote rule, but represents them by the atomless population \(I=[0,1]\). A type is simply a homogeneous voter; a party is a measurable subset of \(I\), and its mass is its population share. For a finite party structure with masses \(w_1,\ldots,w_r\), a quota \(q>1/2\), and a power index \(\psi\), party \(j\) has power per member \(\psi_j(v)/w_j^\delta\), where \(v(J)=1\) exactly when \(\sum_{j\in J}w_j\ge q\). This is a credible high-multiplicity regime: many interchangeable voters, often millions, belong to a small number of large parties. Party membership is the coalition structure being studied, not a different voter type.

The continuous question I would put to a prover is Projective Mass-Core Membership for Majority Blocs. Its input is \(q\), \(\delta\), one of the paper’s indices, and a finite list of positive rational masses \(w_1,\ldots,w_r\) summing to \(1\), describing the current parties. Ask whether that structure is projectively stable. A proposed deviation is a vector \(x\) with \(0\le x_j\le w_j\), \(\sum_jx_j>0\): it transfers mass \(x_j\) from each party \(j\) into one new party, leaving the residual mass \(w_j-x_j\) in the original party. It is profitable exactly when the new party’s per-member power exceeds the current per-member power for every \(j\) with \(x_j>0\). The answer is yes if no such vector exists; otherwise the solution includes a blocking vector. Atomlessness makes every such mass transfer feasible. There is no separate welfare objective in the paper’s stability question.

Proposition 4.3 gives this mirror a meaningful structural boundary. In the mass model, the threshold proportion tends to
\[ \omega(\delta)=\frac{2^{1/\delta}}{1+2^{1/\delta}}. \]
For \(q>\omega(\delta)\), the same two-veto-party argument blocks a structure containing a party of mass at least \(q\): a deviating coalition can take mass just above \(1-q\), making both resulting parties veto parties while improving its members’ power per capita. That preserves the substance of the result rather than simplifying away the coalition-formation question.

For the proposed membership problem, I would expect tractability when the number of parties \(r\) is fixed: there are only finitely many possible winning patterns for the resulting weighted game. With \(r\) variable, the relevant combinatorics remain over subsets of parties, so the complexity is open; discrete hardness might persist, but this paper provides no reduction to establish that. Further questions include complexity in \(r\) and input bit length, and algorithms for finding stable mass partitions.

The weak point is also the decisive limitation: the authors prove no computational result to mirror, so this cannot support a ChoCo complexity claim on the paper’s anchors. The continuous population model is plausible and the stability boundary has a natural mass interpretation, but the computational mirror is a proposed follow-on question, not a result this paper establishes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper gives no computational result to mirror. Propositions 4.1, 4.2, 4.3 and 4.6 concern stability and nonemptiness under axioms on power indices. None classifies an algorithmic problem. Proposition 4.3 is the strongest proposed anchor, but it proves a structural condition for instability; it gives no complexity claim or algorithmic task.

The mass interpretation itself is credible. A large electorate divided among a small number of parties is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the limit does not make the result vanish: with \(q=k/n\), the threshold \(\Omega_n(\delta)/n\) tends to \(2^{1/\delta}/(1+2^{1/\delta})\). So the negative case should not rest on implausible multiplicity, a degenerate limit, or the claim that continuity fails to change the answer.

The proposed Projective Mass-Core Membership problem is a coherent follow-on. It asks whether a rational mass partition admits a profitable projective deviation. But that is a new computational question built from the paper’s stability definition. Proposition 4.3 only settles one sufficient region of that question; it says nothing about the complexity of deciding membership across general inputs. Asking for that complexity could be worthwhile, but it does not turn the paper’s structural theorem into a computational anchor.

A stronger mass programme could classify stable mass partitions under the paper’s axioms, extending Propositions 4.1, 4.2 and 4.6 as well. That would be a genuine continuous-population result, and the paper’s proofs give it a clear starting point. Its contribution would still be a stability characterization. To make it a ChoCo complexity project, one must add an algorithmic question that the paper itself does not pose.

So I would argue against spending ChoCo’s computational-complexity effort on this paper. The honest limit of that case is that it does not establish that no worthwhile continuous follow-on exists: the mass model is sensible, and a computational study of its membership problem might be valuable on its own. The case against is narrower—the paper supplies no computational result whose mirror the programme can evaluate.
