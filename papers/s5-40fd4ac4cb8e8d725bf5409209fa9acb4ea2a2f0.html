The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive angle is a high-volume housing-exchange regime: for example, residents in a large housing system with many repeated combinations of community, current housing class, and preference ranking. A type would be the complete tuple \(t=(j,e,\succ)\): community \(j\), endowed house class \(e\), and strict ranking \(\succ\) over a fixed set \(H\) of interchangeable house classes. The instance is a rational mass distribution \(\mu_t\), with many residents but relatively few supported types.

A candidate continuous question is: given \((H,T,\mu)\), find the integrated and community-restricted core assignments that maximize the total rank loss from integration. An assignment is a matrix \(x_{t,h}\ge 0\), where \(x_{t,h}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving house class \(h\); its row sums are \(\mu_t\), and its column sums equal the supplied mass of each house class. For the segregated assignment, residents can receive only a class endowed within their community. Define \(W(x)=\sum_{t,h}x_{t,h}\operatorname{rk}_t(h)\), with lower rank better. The requested solution is the maximizing pair of integrated and segregated core assignments and the exact value of \(W(x^{I})-W(x^{S})\). This preserves the paper’s comparison of core outcomes and rank-based welfare. Its expected computational status is unclear from the paper.

There is a serious anchor problem, though. Under your definition, the paper has no named computational result: none of its numbered results classifies a computational problem as tractable, hard, or parameterized-tractable, or gives an algorithmic complexity bound. The closest result is Proposition 2, proved here: for every \(n,k\), an extended housing market attains average percentile gains \(-\frac{n^2-n-k^2-k}{2n^2}\), and no market has smaller gains. Proposition 4, also proved here, bounds the expected number harmed in community \(C_j\) by \(n_j-\sqrt{2\pi n_j}-O(\log n_j)\), using the expected number of TTC cycles; the cycle-count result it invokes is cited to Frieze and Pittel. These are welfare results about the outcomes of an algorithm, not results about the complexity of computing them. They cannot honestly serve as the required anchors.

The weak point in the proposed mirror is substantial: the paper ranks agents over \(n\) individually named houses with strict preferences, while a small-\(\tau\) mirror needs interchangeable house classes and an atomless core. That change could alter the core and its welfare comparisons. So the paper offers a plausible continuous-population welfare question, but not an anchored positive case for ChoCo’s computational programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest problem is its own concession: this paper states no computational complexity result. Propositions 1 and 2 bound welfare losses, Proposition 3 computes expected welfare gains, Proposition 4 bounds the expected number harmed, and Proposition 5 gives a preference-domain guarantee. TTC is the algorithm used to obtain the allocations; the paper does not classify the complexity of computing them. Those results do not supply the computational anchors ChoCo asks for.

Neither Proposition 2 nor Proposition 4 fixes that gap. A continuous version of Proposition 2 could ask for an extremal welfare bound over mass distributions. A version of Proposition 4 could study the expected harmed mass under a specified distribution over ranking types. Both might be worthwhile welfare questions, but neither mirrors a computational claim in the paper. Proposition 4’s uniform random rankings are over \(n\) individually named houses; replacing them with a distribution over rankings of fixed house classes also changes the probabilistic model.

The proposed mass-assignment model is a plausible extension, but its status as a mirror is unclear. If it preserves the paper’s unique TTC outcome through a canonical mass-TTC or a limit of replicated finite markets, it asks for welfare comparisons in that new model. If it instead maximizes over all continuous core assignments, it introduces an extremal selection question absent from the paper, whose discrete core outcome is unique. And making the support small requires grouping houses into classes: keeping each named house as a type makes the support grow with the population, while grouping changes the strict rankings and trading structure used in the paper’s results.

That is the strongest case against the proposed anchors, but it does not prove the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous extension exists. Repeated housing classes and large populations are plausible, and a continuous welfare analysis could interest the authors. The honest negative conclusion is narrower: the paper offers no computational result for ChoCo to mirror, and the proposed optimization does not establish one. A broader continuous welfare project may be sensible, but it is not an anchored computational mirror of these propositions.
