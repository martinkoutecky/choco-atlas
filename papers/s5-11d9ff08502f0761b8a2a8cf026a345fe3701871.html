The continuous mirror question

Given ordered rational line types \(a_1<\cdots<a_\tau\), rational masses \(\mu\in\Delta_\tau\), and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), compute the mass-limit Hybrid-Distance expected normalized cost \(C_\lambda(\mu)\), compute \(\OPT_2(\mu)=\min_{u,v\in\mathbb R}\sum_t\mu_t\min\{|a_t-u|,|a_t-v|\}\), and decide whether \(C_\lambda(\mu)\le\rho\,\OPT_2(\mu)\); additionally determine the worst-case fixed-mixture parameter, with target values \(\lambda^*=(5+4\sqrt3)/23\) and ratio \(3+\lambda^*\).

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that this paper admits a clean high-multiplicity mirror, especially for its two-facility results. There is, however, an important formal limitation: none of the paper’s named results is a complexity classification such as membership in \( \mathrm P \), NP-hardness, or FPT. Theorems 1–4 and Proposition 1 concern strategyproofness and approximation guarantees. Thus, under the strictest ChoCo anchor rule, this is not a fully compliant computational-complexity anchor. What follows is the strongest substantive positive case.

My lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It states that, for every population size, every profile, and every metric space, Hybrid-Distance satisfies
\[ \operatorname{SC}(\HD)\le (3+\lambda^*)\OPT, \qquad \lambda^*=\frac{5+4\sqrt3}{23}. \]
The bound is asymptotically tight on the line, so the approximation ratio is exactly \(3+\lambda^*\). Moreover, \(\lambda^*\) minimizes the worst-case approximation ratio among all fixed mixtures
\[ \Mix_\lambda=\lambda\PM+(1-\lambda)\PD. \]
Theorem 1, also proved here, supplies the incentive guarantee used alongside it: \(\HD\) is strategyproof on every Ptolemaic metric space.

A natural continuous population is a large city whose residents fall into finitely many location types. Let \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\) be the relevant location types, with representative locations \(x_t\) in a Ptolemaic metric space \((\Omega,d)\). Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\). Agents of the same type have identical facility costs, so \(x_t\) is a complete type description for this problem. The regime is \(n\gg\tau\): millions of residents, but only a moderate number of relevant locations or standardized neighbourhood types.

The mass-transfer limit of Pairwise-Distance is forced by the paper’s own sampling rule. Define
\[ Z_\mu=\sum_{s<t}\mu_s\mu_t\,d(x_s,x_t). \]
If \(Z_\mu>0\), Pairwise-Distance selects \(\{x_s,x_t\}\) with probability
\[ \frac{\mu_s\mu_t\,d(x_s,x_t)}{Z_\mu}. \]
The mass-transfer limit of Proportional first samples an anchor \(s\) with probability \(\mu_s\), then samples \(t\) with probability
\[ \frac{\mu_t\,d(x_s,x_t)} {\sum_u\mu_u\,d(x_s,x_u)}. \]
Hybrid-Distance mixes these two rules with the fixed probability \(\lambda^*\).

For a facility set \(F\), define the normalized social cost
\[ \operatorname{SC}_\mu(F) = \sum_t\mu_t\min_{f\in F}d(x_t,f), \]
and let
\[ \OPT_2(\mu)=\inf_{|F|\le2}\operatorname{SC}_\mu(F). \]

This gives the precise continuous problem:

“Continuous Fixed-Mixture Two-Facility Location” takes a finite rational type distribution \(\mu\) over a Ptolemaic metric space and a mixing parameter \(\lambda\). It must compute the induced random facility-pair distribution, compute
\[ C_\lambda(\mu) = \mathbb E_{F\sim\Mix_{\lambda,\mu}} [\operatorname{SC}_\mu(F)], \]
and determine whether \(C_\lambda(\mu)\le\rho\,\OPT_2(\mu)\). The associated mechanism-design problem is to choose \(\lambda\) minimizing
\[ \sup_{\mu} \frac{C_\lambda(\mu)}{\OPT_2(\mu)}. \]
A solution consists of the mixing parameter, the pair probabilities, and the resulting worst-case guarantee.

The expected answer is Class A for the fixed-family problem. Given \(\tau\) types, the pair distribution and its expected cost can be evaluated by finite sums in polynomial time, and finite-site \(2\)-median optimization can be done by enumerating facility pairs. Theorem 2 predicts the exact universal solution
\[ \lambda=\lambda^*, \qquad \sup_\mu\frac{C_{\lambda^*}(\mu)}{\OPT_2(\mu)} = 3+\lambda^*. \]
This is genuinely a population continuization: facilities remain integral locations, while only the population becomes mass. For rational \(\mu\), expanding each type into the corresponding number of identical agents recovers the discrete instance; conversely, normalizing a finite profile gives \(\mu\). The pair weights converge because \(n_s n_t/n^2\to\mu_s\mu_t\).

The authors should recognize this as their problem rather than a weakened substitute. The same two mechanisms, the same utilitarian objective, the same Ptolemaic domain, the same fixed-mixture design question, and the same tight line examples all remain. Only the irrelevant naming of individual residents disappears.

A second, independent mirror is anchored by Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that, for three facilities on the line, Gap-Product is strategyproof and achieves a \(6\)-approximation. Its approximation analysis is supported by Lemma 5, which proves for every \(k\ge2\)
\[ \operatorname{SC}(\mathsf{GPM}_k) \le 2k\frac{Z_{k+1}}{Z_k} \le 2k\OPT_k, \]
with the factor \(2k\) asymptotically tight.

Let the location types be ordered as
\[ a_1<\cdots<a_\tau \]
with masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\). The continuous Gap-Product rule for three facilities selects \(a_r<a_s<a_t\) with probability proportional to
\[ \mu_r\mu_s\mu_t (a_s-a_r)(a_t-a_s). \]
Thus
\[ Z_{3,\mu} = \sum_{r<s<t} \mu_r\mu_s\mu_t (a_s-a_r)(a_t-a_s), \]
and the expected cost is
\[ C_3(\mu) = \frac{1}{Z_{3,\mu}} \sum_{r<s<t} \mu_r\mu_s\mu_t (a_s-a_r)(a_t-a_s) \sum_u\mu_u \min\{|a_u-a_r|,|a_u-a_s|,|a_u-a_t|\}. \]

The corresponding problem is:

“Continuous Gap-Product \(3\)-Median” takes a rational distribution over ordered line-location types. It must output the Gap-Product triple distribution, compute its expected social cost, compute
\[ \OPT_3(\mu) = \inf_{|F|\le3} \sum_u\mu_u\,d(a_u,F), \]
and determine whether the ratio is at most a specified \(\rho\). The predicted answer is Class A for finite support: the mechanism’s cost is a finite sum, and weighted \(3\)-median on a line is polynomial-time solvable. Theorem 4 gives the universal bound
\[ C_3(\mu)\le6\OPT_3(\mu), \]
and the paper’s tight examples have direct mass-distribution limits.

This mirror is also faithful. A discrete profile with \(n_r\) agents at \(a_r\) has \(n_rn_sn_t\) triples of the corresponding type, so after normalization the finite Gap-Product probabilities converge exactly to the mass formula above. The selected facilities remain three actual reports; there is no fractional facility or lottery over fractional outcomes.

Theorem 3 is deliberately not part of my positive case. Its Inverse-Square mechanism uses \(k=n-1\) facilities. In a genuine high-multiplicity regime with fixed \(\tau\) and \(n\to\infty\), repeated reports eventually occur, and the paper’s fallback rule covers every occupied location at zero cost. Preserving the all-distinct regime would require \(\tau=n\), which is precisely the opposite of high multiplicity. That result therefore has no comparably convincing continuum mirror.

The main weakness is strategyproofness. For an atomless population, one individual has zero mass and cannot change \(\mu\), so individual strategyproofness becomes vacuous. A nontrivial version would have to study a positive-mass perturbation:
\[ \mu_{\varepsilon,z} = (1-\varepsilon)\mu+\varepsilon\delta_z, \]
and ask whether a cohort whose true location is \(x\) can improve by changing its reported location from \(x\) to \(z\). That is a stronger, group-like notion not established by the paper. The approximation results survive cleanly; the incentive results require a new definition and possibly new proofs.

So the best positive verdict is qualified: Theorem 2 gives a particularly strong and natural Class-A high-multiplicity mirror, and Theorem 4 gives a second one on the line. But the paper has no named computational-complexity result, and its strategyproofness becomes weak unless a positive-mass deviation model is added.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest on the programme’s eligibility criterion: this paper has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 prove strategyproofness; Theorems 2 and 4 prove approximation guarantees; Lemma 5 supplies an approximation analysis. None formulates a computational problem with an input encoding, complexity classification, exact algorithm, parameterized algorithm, or hardness result. The complexity statements in the introduction belong to cited prior work, not to this paper. Thus the proposed mirrors are mathematically possible, but they are not continuizations of a computational contribution made by the paper.

The location-type story for Theorem 2 is genuinely plausible. A large city with many residents at a moderate number of standardized locations is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the proponent’s limiting formulas for Pairwise-Distance and Proportional are correct. That, however, does not make the resulting task a worthwhile computational mirror. Computing the induced pair probabilities and expected social cost from \( \tau \) types is just a finite sum. If facilities are restricted to the reported locations, the associated two-median benchmark is also a small finite optimization. This is a compressed implementation of the existing finite mechanism, not a new population-level computational problem.

The stronger version—optimizing \( \lambda \) over all continuous societies—does not repair this. Restricted to the fixed-mixture family, Theorem 2 already identifies \( \lambda^* \) and its worst-case guarantee for every finite profile. Passing from counts \( n_t \) to proportions \( \mu_t \) merely normalizes the same expressions. If instead one optimizes over arbitrary metric spaces or arbitrary facility locations, the resulting supremum is not a finite-input computational problem without adding a substantial representation model for the metric space. The proponent’s claim that \( \OPT_2 \) can be computed by enumerating pairs of support locations also silently narrows the paper’s benchmark: the paper permits facilities anywhere in \( \Omega \), and an optimal facility need not be a reported location in an arbitrary metric space. Restricting facilities to the support makes the mirror well-defined, but makes it still more plainly a routine finite evaluation problem.

The incentive half exposes a separate obstruction. In an atomless population, one individual has zero mass and cannot change \( \mu \), so strategyproofness is vacuous. If one instead lets a positive-mass type move from \( x \) to \( z \), the question becomes a coalition- or cohort-strategyproofness question. The paper proves only unilateral strategyproofness for one named agent; it gives no reason that a positive mass of identical agents cannot profitably coordinate. Keeping individual deviations restores the original finite model; allowing mass deviations creates a new theorem. There is no non-vacuous continuous version of the paper’s incentive result supplied by the paper itself.

Theorem 4 has the same problem in a cleaner setting. The proposed distribution over triples, with weight \( \mu_r\mu_s\mu_t(a_s-a_r)(a_t-a_s) \), is a faithful high-multiplicity limit. But for \( k=3 \) it reduces the paper’s result to evaluating a weighted triple sum and a weighted three-median problem on a line. That is a valid exercise, yet it is not a computational theorem mirrored from the paper. Theorem 4 already applies to every finite profile, and the continuous expression is obtained by grouping identical agents and dividing by powers of \( n \). Nothing in the proof studies the complexity of computing that expression or of solving a mass-transfer problem.

One could improve the proposed mirror by making \( k \) part of the input, studying the normalizing partition function \( Z_{k,\mu} \), or optimizing over all strategyproof randomized mechanisms rather than Gap-Product. But those would be new computational projects. The paper’s only statement for general \( k \) is the approximation inequality in Lemma 5, while strategyproofness explicitly fails for \( k\ge4 \). A variable-\( k \) partition-function problem or an optimal-mechanism problem may be interesting, but neither is a continuous version of a computational result proved here.

The paper’s actual high-multiplicity content is therefore a collection of population-normalizable performance guarantees, not a complexity landscape. The positive case is strongest as a proposal for future work: both location distributions are natural, and the approximation bounds extend formally. It is weak as a case that this paper supplies a ChoCo anchor. I would not claim that no researcher could ever devise a worthwhile continuous facility-location problem from these mechanisms. I would claim that doing so would require adding the computational question—the paper itself does not contain one—and therefore cannot support a green verdict under the programme’s stated anchor rule.
