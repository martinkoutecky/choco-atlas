The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a mirror of Theorem 6, and I would use no second anchor. Proposition 8 is useful context, but its \(XP\) upper bound is not as clean a continuous-population question.

The anchor is Theorem 6: “\(BM(TTC)\) is W[1]-hard, even when the preference domain is lexicographic.” This result is proved in the paper. Here \(BM(TTC)\) is the decision problem whose input is a generalized housing-market economy \(E=(N,O,\omega,R)\), with agent 1 distinguished, and whose question is whether agent 1 has a beneficial preference misreport under Top Trading Cycles. The parameter is the size of the relevant endowment, in particular the size of agent 1’s bundle in the reduction. The theorem says that the problem is W[1]-hard parameterized this way, even when every preference is lexicographic over bundles. The paper does not prove membership in W[1]; it explicitly leaves a matching upper bound open. The reduction is from the paper’s Lemma 5, which proves that SNAKELESS LADDER is W[1]-hard, and encodes the graph structure into TTC trading cycles.

Here is the continuous-population mirror I would put before the authors.

Consider a large exchange market for workers trading indivisible shifts, tasks, or position tokens. There is a finite catalogue \(Q\) of object types. Each worker owns exactly \(k\) unit objects, and has a lexicographic preference over bundles of those objects. A complete type is \(t=(R_t,b_t)\), where \(R_t\) is the worker’s full preference relation and \(b_t\) is the worker’s initial \(k\)-object endowment. Thus two workers have the same type exactly when they are indistinguishable for TTC: they have the same preferences and the same initial endowment.

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over a finite set \(T\) of such types. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of workers of type \(t\). Object supply is induced by the endowments: the mass of object type \(q\) is \(\sum_t \mu_t b_t(q)\). The market is therefore a genuinely high-multiplicity market: there may be millions of workers but only a moderate number of preference-and-endowment types. A realistic example is a national or multi-campus shift exchange with a finite catalogue of roles, where thousands of workers belong to each recurring preference/endowment cohort.

The mechanism remains TTC with indivisible objects. Its continuous version, \(\mathrm{TTC}_\infty\), is the mass implementation of the paper’s procedure. At every stage, each remaining object unit points to the best remaining object for the type owning it; positive-mass cycles transfer their bottleneck mass around the cycle and are removed. A fixed ordering of cycle templates resolves any aggregate tie. Equivalently, for rational \(\mu\), one can expand the market into a type-symmetric finite market with \(D\mu_t\) copies of every type, run the paper’s TTC procedure, and divide all allocation counts by \(D\). Each unit worker still receives an integral bundle; only the mass of workers receiving each bundle is fractional.

The strategic object is a positive-mass cohort rather than one named worker. Fix a true type \(t^\star=(R^\star,b^\star)\) and a mass \(\delta\leq\mu_{t^\star}\). The cohort may submit a common lexicographic report \(\widehat R\), leaving its endowments unchanged. The action is therefore the mass transfer

\[ \delta\text{ units of }(R^\star,b^\star) \longrightarrow \delta\text{ units of }(\widehat R,b^\star). \]

The natural objective is the cohort’s average true utility. Since the set of bundles is finite, choose any numerical utility \(u_{R^\star}\) representing the lexicographic order. Let \(U^\circ\) be the average utility of the target cohort under truthful \(\mathrm{TTC}_\infty\), and \(U(\widehat R)\) its average utility after the report. The continuous problem is:

\[ \mathrm{BM(TTC)}_\infty: \]

Given \(Q\), \(k\), the finite type set \(T\), rational distribution \(\mu\), a target type \(t^\star\), a target mass \(\delta\), and the TTC tie-breaking convention, does there exist a lexicographic report \(\widehat R\) such that

\[ U(\widehat R)>U^\circ? \]

A solution consists of the report \(\widehat R\), together with the resulting mass allocation under \(\mathrm{TTC}_\infty\), certifying the strict improvement. The closest analogue of the paper’s decision problem takes \(\delta=\mu_{t^\star}\): the entire repeated type cohort coordinates on one report. A useful optimization variant would minimize the successful coalition mass \(\delta\).

This is recognizably the same problem. It retains ownership, heterogeneous indivisible objects, no money, multiple endowments, lexicographic bundle preferences, TTC’s report-dependent cycle structure, and the question of whether a strategic report can improve the resulting allocation. The only change is the one demanded by continuization: “agent 1” becomes a large cohort of indistinguishable agents, and the outcome is recorded as masses of integral bundles rather than as a list of named agents. This is not fractional assignment or probabilistic TTC.

The expected classification is hardness transfer, namely Class B, at least when parameterized by \(k\). The combinatorics in Theorem 6 live in the graph encoded by the good types and preference lists: rungs, snakes, and the existence of a snakeless ladder. They do not fundamentally rely on every gadget being represented by a unique named person. One can replicate each agent and object in the reduction by a large common multiplicity and let the corresponding type cohort submit the same report. The market then has \(N\gg\tau\), while the graph structure remains present at the type level. A successful continuous manipulation should correspond to the original snakeless ladder, so the W[1]-hardness is expected to survive.

That transfer is not automatic. The paper proves hardness for one agent, whereas the mirror gives a positive-mass bloc a coordinated report. A proof would have to control aggregate TTC cycles and rule out new cross-copy trades created by replication. This generates worthwhile follow-up questions: does W[1]-hardness persist when every type has very large multiplicity? Is the problem fixed-parameter tractable in \(k+\tau\)? Can the mass-TTC process be represented by a finite flow or configuration formulation when \(\tau\) is fixed? Could the report-mass version itself exhibit continuum-specific hardness even if the one-agent version becomes easy under some type restriction?

The mirror covers Theorem 6 only. It does not claim that the paper’s reduction already proves the continuous result, nor that the paper’s \(XP\) algorithm in Proposition 8 automatically yields a polynomial algorithm in the mass model.

The weakest point is the change from one strategic agent to a coordinated positive-mass cohort. If the authors insist that manipulation must remain unilateral, this mirror is not exact. A tagged-agent version could preserve one manipulator by adding a distinguished atom to an otherwise continuous society, but that weakens the population-level character of the model. The stronger defence is that high multiplicity naturally turns a repeated agent type into the strategic unit, just as the programme turns individual voter counts into population masses. The mirror therefore changes the strategic scale, but not the underlying TTC question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s case fails at the word “equivalently.” A clone expansion of the economy is not automatically the continuous version of \(BM(TTC)\).

Theorem 6 concerns one identified agent whose private report changes the allocation of one private bundle. In the reduction, agent 1 owns the single goods \(e_\alpha,e_\beta,e_1,\ldots,e_k\), and success means that this same agent receives both \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). That correlation is essential: it encodes one snakeless ladder. The theorem is not merely about the aggregate number of agents of each preference type.

In an atomless population, a unilateral report by one agent changes no mass. If the society is \(\mu\), replacing one agent’s type by another changes \(\mu\) by zero in the continuum limit, so every positive-mass TTC cycle is unchanged. The deviator’s own allocation can be tracked only by retaining a distinguished atom and its individual goods. That produces a mixed discrete–continuous model containing the original manipulation problem, not a genuinely continuous mirror.

The proposed repair makes a positive-mass cohort deviate together:

\[ \delta (R^\star,b^\star) \longrightarrow \delta(\widehat R,b^\star). \]

That is a coalition-manipulation problem, not \(BM(TTC)\). More importantly, the paper gives only an ordinal preference over integral bundles. A positive-mass cohort generally receives a distribution \(\lambda\) over bundles, so the proponent’s average utility is not well-defined from the paper’s data. If \(B_1\succ B_2\succ B_3\), a truthful cohort receiving \(B_2\) and a deviating cohort receiving \(B_1\) and \(B_3\) equally often can be ranked either way by two numerical representations of the same lexicographic order, for example

\[ u(B_1),u(B_2),u(B_3)=(3,2,0) \]

versus

\[ u(B_1),u(B_2),u(B_3)=(100,2,0). \]

Stochastic dominance avoids arbitrary cardinal utilities, but then the proposed problem is no longer the paper’s beneficial-misreport predicate. Requiring every cohort member to receive the same improved bundle is another repair, again a new restriction.

There is also a specifically TTC obstruction. Repeating an agent with a \(k\)-object endowment creates many copies of each \(e_j\). Separate TTC cycles can transfer the copies of \(e_1,\ldots,e_k\) to different clone agents. Aggregate mass records the quantities transferred, but not how those transfers are coupled into individual bundles. The paper’s witness requires one agent to obtain \(\{\alpha,\beta\}\), not merely positive mass of agents obtaining \(\alpha\) and positive mass obtaining \(\beta\).

To restore that coupling, one must introduce copy “lanes,” labelled ownership correlations, or an additional configuration variable specifying which goods end up with the same clone. Lanes retain precisely the identity information that continuization was meant to remove; configuration coupling creates a new allocation problem. If instead the lanes are fixed so that every clone follows an independent copy of the original reduction, the result is just \(D\) synchronized copies of the finite instance, with the mass notation acting as a normalized census. That may preserve hardness, but it does not establish a population-level mirror of unilateral TTC manipulation.

The natural shift-exchange story does show that repeated preference-and-endowment types can be plausible. So the objection is not that high multiplicity is impossible in exchange markets. The obstruction is that the paper’s computational object is an identity-sensitive, single-agent deviation over a multi-object bundle. Its atomless version is inert; its positive-mass version is coalition manipulation; and its exact clone version needs extra identity or coupling structure.

I therefore do not think the proponent has established a worthwhile continuous mirror of Theorem 6. An independently designed mass-coalition TTC problem could certainly be worthwhile, but that is a new problem rather than a continuization of this theorem. The universal claim that no such extension could ever be worthwhile would be too strong; the negative case is strongest as a claim that the paper itself supplies no faithful population-continuous anchor.
