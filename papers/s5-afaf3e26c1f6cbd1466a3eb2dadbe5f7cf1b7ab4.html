The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2>0\) with \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\), and rational thresholds \(0\le\alpha\le\mu_1\), \(0\le\beta\le\mu_2\), a coalition is winning when its yes-mass vector \((x_1,x_2)\in[0,\mu_1]\times[0,\mu_2]\) satisfies \(x_1\ge\alpha\) and \(x_1+x_2\ge\alpha+\beta\). Decide whether there exist type weights \(w_1\ge w_2\ge0\) and quota \(q\) such that every feasible coalition wins exactly when \(w_1x_1+w_2x_2\ge q\).

The model it lives in

A nonatomic two-tier federation voting on one proposal, with interchangeable members within each tier, type masses \(\mu_1,\mu_2\), and coalition decisions represented by all feasible mass vectors \((x_1,x_2)\). The decision variables are type weights and a quota; the objective is feasibility of an exact weighted representation.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers only Theorem 3.3’s weightedness classification; it leaves the enumeration formulas and Shapley–Shubik and Banzhaf rankings unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove the proposed continuous classification: weightedness holds exactly when \(\alpha=0\), \(\beta=0\), or \(\beta=\mu_2\).
  • Determine how weightedness behaves with more tiers or multiple shift-minimal winning vectors.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: mirror the paper’s weightedness characterization, not its enumeration formulas or comparisons of power indices.

Theorem 3.3 says that a non-trivial complete game with one shift-minimal winning vector is weighted exactly when it has one player type, or when it has two types and the second coordinate \(m_2\) of its winning vector is \(1\) or \(n_2-1\). Thus games with three or more types in this class are not weighted. The paper includes a proof; it also notes that the classification first appeared in Freixas [11]. Although the theorem does not state a standard complexity bound, its criterion gives a polynomial-time weightedness test for this restricted family.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large federation with full and associate members voting on a proposal. Members within each tier have the same role; there may be hundreds of thousands of members but only two types, so \(\tau=2\ll n\). Let their population masses be \(\mu_1,\mu_2\), with \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\). A coalition is represented by the masses \(x_1,x_2\) voting yes. For rational thresholds \(0\le\alpha\le\mu_1\) and \(0\le\beta\le\mu_2\), define a winning coalition by \(x_1\ge\alpha\) and \(x_1+x_2\ge\alpha+\beta\). This is the mass version of the paper’s two-type, one-vector rule: with \(n\) members and finite vector \((a,b)\), take \(\alpha=a/n\) and \(\beta=b/n\).

The resulting Two-Tier Mass Weightedness problem is: given \((\mu_1,\mu_2,\alpha,\beta)\), do there exist type weights \(w_1\ge w_2\ge0\) and quota \(q\) such that, for every feasible coalition mass vector, it wins exactly when \(w_1x_1+w_2x_2\ge q\)? A solution is a yes/no answer, with weights and quota as a witness for yes. This is a feasibility problem, without a separate optimization objective.

For the genuinely two-type interior, \(0<\alpha<\mu_1\) and \(0<\beta<\mu_2\), I expect the answer to be no: the winning boundary has a vertical segment and a diagonal segment, while one weighted quota supplies only a single linear boundary. Across the full parameter range, the rule is weighted exactly in the degenerate cases \(\alpha=0\), \(\beta=0\), or \(\beta=\mu_2\), where one constraint becomes redundant. So the mirror is tractable by a direct geometric characterization. It also makes the finite exceptions informative: the paper’s \(m_2=1\) and \(m_2=n_2-1\) cases approach \(\beta=0\) and \(\beta=\mu_2\) under population normalization. In the continuous model, those endpoints collapse a constraint and erase the genuine two-type distinction.

This would mirror Theorem 3.3 only. It does not cover the paper’s counts of weighted games or its Shapley–Shubik and Banzhaf rankings. Useful follow-up questions are whether finite rules near these endpoints approximate the continuous rule in a meaningful distance, and how the weightedness classification extends to more types or multiple shift-minimal vectors.

The weak point is that the paper studies structural representability, not a standard NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity result; the proposed continuous decision problem may be too easy to add much to the programme’s complexity landscape. Its direct connection to the paper’s central question of weightedness, and the change from finite one-voter exceptions to a mass boundary, are the reasons this remains a defensible mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject this anchor, though not because its high multiplicity story is implausible. A large federation with many interchangeable members in two voting tiers is a sensible regime, and the proposed mass model preserves the game’s meaning.

The problem is that Theorem 3.3 is a structural classification, not a complexity result. It characterizes when a finite complete game with one shift-minimal winning vector is weighted; the paper does not pose or analyze an algorithmic problem. The theorem’s criterion is already a direct check on the type count and one threshold coordinate. Calling weightedness recognition a decision problem is reasonable, but it gives the mirror very little computational substance.

The proposed continuous version makes that limitation clearer. Its winning set is
\[ \{(x_1,x_2): x_1\ge\alpha,\; x_1+x_2\ge\alpha+\beta\}. \]
In the interior case, two distinct boundary segments are active, with different normals; at the degenerate cases one constraint becomes redundant. That is a simple geometric test in two dimensions. Extending the same one-vector question to \(\tau\) tiers gives \(\tau\) nested prefix constraints. The continuum fills the gaps in the finite coalition lattice, so the exceptional finite cases become boundary cases, but there is no apparent pricing problem or other algorithmic bottleneck for ChoCo to chart.

The natural alternatives do not make this anchor stronger. The paper’s enumeration results count discrete games; a continuum has no corresponding count without adding an arbitrary measure or prior. Its power-index results rank individual players using swings and permutations. One could define a type-aggregated limit, but that would be a new limit-index question, not a computational result mirrored from this paper.

The negative case is strongest on the mismatch with ChoCo’s computational remit, not on the population model. It is not airtight: a broader problem about continuous weightedness with many shift-minimal vectors might become computationally substantive. But that would be a different, expanded project; this theorem’s natural mirror looks like a short geometric classification rather than a worthwhile complexity study.
