The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational complexity result to anchor a continuous mirror on. It has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting that a problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its complexity. Section 4 gives analytic bounds on the IAC inversion probability, and the paper reports numerical evidence for its asymptotic behavior. Those are results, but not the kind of computational anchors this task requires. Numbered equations do not fill that role, so there is no honest way to supply one continuous problem per anchor.

The strongest positive fragment is the paper’s IAC\(^*\) comparison, rather than its central IAC result. In a continuum model, let a type be a district and a left or right preference, with mass \(\mu_{j,L}=p_j/3\) and \(\mu_{j,R}=(1-p_j)/3\) in district \(j\). If the three district shares \(p_j\) are independent uniform draws, the two-tier winner differs from the popular-vote winner with probability \(1/8\). The paper’s finite-\(n\) IAC\(^*\) probability, \((n^2+2n-3)/(8(n+1)^2)\), tends to that value. This is a plausible continuous-population interpretation of that variant, with three large districts and at most six types. But it is an analytic probability calculation, not a named complexity result.

For the paper’s main IAC model, a continuum mirror loses the phenomenon being studied: one common latent \(p\) gives each district the same left-voter mass \(p\), so district and popular winners agree except at the tie boundary. The paper’s inversion probability comes from finite-population fluctuations around that shared \(p\), and its asymptotic bounds concern how those fluctuations vanish. Treating probability over finite profiles as the continuous object would miss ChoCo’s population-continuization target.

So there is a modest continuous-population connection in the IAC\(^*\) comparison, but no eligible anchor for the requested complexity case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s strongest obstacle is the programme’s entry condition: it gives no named computational result to mirror. Its main result is an asymptotic probability calculation for a finite random profile, supported by numerical computation and analytic bounds. The code is a way to evaluate that probability; the paper does not classify the complexity of a computational problem or establish an algorithmic guarantee of the kind ChoCo is charting.

For the main IAC model, the natural continuum society has the same left-voter fraction \(p\) in every district. Except at the tie boundary \(p=\tfrac12\), the district and popular-vote outcomes agree. The finite-population inversion is therefore a fluctuation around a shared mass vector; in the continuum, the event itself disappears. That is a real degeneracy for this mirror, rather than a claim about whether the paper’s finite-\(n\) result is important.

The proponent’s best escape is \(\mathrm{IAC}^*\): give each district its own mass \(p_j\), with types \((j,L)\) and \((j,R)\). This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s finite-\(n\) formula supports it. Its continuum question—measure the mass vectors for which the two winners differ—is coherent, but it is a geometric probability calculation, not a computational result in the paper. Extending it to variable numbers of districts, seat weights, or richer priors could yield an algorithmic project; that would be a new problem inspired by this setting, not a mirror anchored on one of this paper’s computational theorems.

So the strongest negative case is that neither candidate supplies the missing computational anchor: IAC degenerates at the population limit, while \(\mathrm{IAC}^*\) supports a continuous analytic probability question outside the programme’s stated focus. I would not claim to have ruled out every worthwhile algorithmic generalization; the paper alone does not settle that broader possibility.
