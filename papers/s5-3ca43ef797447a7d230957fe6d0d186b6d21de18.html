The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are about Nash implementation: whether a social choice rule can be implemented by a mechanism under specified reversion functions. None asserts a complexity classification or gives an algorithm for a computational problem.

The closest candidate is Proposition 5, proved here: for a non-rewarding reversion function, if a continuous feasible taxation method is Nash implementable, it must be a serial dictatorship; conversely, every serial dictatorship is implementable for any non-rewarding reversion function. That is an implementation characterization, not a computational result. And “continuous” there describes the taxation method as a function of a finite vector of agents’ incomes; the society still has \(n\) named agents.

A large tax base with a few income and preference types could motivate a new population-continuum version of the taxation question. But Proposition 5 depends on ordering individual agents, and the paper does not establish that its characterization survives aggregation into type masses. That could be a worthwhile extension, but presenting it as a mirror of a named computational result would overstate the case. There are therefore no anchors here on which to base the requested per-anchor continuous problems.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The attached analyst names no continuous question to defend. Their closest candidate is Proposition 5, and they explicitly conclude that it is not a computational anchor. The paper’s numbered propositions characterize Nash implementability, monotonicity, and particular social choice rules. None gives an algorithm or a complexity result, and the paper supplies no computational input representation whose population dimension could be continuized. That is the strongest ground for a negative verdict: a complexity problem inspired by these results would be a new research question, rather than a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Proposition 5 offers the most plausible reconstruction. A large tax authority could face a population with repeated income types, represent it by a distribution over those types, and ask which tax rules are Nash implementable. But the proposition’s “continuous” taxation method is a continuous function of a finite vector of named agents’ incomes. Its serial dictatorship orders those agents individually. A distribution over income types does not record that order or the tax assigned to each person. Adding individual priority ranks restores the identity information while losing high multiplicity; treating each type as a player changes a unilateral deviation into a deviation by a whole group. Either route changes the implementation problem at the heart of the proposition. A mass-level rule with priorities over types could be worth studying, but it would be a new rule and a new implementation question.

The exchange-economy result has a more natural large-population setting: many households may share a small number of endowment and preference types. Yet Proposition 3 establishes that the Constrained Walrasian rule is implementable; it does not study the computation of an equilibrium or mechanism. A population-level algorithm for finding allocations would therefore address a different result. Lifting the implementation theorem itself also faces the individual-deviation issue: a single agent has zero mass in the aggregate description, while the paper’s Nash conditions depend on that agent’s deviation and resulting allocation.

So the negative case is strongest on the programme’s target: this paper contains no computational result to mirror, and its leading examples do not make the implementation questions into population-mass problems without changing their strategic structure. The universal claim is still not airtight. A specified continuum taxation or exchange implementation model might be worthwhile, especially if the authors regard group-level deviations or aggregate rules as part of their problem. The paper alone, however, gives little basis for calling such work a continuous mirror rather than a new project.
