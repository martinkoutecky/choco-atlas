The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many schools and subjects, a rational mass distribution \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) over teacher types, rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_{s,p}\), school rankings over types for each subject, and each type’s ranking of acceptable schools, does there exist a feasible assignment flow \(x_{t,s}\) with no positive-mass blocking deviation? A blocking deviation moves some \(\delta>0\) mass of type \(t\) from an unassigned or less-preferred destination to school \(s\), which can accept it either using slack in both subjects or by one of the paper’s permitted displacement patterns, with capacities feasible after the exchange and the displaced mass ranked below \(t\) in the relevant subject or subjects.

The model it lives in

A non-atomic cohort model: each type records its two subjects, school preference list, and subject-specific priority category; \(\mu_t\) is its population share; \(x_{t,s}\) is assigned mass; and \(\kappa_{s,p}\) is school \(s\)’s per-capita capacity for subject \(p\). Stability excludes positive-mass deviations allowed by the paper’s blocking clauses.

The objection that survived

Theorem 4 uses strict individual master-list positions, whereas the mirror groups applicants into priority bands and needs new rules for mass displacement; its reduction therefore says nothing about the mirror’s complexity.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers Theorem 4’s subject-specific stable-matching existence problem; it does not mirror the other stability variants or the minimum-blocking-pair inapproximability results.

Open questions for a prover

  • Do stable flows always exist for this type-priority model?
  • Can stable-flow existence be decided or a stable flow computed in time polynomial in the number of schools, supported types, and input bit length?
  • How does the flow model relate to finite teacher-assignment instances with repeated profiles?

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I find most convincing is Stable Teachers-to-Schools with Divisible Cohorts and Subject Priorities (\(\mathrm{TAP\text{-}SS}_{\infty}\)), based on Theorem 4, proved in this paper. The theorem says that deciding whether a stable matching exists in \(\mathrm{TAP\text{-}SS}\) is NP-complete even with at most three subjects, partial capacities at most one, schools’ subject-specific preferences derived from master lists of applicants, and applicants’ preferences derived from one master list of schools. The paper proves this through Lemmas 3 and 4, reducing from restricted satisfiability; the underlying SAT hardness is cited to Berman et al.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large regional or national placement of new teachers, where many applicants share a subject pair, a standard list of acceptable schools, and the same credential category used by schools to rank applicants in each subject. The applicants might number in the tens of thousands while the number of such profiles remains modest. A type \(t\) records the teacher’s subject pair, school preference list, and priority category at each subject. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of the cohort with that complete profile. School capacities are stated as fractions of the cohort. A finite set of schools can have capacities that scale with the size of the intake.

The paper assumes strict rankings of individual applicants. For a cohort to be genuinely indistinguishable, the mirror needs schools to rank priority categories, with applicants in the same category tied. That is a natural high-multiplicity extension of the paper’s subject-specific priorities: the same two-subject capacity constraints and the same four kinds of blocking deviation remain central. The authors might see the tie extension as a modeling choice to examine, but the resulting question is recognizably about their stability problem.

Precisely, an instance consists of a finite set of schools and subjects; a rational distribution \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) over teacher types; each type’s two subjects and acceptable-school ranking; subject-specific priority orders over types; and rational per-capita capacities \(\kappa_{s,p}\). A candidate matching is a nonnegative flow \(x_{t,s}\), including an unassigned destination, with \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t:p\in t}x_{t,s}\leq\kappa_{s,p}\). The question is whether there is such a flow with no positive-mass blocking deviation: some \(\delta>0\) of a type prefers a school to its current destination, and that school can accept the mass using slack in both relevant subjects or by dropping incumbent mass according to one of the paper’s subject-specific blocking clauses. In those clauses, counts become masses and comparisons use the school’s priorities over types. A solution is a feasible flow satisfying this stability condition.

I would tentatively expect this problem to be Class A, with an algorithm polynomial in the number of schools, supported types, and input bit length. Divisibility may remove the discrete all-or-nothing choices that make the paper’s SAT gadgets work, leaving a mass-allocation problem amenable to continuous optimization. That is a conjectural direction, not a consequence of Theorem 4: the blocking conditions can create disjunctions, so finding a tractable formulation is itself the challenge. Further questions include whether stable flows always exist, whether one can compute one exactly, and how closely a computed flow can guide a discrete high-multiplicity placement.

The weakest point is that Theorem 4’s strict master lists distinguish applicants by their individual positions. In its reduction, treating those positions as part of the complete type can make the support grow with the number of applicants; treating applicants in a priority category as one type introduces ties absent from the theorem. So the discrete NP-hardness does not establish hardness of this mass problem. That limitation is also the reason the mirror is worth asking: it tests whether the paper’s difficult stability phenomenon survives when large, otherwise indistinguishable cohorts can divide across schools. This case covers Theorem 4’s subject-specific preference variant; it does not claim a mirror for the paper’s other results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is a real computational anchor: it proves NP-completeness for deciding whether a stable matching exists, even with three subjects and both kinds of master-list restrictions. So the negative case cannot be that the paper has nothing computational to mirror.

The strongest objection is about the instance regime. In Theorem 4’s reduction, applicants’ positions in subject-specific master lists are part of how the instance distinguishes them. A type that preserves every preference and priority distinction may therefore be unique to each applicant, making the support grow with the population. This theorem gives no evidence that its hard instances, or the paper’s stated matching problem generally, have a useful high-multiplicity regime.

The proponent’s better version is a credible one: repeated preference templates and credential bands, with schools ranking bands and capacities scaled to cohort size. But this replaces the paper’s strict rankings of applicants with priorities over groups, and requires the authors to specify how ties affect blocking. The mass analogue also needs precise rules for how much incoming mass can displace which incumbent mass under clauses that refer to one or two distinct teachers. Those are legitimate modelling choices, but the theorem does not select among them.

That weakens the connection to Theorem 4; it does not defeat the proposed mirror. A large placement cohort with repeated preference templates and coarse priority bands is plausible, and a continuous stability question for that setting would still look recognizable as a version of the paper’s problem. I can make the negative case against a direct continuation of the theorem’s strict-priority model, but I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile mirror under the coarser, high-multiplicity model. The case against is weak.
