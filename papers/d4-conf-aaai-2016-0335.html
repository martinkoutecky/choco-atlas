The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the anchor stage: the proposed case for names no theorem, lemma, corollary, or other result establishing the complexity of a computational problem. It therefore raises no anchor to defeat. The paper formulates Bayesian inference for one agent and reports model inferences and human experiments. Its exact enumeration over a discretized parameter grid is an implementation choice, not a complexity result. The experiment sample sizes—120, 31, 40, and 50—are judges evaluating an agent’s behavior, not a modeled population whose computational problem the paper studies.

The best mirror I can construct is a recommendation platform with many users: represent users by a distribution over complete behavioral types \(\theta=(p(s),U,Y,k,\alpha)\), then infer cohort preferences from their action histories. That is a plausible population-level research question. But it changes the paper’s target. The paper seeks a posterior about a particular agent from that agent’s sequence of choices. A cohort distribution instead asks about population composition or an aggregate preference. If the system must make personalized recommendations, it still needs to connect each person’s history to that person’s inferred preferences; a mass over types alone does not supply that link. If it only needs a cohort-level policy, it has posed a new task.

Grouping users with the same \(\theta\) does not resolve this mismatch. Their histories are the evidence from which \(\theta\) is inferred. When many users share a model and context, repeated observations can be combined in a likelihood; that is useful data aggregation, but the paper establishes no computational result about it. For heterogeneous users, the cohort problem becomes inference over a distribution of latent agent models. That may be worth studying, but it introduces a new estimand and new algorithmic questions rather than continuizing a result in this paper.

So the paper offers no qualifying result for a continuous mirror, and the natural cohort extension is not a mirror of its stated computational contribution. The broader claim that *no* scenario could yield worthwhile population-level work is less secure: large recommendation systems make cohort inference plausible. The honest negative case is narrower but firm—the paper itself supplies no result to continuize, and its most natural population extension would be a different research problem.
