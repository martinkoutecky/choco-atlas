The continuous mirror question

Given a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), a team-size bound \(B\), and negative-valued embedded rules whose conditions test type counts and absences, compute the maximum normalized social welfare and an optimal feasible pair \((x,G)\). Here \(x_q\ge 0\) is the per-capita number of teams of composition \(q\), with \(\sum_q q_t x_q=\mu_t\); \(G\) is a finite set of zero-per-capita witness compositions drawn from the supported types. A rule applies to a focal team when its focal condition holds and each externality condition is witnessed either by positive mass of teams in \(x\) or by a composition in \(G\).

The model it lives in

A typed partition-function game with population distribution \(\mu\), bounded team compositions \(q\), and allocation variables \(x_q\); finite witness compositions \(G\) preserve the paper’s condition that an externality can be triggered by at least one other coalition. The objective is to maximize welfare per capita under the type-mass constraints.

The objection that survived

The named-agent theorems do not establish results for the proposed type-count rules or bounded team sizes; the paper’s mention of type-based games supports the scenario but does not show that this restricted input language is covered by those theorems.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the negative embedded-rule CSG optimization anchored by Theorem 5, with a parallel positive-rule version anchored by Theorem 3. It leaves the general EWPM transformation results, unrestricted named-agent instances, and the experimental comparison of encodings outside the continuous question.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does optimizing over witness sets \(G\) and allocations \(x\) admit an efficient or parameterized algorithm in \(|T|\), \(B\), the number of rules, and input bit length?
  • Can the negative externality interactions yield continuum-specific hardness, or can they be reduced to a tractable optimization over coalition compositions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The mirror I would defend is a high multiplicity version of coalition structure generation in partition function games. Consider a citywide buying cooperative: there may be hundreds of thousands of customers, but many share the same product needs, budget, prices, eligibility, and willingness to join. Those complete descriptions are the types \(T\); their population shares are rational masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). The number of customers \(N\) is much larger than the number of types \(\tau=|T|\). Teams form purchasing coalitions, and a coalition’s value may depend on which other kinds of coalitions form, as in the paper’s externality model.

Here is a continuous problem I would put to the authors. Let \(B\) be the maximum team size, and let \(\mathcal Q_B\) be the finite set of nonempty integer vectors \(q\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{T}\) with \(\sum_t q_t\le B\). A vector \(q\) describes a team’s numbers of members of each type. An embedded rule has a focal-team condition \(I_0\), externality conditions \(I_1,\ldots,I_k\), and a rational value \(w\). Each condition tests lower bounds on type counts and the absence of specified types. A feasible allocation is \(x_q\ge0\), where \(x_q\) is the number of teams of composition \(q\) per capita, satisfying \(\sum_{q\in\mathcal Q_B}q_t x_q=\mu_t\) for every type \(t\).

The externality test needs care in a continuum. A condition can be witnessed by positive mass of teams satisfying it, or by a finite “witness team” made from types with positive population mass. The latter tracks the paper’s “at least one other coalition” semantics: in a sequence of larger finite societies, a bounded number of such teams has vanishing per-capita mass, but can still affect the value of many other teams. Write \(G\) for the finite set of these witness compositions. For a focal composition \(q\), an embedded rule applies when \(q\) satisfies \(I_0\) and, for each \(j\ge1\), either positive mass of teams satisfies \(I_j\), or some \(g\in G\) satisfies \(I_j\). The objective is normalized social welfare, \(W(x,G)=\sum_q x_q v_{x,G}(q)\), where \(v_{x,G}(q)\) is the sum of the values of the embedded rules applying to \(q\). Given rational \(\varepsilon>0\), the task is to compute the supremum and return a feasible rational pair \((x,G)\) within \(\varepsilon\) of it. For a discrete society of \(N\) agents, a coalition structure gives \(x_q=z_q/N\), where \(z_q\) is the number of teams of composition \(q\). Thus this is a high multiplicity relaxation of the same partition-and-value problem.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper. It says that the paper’s encoding for a CSG instance consisting of positive-value embedded rules, together with the transitivity constraints on agent relations, lets a MaxSAT solver return the correct CSG result. The continuous question \( \mathrm{Typed\ Embedded\ CSG}_{\infty}^{+} \) is the model above restricted to positive rule values: given type masses and a positive embedded-rule system, compute the supremum normalized social welfare and an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal allocation and witness set. The underlying task is still to partition the population into exhaustive coalitions and maximize their aggregate value; the question is not about the MaxSAT encoding.

I expect this positive-only mirror to have a Class A route. Since every rule value is positive, any feasible externality condition can be activated by a finite witness team without affecting normalized population mass. Once those beneficial conditions are accounted for, the objective is linear over coalition compositions and the allocation constraints form a configuration LP. The central algorithmic question is whether the pricing problem—finding a composition \(q\) with best rule value minus its dual type prices—can be solved efficiently from the concise rule input. Theorem 3 does not solve that pricing problem, but it gives a sound computational anchor for the underlying optimization task. Whether the pricing problem is tractable in \((\tau,B,\lvert ER\rvert,L)\) is a natural further question.

A second, distinct anchor is Theorem 5, also proved in this paper. It says that for a CSG instance consisting of negative-value embedded rules, the encoding in Definition 9 together with the transitivity constraints lets a MaxSAT solver return the correct result; the encoded welfare is shifted upward by \(-W_{\mathrm{neg}}\), where \(W_{\mathrm{neg}}\) is the sum of the negative rule weights. Its mirror, \( \mathrm{Typed\ Embedded\ CSG}_{\infty}^{-} \), uses the same instance and solution definition, but restricts all rule values to be negative. The task is to maximize normalized welfare, equivalently to avoid as much negative coalition value as possible. I expect this version to be hard for continuum-specific reasons: finite witness teams no longer help, while even a very small positive mass of teams can activate an externality condition that changes the value of many teams. The resulting support choices are coupled to fractional allocation. Whether that support dependence admits an efficient algorithm, is FPT in the number of types or externality conditions, or yields a genuine continuum-specific hardness result are open questions.

These are formulation-correctness theorems, not named complexity classifications: the paper proves neither that its underlying CSG problems are in P nor that they are NP-hard in Theorems 3 or 5. It does state in the introduction that CSG is NP-complete, citing [10], but that is a background claim rather than a numbered result of this paper, so I would not use it as an anchor. The mirror covers the underlying positive and negative embedded-MC-net CSG problems those theorems compute, not the MaxSAT encoding as a research question. The paper’s experimental speedup is useful context, but it is not itself a named complexity result.

My weakest point is the move from named agents to types. The paper’s rules refer to particular agents; two people who share a customer type can still be distinguished by such a rule. The mirror therefore needs the type-level, symmetric rule language described above, where a rule tests counts of roles or types rather than individual identities. This is a substantive extension. It is plausible as a mirror of the authors’ problem because the objective remains coalition structure generation and externalities still depend on other coalitions, and the paper itself discusses type-based work on games with mixed externalities [17]. But if identity-specific rule structure is essential to the intended problem, the proposed low-\(\tau\) regime would no longer faithfully represent it. The type-level version, rather than the broader claim that every instance in the paper has a continuous counterpart, is the case I would ask the authors and a referee to judge.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 3 and Theorem 5 establish correctness of finite MaxSAT encodings. Their proofs rely on Boolean variables for pairs of named agents and transitivity constraints, which put assignments in correspondence with partitions. Theorem 5 is the negative-weight case of the same encoding argument. Neither theorem classifies the complexity of CSG. A configuration LP over type compositions could be a worthwhile new problem, but its results would not be continuous versions of what these theorems prove; they would be new results about a changed model.

The proposed model also has a more serious limit problem: PFG externalities turn on when *at least one* other coalition satisfies a condition. That fact is invisible in the limiting population masses. For example, take a negative rule that penalizes every focal \(a\)-team if some other team has composition \(ab\). A partition with no \(ab\)-team and one with a single \(ab\)-team can have the same limiting type masses and the same limiting per-capita coalition allocation: they differ by only \(O(1/N)\). Yet the single witness can change the welfare of \(O(N)\) focal teams, changing normalized welfare by a constant. So welfare is not a continuous function of the proposed bulk allocation.

The finite witness set \(G\) records that missing information, but it makes the model a distribution plus a separate finite list of exceptional coalitions. For positive rules, those witnesses can often activate every individually feasible externality at negligible per-capita cost, draining much of the interaction from the optimization. For negative rules, one such witness can impose an extensive penalty. Defining externality by positive density would restore a bulk description, but it would change the paper’s “at least one coalition” semantics. Neither choice follows uniquely from the paper.

There is also a real type-space cost. In a general MC-net, agents can be distinguished by their occurrence across named-agent rules. Their complete types may therefore number \(n\), eliminating the low-support regime. The proposed composition model avoids this by imposing type-count rules and bounded team size \(B\), a substantive restriction on the input language. That restriction may be natural for some applications, but Theorem 3 and Theorem 5 do not establish it as a mirror of their general setting.

These points weaken both anchors and the claimed direct bridge to them; they do not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The paper itself cites type-based work on partition-function games, and large populations forming teams from a small number of complete types are plausible. A carefully specified version could yield meaningful computational questions. The honest negative case is therefore strongest as a warning that the proposed continuum is singular and requires new semantics, and weak as the universal claim that no scenario warrants a mirror.
