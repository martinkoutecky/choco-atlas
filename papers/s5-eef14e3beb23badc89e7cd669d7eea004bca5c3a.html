What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow. Strictly, this paper has no named computational-complexity result: it proves no result classifying a problem as tractable or hard. The only plausible anchor is Theorem 1, if a closed-form solution to a mechanism-optimization problem counts as an algorithmic result. Theorem 1 is proved in this paper.

Theorem 1 says that, with \(N\) agents whose valuations are drawn independently from a distribution \(F\) satisfying the paper’s monotone-hazard-rate condition, the utilitarian-welfare-maximizing social choice function among anonymous, strategy-proof, feasible mechanisms satisfying universal participation can be implemented by qualified majority voting. The proposal is accepted when at least \(\lceil k\rceil\) agents report positive valuations, where
\[ k=\frac{-N\,\mathbb{E}[\theta_i\mid\theta_i\leq 0]} {\mathbb{E}[\theta_i\mid\theta_i\geq 0]-\mathbb{E}[\theta_i\mid\theta_i\leq 0]}. \]
The optimal rule uses no monetary transfers. This is the paper’s strongest possible anchor; Corollary 1 is another mechanism-design characterization, not a computational result.

A plausible mirror is Large-Population Welfare-Optimal Voting. Its instance is a valuation distribution \(F\) with an explicit representation permitting exact computation of its first moments, promised to satisfy the paper’s regularity assumptions. For each population size \(N\), draw \(N\) valuations independently from \(F\). Consider sequences of anonymous direct mechanisms, one for each \(N\), each strategy-proof, feasible without an external budget injection, and satisfying universal participation. The problem is to find the largest limiting expected welfare per person and a sequence of mechanisms attaining it. A solution also specifies the induced rule on limiting empirical valuation distributions \(\mu\).

The candidate solution uses zero transfers and, at each \(N\), accepts when at least \(\lceil N\alpha_F\rceil\) voters have positive valuations, with
\[ \alpha_F=\frac{-\mathbb{E}_F[\theta\mid\theta\leq 0]} {\mathbb{E}_F[\theta\mid\theta\geq 0]-\mathbb{E}_F[\theta\mid\theta\leq 0]}. \]
In the limit, its rule accepts when the mass of positive valuations exceeds \(\alpha_F\); at equality, the finite-size fluctuations can affect the limiting outcome. The theorem’s result gives this mechanism for every finite \(N\), so its limiting per-person welfare is \(\max\{0,\mathbb{E}_F[\theta]\}\). With an efficiently integrable representation of \(F\), computing the threshold is tractable. The expected direction is therefore Class A, though the paper itself makes no runtime claim.

The natural regime is a very large membership organization deciding whether to adopt a proposal: for example, a large cooperative or shareholder body whose members have quasi-linear values drawn from a common prior. The authors would recognize this as their setting taken to a large-population limit: the binary decision, private valuations, incentive constraints, budget feasibility, participation, and welfare objective all remain visible.

The weak point is substantial. With continuously distributed valuations, almost every realized valuation is distinct, so the exact number of types is roughly \(N\), not small relative to the population. Treating positive and negative valuations as the only types would discard valuation intensities, which matter to welfare and incentives. A finite set of repeated valuation types would give the desired high-multiplicity regime, but the paper’s theorem assumes a positive-density distribution, so it would require a new discrete-type result.

There is also an incentive issue at the limit. In a truly atomless population, one person’s report has zero mass and cannot change the outcome; individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. The proposed problem avoids claiming that an atomless mechanism inherits meaningful strategy-proofness: its feasible solutions are sequences of finite, incentive-compatible mechanisms. That makes it a recognizable asymptotic extension of the authors’ problem, but a weaker continuous mirror than one whose mechanism constraints remain meaningful directly at the limit.

So the positive case is an asymptotic, tractable mirror of Theorem 1. Under the strict anchor rule, though, it remains unanchored computationally: Theorem 1 is a closed-form welfare characterization, not a named complexity result. The continuous question is plausible, but that limitation—and the loss of pivotality in the atomless model—is the case’s weakest point.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the anchor itself: Theorem 1 is not a computational-complexity result. It characterizes the welfare-optimal mechanism under specified economic assumptions; the paper gives no input encoding, running-time bound, or classification of an algorithmic problem. Corollary 1 is likewise a mechanism characterization. Calling Theorem 1 an algorithmic anchor does not supply the computational question the continuization programme is meant to chart.

The proposed “Large-Population Welfare-Optimal Voting” problem keeps each member and each finite-\(N\) incentive constraint intact, then asks about a limit of those finite mechanisms. That is a reasonable asymptotic extension, but it does not make the society itself the distribution-valued instance in ChoCo’s model. Here \(F\) is a prior over each person’s private valuation; it is not a supplied mass distribution describing the realized society. Nor is the proposed computation well specified: its complexity depends on how \(F\) is encoded and how its conditional expectations are computed. Assuming those moments are efficiently available makes the threshold calculation a different, small numerical task; leaving the representation open leaves no complexity problem to classify.

The better atomless mirror runs into a more basic obstruction. The paper’s central constraints are about what an individual can gain by changing their report. In a nonatomic society, one person’s report does not change the aggregate report distribution or the public decision, so those incentive constraints cease to constrain the allocation rule. Truthful reporting may be weakly optimal, but so may every other report. One can preserve meaningful individual incentives by retaining finite populations and studying sequences, as the proponent does; then the object remains a sequence of discrete mechanisms, not a mechanism problem over a continuous society. Replacing unilateral incentives with profitable mass deviations would keep a constraint in the model, but it would pose a different, coalition-level question.

A finite set of repeated valuation types is the strongest alternative scenario: it could make a high-multiplicity population plausible. But it does not repair this anchor. The paper assumes independent draws from a positive-density valuation distribution and optimizes over mechanisms with individual incentive constraints. A finite-support, high-multiplicity Bayesian variant would need its own specification of the prior, the population composition, and the incentive constraints, and then a new computational problem. It may be worthwhile research, but it is not a continuous mirror established by this paper’s theorem.

I would reject Theorem 1 as a strong ChoCo anchor. The negative case is not a proof that no related model could be worthwhile; it is that every route here either retains the finite-agent mechanism problem or makes its defining incentive constraints vacuous, while the paper supplies no named computational result for the proposed limit to mirror.
