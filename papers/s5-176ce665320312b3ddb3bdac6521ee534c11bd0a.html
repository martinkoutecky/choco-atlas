The continuous mirror question

Given a finite valuation support \(V\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) and rational masses \(\mu_v>0\) summing to \(1\), let \(Q\) be a common denominator and form, for each \(n=rQ\), the finite gain game with \(n\mu_v\) players of valuation \(v\). For a player \(i\) of type \(v\), determine and compute \(\Phi_\mu(v)=\lim_{r\to\infty} n\phi_i^{(n)}\), where \(\phi_i^{(n)}\) is that player’s Shapley value; establish the limit and an exact running-time bound in \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

The model it lives in

Types are valuations \(v\in V\), with population mass \(\mu_v\). A coalition is a subdistribution \(\rho\le\mu\), with \(q=\sum_v\rho_v\), and its gain is \(\mathcal{G}(\rho)=(1-q)\sum_v\rho_v(b(\rho)-v)\), where \(b(\rho)\) is its highest represented valuation. The output is the per-unit-mass Shapley density \(\Phi_\mu(v)=\lim_{r\to\infty}rQ\,\phi_i^{(rQ)}\) for each type; it has the explicit form \(\Phi_\mu(v)=((M-v)-D)/2\), where \(M=\max V\) and \(D=\sum_v\mu_v(M-v)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the Shapley computation in Corollary 3.4, supported by Theorem 3.1, and its interpretation as colluding power by type. It leaves the ranking-position claims in Section 4 and coalition maximization in Section 5 alone.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can total- and per-capita-gain maximizing coalitions be characterized as mass subdistributions with a principled attainment convention when a vanishing mass of the highest-valued type can determine \(b(\rho)\), and do they agree with limits of finite optimal coalitions?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would make one case, anchored on the paper’s clearest computational result. Corollary 3.4, proved here, says that for the \(n\)-player Knaster gain game, the Shapley value can be computed in \(O(n^2)\) time. Theorem 3.1 supplies the closed form behind that bound: each player’s value is a linear combination of adjacent differences in the ordered valuations, with coefficients determined by \(n\). The paper states no named hardness result to balance against this algorithmic anchor.

Lead mirror: Continuous Knaster Gain Shapley Density. Take valuation types \(V=\{v_1,\ldots,v_\tau\}\subseteq\mathbb Q_{\ge 0}\), with positive rational masses \(\mu_v\) summing to \(1\). A type is a valuation of the object; the agents also share the paper’s assumptions of equal rights, complete risk aversion, and a common valuation of money. The mass \(\mu_v\) is the fraction of the population with valuation \(v\). A plausible regime is a very large pool of equal-rights claimants in a Knaster-style division of one asset, with valuations falling into a small number of common or administratively generated bands: \(n\) is large while \(\tau\) is small.

A coalition is a subdistribution \(\rho\le\mu\), with mass \(q=\sum_v\rho_v\). Let \(b(\rho)\) be its highest represented valuation, and define its gain by
\[ \mathcal G(\rho)=(1-q)\sum_v\rho_v\bigl(b(\rho)-v\bigr), \]
with \(\mathcal G(0)=0\). This is an exact embedding of the paper’s finite gain game: for a discrete coalition \(S\) in a population of \(n\), set \(\rho_v=|\{i\in S:v_i=v\}|/n\). Then \(\mathcal G(\rho)=v_g(S)\).

The problem is: given \((V,\mu)\), compute for each type \(v\) its expected marginal gain per unit mass when an infinitesimal amount of that type joins a randomly ordered continuum coalition. More precisely, let \(u\) be uniform on \([0,1]\), so the preceding coalition has mass distribution \(u\mu\), and define
\[ \Phi_\mu(v)=\int_0^1 \left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\mathcal G(u\mu+\varepsilon\delta_v)\right|_{\varepsilon=0+}\,du. \]
The endpoint values do not affect the integral. This is also the replication limit: if \(n\mu_v\) is integral and \(\phi_i^{(n)}\) is the paper’s Shapley value for an agent of type \(v\), then \(\Phi_\mu(v)=\lim_{n\to\infty}n\phi_i^{(n)}\). The factor \(n\) expresses marginal contribution per unit population mass as individual mass tends to zero.

Writing \(M=\max V\) and \(D=\sum_v\mu_v(M-v)\), the continuum problem has the explicit answer
\[ \Phi_\mu(v)=\frac{(M-v)-D}{2}. \]
Thus it is tractable: \(M\), \(D\), and all type values can be computed in \(O(\tau)\) rational arithmetic operations, polynomial in the support size and input bit length. The weighted mean \(\sum_v\mu_v\Phi_\mu(v)\) is zero, matching Shapley efficiency in the finite game. This mirrors the paper’s calculation of colluding power by type, rather than introducing a new objective or changing the game being measured.

The case covers Corollary 3.4’s Shapley computation and its colluding-power interpretation. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s coalition-formation results in Section 5. Natural follow-up questions are to maximize \(\mathcal G(\rho)\) over \(\rho\le\mu\), and to determine what fraction of the population has positive or negative \(\Phi_\mu\).

The weakest point is the move from a Shapley value on named players to a type-level density. A standard Shapley value is not defined for a nonatomic population. The random-order, per-unit-mass limit makes the proposed quantity canonical and ties it directly to replicated finite instances, but an author or referee could still regard that normalization as a new measure of colluding power rather than their original one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 3.4 is a genuine computational result, so the negative case cannot dismiss this paper for lacking one. And the population mirror is sensible: agents with the same valuation are interchangeable, and a large pool of equal-rights claimants with a few valuation bands is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The objection is narrower: this result offers little computational landscape for ChoCo to chart.

The strongest mirror is the proposed type-level Shapley density, obtained as the replication limit of \(n\phi_i^{(n)}\). That limit is well motivated, but its computation is almost entirely settled by the paper’s own structure. Once valuations are grouped into \(\tau\) types, the density follows from the maximum valuation \(M\) and the single aggregate \(D=\sum_v\mu_v(M-v)\). Computing all type values then takes \(O(\tau)\) arithmetic operations. This is a clean limit calculation, but there is no pricing problem, difficult optimization, or apparent boundary between tractable and hard cases. The finite \(O(n^2)\) calculation already has an explicit formula; the continuum version compresses that formula to a few statistics.

There is a related, potentially more substantial mirror: optimize the gain of a coalition of valuation mass, or find a mass maximizing per-capita gain. But the gain depends on the coalition’s highest valuation. In a continuum, a vanishingly small amount of the highest-valued type can determine that maximum while contributing almost no mass. Requiring positive mass of that type can make the optimum unattained; allowing a measure-zero member to set the maximum changes the coalition model. One can impose a minimum coalition mass or a closure convention, but each adds a modeling choice absent from the paper. With finite valuation support, the resulting optimization also appears to reduce to a small number of threshold cases.

This does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The proposed density is a legitimate high-multiplicity limit, and its normalization gives a coherent type-level statistic even though individual Shapley values vanish. My case against is that the best-supported mirror looks too algebraically immediate to offer much of the computational programme’s distinctive payoff, while the richer coalition question exposes a real modeling singularity. That is a priority argument, not a decisive refutation.
