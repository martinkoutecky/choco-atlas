The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\), finite-support demand scenarios for each of several blocks of types, the known block marginal distributions defining a coupling set \(Q\), rational \(0<c<p\), a feasible coalition composition \(x\) with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), and a grand-coalition order \(y\) satisfying \(v_q(y,\mu)>0\) for every \(q\in Q\), compute \(\rho_y(x)=\max_{\gamma\ge0,\ q\in Q} v_q(\gamma,x)/v_q(y,\mu)\) and return a maximizing order and coupling. Here \(v_q(\gamma,x)=\mathbb{E}_q[p\min\{\sum_t x_tD_t,\gamma\}-c\gamma]\), with \(D_t\) the per-capita demand exposure of type \(t\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of retailer types carries population mass \(\mu_t\); each type has a shared per-capita demand exposure under the scenario model, and cross-block dependence is represented by couplings \(q\in Q\). A coalition chooses masses \(x_t\le\mu_t\) and a per-capita order \(\gamma\). Its robust deviation value is its largest profit relative to the grand coalition’s profit at order \(y\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers deviation pricing for a supplied coalition composition, corresponding to the computation using Proposition 1. It leaves open the optimization over all coalition compositions needed for full core separation and least-core computation.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the deviation value be computed efficiently when the block marginals are compactly encoded and their joint support has size \(K=\prod_r K_r\)?
  • Can one optimize the deviation value minus allocated type shares over all feasible coalition compositions \(x\), giving a core-separation algorithm?

The case FOR (proponent)

The continuous mirror I find convincing is the robust deviation-pricing problem inside the paper’s core computation.

Picture a large network of small retailers, with many outlets in a few demand-exposure cohorts. A type records an outlet’s demand exposure, price and cost, and its place in the paper’s dependence structure. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of outlets of type \(t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\); there are \(\tau\) types, with \(\tau\) small relative to the number of outlets. Shared regional demand shocks keep aggregate demand uncertain as the network grows. This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s own centralization question: which retailers should procure together, how much should the coalition order, and how should it divide the profit?

Concretely, partition the types into blocks \(B_1,\ldots,B_R\). For each block, the joint distribution of its type-level per-capita demands is known and has finite support; dependence between blocks is unknown. Write \(Q\) for the set of joint distributions consistent with those block marginals. For coalition masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), its demand in scenario \(k\) is \(\sum_t x_t d_t^k\). If it orders \(\gamma\), its expected profit under coupling \(q\in Q\) is \(v_q(\gamma,x)=\mathbb E_q[p\min\{\sum_t x_tD_t,\gamma\}-c\gamma]\), with the paper’s common \(0<c<p\).

My lead question is Continuous Robust Deviation Pricing. Given rational masses \(\mu\), a rational coalition composition \(x\), the finite-support block marginals, \(p,c\), and a grand-coalition order \(y\) for which \(v_q(y,\mu)>0\) for every \(q\in Q\), compute
\[ \rho_y(x)=\max_{\gamma\ge0,\ q\in Q}\frac{v_q(\gamma,x)}{v_q(y,\mu)} \]
and return a maximizing order and coupling. The value is the largest normalized profit the coalition can secure under one admissible dependence structure. In the continuous core, if \(z_t\) is the grand coalition’s profit-share density for type \(t\), the coalition has already been allocated share \(\sum_t x_tz_t\); stability requires that this cover \(\rho_y(x)\) for every coalition composition.

The paper’s anchor is Proposition 1, proved here. For Problem (33), there is an optimal solution \((\gamma^*,\theta^*,\psi^*)\) with \(\gamma^*\in\{d_1(S),\ldots,d_K(S)\}\), where the \(d_k(S)\) are the coalition’s demand values across the finite joint support. The authors use this to compute \(v_{\max}(y,S)\): fix each candidate order in turn, transform the resulting linear-fractional problem, and solve at most \(K\) linear programs. The continuous mirror has the same structure: for fixed \(x\), its candidate orders are the finite values \(\sum_t x_td_t^k\). So this pricing question looks tractable in the explicit-support model, with runtime polynomial in the listed support size \(K\), the LP size, and the bit length. Since \(K=\prod_r K_r\) can itself grow rapidly with the number of blocks, this is not a claim of polynomial time in a compact marginal-only encoding.

This is a computational anchor, but it is not a theorem classifying a problem as P or NP-hard; the paper gives no such complexity classification. The mirror covers the exact coalition-pricing subproblem the authors use when computing robust cores and least cores. It does not settle the full continuous least-core problem, where one must also find the most blocking \(x\) among a continuum of coalition compositions. That is the next question: does the pricing oracle combine with an efficient separation algorithm over \(x\), perhaps for fixed \(\tau\), or does optimizing the coalition’s composition introduce new hardness?

The weakest point is the cohort shock assumption. If outlet demands are independent, the law of large numbers may wash out much of the uncertainty that drives this paper. The mirror is strongest for many similar retailers exposed to shared market or regional shocks, which preserves the paper’s distributional ambiguity while making mass-based coalitions natural. The paper also cites work on *large newsvendor games*; that high-multiplicity precedent supports the regime rather than colliding with the proposed computational question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is to the mirror’s demand model. Its coalition demand \(\sum_t x_tD_t^k\) makes every member of a type move in exact proportion to the same scenario shock. That is a valid common-shock model, but it is a substantive assumption: with independent individual demand, aggregation instead washes out much of the uncertainty; with more general dependence, type masses alone do not specify the demand distribution of every fractional coalition. The paper’s ambiguity over couplings of finite retailers does not by itself supply that missing population-level model.

Even granting the common-shock model, the proposed fixed-composition problem is a close lift of Proposition 1. For a given \(x\), its candidate orders are the finite support values \(\sum_t x_td_t^k\), and the same linear-fractional transformation yields at most \(K\) LPs. That is a real algorithmic analogue, but its polynomial bound is in the explicit joint support \(K\), which can be the product of the block support sizes. It establishes no complexity result for compactly encoded marginals, and it computes a deviation value for a supplied composition rather than finding the most blocking coalition.

The strongest repair is to ask for the continuous core-separation problem: maximize \(\rho_y(x)-\sum_t x_tz_t\) over all feasible coalition masses \(x\), and then use that oracle in core or least-core computation. That is a meaningful computational question the authors could recognize. But it also shows the limit of the negative case: the paper’s cited *large newsvendor games* supports the plausibility of a large-population regime, and this stronger separation problem is not answered by the paper’s fixed-coalition Proposition 1. I cannot make a sound case that this repaired mirror is not worth asking. The negative case is therefore weak at its central burden: it can narrow the proponent’s claim, but it cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous mirror.
