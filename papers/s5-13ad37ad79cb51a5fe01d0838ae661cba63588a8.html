But its type delegates replace the paper’s individual sequential bidders with bloc players, changing the strategic game the proposition solves. No continuous-population mirror of the anchored result survives that change.

What the mirror covers

The proposal covers Proposition 1’s equilibrium-strategy and first-round-agreement claims; it does not cover Propositions 2–3 or the future incomplete-information case. The paper already has divisible resources; the proposed population masses do not preserve its individual-player protocol.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of Proposition 1, proved in this paper. It says that, in the finite-player game with complete information, divisible resources, a fixed bidding order, a deadline, and a discount factor, the recursively defined optimal actions in Algorithm 1 form a subgame-perfect equilibrium; if an acceptable agreement exists, that equilibrium reaches one at the end of round 1. This is the paper’s closest algorithmic anchor. It gives a strategy construction, but no complexity bound, and Algorithm 1 ranges over continuous bids and unspecified monotone valuation functions. If “computational anchor” strictly requires a result classifying a problem as, for example, in P or NP-hard, then the paper has no qualifying anchor.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large resource-sharing consortium: millions of users, divided into a small number \(\tau\) of cohorts with identical per-capita valuations over compute, bandwidth, or storage. Each cohort authorizes one delegate to negotiate its package. The types are the complete per-capita valuation vectors; \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of users in cohort \(t\). Here \(N\gg\tau\), and mass measures how much of each resource the cohort’s per-capita allocation consumes.

The lead mirror would be Type-Bloc Equilibrium Action. Its instance consists of rational masses \((\mu_t)_{t\in T}\) summing to \(1\), \(m\) resources, rational strictly increasing affine valuations \(v_t(y)=a_t^\top y-b_t\), a rational discount factor, a deadline, and a fixed order of type delegates in each round. A delegate bids a complete per-capita package \(y_t\in[0,1]^m\) or rejects the preceding bids. A completed agreement is feasible when \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t y_{t,k}\leq1\) for each resource \(k\); a type’s agreement utility in round \(r\) is \(\delta^{r-1}\mu_t v_t(y_t)\), and disagreement gives utility \(0\). Use the paper’s fully benevolent tie-breaking rule. Given a rational history in a round, the problem is to return the action prescribed by a subgame-perfect strategy and its continuation payoff; from the initial history, return the equilibrium play. A solution is an exact strategy action and payoff for that input history, or the first-round agreement and payoffs when the input is initial.

I would tentatively expect the restricted affine-valuation version to be Class A: feasibility and each delegate’s own objective are linear, so backward induction may reduce to a sequence of linear optimizations. Whether the continuation values admit a compact representation is the central open issue. The mirror covers Proposition 1’s equilibrium-strategy result, not the paper’s Pareto-optimality or individual-rationality propositions, and not its future incomplete-information case.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper’s players are individual strategic negotiators, whereas this mirror makes each type cohort a bargaining bloc acting through a delegate. Mass-weighted supply is a natural per-capita adjustment, but bloc representation changes the strategic model. It is plausible for a consortium that actually bargains through cohort representatives; it is not a general continuization of the paper’s protocol. The strategy-construction anchor is also weak by the programme’s standards: without a finite encoding and a running-time result in the paper, this is a concrete research question suggested by Proposition 1, not a complexity result the authors already established.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 1 is the only plausible anchor, but it does not establish the complexity of a computational problem. Its “algorithm” chooses an argmax over the continuum of bids \(B=(0,1)^m\), for arbitrary monotone valuation functions with no finite encoding. The paper gives no running-time or computability result for carrying out that choice. So there is no complexity result here to continuize in the programme’s usual sense.

The proposed Type-Bloc Equilibrium Action makes the missing problem precise, but it also changes what the players are. In the paper, each of the \(n\) players has a separate allocation, takes a turn in a fixed order, and can reject earlier bids. Turn position affects what a player observes and can do, so equal valuations alone do not make players interchangeable. If the population grows while the number of types stays fixed, the protocol still has an unbounded sequence of individual decision makers. Treating each type as one delegate compresses those decision makers into a coalition with authority to bind its members. That may be a sensible bargaining model for a consortium, but it is not simply the paper’s game with its population represented by masses.

The natural alternatives have the same difficulty. Keeping individual turns makes the finite ordering and each person’s veto consequential; in a continuum, individual vetoes may disappear unless unanimity is specially redefined. Giving each type a collective veto or representative restores a workable finite game, but requires new assumptions about representation and what the bloc maximizes. The proposed mass-weighted payoffs and resource constraints are defensible choices, yet they do not follow from Proposition 1. Restricting valuations to rational affine functions and bids to a closed domain also supplies the finite input and existence conditions missing from the paper. Those choices might support a useful study, but they build a new computational bargaining problem around the proposition rather than mirror a computational result it proved.

The negative case is weakest on the underlying application: large resource-sharing groups with stable cohorts and authorized representatives are plausible, and their type-level game could be worth analyzing. But the paper’s central strategic object is negotiation among individually acting players, while the proposed tractable population object is negotiation among representative blocs. Unless the authors would recognize that change of players and protocol as their problem, Proposition 1 does not provide a worthwhile continuous-population mirror for ChoCo.
