The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying positive case under the anchor rule. The paper has many numbered results, but none asserts a computational complexity classification or an algorithmic result for a problem. For instance, Proposition 6.1 says that after the first topic on which an agent is \(\eta\)-intelligent or \(\eta\)-wise, the society reaches consensus on later topics; for earlier topics, consensus depends on the initial weight matrix. That is a result about opinion dynamics, not whether a computational problem is tractable or hard. The paper’s continuous opinion values in \(\mathbb{R}\), and its limits as the number of agents grows, do not make the population itself continuous.

A population mirror is conceivable—for example, mass over recurring cohorts with shared belief distributions, biases, and conformity parameters, interacting through block-structured networks. But asking whether such a society reaches a wise consensus would continue the paper’s analytic dynamics questions. The paper supplies no computational anchor from which to state the required continuous problem without inventing a new research question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no qualifying mirror to defeat. Proposition 6.1 is their example, but they correctly identify it as a consensus result, not a computational one.

That absence is decisive for this paper. It states no computational problem and gives no complexity classification, algorithm, or runtime result. Proposition 6.1 and the wisdom results in Section 6 analyze consensus and limiting beliefs; the opposition and conformity sections analyze how dynamics and social influence change; the homophily section is largely simulation. Proposition 8.1 characterizes a Nash equilibrium in closed form, but does not study the complexity of computing one. The continuous opinion values and large-population limits do not make the population itself continuous.

One could build a continuum model over groups sharing bias distributions, conformity parameters, and network roles. One could then ask whether the resulting society becomes wise, or how to adjust its weights to make that happen. But the first is an analytic dynamics question; the second adds an intervention or optimization problem that the paper does not study. Neither mirrors a computational result in the paper. A network with repeated roles might make the continuum model sensible, while a network organized around individual ties might resist aggregation; neither changes this mismatch.

So the negative case is strong within ChoCo’s stated scope: this paper offers no computational anchor for a continuous mirror. That does not establish that no useful computational follow-up could ever be built from its model. It establishes that such a follow-up would be a new question, not a continuization of a computational result the paper contains.
