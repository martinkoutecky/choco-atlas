The continuous mirror question

Given schools \(S\), a finite support \(T\) of complete extended-preference and priority-tier types, rational type masses \(\mu_t\), and per-capita capacities for both periods, find a feasible mass allocation \(x_{t,a,b}\) assigning each unit of mass an integral bundle \((a,b)\). Require individual rationality and no positive mass that can block under the paper’s blocking tests, with schools applying priority tiers and splitting a cutoff tier symmetrically; report if no such allocation exists.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass cohort distributed over complete preference and priority-tier types, with per-capita school capacities. The decision variables are masses \(x_{t,a,b}\) assigned to integral two-period bundles; the objective is feasibility of stability, with no cost objective.

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for every finite childcare allocation instance whose student preferences lie in the domain of reasonably extended preferences \(P\), a stable matching exists. The proof constructs one using aSPDA. This is the paper’s strongest constructive anchor. Strictly, though, the paper gives no named complexity result—no theorem that classifies a problem as polynomial-time, NP-hard, or similar—and Theorem 1 gives no running-time bound. So this is a case for a continuous mirror of its constructive stability result, not a claimed mirror of a complexity classification.

I would call the mirror Continuous Stable Childcare Allocation. Take a large yearly cohort seeking places at a fixed set \(S\) of schools over the paper’s two periods. The population is unit mass, distributed over a finite support \(T\) of types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Each type specifies a complete extended preference ranking in \(P\), plus its priority tier at each school. Thus people of one type share both preferences and every priority attribute used by the mechanism. Let \(q_s^r\) be school \(s\)’s per-capita capacity in period \(r\in\{1,2\}\).

A solution assigns each unit of mass an integral bundle \((a,b)\in(S\cup\{\varnothing\})^2\), where the entries are that child’s period-one and period-two assignments. Equivalently, output rational masses \(x_{t,a,b}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_{a,b}x_{t,a,b}=\mu_t\), respecting every school’s capacity in both periods. Require individual rationality and no positive mass of any type able to block by moving to a preferred school or school pair under the paper’s blocking rules. Schools apply their priority tiers to aggregate mass, accepting up to capacity and splitting a tier at the cutoff if necessary. The problem is: given \((S,T,\mu)\), the type rankings and priority tiers, and the per-capita capacities, either produce such a stable mass allocation or report that none exists. The expected direction is Class A: a mass version of aSPDA, or a finite optimization method, may yield an algorithm polynomial in the number of schools, support size, and input bit length. The paper itself does not establish that.

This regime is plausible for a large municipal or regional childcare cohort: many applicants compete for a comparatively small set of facilities, and families can share rankings and institutionally defined priority categories. The support size \(\tau=|T|\) could then be much smaller than the number of children. The mirror keeps the paper’s distinctive object—complementary period assignments tied to school priorities—rather than replacing it with a divisible childcare slot. Each child still receives an indivisible bundle; only the aggregate allocation is fractional.

The main weakness is priority. The paper assumes strict rankings of individual students at each school. If those ranks distinguish every applicant, then priority rank belongs in a complete type and \(\tau\) may grow with the cohort, defeating the high-multiplicity advantage. The proposed regime therefore needs priority tiers, with equal-priority applicants treated symmetrically at the cutoff. That is a meaningful co-modification of the institution. The continuous blocking definition also needs care: a single atomless child has zero mass, so the mirror uses positive-mass deviations. I think a childcare-allocation author could recognize this as a sensible large-cohort analogue, but it is the part most open to challenge.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper’s strongest anchor is Theorem 1: aSPDA produces a stable matching on the stated finite domain. But that is an existence and mechanism-design result, not a computational result. The paper gives no complexity bound, hardness result, optimization problem, or algorithmic bottleneck. A complexity analysis of aSPDA could be done, but it would be a new question layered onto the paper. Under ChoCo’s computational scope, that is a substantial reason against treating this as a worthwhile mirror.

The proposed mass-allocation model gets the population axis right, and its per-capita capacities and integral bundles are sensible co-modifications. The difficulty is preserving the paper’s priority structure while obtaining high multiplicity. Priorities are strict rankings of named students. Since any two students are ordered at every school, their priority attributes differ; including those attributes in a complete type gives at least \(n\) types for \(n\) students. Scaling the cohort then scales the support too, so the proposed model does not gain the compact high-multiplicity representation that motivates the programme.

Priority tiers could create repeated types, but they change how schools choose among students at a capacity cutoff. The paper’s blocking tests depend on its strict priorities. Splitting a tier at the cutoff requires a new rule for which mass is admitted, and that rule changes which deviations block. Individualized tie-breaking restores strict priorities and the large support; symmetric tier treatment defines a different stability question. That may be a sensible market-design project, but the paper’s theorem does not establish or select that model.

The other named results do not supply a stronger computational anchor. Theorem 2 concerns individual strategy-proofness; for an atomless population, one person’s report changes zero mass, so that property risks becoming vacuous. Requiring positive-mass deviations instead changes the incentive question. Proposition 1 is a maximal-domain claim supported by a finite counterexample, not a complexity result.

I cannot rule out a worthwhile continuous stability project for a large cohort with shared priority categories. That is the weak point in a universal negative: the tiered-priority scenario is plausible. The case against is that it would need to define new priority and blocking rules, while none of the paper’s results asks a computational question for that model.
