What the mirror covers

No computational result is available to mirror; the paper’s axiomatic characterizations of the Diversity Owen and Diversity Shapley values, and their simple-game variants, are outside the computational-result criterion.

The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered results are axiomatic characterizations: Proposition 1 characterizes the Diversity Owen value; Proposition 2 characterizes the Diversity Shapley value; Propositions 3 and 4 give alternative characterizations; and Corollaries 1 and 2 adapt the first two results to simple games. None asserts a complexity classification or presents a computational formulation that the paper then uses to solve an underlying problem.

There is a plausible *follow-on* mirror: take a large electorate divided into a few communities, with agents in each type having identical voting roles and contribution behavior. Let mass represent each type’s population share, and let diversity quotas require a minimum mass from each community. One could ask for the limit of the communities’ or types’ shares of Diversity Shapley–Shubik power as the electorate is replicated. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s simple-game results make it a natural direction. But it would be a new computational question, not a continuous version of a named computational result here; the paper’s contribution is an axiomatic characterization, which the programme places outside scope.

So there is no honest per-anchor prover question to state for this paper. The positive case is only that its voting-power setting could motivate a later continuous-population complexity study—not that this paper supplies an anchor for one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest candidate is Proposition 2 and Corollary 2: in a growing, anonymous voting body, ask whether each type’s *aggregate* Diversity Shapley–Shubik power converges, and how to compute the limit from the type masses and diversity quotas. That is a sensible high-multiplicity setting; the parliamentary examples make it plausible. Aggregating power by type also avoids the weak objection that an individual’s share tends to zero.

But this remains a new limit and value-computation problem, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper. Propositions 1–4 and Corollaries 1–2 characterize allocation rules; none formulates a computational problem, specifies a compact input representation, or establishes a complexity result. Extending the question to a continuum would require choosing a family of finite games, an aggregation convention, and a notion of convergence. Those choices may make a good follow-on project, but they do not supply the missing computational anchor. The same issue applies to the Diversity Owen value and its characterizations.

So the case against a mirror is strong under ChoCo’s stated criterion. It is weaker as the absolute claim that no worthwhile continuous research could grow from this paper: the aggregate-power limit is a credible direction. I would classify it as a promising adjacent question, not as a continuous computational mirror of the paper’s results.
