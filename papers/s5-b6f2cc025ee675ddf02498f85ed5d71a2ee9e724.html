The case FOR (proponent)

There is no admissible computational anchor in this paper. Its numbered propositions and corollaries establish axiomatic properties of consensus measures—anonymity, replication, reinforcement, and related results—not that a computational problem is in P, hard, or parameterized tractable. The paper mentions a computational comparison in a footnote, but says it was outlined elsewhere. I would not present any of these as a complexity anchor.

The best positive case is a faithful, modest computational question suggested by the paper’s central task: Continuous Referenced-Consensus Evaluation. Let \(k\ge 2\), let \(X\) be the alternatives, and let the input be a support \(T^+\subseteq W(X)\) of \(\tau\) complete weak-order types with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Choose the paper’s Borda benchmark \(C_B\) or Copeland benchmark \(C_C\). Compute the resulting consensus order \(R_\mu=C(\mu)\) and the exact referenced consensus value
\[ \operatorname{Cons}_C(\mu) =\sum_{t\in T^+}\mu_t \frac{\left|\left\{\{a,b\}\subseteq X: \operatorname{cmp}_t(a,b)=\operatorname{cmp}_{R_\mu}(a,b)\right\}\right|} {\binom{k}{2}}, \]
where \(\operatorname{cmp}\) records which alternative is preferred, or whether the pair is tied. The output is the consensus preorder and this rational value; there is no intervention to optimize, because the paper’s underlying task is measurement.

For \(C_B\), rank alternatives by the mass-weighted Borda scores
\[ \beta_\mu(a)=\sum_{t\in T^+}\mu_t \bigl(|\{b:a\succ_t b\}|-|\{b:b\succ_t a\}|\bigr). \]
For \(C_C\), compare the masses preferring each alternative strictly over the other, then rank by the resulting Copeland scores. Both the consensus and the agreement value can be computed by exact rational arithmetic in time polynomial in \(k,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. This is a population-continuous question: \(\mu_t\) is a fraction of society, and a large electorate with repeated ballot types is represented by its support rather than by individually listed voters. It does not continuize outcomes or add noise. On an empirical distribution from a finite profile, it reproduces the paper’s measure by definition.

I would expect this proposed problem to be tractable. The authors would likely recognize it as a computational version of the measurement they define, and it covers their explicit RCM-B and RCM-C proposals. But it is not a result this paper proves, and the direct algorithm is routine weighted aggregation. The paper therefore supplies neither a hardness anchor whose fate in the continuum could be studied nor a named algorithmic result to mirror. Its axiomatic conclusions remain outside this computational mirror. Natural follow-up questions would be whether exact evaluation stays tractable for other focal consensus rules or referenced-measure functions, and which choices make the evaluation problem hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case begins with the missing anchor: the paper proves no complexity result. Its numbered propositions and corollaries concern axiomatic properties of consensus measures. The footnote’s computational comparison is credited to separate work. There is therefore no algorithmic theorem here whose continuous counterpart could complete a result the paper started.

The proposed evaluation task is a sensible population model. A society of types with rational masses is meaningful, and the consensus value remains meaningful for it. That makes it a good candidate for a new computational question, but it does not supply the missing anchor. For the paper’s explicit Borda and Copeland proposals, the input is a weighted collection of rankings, and the output follows directly from the weighted scores and pairwise agreement counts in the definitions. For the general referenced-consensus framework, the functions are abstract mappings with no computational encoding. Restricting them to explicit rules makes the task concrete; making them richer enough to pose a new algorithmic challenge requires choices the paper does not make.

So the best case against is that this programme would be adding an evaluation problem to an axiomatic paper, rather than continuizing one of its computational results. More ambitious tasks—such as choosing a benchmark or optimizing over consensus measures—would require a new research question. I cannot make that into a strong universal objection: the proposed evaluation is faithful and in scope, and the paper’s central measurement gives it a plausible continuous formulation. The negative case is weakest precisely at that anchor.
