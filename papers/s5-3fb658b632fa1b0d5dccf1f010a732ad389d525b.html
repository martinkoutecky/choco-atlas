The continuous mirror question

Given a known agenda \((p_j,q_j)_{j=1}^m\) with rational coordinates and a finite support of complete ballot-record types \(t\), each with positive rational mass \(\mu_t\), compute \(\rho(\mu)=\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathbf{1}[\text{Theorem 1’s conditions hold for }V_t]\) exactly and decide, for a rational threshold \(\alpha\), whether \(\rho(\mu)\ge\alpha\).

The model it lives in

The society is a distribution \(\mu\) over complete response-record types on a shared known agenda. For each type, set \(z_t=1\) exactly when its record \(V_t\) is strictly rationalizable by a concave utility under Theorem 1; report \(\sum_t\mu_t z_t\) and the requested threshold decision. This measures compatibility of recorded behavior, not the share of residents whose latent preferences are actually concave.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s concave rationalizability test and its verification procedure, applied across supported ballot types. It leaves aside the ideal-point results in Theorems 3–6, embedding in Theorem 5, and vote prediction in Corollary 1.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is a population measure of individual concave rationalizability. Imagine a large civic consultation in which millions of residents answer the same fixed set of binary questions about policy alternatives. The alternatives have known coordinates in a finite-dimensional policy space, and many residents submit the same complete answer pattern. Let \(N\) be the number of residents and \(\tau\) the number of distinct patterns, with \(\tau\ll N\). A type is one such full pattern on the shared agenda. For this question, duplicate records are indistinguishable: the test uses the record and the agenda, while the utility function is an unknown witness.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. For a voting record \(V=\{(y_j,z_j)\}_{j=1}^m\) in \(\mathbb R^d\), it says the following are equivalent: for every nonempty restricted record \(V'\subseteq V\), some extreme point of its observed alternatives \(X(V')\) is not among the alternatives the voter supported, \(Y(V')\); or, equivalently, there is a sequence of at most \(m\) steps that repeatedly chooses such an extreme point \(x_t\) and removes comparisons whose disfavored alternative is \(x_t\), until the record is empty. Either condition holds exactly when \(V\) is strictly rationalized by a strictly concave utility, by a concave utility, by a strictly quasiconcave utility, and by a quasiconcave utility. In one dimension, the condition can be checked using only two-item restricted records. The paper’s Algorithm 1 implements this characterization with at most \(m\) rounds of linear-programming tests. So the anchor is the paper’s tractable individual rationalizability test, not a claim that the theorem itself is labelled “in P.”

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Concave-Rationalizability Coverage takes a known agenda \((p_j,q_j)_{j=1}^m\) with \(p_j,q_j\in\mathbb Q^d\), a finite support of ballot types \(t\in\mathcal T\), and positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\). Each type’s ballot vector determines a record \(V_t\) by orienting each agenda pair toward the alternative it supported. The problem is to compute
\[ \rho(\mu)=\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t\,\mathbf 1\!\left[\exists\text{ a strictly concave }u_t:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R\text{ that strictly rationalizes }V_t\right], \]
and, given a rational threshold \(\alpha\), decide whether \(\rho(\mu)\ge\alpha\). A solution is the exact rational value \(\rho(\mu)\), the passing types, and the threshold answer. The per-type indicators \(z_t\) are computed classifications, and the population objective is \(\sum_t\mu_t z_t\); the mass represents residents, not fractional votes. Applying Theorem 1’s test to each supported type gives a running time polynomial in \(d,m,\tau\), and the total input bit length \(L\), under exact rational arithmetic.

This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: the number of people can be enormous while the number of distinct complete records stays modest. A discrete cohort with \(n_t\) people of type \(t\) maps to \(\mu_t=n_t/N\); the continuous version accepts rational proportions directly. The population question—what share of recorded behavior is compatible with concave preferences?—is a meaningful aggregate report, and it preserves the paper’s underlying individual question rather than imposing a shared utility across voters or changing the outcome space.

The scope is deliberately narrow: strict rationalizability under the paper’s concavity and quasiconcavity assumptions, with a known common agenda. It does not cover Theorems 4 or 5’s results about unknown alternative locations and embedding multiple voters’ records. Natural follow-ups include finding the range of ideal-point locations compatible with a given mass of types, using the paper’s ideal-point conditions, and studying how coverage changes when some agenda coordinates are uncertain.

My weakest point is that the population computation decomposes into \(\tau\) applications of the paper’s individual test. Continuization adds a population-level coverage quantity, but no new interaction among agents or continuous-optimization difficulty. I still think it is an honest mirror: the many-agent, few-record regime is credible, mass has a clear interpretation, and the authors’ exact rationalizability question remains intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the anchor to attack, and it survives the obvious objections. It is a numbered characterization with a tractable verification algorithm, so it is a computational result worth mirroring. A large consultation in which many residents answer the same fixed agenda could have a small number of repeated complete response records. For the proposed coverage statistic, each record is a sufficient type; the mass is meaningful, and \(\rho(\mu)\) remains a population quantity. This is not outcome-space continuity or a continuous treatment already present in the paper. High-multiplicity work supports the plausibility of the regime.

The strongest case against is that coverage adds a cohort report to an individual recognition problem. Theorem 1 tests each record independently; its condition never uses the population masses. Thus \(\rho(\mu)\) reports the weighted prevalence of records passing the test, rather than posing a new question about collective choice, intervention, or interaction among voters. The passing types themselves give a more detailed classification, and the population calculation simply summarizes them. That makes the proposal look like a useful descriptive statistic, but a thin contribution to a programme seeking a computational landscape for problems such as bribery, control, and robustness.

The most natural stronger version is to aggregate the paper’s ideal-point restrictions or vote predictions: for a proposed future comparison, compute the mass of voters whose past records force one preference over the other. That is more closely tied to the paper’s prediction results, but still combines independent per-record conclusions. To create a coupled population optimization, one would need additional structure—such as a constrained agenda-design problem, shared preferences, or costs for changing records. Agenda design could be interesting, but it would add a new problem rather than reveal a failure of the paper’s existing individual characterization.

There is also a limit on how to interpret the coverage measure. It is the share of observed records compatible with some concave rationalization, not the share of people who actually have concave preferences. Identical records need not identify identical underlying preferences. That does not invalidate the proposed statistic, but it narrows the substantive claim it can support.

So the negative case is that the mirror’s population layer may be too thin to merit this programme’s attention, and stronger variants require added assumptions. But the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror is not convincing here: a high-multiplicity consultation is plausible, and the weighted compatibility share is a coherent question about the population. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor on the stated grounds.
