The continuous mirror question

Given an explicit finite set of complete agent types \(\Theta\), rational masses \(\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)\), a world \(w\in\{L,H\}\), the common prior and signal channel, and the paper's fixed Wisdom-of-the-Crowd mechanism, compute the winner \(a_w\) under truthful reports and decide whether, for every \(w\), it equals the continuous majority wish: \(a_w=\mathrm{acc}\) iff \(\mu(\{\theta:v_\theta(w,\mathrm{acc})>v_\theta(w,\mathrm{rej})\})>1/2\).

The model it lives in

A finite-support high-multiplicity society in which each \(\theta\) carries complete utilities, role, and signal technology, with mass \(\mu_\theta\) summing to \(1\). Under truth-telling, the treated signal mass is \(q_w=\mu_F+\mu_C P_{hw}\); the mechanism takes the mass median of prediction reports, applies its \(F/U\) override, and outputs \(\mathrm{acc}\) or \(\mathrm{rej}\).

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a genuinely natural continuous-population mirror here, but it is a qualified case: the paper contains no named NP-hardness, P-membership, FPT, or comparable computational-complexity theorem. Its named results concern accuracy, equilibrium, and impossibility. Thus the mirror is substantively good for ChoCo, but it is not a fully compliant computational-complexity anchor unless the programme accepts mechanism-design results as computational formulations.

My lead mirror is the paper’s main wisdom-of-the-crowd mechanism.

Take a unit-mass population with a finite set of complete agent types \(\Theta\). A type contains the agent’s utility function \(v_\theta\), whether the agent is candidate-friendly, contingent, or candidate-unfriendly, and the signal technology relevant to the problem. The population is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)\). In the simplest faithful version, \(\Theta=\{F,C,U\}\), with masses \(\mu_F,\mu_C,\mu_U\). Thus mass means the fraction of a large population, not a probability attached to an individual.

There is a common hidden world \(W\in\{L,H\}\), a prior \((P_L,P_H)\), and private signals \(S\in\{\ell,h\}\) drawn according to the paper’s signal channel. A strategy is a measurable report rule mapping a type and signal to the mechanism’s report: reported class, reported signal, and, for contingent agents, a prediction of the aggregate treated-\(h\) mass. The mechanism observes the distribution of reports, takes the median of the reported predictions, and outputs \(\acc\) or \(\rej\) exactly as in the paper’s Wisdom-of-the-Crowd-Voting Mechanism.

The continuous majority wish is \(\acc\) in world \(w\) precisely when \(\mu(\{\theta:v_\theta(w,\acc)>v_\theta(w,\rej)\})>\frac12\). The designer’s objective is to maximize the probability of selecting this majority wish subject to truthful reporting being an \(\varepsilon\)-strong Bayes Nash equilibrium. In the fixed-mechanism version, the question is whether the mechanism attains the majority wish and whether a positive-mass coalition can profitably deviate.

This is a plausible regime. Imagine a large organization or electorate deciding whether to adopt a policy whose eventual quality is unknown. Many agents occupy repeated roles with the same preference threshold and the same information environment: committed supporters, committed opponents, and swing agents who receive private performance signals. Here \(N\gg|\Theta|\), and the high multiplicity is exactly the point. If utility values differ, those differences refine \(\Theta\); they do not invalidate the model.

The strongest anchor is Theorem 3.2, proved in this paper. It says that if all agents play the truthful strategy \(\Sigma^\ast\), then the mechanism outputs an alternative favored by more than half of the agents with probability at least \(1-2\exp(-2c^2\alpha_C T)\). When either predetermined camp is a majority, the mechanism chooses that camp’s alternative deterministically. Otherwise, the contingent agents’ signals determine the majority wish, and the median prediction separates the two worlds with exponentially small failure probability.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\(\mathsf{Continuous\text{-}Wisdom\text{-}Accuracy}\). Given rational \(\mu\), the finite world and signal spaces, the prior, signal channel, utility functions, and the paper’s mechanism, compute the state-contingent winner under truthful reporting and decide whether it equals the continuous majority wish in every world.

In the continuum, conditional independence and the exact law of large numbers make the aggregate signal mass deterministic. Under truth-telling, the treated \(h\)-mass is \(\mu_F+\mu_C P_{hw}\). A contingent agent reporting after signal \(s\) predicts \(\mu_F+\mu_C T_{hs}\). Theorem 3.1 gives \(P_{hL}<T_{h\ell}<T_{hh}<P_{hH}\), so, when \(\mu_F,\mu_U<\frac12\), the median prediction lies strictly between the treated \(h\)-masses generated in worlds \(L\) and \(H\). The mechanism therefore selects \(\rej\) in \(L\) and \(\acc\) in \(H\) exactly. If \(\mu_F>\frac12\) or \(\mu_U>\frac12\), the override step is already correct.

I would expect this fixed-mechanism problem to be Class A-like: it reduces to finite rational arithmetic, order statistics, and checking finitely many type masses. The finite-\(T\) theorem supplies the bridge: rational proportions \(\mu_t=n_t/T\) recover the discrete model, while the exponential error bound tends to zero as \(T\) grows. This is not outcome-space continuity; the alternatives, worlds, signals, and individual reports remain discrete. Only the population becomes a mass distribution.

A second, closely related anchor is Theorem 3.3, also proved here. It says that the truthful strategy profile is an \(\varepsilon\)-strong Bayes Nash equilibrium for \(\varepsilon=(2B^2+4B)\exp(-2c^2\alpha_C T)\). Theorem 3.4 gives the limiting form: as \(T\to\infty\), truthful reporting is a strong Bayes Nash equilibrium.

Its continuous problem is:

\(\mathsf{Continuous\text{-}Wisdom\text{-}Stability}\). Given the same data and a measurable positive-mass coalition \(D\), determine whether there is a deviation by agents in \(D\) such that every member weakly improves in ex-ante utility and a positive-mass subset improves by more than \(\varepsilon\). A solution is either such a coalition and deviation rule, or a certificate that none exists.

The continuum version makes the paper’s proof especially transparent. Under truthful reporting, contingent agents already obtain their preferred alternative in each world. A candidate-friendly and a candidate-unfriendly agent have opposing incentives, so a profitable coalition cannot contain both. A coalition consisting only of candidate-friendly agents has mass below \(\frac12\), cannot trigger the majority override, and cannot improve on the mechanism’s built-in treatment of its signals and predictions. The same argument applies symmetrically to candidate-unfriendly agents. Thus the expected answer is Class A-like for this mechanism: the median structure gives a direct certificate of stability. The general mechanism-synthesis problem remains open.

The paper also contains a useful boundary result, Theorem 4.2, proved here: with an unknown distribution of agent types, there is a constant \(\tau>0\) such that no anonymous mechanism always outputs the majority wish with probability greater than \(1-\tau\) in any \(\tau\)-strong symmetric Bayes Nash equilibrium. Corollary 4.3 specializes this to truthful mechanisms: no anonymous mechanism can simultaneously achieve high-probability majority-wish accuracy and make truth-telling a \(\tau\)-strong equilibrium.

Its continuous analogue is quite natural. The latent population composition \(\mu\) is drawn from a distribution over type-mass vectors, while the mechanism observes only the aggregate report measure. The question is whether an anonymous mechanism can recover the majority wish and sustain truthful equilibrium uniformly over those latent compositions. In the continuum, sampling fluctuations disappear, so environments that had merely similar binomial report distributions in the finite proof can become exactly observationally identical. I would expect a continuum-specific impossibility, not a tractability result. This would be a valuable boundary case showing that continuization does not solve informational non-identification.

I would, however, use Theorem 4.2 only after correcting the supplied draft: its displayed signal probabilities do not sum to one and conflict with the subsequent binomial calculation. The theorem’s conceptual point is strong, but the numerical instance is not currently safe as a formal anchor.

The authors would likely recognize the first two mirrors as their problem. The paper already works with type fractions \(\alpha_F,\alpha_C,\alpha_U\), reasons in terms of aggregate signal fractions, fixes the primitive parameters independently of \(T\), and explicitly studies \(T\to\infty\). The continuous formulation simply makes the limiting population an input object and replaces finite-sample error by exact mass accounting. It also exposes a natural computational agenda the paper does not pursue: varying \(\mu\), refining the type space, synthesizing anonymous mechanisms, checking coalition stability, and quantifying finite-population approximation.

The weakest point is that this may be viewed as formalizing the paper’s asymptotic analysis rather than discovering a new continuous computational landscape. More seriously, the paper never proves a conventional complexity result. Therefore I would present this as a strong substantive continuous mirror of the paper’s truthful-aggregation problem, with Theorems 3.2 and 3.3 as its anchors, but not claim that the paper itself supplies a qualifying Class A/B/C complexity theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is stronger than the proponent admits: none of the three anchors is a qualifying computational result under ChoCo’s gate. The paper proves accuracy, equilibrium, and an informational impossibility theorem; it does not formulate or solve a computational problem with an input-size parameter, an algorithm, or a complexity classification. The proposed mirrors therefore add a new computational agenda rather than continuize a computational contribution of the paper.

Theorem 3.2 is the weakest anchor. With \(\Theta=\{F,C,U\}\), the continuous version has only the quantities
\[ q_w=\mu_F+\mu_C P_{hw} \]
for the treated signal mass and
\[ \delta_s=\mu_F+\mu_C T_{hs} \]
for the two possible predictions. The winner is obtained by comparing these finitely many numbers. That is not an LP, separation problem, optimization problem, or complexity landscape; it is finite arithmetic and an order-statistic check. More importantly, the paper already performs exactly this passage in Theorems 3.4 and B.3 by taking \(T\to\infty\). Calling that argument \(\mathsf{Continuous\text{-}Wisdom\text{-}Accuracy}\) changes the input notation, not the mathematical object.

Allowing an explicit finite type space \(\Theta\) does not rescue it: aggregate masses and predictions can still be computed by summation and sorting in time polynomial in the explicit support. Allowing succinctly represented types or asking for mechanism synthesis could produce a new computational problem, but then the complexity comes from the representation or from designing mechanisms—not from the population continuization—and neither question is a result of this paper.

Theorem 3.3 has a deeper problem. Strong Bayes–Nash equilibrium is defined over subsets of named finite agents. In an atomless population, an individual has measure zero and cannot alter the aggregate report distribution, so all individual deviation constraints become payoff-null. If one retains only positive-mass coalitions, as the proponent suggests, one has introduced a new blocking concept rather than preserved the paper’s equilibrium notion. That concept may be sensible, but it requires choices about measurable coalitions, signal-contingent coordination, and coalition utilities that the paper never makes. If the type and signal spaces remain finite, checking the resulting fixed-mechanism condition again reduces to finitely many aggregate threshold cases. Thus the continuous analogue is either degenerate, newly defined, or already contained in the paper’s limiting proof.

Theorem 4.2 does not provide a better anchor. As printed, its signal probabilities are inconsistent: the displayed probabilities do not form distributions, and the subsequent binomial calculation does not follow from them. Even after repairing the example, the impossibility proof is driven entirely by finite-population sampling noise: it compares \(\operatorname{Bin}(T,1/6)\) with \(\operatorname{Bin}(T/3,1/2)\). In a genuine continuum, those empirical masses become exact. The two environments produce Dirac masses at distinct aggregate report measures, while truthful type reports reveal the type composition exactly. The total-variation ambiguity used in the proof disappears.

To preserve that ambiguity one must reintroduce finite sampling, a random finite population, or an exogenous noise process. Those are precisely finite-population or noise models, not a continuous society in ChoCo’s sense. Alternatively, one can hide type reports from the mechanism, but that changes the paper’s information-elicitation model. A continuum impossibility under such altered assumptions could be worthwhile, but it would be a new theorem, not a mirror of Theorem 4.2.

The honest concession is that the first two constructions are perfectly natural high-multiplicity models: large organizations with repeated preference and information roles are plausible, and the paper itself discusses type fractions and \(T\to\infty\). What they lack is a worthwhile computational question. The paper’s contribution is an asymptotic mechanism-design argument, and its continuous limit is already written into that argument. Under the programme’s computational-anchor requirement, this is a clean negative verdict: the paper offers a sensible continuous reinterpretation, but no worthwhile continuous computational mirror anchored in any of its named results.
