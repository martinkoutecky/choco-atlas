The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a qualifying case for a ChoCo mirror: the paper contains no named computational result—no theorem about an algorithm, complexity class, or computational problem.

The nearest anchor is Theorem 2.1, which states that for a finite group of individuals and at least three alternatives, a social choice function is unanimous and strategy-proof if and only if it is dictatorial. The paper cites Gibbard and Satterthwaite for that result and supplies a proof using Lemmas 3.1–3.3. It is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational claim.

One could ask whether an analogous characterization holds for a continuum of voters whose types are rankings and whose masses sum to one. But that would continuize the population to pose an axiomatic question—the kind of question ChoCo explicitly puts out of scope. Defining manipulation in an atomless population would also need care: a single agent has zero mass, so individual reports cannot change the distribution. That is a possible separate research question, but it is not a computational mirror anchored in this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The sole substantive anchor is Theorem 2.1: unanimity and strategy-proofness characterize dictatorships. Lemmas 3.1–3.3 prove that characterization; none states a computational result. The paper gives no algorithmic input/output problem, encoding of a rule, or complexity claim to continue. Its discussion of proof styles does not supply one.

The strongest computational rescue would be to ask, given a society and a rule, whether someone can manipulate, or how much mass must change reports to alter the outcome. But neither version preserves the theorem’s object. For arbitrary social choice functions, the distribution of rankings loses which individual holds each ranking, and the rule may depend on those identities. Keeping identity in the types leaves no useful aggregation when each person is unique. If instead the rule depends only on the distribution, a single voter has zero mass in an atomless population, so exact individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. Giving a deviator positive mass makes the question one about coalitions; that is a different property.

Restricting to anonymous finite rules does not restore the theorem’s full content: an anonymous rule cannot be dictatorial, so unanimity and strategy-proofness cannot both hold. One could still study the complexity of finding manipulations for particular rule families, or of finding a coalition of a given mass that changes the winner. Those may be worthwhile ChoCo questions, but they require choosing the rule representation, deviation model, objective, and cost. This paper chooses none of them or claims anything about their complexity. They would be new projects using the paper’s subject matter, not continuous mirrors of its results.

Its weak point is that no paper can rule out a valuable adjacent project someone might build from its ideas. But that possibility does not give this manuscript a continuous computational mirror.
