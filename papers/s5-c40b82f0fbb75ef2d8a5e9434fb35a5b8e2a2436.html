The continuous mirror question

Let \(\mathcal U\) be a finite set of complete user types, each specifying a reward vector \(u\in[0,1]^M\) with \(\sum_a u(a)=1\) and any behavior parameters used by the interaction model. The society is an unknown rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal U\), with support size at most \(\tau\). A protocol adaptively selects users from this population, observes their identities and integral arm demonstrations but not their rewards, and outputs one arm; users respond according to the paper’s demonstration-plus-deployment utility model. For a query budget \(q\), what is the minimum worst-case distortion achievable by a protocol that makes truthful ordinal preferences a best response, over all such \(\mu\) and user best responses?

The model it lives in

Types carry complete reward and behavior parameters; their masses \(\mu_u\) determine per-capita welfare \(W_\mu(a)=\sum_u\mu_u u(a)\). The decision is an adaptive protocol for selecting users and a final arm, with individual demonstrations remaining integral. The objective is to minimize \(\sup_{\mu,\psi\in\mathrm{BR}}\max_a W_\mu(a)/\mathbb E[W_\mu(A_{\mathcal A})]\), subject to truthful ordinal behavior being a best response and a specified query budget.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive anchor is Theorem 9, proved in this paper. It gives a distortion guarantee for the authors’ shared-control mechanism: for \(M\) arms, distortion is the best social welfare achievable by any arm divided by the expected welfare of the arm the mechanism selects, when users respond to the mechanism according to the paper’s best-response model. The theorem states that the mechanism’s distortion is \(O(\sqrt{M\log M})\). The paper builds the mechanism using ordinal-voting results; it does not merely cite this bound as an existing theorem. The text describes a protocol that gives users control, uses their demonstrated actions, and periodically explores with a random arm.

There are no named results here establishing a classical complexity classification such as \(P\), NP-hardness, or FPT. Theorem 9 is an algorithmic performance result, so I would not present it as a polynomial-time complexity theorem. It is still a meaningful anchor for the programme’s approximation-algorithm questions.

A concrete mirror is Continuous Shared-Control Distortion. Let \(A=\{1,\ldots,M\}\) be the arms, and let \(\mathcal U\) be a finite set of complete user types. A type \(u\in\mathcal U\) specifies a user’s reward vector \(u\in[0,1]^M\), with \(\sum_{a\in A}u(a)=1\), along with any interaction parameters—such as the relative value of demonstration and deployment—that affect their behavior. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal U\), with support size \(\tau\). Its mass \(\mu_u\) is the fraction of the population with type \(u\), and its per-capita welfare from deploying arm \(a\) is

\[ W_\mu(a)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal U}\mu_u u(a). \]

The regime is a large user population—say, customers of a shared recommendation or service system—with many people but relatively few recurring preference types: \(n\gg\tau\). People of the same type have the same relevant rewards and interaction parameters. The type distribution is latent to the mechanism: if the robot were simply handed the cardinal values and masses, it could choose the welfare-maximizing arm directly. Instead, it uses the paper’s interaction model: it selects people to demonstrate arms, observes identities and actions but not rewards, and eventually deploys one arm for the society. Individuals’ choices remain integral; the average welfare is computed over type masses.

The problem is to design an adaptive shared-control protocol that works without seeing \(\mu\). It chooses whom to give control to and when, observes the resulting demonstrations, and outputs a deployment arm \(A_{\mathcal A}\). The objective is to minimize worst-case distortion over societies with support at most \(\tau\):

\[ \sup_{\mu,\ \psi\in\mathrm{BR}(\mu,\mathcal A)} \frac{\max_{a\in A}W_\mu(a)} {\mathbb E[W_\mu(A_{\mathcal A})]}. \]

Here \(\mathrm{BR}\) uses the paper’s learning-versus-deployment payoff model. A solution is a protocol, together with a proof of its incentive property and distortion bound. The continuous analogue of Theorem 9 would ask whether a protocol can make truthful revelation of users’ ordinal preferences a best response and achieve distortion \(O(\sqrt{M\log M})\). I expect a Class A-style approximate result: the aggregate objective is linear in \(\mu\), and every rational \(\mu\) is an empirical distribution of a finite population. That makes the welfare ratio transfer look plausible. It does not, by itself, show a runtime polynomial in \(M,\tau,L\).

This mirror preserves the paper’s central question: how shared-control demonstrations can elicit preferences well enough to choose a socially beneficial arm despite strategic users. It changes the roster of individually modeled principals into a high-multiplicity society with type masses. The ratio is unchanged under finite replication because dividing both welfare terms by population size cancels; extending the result from rational masses to a type-compressed implementation would be a result of the mirror, not a novelty collision.

The weakest point is that the mechanism tracks selected individuals and their past actions. A type distribution records how many people share each preference, but it does not automatically preserve those individual histories or the incentives they create. A referee could reasonably ask for an explicit model of how interactions are sampled from the mass and how histories are represented. That is a real modeling burden, though a plausible one in a large service population with recurring users and a small number of shared preference types.

This case covers the paper’s shared-control mechanism-design result, especially Theorem 9. It does not claim to continuize the paper’s trajectory-learning impossibility results or its finite-profile manipulability calculation. Nor does it make the arm space or the time horizon continuous: the population is the continuous object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 9 is the only strong anchor, but it gives a distortion bound for a mechanism; it does not classify the complexity of a computational problem. The other named results concern regret, incentives, and manipulability. None asks whether a problem is in \(P\), NP-hard, or solvable by an exact or approximate algorithm within a stated running time. Calling Theorem 9 an approximation result does not close that gap: its approximation is in social welfare under strategic responses, not in computing a solution to an optimization problem on a society supplied as input.

The proposed mirror has a difficult choice about what the mechanism knows. If it is given the support, masses, and reward vectors, then it can calculate \(W_\mu(a)\) for each arm and choose the best one in \(O(M\tau)\) time. The preference-inference problem that motivates Theorem 9 has disappeared. If the distribution is hidden and the mechanism only sees demonstrations, the task becomes statistical learning and mechanism design from sequential samples. Its inputs and costs are queries, rounds, and individual interaction histories—not a continuous society supplied as a distribution over types.

That distinction matters to incentives. The paper’s mechanism selects particular people and uses their own demonstration histories and rewards. In an atomless population, any one person has zero mass, so an individual demonstration has no effect on an aggregate outcome; the incentive question in Theorem 9 can become vacuous. Preserve pivotality by querying a finite set of identified or recurring users, and the strategic game is still played by those finite users; the continuous distribution is the source from which they are sampled. Query positive-mass cohorts instead, and the model needs new assumptions about how those cohorts act and how their histories evolve. That could be an interesting mean-field mechanism-design problem, but it is additional work, not a continuous version already anchored by this theorem.

The proponent’s service-population scenario is plausible, so I cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The strongest case against is narrower: this paper does not supply a named computational-complexity result for ChoCo to continuize, and its distortion theorem does not by itself bridge the gap. I would not count the proposed mirror as a strong ChoCo anchor unless the programme deliberately broadens to strategic learning from a latent population.
