The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t honestly supply an anchor-compliant case for a continuous computational mirror: this paper contains no named computational-complexity result. Theorem 1 states existence of firm-optimal and worker-optimal stable matchings; Theorem 2 gives comparative statics when the worker set grows; and Theorem 3 gives the Roth–Sotomayor result on firms and workers who gain or lose when one worker is added. These are structural results, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. Theorem 1 is attributed to Gale and Shapley; Theorems 2 and 3 are credited to Gale and Sotomayor, and Roth and Sotomayor, respectively, with proofs supplied in this paper. The deferred-acceptance procedure in the appendix is an algorithm, but it is not a numbered complexity result.

A continuous stable-matching model with preference types and rational masses might be a plausible research direction. But under your anchor rule, it would be a new question rather than a continuous version of a named computational result in this paper. So there are no qualifying anchors and no per-anchor continuous problems to state.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises zero computational anchors. Theorem 1 is an existence result; Lemma 2 is a preference implication; Theorems 2 and 3 give comparative statics for finite markets. None states a complexity bound or classifies a computational problem. The deferred-acceptance procedure in the appendix constructs a matching for a finite instance, but the paper gives no complexity result for that computation. Under ChoCo’s scope, there is no named computational result here to mirror.

The strongest rescue would be to turn Theorem 1 and deferred acceptance into a question about computing an optimal stable matching for a society represented by masses of preference types. That could be a worthwhile new project. It would ask for existence or efficient computation in a continuous matching model; neither question mirrors a computational claim made in this paper. Theorems 2 and 3 offer another tempting route: replace the added worker with a positive-mass cohort and study how stable outcomes change. That is a reasonable continuous comparative-statics question, but it still does not supply the missing computational anchor.

So the case against is strong as a verdict on this paper as a ChoCo target: its results do not make the computational claims the programme is set up to continuize. The broader claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous matching problem is much weaker. A continuous matching problem may be sensible and valuable; it simply would not be a computational mirror of this paper’s results.
