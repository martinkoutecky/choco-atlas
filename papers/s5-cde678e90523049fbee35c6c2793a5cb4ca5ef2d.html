The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor the required mirror. Propositions 1–3 establish perfect Bayesian equilibria with efficient late matching; Proposition 4 gives weak-dominance claims; Propositions 5–6 characterize strategies surviving iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies. The paper proves these results, but none classifies a computational problem as tractable or hard, gives an algorithm, or computes with a formulation.

The closest result is Proposition 5, proved here: in the open-offer treatment, every strategy profile surviving iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies has no firm making an offer before period 7; in periods 7, 8, or 9, each firm \(j\) offers to the applicant of quality \(j+1\); and before period 7, applicants hold their best offers, with the option to accept an offer from the highest-quality firm still unmatched. That is a meaningful structural result about the paper’s market, but it is not a computational-complexity anchor under your stated criterion.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting for a future extension would be a large graduate-admissions or residency cohort. Applicant types would need to include their relevant preferences, signal process, payoffs, and reneging incentives; institution types would include quality, capacity, and payoffs. Mass would be each type’s share of the cohort, while matches would remain integral for individuals and fractional only in type-level totals. That is a credible population regime, but the paper’s five-firm, six-applicant model does not itself establish that regime or analyze its continuum version.

So I can’t honestly give one continuous computational problem per eligible anchor, or assign such a problem to Class A, B, or C: doing so would invent the missing computational question and present it as the paper’s result. The weak point for any positive case is precisely that moving Proposition 5 to a continuum population could change the strategic incentives it characterizes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 5 gives a credible object to computationalize: which strategies survive iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies, and whether those strategies force matching to wait until applicant quality is revealed. A continuous version could ask: given rational masses of employer types and applicant types, can we compute the strategy outcomes that survive under open offers, and determine whether they leave positive mass matched early? Complexity could be measured in the support size, number of periods, and input bit length. Proposition 6 supplies the parallel question for reneging.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. Large admissions or residency cohorts contain many applicants with the same relevant preferences, signal process and payoff parameters, and institutions with the same quality and per-capita capacity. Represent those groups by their mass shares, scaling capacities with cohort size. Individual matches remain integral; totals between types can be fractional. This keeps the market, its offer rules and its timing problem at the center. Applicant names need not matter, while differences in preferences or incentives can be included in the type definition.

The computation could be substantial. Proposition 5 establishes its characterization in a market with five firms and six applicants. With heterogeneous type masses, capacities and information histories, aggregate offers and acceptances interact: an offer to one type can affect which opportunities remain for others. An algorithm could test whether the late-matching characterization persists, identify the conditions under which early matching has positive mass, or compute the range of attainable welfare and early matching across surviving strategies. Each answer would inform the paper’s question about how offer norms affect market performance.

The proponent is right about an important limitation: the paper does not state a theorem classifying the complexity of an algorithmic problem. So this would not be a direct high-multiplicity lift of an existing complexity result. The case for a mirror is that Proposition 5 provides a precise strategic result whose large-population computational counterpart is both definable and relevant to the paper’s subject. The argument is weakest at the boundary of what ChoCo calls a mirror: if the programme requires the source paper itself to pose a computational problem, this extension falls outside that rule. That supports “no direct computational mirror”; it does not establish “no worthwhile continuous computational question in any scenario.”
