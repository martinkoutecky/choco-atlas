Its closest result is Lemma 2, proved here: with three alternatives of ordered positive performances and judgment probabilities proportional to an increasing function of performance, the pairwise method gives the best alternative a higher selection probability than the holistic method and the worst alternative a lower one. The following corollary concludes that pairwise comparison has higher expected performance. Those are mathematical comparison results, not computational-complexity results.

A continuum of expert types could be imagined, but the paper’s probabilities describe uncertainty about an individual expert’s choice; they are not mass in a continuous society. The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about the decisive point: the paper has no named result about the complexity of a computational problem. Lemma 1 is an identity between probabilities of two judgment patterns; Lemma 2 compares the best and worst alternatives’ selection probabilities for three alternatives; Lemma 3 turns those probability changes into an expected-performance comparison. The corollary combines them. These are mathematical statements about one expert’s choice, not results about an algorithm, reduction, or complexity class.

The best repair would treat a large panel of experts as a continuous society, with types defined by their expertise or judgment process, and ask which DSS gives the panel greater expected performance. But with a per-capita objective, this is just the weighted average \(\sum_t \mu_t \sum_i V_i p_i^{(t)}\). For three alternatives, if every type satisfies the paper’s assumptions, the pairwise advantage follows pointwise and survives averaging. The mass mixture adds no interaction or new computational issue; with finitely many types, it is a weighted sum. A continuous expertise distribution would also need its own input representation, which would determine much of the computational question.

To make mass matter, one could add a collective voting rule, constraints on how many experts receive each elicitation method, or a budget for pairwise judgments. Those could be worthwhile questions, and the paper’s mentions of group decision and cost-benefit analysis make them natural extensions. But they add the aggregation or resource-allocation problem the paper does not study. They do not mirror Lemma 2 or its corollary.

So the negative case is strong for the paper’s results: none supplies a prover-ready continuous computational question. The broader claim that *no scenario* could yield a worthwhile mirror is weaker. A panel-level model might be valuable, but it would be a new computational social-choice project motivated by this paper, rather than a continuous mirror of its results.
