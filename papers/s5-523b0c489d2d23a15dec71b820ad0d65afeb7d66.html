The continuous mirror question

Given two role types \(V=\{a,b\}\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_v\) summing to \(1\), and a multiset of chore classes \(E\) with rational masses \(\lambda_e\geq 0\) and positive symmetric costs \(d_e\), choose assignment masses \(x_{e,v}\geq 0\) for eligible endpoint types, with \(\sum_{v\in e}x_{e,v}=\lambda_e\). For each type \(v\), its average chore burden is \(\mu_v^{-1}\sum_{e\ni v}d_e x_{e,v}\); its burden from type \(w\)'s average bundle is \(\mu_w^{-1}\sum_{e\in E(v,w)}d_e x_{e,w}\). Does a complete assignment exist such that the first burden is at most the second for each ordered pair \(v\ne w\)?

The model it lives in

A two-type multigraph of role groups carrying population masses \(\mu_v\), with repeated chore classes carrying supply masses \(\lambda_e\). The variables \(x_{e,v}\) distribute each class across its eligible types; linear per-capita burden comparisons test envy between type-average bundles. Rational feasibility is an LP.

The objection that survived

The paper makes each chore relevant to particular individual endpoints, while the proposed task classes are eligible to whole role groups; the case stipulates this role-level eligibility but does not derive it from a finite family preserving individual endpoint incidence.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

Covers a cohort-average continuous analogue of the chores-orientation feasibility setting anchored by Theorem 3.6; it does not preserve individual EF1 or EFX$_0$ guarantees, and leaves the MMS, goods, graph-only, and Pareto results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For which finite role-based chore instances can a feasible mass assignment be rounded to integral worker assignments while controlling individual EF1 envy as a function of cohort size?
  • Can the rounding preserve the paper's individual endpoint-incidence structure, or does the role-group eligibility assumption require a separate model?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a high-multiplicity mirror of Theorem 3.6 (the result labeled mainContributionThreeC), proved in the dissertation by reduction from Partition. It says that deciding whether a multigraph of chores has an EF1 orientation is NP-complete, and that deciding whether it has an EFX\(_0\) orientation is also NP-complete, even with only two vertices and additive, symmetric utilities. The reduction uses parallel chores between the two vertices and self-loop chores.

A plausible regime is a large workforce divided into a small number of stable roles—for example, workers grouped by zone, shift, and qualification, with many workers in each group. Repeated task classes are chores that either of two eligible role groups can handle, and members of a group have identical utilities for every task class. If \(N\) is the workforce size, the number of workers is much larger than the number of role types. Task supplies scale with the workforce, so a type’s share of the chores has a meaningful per-worker average.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Mass-EF Chore Orientation. An instance is a finite multigraph \(H=(V,E)\) of role types, positive rational population masses \(\mu_v\) summing to \(1\), and rational task masses \(\lambda_e\). Each task class \(e\) has a positive symmetric chore cost \(d_e\) for its eligible endpoint types; a loop is eligible only for its one type. Find nonnegative assignment masses \(x_{e,v}\), for eligible \(v\), such that each class is fully assigned:
\[ \sum_{v\in e}x_{e,v}=\lambda_e. \]
The average bundle per worker of type \(v\) assigns mass \(x_{e,v}/\mu_v\) of class \(e\). Its utility for type \(v\) is
\[ U_v(B_v)=-\frac{1}{\mu_v}\sum_{e\ni v}d_e x_{e,v}. \]
For distinct types \(v,w\), type \(v\)’s value for \(w\)’s average bundle is
\[ U_v(B_w)=-\frac{1}{\mu_w}\sum_{e\in E(v,w)}d_e x_{e,w}. \]
The question is whether there is such an assignment with \(U_v(B_v)\ge U_v(B_w)\) for every pair \(v\ne w\). A solution is the assignment vector \(x\), not an encoding of it. These are linear constraints, so rational feasibility gives a polynomial-time algorithm in the type graph size and input bit length.

This is a limit version of the theorem’s fairness question: at finite resolution, one worker’s single chore changes a cohort average by an amount on the order of \(1/N\), assuming bounded per-task costs. Thus the one-item relief in EF1, and the item-level relief in EFX\(_0\), vanishes in the type-average limit. The Partition reduction’s discrete choice of which endpoint receives each chore becomes a choice of how much chore mass goes to each cohort. In the two-role case, those choices can be fractional, which is why I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A).

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem’s high-multiplicity form: it preserves chores, graph eligibility, orientation, symmetry, additivity, and comparisons of bundles, while expressing those comparisons per worker type. It covers the multigraph chores-orientation feasibility results of Theorem 3.6, not the dissertation’s MMS or goods results.

The weak point is substantial: the theorem’s EF1 and EFX\(_0\) guarantees concern realized bundles of individual agents, whereas this mirror compares type averages. If the authors require fairness for every pair of realized workers, this LP answers a different question. The mirror also assumes bounded task costs so that a single chore becomes negligible; the Partition reduction permits large costs, and its exceptional self-loop chores need not be negligible under every scaling. So this is a credible mirror for large cohorts with no macroscopic chores, not a continuous restatement of the theorem’s full worst-case domain. It leaves a useful follow-up: when can a feasible mass orientation be rounded to an individual EF1 orientation, with a bound on the resulting envy as a function of cohort size?

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has one real anchor: mainContributionThreeC, which proves NP-completeness for both EF1 and EFX\(_0\) chore orientations of multigraphs. The problem is not that a continuous version might be easy; an easy answer would still count. It is that the proposed LP changes what is being tested. It asks whether *type-average bundles* are envy-free. The theorem asks whether an allocation of indivisible chores is EF1 or EFX\(_0\) for individual agents. Those “up to one item” conditions do real work in the Partition reductions.

The self-loops expose the mismatch. In the EF1 reduction, a self-loop gives each of two agents a private chore that can be ignored when testing envy. In the EFX\(_0\) reduction, a zero-utility loop makes the test require exact balance after ignoring it. The proposed LP replaces both criteria with ordinary envy-freeness between averages. A type’s private chore burden is then compared with another type’s bundle, which has no value for that chore; the LP can reject instances the theorem’s criteria accept. That rejection would not be a disappointing answer to the same question. It would be an answer to a different fairness question.

A stronger mirror could keep individual EF1 and EFX\(_0\) comparisons by representing, for each role type, a distribution over *integral* bundles and requiring fairness across the bundles that workers actually receive. But this brings back the central modeling difficulty. In the paper’s graph model, an item is incident to particular agents. If that incidence and the agents’ complete utility descriptions are retained, workers with different neighbors are different types, so the support can grow with the workforce. If items instead become task classes available to whole role groups, the model no longer has the paper’s two-endpoint relevance structure: it is a new role-based allocation model. It may be sensible, but it does not follow from the theorem merely by taking its population to a limit.

That distribution-over-bundles version is the best opening for the proponent. A workforce with stable roles and repeated task classes could make it recognizable to the authors, and a useful continuous problem might emerge. But it needs a justified model of eligibility, within-type fairness, and the individual item-removal rule; the average-bundle LP supplies none of these. I would therefore lean against spending the programme’s time on this anchor as stated, while stopping short of a universal dismissal: a well-motivated role-based model that preserves individual EF1 or EFX\(_0\) could still be worthwhile.
