What the mirror covers

The proposal uses Theorem 1's winner-bid payoff characterization; it leaves Theorem 2 and Lemma 2 aside and does not extend the paper's game-level results to a society of agents.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a narrow one: a large institution handles many independent partnership dissolutions, and we ask how much of the equity surplus can go to lower-valuation agents while each case’s outcome remains consistent with the paper’s empirical-equilibrium analysis. That is a plausible setting for repeated use of these auctions, with many cases but relatively few distinct valuation-pair types.

There is an important limitation: the paper has no named computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor requirement specifies. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, is the closest foothold: it characterizes efficient empirical-equilibrium payoffs in the winner-bid auction. It does not classify a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 2 gives the corresponding loser-bid characterization; Lemma 2 concerns Nash-equilibrium outcomes across mechanisms. These are analytical game-theory results, not computational-complexity anchors. So the paper supplies no qualifying anchor, and the following is an exploratory extension rather than a mirror that satisfies that gate.

Theorem 1 says that, for a winner-bid auction with \(v_l<v_h\), an efficient Nash-equilibrium payoff \(\pi(\sigma)\) is an empirical-equilibrium payoff exactly under these conditions, where \(ES(v)=|v_h/2-v_l/2|\):

  • If \(ES(v)\le 2\), then \(\pi_h(\sigma)=v_h/2+ES(v)\).
  • If \(ES(v)>2\) and \(v_l\le 3v_h/8\), then \(\pi_h(\sigma)\ge v_h/2+ES(v)/2+v_l/4-1/2\).
  • If \(ES(v)>2\), \(v_l>3v_h/8\), and \(v_l<7v_h/12-7/6\), then \(\pi_h(\sigma)\ge v_h/2+4ES(v)/5-4/5\).
  • If \(ES(v)>2\), \(v_l>3v_h/8\), and \(v_l\ge 7v_h/12-7/6\), then \(\pi_h(\sigma)>v_h/2+4ES(v)/5-4/5\).

The paper proves this result. Its content is that empirical plausibility restricts how much equity surplus the higher-valuation agent can lose to the lower-valuation agent.

A precise cohort question built from that characterization would be: Winner-Bid Cohort Equity Frontier. The input is a finite support \(T\) of valuation pairs \(t=(v_l,v_h)\) with \(v_l<v_h\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) summing to one. Each unit of mass represents a partnership-dissolution case, and many cases share the same valuation pair. For each \(t\), let \(R_t\) be the set of lower-valuation agents’ equity-surplus shares induced by efficient empirical equilibria characterized by Theorem 1. Choose how much mass \(x_{t,r}\) of each pair type receives each permissible share \(r\in R_t\), subject to \(\sum_{r\in R_t}x_{t,r}=\mu_t\). The question is to find the largest average lower-valuation share, \(\max \sum_{t\in T}\sum_{r\in R_t}x_{t,r}r\), and a mass assignment attaining it. This asks how much equity the lower-valuation side can receive across the cohort while every case remains within the paper’s empirical-equilibrium prediction.

I would expect this cohort problem to be tractable by applying Theorem 1 type by type and aggregating the results. That would be a Class A answer in the limited sense that the stated problem admits an efficient solution. It would not uncover a new continuous-optimization advantage or show that discrete hardness dissolves: the original paper provides no such hardness anchor, and its theorem already gives the payoff characterization being aggregated.

The soft spot is that the continuum here is a population of *partnership cases*, not a population of agents inside the paper’s two-agent game. A court or arbitration service handling many similar dissolutions makes the repeated-case scenario plausible, and the mirror preserves each case’s auction and equilibrium question. But an author or referee could reasonably regard the cohort frontier as an aggregate application of their result, rather than the continuous-population version of their underlying problem. I would not claim a stronger mirror than that.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that the paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes which efficient Nash payoffs are empirical-equilibrium payoffs in a two-agent auction. Theorem 2 gives the corresponding characterization for the other auction, and Lemma 2 establishes a mechanism-design result. None classifies the complexity of a computational problem.

The proposed Winner-Bid Cohort Equity Frontier does not supply that missing anchor. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is mass over *independent partnership cases*: each unit is still a separate two-agent game with its own object. The assignment variables \(x_{t,r}\) choose a payoff for each case type; they do not model transfers among types or agents interacting in one society. The objective and constraints separate by type, so the calculation is just a weighted aggregation of the payoff sets Theorem 1 already characterizes. Adding a shared budget or fairness constraint could create an optimization problem, but that would be an extra planning problem imposed across cases, not a continuous-population version of the paper’s auction analysis.

The strongest more faithful alternative would put many agents into one dissolution problem. But the paper’s mechanism allocates one indivisible object between exactly two partners, with the winner paying the other partner. For a continuum of partners, the paper provides no corresponding allocation or payment rule; selecting one person from an atomless population also does not produce a positive-mass winner. Defining an \(n\)-agent or continuum-owner auction might be worthwhile, but it introduces a new strategic model rather than continuizing a result this paper proves.

So the negative case is strong against the proposed anchor: it is an aggregate of repeated games, not a continuous society inside the studied game, and it lacks a computational result to mirror. It cannot prove that no worthwhile generalization exists. But the proponent’s own cohort proposal does not establish one, and the natural route to a single continuous society leaves the paper’s model behind.
