The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. It reports a multi-agent system and a questionnaire of 50 users, and says negotiation reduces computational complexity, but gives no formal complexity analysis. So there are no qualifying anchors to quote or restate, and I can’t make a compliant anchor-by-anchor case.

A plausible *unanchored candidate* is Continuous Academic Resource Allocation. Consider a large university or consortium scheduling many repeated sections, where the number of section requests \(n\) is much larger than the number \(\tau\) of distinct request profiles. A type \(q\) completely specifies a section’s resource needs, hard constraints, and preference weights; a resource bundle \(b\) specifies a professor, room, and time slot. The mass \(\mu_q\) is the fraction of total section demand of type \(q\). Resource types \(j\) have per-capita capacities \(\sigma_j\), and bundle \(b\) consumes \(a_{bj}\) units of each. This uses the paper’s setting—academic resource allocation with requests coupled through shared resources—but shifts the many agents to repeated section requests.

Given rational \(\mu_q,\sigma_j,a_{bj}\), eligibility constraints, and rational AHP-derived scores \(u(q,b)\), ask for an allocation \(x_{qb}\geq 0\) maximizing \(\sum_{q,b}u(q,b)x_{qb}\), subject to \(\sum_b x_{qb}=\mu_q\) and \(\sum_{q,b}a_{bj}x_{qb}\leq\sigma_j\). Each unit of mass receives one complete bundle; fractional \(x_{qb}\) means that this fraction of a request type receives that bundle. If the allowable bundles are explicitly listed, this is an LP and is tractable. If bundles are implicit—for example, all eligible professor–room–slot combinations—the pricing problem becomes a further question rather than a result this paper establishes.

The case is strongest as a mirror of the paper’s underlying resource-allocation problem, not of a named result or of its negotiation protocol. The main weakness is that the paper’s distinctive contribution is interactive, round-based negotiation with potentially changing preferences; the static LP does not capture that process. Also, the high-multiplicity scenario depends on repeated section requests really sharing every feature the model uses. The paper does not establish either that regime or the computational claims needed to make this candidate a result of the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational result. That is the strongest objection: it never formally specifies the allocation problem’s feasible set or objective, how AHP scores are aggregated, how offers are generated and updated, or when negotiation ends. Its claim that negotiation “reduces the computational complexity” is not backed by a complexity analysis. The reported results concern a system design and a questionnaire, not the complexity of a computational problem.

The proposed LP is a reasonable model someone could study, but it adds the key choices itself: repeated request types, additive scores, per-capita resource capacities, and a static welfare objective. The paper describes interactive negotiation, where preferences may change as offers and intermediate results are shared. Its architecture does not define that process precisely enough to derive a complexity question from it. A stronger mirror might represent repeated course requests as masses and model negotiation between their types, but specifying the negotiation rules and their computational task would still mean inventing a new formal problem.

So the candidate may be worthwhile as a research question inspired by the university-allocation setting; it is not a continuous version of a computational result this paper establishes. The universal claim is the weak point: repeated course sections could make a continuous resource-allocation model sensible. I cannot rule out every useful model in that setting. But the paper provides no computational result for such a model to mirror, and the proponent’s candidate does not supply one on the paper’s behalf.
