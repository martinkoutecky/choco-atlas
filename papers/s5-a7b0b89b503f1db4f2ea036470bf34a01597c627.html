What the mirror covers

The proposed robustness extension covers a new winner-change optimization; it does not mirror a qualifying computational result among the proposed anchors.

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational complexity result, so it offers no anchor that meets your requirement. The closest is Theorem 13.1: it states that the projected scores, rank-like rates, and fraction-like rates depend continuously on the Llull matrix. Corollary 13.2 extends that continuity to the relative frequencies of vote types. Those are mathematical properties, not results that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. The paper describes computational steps, including Floyd–Warshall, quadratic programming, and a numerical iteration, but gives no complexity classification for them.

There is a strong positive seed, with a collision: the paper already represents votes as a weighted distribution, \(v_{xy}=\sum_k\alpha_k v^k_{xy}\), and proves that its ratings vary continuously with those weights. A continuous society over ranking types is therefore already part of its model. Simply asking for CLC ratings from such a society would restate the paper, not supply a new continuous mirror.

One plausible follow-on would be to compute the minimum mass that must be moved between ranking types, at a specified cost such as Kendall distance, to change the CLC winner. That is a concrete population-level robustness question, but it is a new optimization problem with no qualifying anchor in this paper. The honest positive case is thus that the paper’s model is already naturally continuous in population shares—not that it establishes a computational result for continuization.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s strongest named anchor, Theorem 13.1, is a continuity theorem, not a computational complexity result. Corollary 13.2 makes the point even more directly: it states continuity with respect to the relative frequencies of vote types. The paper already takes a weighted distribution of ballots as input and computes ratings from it. For a fixed candidate set, repeated ballots form a sensible high multiplicity regime, but asking for CLC ratings on that continuous society would restate the paper’s model and result.

The stronger-looking alternatives do not supply a computational anchor either. The majority, clone consistency, and monotonicity results are properties of the rating rule. Replacing counts with population shares gives their mass versions, but none asks for the complexity of a computational problem. The paper’s Floyd–Warshall, quadratic programming, and iterative steps describe how to calculate ratings; they do not classify a named problem’s complexity.

A mass-robustness question—minimum cost to transfer ballot mass until the CLC winner changes—could be a sensible new problem. But it adds an intervention and cost model, and the paper gives no discrete complexity result for it to mirror. That may merit separate study; it does not establish a continuous mirror of this paper under the programme’s stated gate. So the negative case is strong for rejecting this paper as a ChoCo mirror, though the broader claim that no worthwhile adjacent question exists is too sweeping to defend honestly.
