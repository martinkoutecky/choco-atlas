Why no mirror

Proposition 2.2 proves a set-function property for one finite exchange graph; it gives neither an algorithm or guarantee nor a complexity bound. The proposed type-mass screening problem is a coherent extension, but it cannot make this proposition a computational result under the stated gate, so bit (a) fails.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers single-stage screening under the max-weight policy and the complementarity question; it leaves the multi-stage setting and failure-aware policy result aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the computational complexity of optimizing query masses and the induced packing as the number of types and allowed cycle and chain patterns grow?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the single-stage choice of which potential transplants to pre-screen under a fixed max-weight matching policy. It continuizes the exchange pool itself: many patient–donor pairs share a finite set \(T\) of complete clinical and policy-relevant types, with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of the pool of type \(t\), \(\mu_t\ge 0\), and \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). A large national or regional exchange is a plausible regime if many pairs share those profiles, so the number of pairs \(n\) greatly exceeds the number of types \(\tau=|T|\). Screening capacity could scale with the pool, with a small fixed fraction \(g\) of the population available for queries. Pair-specific compatibility and risk belong in the type-to-type edge classes; the type description must preserve everything the matching policy uses.

My anchor is Proposition 2.2, proved in this paper in Appendix D.2. It states that, under the max-weight matching policy \(M_{\mathrm{MAX}}\), the single-stage edge-selection objective \(V^S\) is not submodular in the set of queried edges. The paper proves this with the six-vertex example in Figure 5: all edges have weight \(1\) except \((E,B)\), which has weight \(1.5\); rejection and failure probabilities are \(P_R=0.5\), \(P_Q=1\), and \(P_N=0.5\). For \(X=\{e_3\}\), the paper obtains \(V^S(X)=27/32\), \(V^S(X\cup\{e_1\})=43/64\), \(V^S(X\cup\{e_2\})=31/32\), and \(V^S(X\cup\{e_1,e_2\})=35/32\). Thus \(V^S(X\cup\{e_1,e_2\})+V^S(X)>V^S(X\cup\{e_1\})+V^S(X\cup\{e_2\})\): the two queries can be complementary.

Here is a continuous problem that keeps that decision recognizable. Call it Type-Mass Pre-Screening under Max-Weight Policy. An instance gives rational type masses \(\mu_t\); a finite set \(A\) of compatible transplant edge classes; and, for each class \(a\), its per-capita opportunity mass \(\lambda_a\), transplant weight, pre-screen rejection probability \(\rho_a\), and post-match success probabilities \(p_a^Q\) and \(p_a^N\) for screened-and-accepted and unscreened transplants. It also gives the allowed type-level cycles and chains up to the exchange’s length limit \(K\), with capacities ensuring each unit of pair mass participates at most once.

The decision variable is a query-mass vector \(q\), where \(0\le q_a\le\lambda_a\) and \(\sum_a q_a\le g\). In the population limit, screening class \(a\) yields accepted mass \((1-\rho_a)q_a\); rejected mass \(\rho_aq_a\) is removed, while unscreened mass \(\lambda_a-q_a\) remains available to the policy. For each \(q\), the policy selects a feasible fractional mass packing of cycles and chains that maximizes their total pre-match weight, with a fixed tie-breaking rule. The objective \(V(q)\) is the expected final transplant weight after independent post-match failures, using \(p_a^Q\) and \(p_a^N\). The solution is a globally optimal \(q^\star\) and its induced packing. Complexity should be stated in \(\tau\), \(|A|\), \(K\), the number of cycle and chain patterns, and input bit length.

Fractional cycle mass has a concrete high-multiplicity meaning: over a large pool, it can be realized by assigning different individuals of each type to integral cycles and chains. The mirror preserves the paper’s central choice—where to spend screening effort before an unchanged max-weight policy acts—and the same uncertain acceptance and failure structure enters the objective. This covers Problem 1 with \(M_{\mathrm{MAX}}\), and in particular gives a population-scale question about the complementarity identified by Proposition 2.2. It does not try to cover the paper’s multi-stage problem or its failure-aware policy result.

My provisional prediction for this continuous problem is continuum-specific hardness, though Proposition 2.2 proves no complexity lower bound. For any fixed \(q\), the aggregate matching policy can be an LP. The outer choice of \(q\), however, may remain difficult because changing query masses can switch the optimal packing and create complementary screening choices. The direct follow-up is to determine whether this outer problem admits a tractable optimization formulation or is hard as the number of types grows; another is to test whether the strict complementarity witness in Proposition 2.2 survives fractionalization. A discrete-to-continuous approximation theorem, with error controlled by \(n\), \(\tau\), and the screening budget, would establish when this model predicts finite exchanges well.

The weak point is substantial: the paper’s examples use a small number of individually chosen edges, and transplant feasibility depends on sparse graph structure and edge identities. Aggregating to edge classes and fractional packings can lose both; the proposed regime also assumes screening capacity scales with the pool. If compatibility profiles are mostly unique, or screening capacity stays fixed as the exchange grows, the continuous model may no longer represent the authors’ problem. The case for the mirror rests on exchanges where many pairs are genuinely interchangeable in all policy-relevant respects and the aggregate cycle structure captures the constraints.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would not treat Proposition 2.2 as a strong ChoCo anchor. It proves that one set function on one finite exchange graph is non-submodular; it does not classify the complexity of edge selection. The paper calls edge selection NP-hard because evaluating its matching policy requires solving an NP-hard clearing problem, and then treats that policy as an oracle in its algorithm comparisons. That leaves a continuous complexity question open, but Proposition 2.2 alone gives little reason to expect the complementarity it exhibits to survive a population limit.

The type problem is structural. A vertex is a particular patient–donor pair, and an edge is a particular possible transplant between two such pairs. The matching policy depends on which edges exist and how they overlap in cycles and chains. A type that records only clinical and policy attributes will generally merge pairs with different compatibility neighborhoods. To preserve the exact graph, a type must also encode its relationships to other pairs; in general that makes the number of types grow with the pool, removing the high-multiplicity regime.

The proponent’s best repair is to assume exchangeable pairs: a finite set of profiles, type-to-type edge classes, and fractional cycle packing. That can define a coherent new model if compatibility opportunities really are interchangeable within each class. But the paper’s witness depends on the incidence pattern of a few specific edges. A single copy has vanishing mass in a large population. Repeating the six-vertex gadget preserves that pattern only if copies stay isolated; then the repeated components have finitely many possible query plans, which can be tabulated and optimized over their mass. The witness’s local complementarity survives, but it supplies no hard population-scale computation. Connecting the copies to obtain global matching interactions changes the incidence structure that made the witness work. Any resulting hardness would need its own argument; Proposition 2.2 does not establish it.

So the strongest negative case is that this mirror faces a choice: preserve the paper’s pair-specific graph structure and lose bounded type support, or impose exchangeability and ask a new type-level stochastic matching problem whose connection to the named result is unproven. That is a reason not to prioritize this anchor on the evidence in the paper.

The negative case is not airtight. A large exchange with many genuinely interchangeable pairs and screening capacity that grows with the pool is conceivable, and the authors might recognize that type-level problem as theirs. I cannot rule out a worthwhile mirror in that regime. The paper itself, however, does not establish that compatibility and edge incidence aggregate that way, nor that its non-submodularity result survives the aggregation.
