The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is qualified but real: this paper supplies a very natural high-multiplicity school-choice model, although it supplies no qualifying computational anchor.

Strictly under the ChoCo rule, the paper has no named result asserting NP-hardness, membership in \(P\), fixed-parameter tractability, or another computational classification. Theorem 1, Proposition 2, Proposition 3, and Corollary 1 are mechanism-design and axiomatic results; the TTC, DA, and empirical procedures are described and run, but no named complexity theorem is proved. Thus there is no genuine computational anchor here. The best positive case must use the paper’s structural results as provisional anchors, while saying clearly that this is an extension of the paper, not a computational result already contained in it.

The natural regime is a large school district assigning an annual cohort of many students to a fixed set \(S\) of schools. A type \(t\) consists of a complete preference ordering \(P_t\) over \(S\cup\{\varnothing\}\), together with the student’s entire priority profile: sibling status, catchment status, eligibility, and any other priority-relevant characteristic. Two students of the same type are indistinguishable for the assignment problem. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over a finite type set \(T\), with \(\mu_t\) the fraction of students of type \(t\). This is plausible for a district with tens of thousands of students but a much smaller number of preference-and-priority profiles.

Because capacities must scale with the population, school \(s\) has a capacity share \(\kappa_s\). An allocation is a mass assignment \(x_{t,s}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_s x_{t,s}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_{t,s}\le \kappa_s\); unassigned mass can be represented by \(\varnothing\). Every individual still receives one indivisible school seat, but the aggregate allocation of a type is fractional. Thus continuity is applied to the population, not to the school seats.

For an allocation \(x\), a type-school pair \((t,s)\) is blocking if a positive mass of type \(t\) is assigned to a less-preferred option than \(s\), and either \(s\) has unused capacity or \(s\) assigns positive mass to a lower-priority type. Let \(\mathcal B(x)\) be the set of such blocking type-school pairs. Pareto efficiency is defined by forbidding a feasible reassignment in which every moved unit of mass weakly improves and some positive mass strictly improves.

My lead structural anchor is Proposition 2, proved in this paper. It states, in full, that if some school has more than one seat, then TTC-Counters, TTC-Clinch and Trade, and Equitable TTC are each not justified-envy minimal within the class of Pareto-efficient and strategy-proof mechanisms. Moreover, for each of the three mechanisms there exists a justified-envy-minimal, Pareto-efficient, strategy-proof mechanism with strictly less justified envy. This is the best anchor because the continuum regime naturally has positive capacity shares and therefore corresponds to the paper’s multi-seat setting.

The corresponding problem would be:

Continuum Multi-seat TTC-Dominance. An instance consists of a finite type set \(T\), schools \(S\), rational type masses \(\mu\), rational capacity shares \(\kappa\), preference orders \(P_t\), and priority orders over types. Define the mass version \(M_\infty\) of one of the three TTC variants by running the same cycle procedure on type masses: when a cycle is found, execute the largest feasible mass \(\delta\) permitted by the residual type masses and school capacities. The problem is to determine whether there exists a mechanism \(F\) over the same type domain such that, for every rational society distribution \(\nu\),

  • \(F(\nu)\) is feasible and Pareto efficient;
  • \(F\) is strategy-proof in the non-vacuous continuum sense that no positive submass of agents can jointly misreport and make every participating agent weakly better off, with at least one strictly better off;
  • \(\mathcal B(F(\nu))\subseteq \mathcal B(M_\infty(\nu))\); and
  • the inclusion is strict for at least one \(\nu\).

A yes-solution is an explicit finite description of \(F\), together with a proof of those properties. A no-solution is a certificate that no such rule exists.

The coalition formulation is necessary: ordinary individual strategy-proofness becomes vacuous in an atomless population because one individual has zero mass and cannot change \(\mu\). Alternatively, one could define admissible continuum mechanisms as limits of strategy-proof finite replicas, but some such repair is unavoidable.

The expected computational split is interesting. Computing \(M_\infty(\mu)\) and its blocking set should be Class A: it is a finite type-level cycle or flow computation, with mass rather than named individuals as the active variable. The full search for a dominating mechanism is more difficult because strategy-proofness quantifies over all reports and all distributions. I would regard it as a plausible continuum-specific complexity problem, not claim that Proposition 2 settles its complexity. Proposition 2 nevertheless predicts that the answer should be yes in the multi-seat regime: its finite counterexample can be replicated into positive type masses, so the improvement is not dependent on one named student.

Theorem 1 provides supporting structural evidence. It says, proved here, that when every school has one seat, if a Pareto-efficient and strategy-proof mechanism \(\phi\) has less justified envy than TTC at a fixed priority profile, then \(\phi(\cdot,\succ)=\mathrm{TTC}(\cdot,\succ)\). A continuum analogue could study whether the same constrained-optimality phenomenon survives for a fixed priority-class domain under a finite-replica or coalition strategy-proofness notion. But literal one-seat capacities do not produce a useful population limit; the multi-seat scaling in Proposition 2 is the more faithful mirror.

The scenario is especially plausible because the paper itself discusses large markets and cites Leshno and Lo’s “A Continuum Model” of TTC. That prior work supports the population regime, but it is not a computational collision: merely proving a continuum TTC limit would be close to existing work, whereas the proposed question asks for the complexity of computing mass assignments and certifying mechanism dominance.

The weakest point is decisive: the paper’s central contribution is axiomatic, not computational. A referee could reasonably say that the proposed problem is a ChoCo extension inspired by the paper rather than the continuous form of one of its computational problems. The second weakness is that anonymous priority classes and per-capita capacities modify the original individual-priority model. Still, those modifications are forced by high multiplicity and are already close to the paper’s own multi-seat and weak-priority extensions. So this is a credible positive mirror of the paper’s school-choice problem, but not a strong anchor for a computational-continuization claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case begins with a category error that the proponent expressly concedes: this paper contains no named computational result. Theorem 1, Proposition 2, Proposition 3, and Corollary 1 are axiomatic mechanism-design statements. The tables report simulations, but do not establish a complexity classification. Thus “Continuum Multi-seat TTC-Dominance” would be a new computational research problem inspired by the paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its computational results. Under ChoCo’s stated gate, that is decisive.

Proposition 2 is the proponent’s strongest anchor, but it does not survive the proposed translation in the way claimed. Its second part relies essentially on finiteness: with fixed finite \(I\) and \(S\), there are finitely many preference profiles, priority profiles, and mechanisms, so one can repeatedly improve justified envy until reaching a minimal mechanism. In the proposed continuum model, even with finite \(T\), the society \(\mu\) ranges over a continuum and a mechanism is a function over that continuum. The finite descent argument disappears. Proposition 2 therefore does not imply the existence of a globally defined \(F\) that is Pareto efficient, strategy-proof, and uniformly dominates \(M_\infty\).

Replicating the paper’s four-person counterexample only produces a single mass instance where a different allocation reduces blocking. It does not produce a mechanism that has the required properties at every distribution \(\nu\). The finite proof also establishes ordinary individual strategy-proofness, whereas the proposed continuum definition requires that no positive-mass coalition can jointly misreport. Those are different incentive constraints. If one instead retains ordinary individual deviations, an individual has measure zero and cannot change \(\mu\), making strategy-proofness vacuous. If one takes limits of finite replicas, one obtains a family of finite mechanisms rather than a well-defined mechanism on mass distributions. This is a fundamental problem for the paper’s central axioms, not a cosmetic issue about notation.

The proposed type model also changes the role of priorities. In the paper, priorities are strict rankings of named students. A positive mass of indistinguishable students cannot simultaneously occupy distinct positions in such a ranking without introducing weak priorities or an additional tie-breaking structure. That modification may be legitimate, but it means the proposed problem is a new anonymous-priority model. Proposition 2 does not establish its mechanism-existence or incentive properties.

Theorem 1 is no better as an anchor. Its one-seat assumption is incompatible with a nondegenerate population limit. With a fixed number of schools and one seat per school, scaling the population leaves each seat with vanishing capacity share; scaling capacities to preserve positive shares moves the problem into the multi-seat regime covered by neither Theorem 1 nor its proof. The induction in the proof depends on finite TTC rounds, named students, and a highest-priority student at each school. None of that yields a continuum constrained-optimality theorem automatically. The proponent correctly treats Theorem 1 as secondary, but it cannot repair the missing computational anchor.

Computing a mass version of TTC is also not enough. It would be a reasonable implementation question for a newly defined anonymous school-choice model, but the paper already gives the finite TTC procedure and never claims a complexity result about it. A type-level cycle or flow computation would therefore be an algorithm for a new model, not a continuization of a named computational problem from this paper. The more ambitious question—synthesizing a mechanism \(F\) satisfying universal incentive and dominance conditions—is likewise a new mechanism-design existence problem, with no input encoding, output representation, or complexity measure specified.

The positive case is right that large school districts provide a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Students can plausibly be grouped by preference and priority classes, and per-capita capacities are a sensible co-modification. The paper’s citation of continuum school-choice work strengthens that modelling point, though it is not a computational collision. This is the main weakness of the negative case: one could write a valuable computational paper about anonymous large-market TTC or mass mechanism synthesis. But that would be a new ChoCo project built around school choice, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper’s contribution. Since the paper has no qualifying computational theorem, and each proposed axiomatic anchor loses its content or requires stronger assumptions in the continuum, this paper should not receive credit as a computational continuization candidate.
