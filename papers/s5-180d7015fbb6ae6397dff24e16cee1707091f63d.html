The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest positive case under the required anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational result. Its numbered results concern equilibrium behavior, not whether a computational problem is in P, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar.

The strongest tempting candidate is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that in a constant-sum homogeneous weighted majority game where parties make demands in decreasing order of weight, every subgame-perfect equilibrium forms the minimal winning coalition identified by Lemma 2, and each party \(i\) demands \(\omega_i/q\). This gives a sharp equilibrium characterization; one can infer an easy finite-input procedure for finding the coalition and demands. But the theorem does not state a computational result, so treating it as the required anchor would stretch your criterion.

A continuous version would also need a convincing account of the bargaining parties as a high-multiplicity population. Parties’ sequential positions and strategic choices are central to the result; replacing them with type-level masses does not preserve those roles automatically. So I don’t have a precise continuous problem per eligible anchor to offer. The theorem is a possible starting point for later work, but it is not a sound basis for this paper’s continuous-mirror case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent names no eligible anchor, so there is no computational result to defeat one by one. The closest is Theorem 1, but it characterizes subgame-perfect equilibrium; it does not give a complexity result for a computational problem. In its stated setting, the outcome is found by locating the minimal winning prefix in the prescribed order. One could invent a broader problem about computing equilibria, but that would add a computational question the paper does not study.

The strongest attempted mirror is to treat parties as a large population of similar bargaining blocs. It runs into a problem with what counts as a type here: a party’s position in the move order affects its strategic role. The paper’s counterexample shows that changing the order can change who captures the surplus. If order or role goes into the type, the parties no longer collapse into a small set of types. If many parties instead become infinitesimal members of an atomless population, an individual cannot tip the quota or claim a positive share, and the decisive move at the threshold has no individual mover. Preserving strategic force would require blocs with positive mass or coordinated group actions; that changes the player unit or the bargaining model.

A continuous electorate does not fix this mismatch. Voters may supply the weights, but they do not make demands or form coalitions in this game. Making their population continuous while keeping the parties as the bargaining actors changes the input weights, not the population of agents being analyzed. This is not an objection to high-multiplicity election models, which can make voters a sensible population object; it is specific to the actors and actions in this paper.

I cannot rule out a separately designed model of mass bargaining among many caucuses. But that would be a new bargaining model, and this paper supplies neither a named computational result to mirror nor a convincing high-multiplicity regime for its strategic parties. The negative case is strongest on that fit with the programme; it is not a proof that no adjacent model could ever be worthwhile.
