The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot name a qualifying anchor: the paper contains no numbered result about computational complexity. Theorem 1 says that if the political rule is dynamically consistent and private decisions are inessential in a rule \(\theta\), then \(\theta\) is stable in every equilibrium. Theorem 2 gives sufficient equilibrium conditions for stability or reform using recursive self-selection and self-denial. Both are equilibrium results, not claims that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. So there are no anchors here under your criteria, and no per-anchor complexity verdict to give.

There is still a plausible population mirror of the paper’s underlying problem. Imagine a large polity with many citizens but relatively few payoff-relevant types: for example, types defined by policy preferences, wealth, and private-action costs. Let \(\lambda_t\) be the fraction of the population of type \(t\). A type must include every feature relevant to payoffs and behavior, so citizens within it are interchangeable. Public decisions remain a policy \(p\) and a next-period institution \(\theta'\); each type chooses its private action to maximize its discounted payoff. Voting or other aggregation rules use the mass of citizens supporting each option. This preserves the paper’s central question—when does an institution delegate future authority to a different institution—while making the population itself continuous.

One precise, but unanchored, computational follow-on would be Mass-DPG Reform. Its input is a finite rational description of economic states, political rules, policies, private actions, type-specific payoffs, transitions, a mass-based political rule, and an initial state \((\omega_0,\theta_0)\). It asks whether there is a Markov equilibrium in which the next-period rule differs from the current one at that initial state. A solution is a type-contingent Markov strategy profile and public policy and institution strategies that satisfy each type’s private-action optimality and the political rule at every state. The paper gives no basis for classifying this problem as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard; I would leave that open rather than infer a complexity result from its equilibrium theorems.

The regime is credible for a large electorate where millions of citizens fall into a modest number of relevant preference and economic types. The weakest point is also clear: the paper is about equilibrium theory, not computation, and atomless individuals may respond differently from its finitely many strategic citizens. That makes this a defensible continuous formulation of its modeling question, but not a mirror of any named computational result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There are no qualifying anchors to defeat: Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are equilibrium claims, not computational complexity results. That is the strongest objection for ChoCo. The paper does not give a finite input encoding or an algorithmic problem to continuize. It permits arbitrary compact state, policy, and action spaces, continuous payoffs and transition laws, and Markov strategies that are functions over those spaces. Asking for computational complexity requires adding substantial restrictions before there is even a finite instance.

The proposed Mass-DPG Reform question is related to the paper, but it is an unanchored computational sequel. Theorem 1 says *every* equilibrium is stable under stated conditions; Theorem 2 gives conditions under which *some* equilibrium is stable or admits reform. Deciding whether a reforming equilibrium exists does not compute either theorem’s conditions. More fundamentally, it requires resolving nested private best responses, political fixed points, and equilibrium selection. Replacing voter counts with masses does not turn those into a pricing problem or otherwise supply an evident algorithmic lever.

The strongest version of the proposal is plausible: use finitely many complete citizen types with rational masses, finite rational economic states and actions, mass-based voting, and retain any uniquely empowered dictator as a separate player. That avoids the objection that no large-population scenario exists. But it produces a restricted nonatomic stochastic game whose equilibrium computation is a new computational project. To make it a ChoCo mirror, one would still need a precise finite encoding and a meaningful comparison with the corresponding high-multiplicity discrete game—for example, a result connecting their equilibria or algorithms. Neither follows from this paper’s stability and reform theorems.

So the case against a *computational mirror of this paper’s results* is strong: there are no computational results in the paper, and the proposed problem needs a separate formal and computational agenda. The universal claim that no continuous mirror is worthwhile in any scenario is weaker. A carefully restricted mass-DPG study could be worth doing, but on this evidence it is a computational continuation of the model, not a mirror of a named result.
