The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that this paper gives ChoCo a natural *follow-up*, but it has no named computational result to serve as an anchor. Its population is already continuous: a society is a distribution \(s\in M(T)\), and an anonymous social choice function maps that distribution to an outcome. So continuizing the population would restate the paper’s model. The new question would have to be computational.

The closest result is Theorem 3.4, proved in this paper. It characterizes an anonymous social choice function with arbitrary range as weakly group strategy-proof exactly when it has the MBV property: there is a nondecreasing, right-continuous threshold function over outcomes, and whenever \(F(s)=x\), the shares of types preferring to move up satisfy the threshold inequalities below and at \(x\); outcomes chosen at different distributions must also satisfy the theorem’s strict share-comparison condition. That is a characterization of which rules are incentive-compatible, not a result that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. Propositions 3.1–3.2 and Theorems 4.1 and 4.3 are likewise voting or incentive characterizations. None is an eligible computational anchor.

A plausible computational continuation would use Theorem 3.4’s thresholds to study ex ante welfare design: given finitely many rational type distributions over a finite ordered outcome set, choose nondecreasing MBV thresholds to maximize expected welfare. The paper’s Section 5 motivates that question, and its threshold characterization gives it a credible connection to the authors’ work. But the paper does not formulate or compute this optimization problem, so it cannot honestly be presented as a mirror anchored in one of its results.

Thus my positive case stops at a promising research direction. The soft spot is decisive under the requested standard: there is no named computational result to mirror, and the paper’s own central contribution is an axiomatic characterization—outside ChoCo’s scope.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.4 is the strongest anchor, but it characterizes which social choice functions are weakly group strategy-proof; it makes no claim about computing a function or the complexity of a computational problem. Its domain is already the space of population distributions, \(F:M(T)\to\mathbb{R}\). The paper starts after the population has been made continuous. So it offers no discrete computational result whose population-continuous mirror could be studied.

The strongest proposed continuation is ex ante welfare design: given a finite set of possible societies and a prior over them, choose nondecreasing MBV thresholds to maximize expected welfare. Section 5 gives this idea real footing, and I would not dismiss it as uninteresting. But making it computational requires adding an encoded prior, a welfare objective, and finite representations of types and outcomes. That is a new mechanism-design optimization problem on the paper’s already continuous model. The theorem supplies constraints on the feasible rules; it does not turn that problem into a mirror of one of the paper’s computational results.

A simpler task—evaluating an explicitly supplied threshold rule on a finite-support distribution—would use the same continuous society, but the rule is input rather than something the theorem computes. For an arbitrary \(F\), the paper gives no finite encoding from which to evaluate it. Any more substantial computational question therefore needs additional choices that determine the problem being studied.

This makes the case against a *continuous mirror* strong, but not against follow-up research. Section 5 leaves a plausible computational design question open. The point is narrower: that question would extend the paper’s continuous model; it would not continuize its population or mirror a named computational result.
