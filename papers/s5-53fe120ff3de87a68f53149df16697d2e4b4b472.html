The continuous mirror question

Given ordered service times \(s=1,\ldots,q\), types \(t\in T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), and each type’s jobs, earliest feasible times, and per-operator endowments \(h_t(s)\), define aggregate supply \(b_s=\sum_t\mu_t h_t(s)\). Decide whether there is a feasible allocation \(x_{t\pi}\) of type mass to whole-job schedules \(\pi\) such that no positive-mass coalition can reassign its selected members to schedules using only their collective initial endowment, with every participating operator weakly earlier on every job and strictly earlier on at least one; return such an allocation or report that none exists.

The model it lives in

Each type \(t\) consists of indistinguishable operators with the same job set, earliest feasible times, and integer per-operator endowment \(h_t(s)\). The allocation \(x_{t\pi}\) splits type mass across whole feasible schedules, while aggregate use of each time \(s\) is bounded by \(b_s=\sum_t\mu_t h_t(s)\); fractional values represent mass receiving an integral schedule, not fractional jobs for an operator.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3’s weak-core selection guarantee. It leaves Theorems 1 and 2 on strategyproofness, the Compression Algorithm’s core failure, and the slot-destruction examples unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a weak-core allocation always exist for this distributional model with componentwise earlier-is-better preferences?
  • Can core existence and selection be computed efficiently in \(q\), support size \(\tau\), maximum jobs per operator, and input bit length \(L\), perhaps using a configuration LP and pricing?
  • How do continuous core allocations relate to core schedules in large finite economies with repeated operator types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is the paper’s Theorem 3, proved in Section 4: “TradeCycle returns an assignment in the weak core.” In full, for any finite instance and its initial slot endowments, TradeCycle produces a landing schedule that no coalition of airlines can improve upon by reallocating only the slots it initially owns so that every airline in the coalition strictly benefits. Here “strictly benefits” means that all of an airline’s flights arrive no later and at least one arrives earlier. This is a constructive algorithmic guarantee, though the paper does not state a complexity-class result such as membership in P. Its mirror is about computing a core assignment, not about the encoding used to compute one.

A credible high-multiplicity setting is a shared-service queue of the kind the authors themselves suggest in the conclusion. Imagine many independent operators, each controlling several jobs that need sequential service. A job’s earliest feasible service time plays the role of a flight’s ETA; service slots play the role of landing slots. Operators with the same job template, feasible times, and initial service-slot endowment are the same type. Mass is the fraction of operators of each type. There could be thousands of operators but only a modest number of recurring templates. Service capacity and each type’s endowment scale with population mass; an individual operator still receives an integral assignment of its jobs. That makes the population continuous while retaining the paper’s multi-job owners, endowment-based property rights, and “earlier is better” preference.

I would pose the mirror as the Continuous TradeCycle Core Selection problem. An instance gives an ordered set of \(q\) service-time slots; a finite set of types \(T\); rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); and, for each type \(t\), its named jobs \(F_t\), earliest feasible times \(e_{t,f}\), and initial endowment \(h_t(s)\) of slots at each time \(s\). A schedule configuration \(\pi\) for type \(t\) assigns every job to a distinct feasible slot. A candidate allocation is a set of nonnegative masses \(x_{t,\pi}\), with \(\sum_\pi x_{t,\pi}=\mu_t\): \(x_{t,\pi}\) is the mass of type-\(t\) operators receiving configuration \(\pi\). Total use of each slot time must not exceed its aggregate endowment, \(\sum_t\mu_t h_t(s)\).

The question is: given this instance, find a feasible mass allocation that is in the weak core, or determine that none exists. Precisely, a block would select positive mass \(y_{t,\pi}\le x_{t,\pi}\) of operators and reassign their jobs using only their collective initial endowment. Each selected operator must receive a configuration \(\rho\) with \(\rho(f)\le\pi(f)\) for every job and \(\rho(f)<\pi(f)\) for at least one. A core allocation is one for which no such positive-mass blocking coalition and reassignment exist. The output is the allocation \(x\); fractional values describe how much of a type receives each whole configuration, not fractional jobs or fractional assignments for an individual.

I would expect tractability in a regime with a bounded number of jobs per operator and moderate support size \(\tau=|T|\). The population supplies mass that can be divided among configurations, which suggests a configuration-LP approach; the remaining question is whether finding or ruling out a blocking reassignment admits efficient separation. The template’s slot assignment structure gives a plausible route through matching or column-generation methods, but that is a research conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 3. This mirror also generates questions about how runtime depends on \(q,\tau\), and the input bit length; whether a core always exists in the mass model; and whether its solutions approximate core schedules in large finite instances.

The scope is deliberately narrow: it mirrors Theorem 3’s core-selection guarantee, not Theorem 1’s strategyproofness or the paper’s examples about slot destruction. Theorem 1 is a weaker continuous anchor because individual misreports by zero-mass agents can become vacuous. The main weakness here is that TradeCycle’s proof uses a finite-agent exchange structure, while the continuum model changes both the resources and the blocking notion. The queueing application is explicitly suggested by the authors, but the large population of repeated operator types is a scenario one must supply. So this is a plausible mirror of their core problem, not a claim that the theorem already extends to continuous populations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that Theorem 3 is a result about a mechanism’s property, not a complexity result. It proves that TradeCycle returns a weak-core schedule for a finite instance. The paper gives no runtime bound, hardness result, or parameterized or approximation analysis. A continuous core-selection problem could be worth studying, but it would add a computational question the paper itself does not ask.

The proposed mirror also needs substantive modeling choices. It replaces individually owned slots with per-capita time-bin capacities, and replaces each airline’s schedule with a distribution over whole job configurations. Blocking then means that a positive mass of operators can improve using their aggregate initial endowments. Those choices are coherent, but they define an atomless exchange economy. The paper’s TradeCycle proof relies on individually owned slots and a finite sequence of exchanges; it does not determine how those exchanges, or their core guarantee, should translate to aggregate capacities. The result would be a new model whose value depends on whether that setting matters to ChoCo.

That objection has a limit. The authors themselves suggest job owners with multiple jobs as an application. Repeated operators with the same job templates, deadlines, and per-capita service entitlements are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. In that setting, whole configurations distributed across population mass preserve the essential multi-job preferences and endowment-based blocking. The proposed question is therefore more than a clumsy restatement, and any difficulty extending TradeCycle would be a legitimate research question.

Theorem 1’s strategyproofness result is a weaker fallback: a report by one zero-mass operator has no effect in an atomless model. A mass of operators making the same report could still matter, but that requires defining collective deviations and changes the incentive question.

So the best case against is about fit and priority: this paper offers little complexity landscape to continue, and its most plausible continuous version requires a new exchange model. That does not defeat the improved mirror. The queue setting is plausible enough that I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
