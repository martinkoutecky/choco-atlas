The continuous mirror question

Given finite left and right type sets with rational masses, compatible type pairs, strict rankings of opposite-side types, and type-pair costs, represent each type by an atomless population and unmatched status as its last choice. Find a minimum-cost measurable matching \(\pi\) such that every measurable challenger \(\sigma\) on the same agents satisfies \(\Phi(\sigma,\pi) \le 2\Phi(\pi,\sigma)\), where \(\Phi\) counts the mass on both sides that prefers the first matching and ties abstain; report infeasibility if none exists.

The model it lives in

Use atomless intervals of lengths \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) for left and right types. A feasible matching is a measurable partial, measure-preserving bijection across compatible pairs; its aggregate type-pair masses \(x_{ab}\) determine cost \(\sum_{(a,b)} c_{ab}x_{ab}\), while comparisons use the actual same-agent realizations of candidate and challenger matchings. A finite coupling representation for those comparisons is an open part of the problem.

The objection that survived

Type-pair flows alone do not preserve which individuals vote for or against a challenger, and the case does not supply a finite encoding of the joint realizations needed for comparisons.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1's hardness of minimizing the cost of a quasi-popular matching. It leaves the paper's algorithms, popular-fractional-matching results, and extension-complexity theorems without mirrors.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paired realizations under \(\pi\) and \(\sigma\) be represented by a finite type-indexed coupling that preserves voter identity and supports an efficient separation oracle?
  • Does the paper's 3SAT gadget remain hard when each profile has divisible atomless mass, or does splitting yield a tractable optimization formulation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a mirror of the paper’s hardness result for minimum-cost quasi-popular matching, in a large matching market whose participants fall into a relatively small number of exact preference-and-cost profiles.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper in Section 5 by a reduction from 3SAT. It states: given a marriage instance \(G=(A\cup B,E)\) and edge costs \(\mathrm{cost}:E\to\{0,1\}\), it is NP-hard to compute a minimum-cost quasi-popular matching; moreover, it is NP-hard to approximate its cost within any multiplicative factor. Here “quasi-popular” means that no matching defeats the chosen one by an unpopularity factor greater than \(2\). The paper studies a genuine computational optimization problem: choose a matching that meets this population-wide election condition while minimizing assignment cost.

Min-Cost Quasi-Popular Mass Matching is the continuous version I would put to a prover. An instance consists of finite sets of left and right types, a compatibility graph \(F\) between them, rational masses \(\mu_t\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), strict rankings of compatible opposite-side types for each type, and costs \(c_{ab}\in\{0,1\}\) for \((a,b)\in F\). Unmatched is each type’s last choice. A type is complete for this market: its members share the same preferences and assignment costs.

Represent each type’s population by an atomless interval of length \(\mu_t\). A feasible matching \(\pi\) is a measurable partial, measure-preserving bijection between left- and right-side agents along compatible edges. Thus every person is either unmatched or receives one actual partner; fractional values describe only the mass of a type assigned along an edge. Its cost is
\[ C(\pi)=\sum_{(a,b)\in F}c_{ab}x^\pi_{ab}, \]
where \(x^\pi_{ab}\) is the mass matched between types \(a\) and \(b\).

For two such matchings, let \(\Phi(\pi,\sigma)\) be the mass of agents who prefer their assignment under \(\pi\) to their assignment under \(\sigma\), counting both sides and letting ties abstain. Call \(\pi\) quasi-popular if, for every feasible matching \(\sigma\),
\[ \Phi(\sigma,\pi)\le 2\Phi(\pi,\sigma). \]
The problem is to find a minimum-cost quasi-popular \(\pi\), or report that none exists. A type-flow matrix \(x^\pi\) records the aggregate assignment; a realization on the agent intervals makes every head-to-head comparison well-defined.

A plausible regime is a large residency or school-placement clearinghouse with repeated applicant and position profiles. For example, many residents may share a ranking over hospital-position categories and the same relocation-cost profile, while many positions share a ranking over resident categories. The number of people \(N\) can be much larger than the number of distinct profiles \(\tau\). This is the natural high-multiplicity setting: mass is the fraction of a cohort receiving an assignment, while each individual’s bundle remains integral.

My provisional expectation is Class A, though with limited confidence: dividing cohorts could let the prover use an LP over joint old-and-new assignment masses, with a separation problem for the strongest challenger. Theorem 1’s discrete hardness is a reason to investigate this mirror, not evidence that its hardness transfers. The reduction uses combinatorial preference gadgets, but the mass model may permit mixtures that erase their all-or-nothing structure. The first useful questions are whether quasi-popularity admits a polynomial separation oracle in the number of types and input bit length, and whether the paper’s hardness gadgets survive cohort splitting.

The soft spot is the move from rankings over named agents, as in the paper, to rankings over counterpart types. Agents within a type are indifferent between clones, and a flow alone does not describe how two matching realizations line up across voters. The measurable-matching definition handles that issue, but a compact finite encoding and an efficient separation method still need to be established. A referee might also view the category-level preferences as a meaningful variant rather than the exact same marriage model. Even so, the mirror keeps the paper’s central ingredients—two-sided matching, head-to-head popularity, the factor-\(2\) relaxation, and assignment cost—while making the population itself continuous. It covers Theorem 1, rather than claiming a mirror for the paper’s polyhedral results or its whole algorithmic programme.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is one anchor here: Theorem 1’s NP-hardness of minimum-cost quasi-popular matching. The strongest objection is that the proposed type-flow model does not yet preserve the paper’s comparison between two matchings. Popularity counts how the *same people* vote under each matching. A flow records how much mass uses each type pair, but not which people receive which outcomes. Two realizations of the same flows can therefore produce different head-to-head tallies. For example, a group of one preference type could receive the same proportions of two partner types under both matchings, while a different assignment of those outcomes to individuals changes some votes from abstentions into votes for or against the challenger.

The proponent acknowledges this gap, but saying that measurable realizations make comparisons well-defined does not by itself give a finite input or a computational problem over type masses. A stronger formulation could define quasi-popularity against every feasible challenger realization, or carry enough joint data to track how agents’ old and new partners relate. Those choices are plausible, but they add a substantive coupling question that the paper’s finite-graph theorem does not answer. With the universal version, the prover must account for the most unfavorable identity-level rearrangement; with a chosen coupling, the result can depend on information absent from the population distribution.

That objection defeats the stated formalization as a complete mirror, but it does not defeat the better mirror. A market with many people sharing exact preference and cost profiles is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and matching categories with strict rankings between them are a recognizable model. Identity-level realizations could be retained, or their effects could be incorporated into a mass-based definition. The programme permits precisely this kind of co-modification. Whether it yields a useful computational problem is open; that uncertainty is a reason to specify the mirror carefully, not evidence that no worthwhile mirror exists.

So I cannot make the universal negative case honestly. The identity-coupling issue is the best objection, but it leaves a plausible continuous question standing. The paper has a named computational result, matching markets admit credible large-cohort scenarios, and the proposed cost objective can be defined over type pairs. Claiming that no scenario or population model could make the mirror worthwhile would go beyond what this objection supports.
