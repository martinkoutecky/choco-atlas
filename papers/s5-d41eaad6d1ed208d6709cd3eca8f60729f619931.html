What the mirror covers

No paper result is mirrored. The proposed reach problem uses the simulated outcome of misled population mass but adds campaign optimization and block mixing in place of the paper's Watts–Strogatz network.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying anchor in this paper: it has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity or algorithmic result. Equations (1)–(6) define the dynamics; Sections 3.1–3.3 report simulation findings. I can’t honestly turn those findings into a named hardness or tractability result.

The strongest positive candidate is therefore an unanchored follow-on, not a mirror of a qualifying result: Continuum Poster-Mass Reach. It captures the paper’s central experiment—varying paid posters and measuring how many normal agents are misled—and makes campaign intensity a computational decision.

For a plausible high-multiplicity regime, take a platform with many users but relatively few behavioral and mixing types: users in a type share their role, initial opinion, confidence radius, update parameters, and offline contact proportions. Let type \(a\) have human mass \(\mu_a\), with \(\sum_a\mu_a=1\). A rational matrix \(P\) gives the type-level offline mixing proportions. Paid posters have mass \(\kappa\) per unit mass of human users and remain online-only agents with opinion \(0\). This is a block-mixing scenario, not an exact continuum limit of the paper’s Watts–Strogatz graph.

For a normal type \(a\), the online mean includes all human mass within its confidence radius, plus poster mass in the denominator when posters are within that radius. The offline mean is \(D_{a,t}=\sum_b P_{ab}x_{b,t}\). With \(\mathsf B_q(x)=\frac{(1-q)x}{(1-q)x+q(1-x)}\), normals update by the paper’s weighted combination of online mean, offline mean, and Bayesian update. Fence-sitters update to the mean opinion on the majority side, counting the poster mass on the misinformation side; posters stay at \(0\). Specify a tie rule, such as assigning ties to the truth side.

The decision problem is: given rational type data, a horizon \(H\), cutoff \(\varepsilon\), target mass \(B\), and budget \(K\), does there exist a poster mass \(\kappa\in[0,K]\) such that
\[ \sum_{a\ \mathrm{normal}}\mu_a\,\mathbf 1\{x_{a,H}(\kappa)<\varepsilon\}\ \ge B? \]
A solution is YES with a witnessing \(\kappa\), or NO. Taking \(\varepsilon=10^{-2}\) tracks the paper’s “completely misinformed” measure; taking \(\varepsilon=0.5\) tracks its measure of normals on the misinformation side. The paper’s finding that more posters can sometimes make misinformation *less* successful is a reason to ask this existential control question rather than assume reach is monotone in \(\kappa\).

I’d tentatively expect tractability when \(H\) and the support size \(\tau\) are fixed: there are finitely many confidence and majority switches, and between switches the finite-type dynamics are rational functions of \(\kappa\). For unrestricted \(H\) and \(\tau\), I’d treat continuum-specific hardness as a plausible conjecture, driven by those switches—not as a conclusion supported by this paper. Natural follow-ons are minimizing the poster mass needed to reach \(B\), and studying robustness when the type masses or contact proportions are uncertain.

The weakest point is also decisive for grading: the paper studies simulation outcomes, not campaign optimization or the complexity of computing those outcomes. The authors may recognize this as a computational extension of their question, but it cannot be presented as a continuous mirror of a named result. Population mass is the continuization here; the paper’s already-continuous opinion values are not.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strongest at the anchor level: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Equations (1)–(6) define a dynamical model; Sections 3.1–3.3 report simulation findings. Replacing counts of misled normals with mass fractions would restate those measurements for a population model, but it would not turn a simulation finding into a complexity or algorithmic result.

The proposed Continuum Poster-Mass Reach adds the computational problem itself. The paper does not ask an actor to choose poster mass, impose a budget, or meet a target by a specified horizon. Those additions might support a worthwhile control problem, but they make it a follow-on rather than a mirror. The proposed type-level contact matrix also replaces the paper’s Watts–Strogatz offline network. Preserving that network structure in a high-multiplicity model requires retaining enough neighborhood information to determine each agent’s updates; collapsing it to finitely many contact proportions changes a feature the paper treats as part of its model. And choosing a finite horizon avoids the paper’s numerical stopping rule, while a stationary-outcome version must contend with the fence-sitters that the paper says do not converge.

A stronger project could optimize where to place posters, or how much poster mass to use, in a specified block-mixing population. But its intervention costs, constraints, and population structure would all need to be supplied independently. That may be a sound research direction; it does not rescue a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

I can therefore defend “no worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper” on the evidence given. I cannot honestly defend the literal universal claim that no related continuous computational study could be worthwhile: a newly specified misinformation-control problem might be. Its value would be as a new problem, not as a mirror of this simulation study.
