The continuous mirror question

Given finite lenders \(A\) and borrower types \(T\) with rational masses \(\nu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita demands \(d_t\), lender budgets normalized per unit of population, and the paper’s weak preference orders, compute a feasible flow \(x_{it}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_t x_{it}\le B_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{it}\le \nu_t d_t\). Distribute each type’s flow uniformly across its mass. Is there an algorithm polynomial in \(|A|\), \(|T|\), the acceptability graph size, and the input bit length that returns a flow whose induced allocation is stable and Pareto efficient under type-level rank-prefix comparisons?

The model it lives in

A mass version of the paper’s category-level lending market: each borrower type carries rational mass and a common demand and preference order; lenders have per-population budgets and preferences over types. The decision variables are flows \(x_{it}\); feasibility is by lender budget and type demand, and the output must be stable and Pareto efficient, with uniform within-type allocation.

What the mirror covers

Covers the market-clearing guarantees of Theorem 3.2 and the assignment computation of Theorem 4.7. Uniform allocation gives within-type envy-freeness; diversity becomes a weaker maximal-mass-coverage condition and does not retain its count-based meaning.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the paper’s sequence of maximum-flow improvements be carried through with rational mass capacities, with polynomial bit complexity and output size?
  • Does type-level Pareto efficiency under uniform within-type allocation capture the individual-level Pareto notion the authors would want for a mass market?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is a mass version of the paper’s market-clearing assignment, with a large borrower population divided among a small set of complete, repeated types. This is a particularly plausible regime for standardized lending: many applicants share the same loan demand, credit profile, term, and resulting preferences over lenders, while a modest set of lenders or funding pools has specified budgets and rankings over those profiles. If the population has size \(N\) and only \(\tau\) borrower types, then \(N\gg\tau\); supply and demand are measured per unit of population. The object being continuized is the borrower population. Loan allocations remain the decisions.

My anchor is Theorem 3.2 (Main), proved in this paper. It says that the final assignment \(Y\) can be computed in time \(O(|A|^4+|A||B|)\), and has four properties: it is stable in the original market, with no blocking pair; it is Pareto efficient, so no agent can be made better off without making another strictly worse off; it is weakly envy-free among borrowers in the same category; and, conditional on the category-level assignment \(X^*\), each lender spreads its budget among the maximum number of distinct borrowers. The paper also proves the general algorithmic result in Theorem 4.7, but Theorem 3.2 is the closer anchor because it includes the market-clearing application and the category aggregation.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover, Mass-Category Pareto-Stable Lending Clearing. An instance has a finite set \(A\) of lenders and a finite set \(T\) of borrower types. Type \(t\) has rational mass \(\nu_t\), with \(\sum_{t\in T}\nu_t=1\), and each borrower in \(t\) has the same per-capita demand \(d_t\). Each lender \(i\) has a budget \(B_i\), a weak preference order over acceptable borrower types, and an offer schedule that determines the types’ weak preference orders over lenders. A borrower type includes everything the problem uses: demand, borrower profile, acceptability, and its ranking of lenders. Thus agents grouped into a type are indistinguishable for this market. Budgets \(B_i\) and demands \(\nu_t d_t\) are aggregate amounts normalized by population size; in the corresponding finite market, they scale with \(N\).

The decision variable is a nonnegative rational flow \(x_{it}\): the amount lent by lender \(i\) to all borrowers of type \(t\). It must satisfy
\[ \sum_{t\in T}x_{it}\le B_i,\qquad \sum_{i\in A}x_{it}\le \nu_t d_t, \]
with \(x_{it}=0\) for unacceptable pairs. Distribute each type’s incoming flow uniformly among its members, so each borrower in \(t\) receives \(x_{it}/\nu_t\) from lender \(i\). The requested output is a feasible \(x\) whose induced allocation is stable and Pareto efficient; there is no scalar welfare objective, just as in the paper.

For stability, an acceptable pair \((i,t)\) blocks if both sides have positive capacity they would strictly prefer to move to that pair. On the lender side this means unused budget or budget currently lent to types strictly worse than \(t\); on the borrower side it means unfilled demand or demand currently funded by lenders strictly worse than \(i\). A mass blocking pair exists whenever both amounts are positive, since a positive amount can be shifted to the pair. Define Pareto efficiency by the same rank-prefix comparison used in the paper: for every lender and every preference prefix, and every borrower type and every lender-preference prefix, no other feasible flow may weakly increase all the amounts assigned within those prefixes and strictly increase at least one.

This is close to the authors’ problem on its own terms. They explicitly group equivalent borrowers into categories, create meta-borrowers with aggregate demand, find a Pareto-stable assignment there, and distribute category allocations among the borrowers. The mirror replaces the finite count in a category with rational mass. It does not claim that the paper is already continuous: its input and output remain finite-market assignments. Nor does it continue the outcome space; it continues the population over borrower profiles.

I expect this mirror to be tractable (Class A) in \(|A|\), \(|T|\), the acceptability graph size, and the binary encoding length \(L\). The paper’s construction uses maximum flows over networks sized by agents and preference levels, and its runtime does not depend on traded money in unary. With rational aggregate capacities, clearing denominators gives an integer-capacity instance with encoding length polynomial in \(L\); exact maximum-flow computations should therefore support the same type-level approach. Establishing the precise bit complexity and output-size bound is part of the proposed result, rather than something Theorem 3.2 itself proves.

The bridge to discrete high multiplicity is direct when the masses align with a finite population: a market with \(N\nu_t\) borrowers of each type maps to normalized flows \(x_{it}\), and an integral type-level flow can be realized by assignments of the finite borrowers. Conversely, a finite assignment maps to a mass flow. For arbitrary rational masses, one can ask how well the mass solution is approximated by a nearby finite market, particularly when ties make stability sensitive to small changes.

The scope is Theorem 3.2’s market-clearing guarantee, not the paper’s dynamic-market exclusions or every result in matching theory. Its diversity clause has a weaker continuous reading: conditional on \(X^*\), if \(x_{it}>0\), spread it across all borrower mass of type \(t\), which gives maximal recipient mass within that funded type. With no minimum loan size, this coverage notion is less informative than counting discrete borrowers. A further question is whether a minimum-ticket constraint gives diversity a nontrivial mass analogue while preserving the other guarantees.

The paper mentions NP-hardness of maximum-size assignment in an unnumbered aside citing Iwama et al. (1999), but that is a different objective and not a numbered hardness anchor for the market-clearing problem. Theorem 3.2 is therefore the appropriate algorithmic anchor here. My weakest point is the welfare interpretation of pooling: Pareto efficiency among type-level allocations need not automatically equal individual-by-individual Pareto efficiency for every heterogeneous allocation within a type. The mirror addresses that by specifying a uniform per-capita disaggregation and a rank-prefix notion of mass Pareto efficiency, but a referee could still ask whether this is the authors’ preferred limit notion. Their own meta-borrower construction and proportional redistribution make that objection manageable, and keep this a narrow, credible case rather than a claim that the whole paper has been continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper’s borrower categories are not necessarily ChoCo types. A category groups borrowers whom lenders cannot distinguish, but its members can have different demands \(c_j\); the paper retains those individual demands when it redistributes the category allocation. Under ChoCo’s complete-type standard, demand belongs in the type. Refining categories by demand, offers, and preferences could therefore leave many distinct types, especially where loan amounts vary widely. The paper’s small number of categories does not by itself establish a small support of complete types. Its diversity guarantee also loses its count-based meaning in a continuum: without a minimum loan size, any positive allocation can be spread across arbitrarily many borrowers.

That objection does not defeat the best mirror. A market with standardized loan products, many applicants sharing the same demand and profile, and a finite set of lenders is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. Theorem 3.2 already aggregates borrowers into meta-borrowers, so a mass version with positive-measure blocking pairs and type-level Pareto comparisons is close to the authors’ construction. Theorem 4.7’s many-to-many algorithm likewise has a clear mass-flow counterpart. The core guarantees—stability and Pareto efficiency—do not depend on borrower identities once types are complete; only diversity needs a weaker coverage interpretation.

So the negative case is weak. The paper does not establish that its actual categories have small complete-type support, and it does not state a continuous-population complexity result. But the standardized-cohort version repairs the first point, and the second is precisely a question the mirror could ask. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario; the best I can say is that its empirical fit to the paper’s own category model is less secure than the proponent suggests.
