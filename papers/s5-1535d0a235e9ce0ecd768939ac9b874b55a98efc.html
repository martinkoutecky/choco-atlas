The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result to anchor a continuous mirror. It contains no Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classifying its complexity. Its numbered results are empirical tables. For example, Table 4 reports that the share ranking a school in the old neighborhood first fell from 20.71% in 2006 to 9.72% in 2007; Table 5 reports that the share ranking a newly prioritized school first rose from 9.35% to 17.23%. Those findings motivate a continuous model, but they are not computational anchors. The theoretical results cited in the paper belong to other works.

The strongest positive angle I can offer is therefore an unanchored follow-on question: compute the Boston-mechanism allocation for a continuous population and compare it under the old and new priority rules. An instance would specify a finite school set \(S\), per-capita capacities \(q_s\), and a distribution \(\mu\) over complete family types. A type records its school ranking, priority points for each school, and outside-option status. Hold \(\mu\) fixed while changing the priority map. The decision variable is \(x_{t,s}\), the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(s\). Run the Boston rounds on masses: allocate by priority, and when capacity runs out within a tied priority tier, assign the same fraction of each type in that tier, corresponding to the large-population limit of lottery tie-breaking. The output is the assignment by type and school under each priority map, including the change in first-choice assignments.

A plausible regime is a large school-entry cohort, where many families share the same ranking and priority profile. The authors would likely recognize this as a continuous version of evaluating their allocation mechanism. Computing the allocation looks tractable for finite \(S\) and type support. But the paper does not establish that its cohorts have a small support of complete types, and this question computes the mechanism’s consequences; it does not by itself identify why families choose as they do. That is the weak point, and no result in the paper supplies the required complexity anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest point against a mirror is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Its main findings are empirical: for example, Tables 4 and 5 report how families’ first-ranked schools changed after a priority reform. The paper neither classifies the complexity of a problem nor proposes an algorithm. Its contribution is an identification strategy for whether priorities or preferences drive observed choices.

The proponent’s best follow-on is coherent as a population model. A large cohort could be represented by masses of complete family types, with per-capita school capacities and proportional allocation within lottery ties. But, given reported rankings, this is simply running the Boston mechanism on those masses. It does not reproduce the paper’s central result: how applications change when priorities change. Holding the distribution of reports fixed removes that response; letting it change requires a model of families’ choices.

A stronger proposal would add utilities, beliefs, outside options, and strategic behavior, then ask for an equilibrium or an optimal mass intervention. That might become a worthwhile research question, but the paper supplies no canonical version of it. Its evidence about behavior comes from the unexpected reform and observed applications, assignments, enrollments, and family characteristics—not from solving an equilibrium or optimization problem. The added model would be a new agenda, rather than a continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

I would not object that a school-entry cohort lacks meaningful multiplicity: such a regime is plausible, and complete types can include the relevant priority and family attributes. The honest negative case is narrower: this paper offers no computational result to mirror, and its natural continuous restatement computes a mechanism’s output rather than continuizing its empirical contribution. I cannot rule out every worthwhile adjacent project; claiming that none could exist would go beyond what the paper supports.
