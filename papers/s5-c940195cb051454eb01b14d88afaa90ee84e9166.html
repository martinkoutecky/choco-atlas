The continuous mirror question

Given binary-rational masses \(\mu_i\geq 0\) for \(\tau\) types, with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), a symmetric matrix \(A\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{\tau\times\tau}\), and a rational threshold \(r\), partition an atomless population into teams with type-mass vectors \(x^k\geq 0\), allowing leftover mass to be dispersed into infinitesimal teams. A team’s welfare is \(\frac12\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}x_i^kx_j^k\); decide whether the supremum of total welfare is at least \(r\). Determine the exact and additive-\(\epsilon\) computational complexity in \(\tau\), input bit length, and \(\epsilon\).

The model it lives in

A complete type-blow-up WGG: each type \(i\) has mass \(\mu_i\), every pair of types has synergy \(A_{ij}\), and teams are chosen to maximize the sum of their quadratic mass-based welfare.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the typed-population versions of Theorems 3 and 4. It leaves Theorem 5’s planar result and the treewidth and graph-class algorithms aside because the type-mass model does not retain individual graph topology.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the signed typed-population problem admit an efficient exact or additive-\(\epsilon\) algorithm, or is it hard for reasons that remain in the continuous model?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is the paper’s Theorem 4, because it establishes hardness even when every present interaction has the same magnitude. The theorem says that \(\mathrm{OPT\text{-}CS\text{-}WGG}^{\pm1}\) is NP-complete; unless \(P=NP\), for every \(\epsilon>0\) it has no polynomial-time \(O(n^{1/2-\epsilon})\)-approximation, and unless \(NP=ZPP\), it has no polynomial-time \(O(n^{1-\epsilon})\)-approximation. The authors prove the game reduction here; the approximation barriers come from Independent Set hardness, relying on Håstad [18]. Theorem 4 is a good anchor for a mirror because it asks whether coalition formation remains difficult when synergies are just positive, negative, or absent.

Here is the continuous question I would put to a prover: Signed-Type Continuous OPT-CS. An instance has a finite type set \(T=\{1,\ldots,\tau\}\), rational masses \(\mu_i\geq0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric matrix \(A\) with \(A_{ij}\in\{-1,0,+1\}\), and a rational threshold \(r\). Each pair of agents of types \(i,j\) has synergy \(A_{ij}\); zero means no interaction. A coalition structure partitions an atomless population of these types into teams. If team \(k\) contains mass \(x_i^k\) of type \(i\), its total welfare is measured by

\[ F=\frac12\sum_k\sum_{i,j\in T} A_{ij}x_i^kx_j^k, \qquad \sum_k x_i^k\leq \mu_i. \]

The leftover mass can be dispersed into infinitesimal teams. The decision question is whether the supremum of \(F\) over such structures is at least \(r\). Equivalently, the optimization problem asks for the supremum and, for an additive-\(\epsilon\) solution, a finite list of coalition mass vectors attaining welfare within \(\epsilon\) of it. Each agent is assigned to one team; it is the mass of a type that can be divided among teams.

This is a natural regime for very large cooperative workforces or project pools: many interchangeable agents occupy a modest set of roles, and the synergy between two agents depends on their roles. The finite model is a weighted graph whose vertices of the same type have the same interaction pattern with every type. If the population size is \(N\), its type counts can grow with \(N\) while \(\tau\) stays fixed or moderate. Dividing welfare by \(N^2\) gives the displayed limit and does not change which coalition structure is optimal. The paper’s objective—partition agents into teams to maximize the sum of pairwise synergies—remains intact. What changes is that agents with the same interaction profile are represented by mass.

My working expectation for this problem is Class C, but only as a conjecture. The signed interaction matrix can encode complicated structure among the \(\tau\) types, while the objective optimizes over decompositions of their masses. That gives a credible continuum complexity question. I would not claim Theorem 4 proves the continuous problem hard: the continuous model permits splitting a type across teams, which the discrete reduction may not survive.

A broader, worthwhile companion is Weighted-Type Continuous OPT-CS. It has the same instance, feasible coalition structures, objective, threshold question and output convention, but \(A_{ij}\) may be any rational number. Its anchor is Theorem 3, which says that \(\mathrm{OPT\text{-}CS\text{-}WGG}\) is NP-complete and, assuming \(P\ne NP\), has no polynomial-time \(O(n^{1/2-\epsilon})\)-approximation for any \(\epsilon>0\); unless \(NP=ZPP\), it has no polynomial-time \(O(n^{1-\epsilon})\)-approximation. The paper proves the reduction from Independent Set here; the approximation lower bounds again rely on Håstad [18]. The weighted version is a distinct question because type-pair synergies can have heterogeneous strengths, as well as different signs. I also lean toward Class C for it, with a somewhat stronger prior: heterogeneous rational weights give the optimization additional room to encode structure. A prover could instead find an LP or other continuous optimization formulation, so that prediction is not a result.

The key plausibility test is satisfied in both questions: they preserve the paper’s underlying coalition-structure problem and welfare objective, rather than switching to a stability concept, an outcome lottery, or a different solution task. A large workforce with recurring roles is a plausible instance regime even if the paper’s country example, with only a small number of agents, is not. The paper’s high-level objective therefore admits a recognizable high-multiplicity version.

My weakest point is also substantial: in an arbitrary WGG, two vertices may have distinct neighbors and edge weights, so their interaction profiles make them distinct types. Then \(\tau\) can grow with \(N\), and the high-multiplicity gain is small. Worse, the paper’s Independent Set reductions may use precisely that vertex-level structure; when each vertex becomes a type with divisible mass, the discrete hardness need not carry over. The typed model is still a meaningful restricted WGG regime, but the cited theorems do not establish its complexity. The questions above are the mirror; whether their answer is Class A or C is work for the continuation programme.

I would limit the claimed scope to Theorems 3 and 4’s \(\mathrm{OPT\text{-}CS\text{-}WGG}\) hardness results, within the interaction-homogeneous regime. I would not claim to mirror Theorem 5’s planar result or the treewidth and graph-class algorithms: those results depend on individual graph topology that the type-mass model does not retain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with what Theorems 3 and 4 actually encode: arbitrary graphs on individual agents. A type that preserves a vertex’s full interaction profile must preserve its neighbors and edge weights. In a general WGG, that can make almost every vertex its own type, so the theorem’s input size \(n\) becomes support size \(\tau\), with little or no high multiplicity.

The proposed matrix model avoids that problem by making every member of type \(i\) interact identically with every member of type \(j\). It is a valid WGG family: a dense, complete type blow-up. But it changes the graph structure the reductions use. Their hardness results therefore do not establish hardness for the continuous questions, and their approximation bounds do not transfer. The same gap applies to the signed \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) anchor in Theorem 4 and the rational-weight anchor in Theorem 3. A faithful sparse model would need to preserve how interactions are arranged among individuals; type masses and a pairwise type matrix alone do not record that arrangement. Adding it may bring the central graph structure back into the input.

That is a substantial limit on the anchors, but it does not defeat the better mirror. A large workforce with recurring roles and role-to-role synergies is a plausible dense WGG regime. Its pairwise objective, scaled by population size, is a coherent continuous problem. The paper studies general graphs, so this family is within its model even though the reductions do not address it. The population can have very large multiplicities while \(\tau\) stays moderate. The fact that the mirror asks a new question about this restricted family is not, under ChoCo’s stated standard, a reason to reject it.

So the best negative case is that the cited theorems provide little evidence about the proposed mirrors, and a sparse version would need more structure than a distribution over types. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no scenario yields a worthwhile mirror: the dense typed model is a credible one, and the paper’s WGG objective survives in it. The case against is weak at the decisive point—whether the authors would recognize that typed dense regime as their problem.
