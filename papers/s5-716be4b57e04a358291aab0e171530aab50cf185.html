The case FOR (proponent)

Theorem 4, proved here, characterizes an equilibrium bidding function and pricing strategy; Corollary 5 says that equilibrium is ex-post efficient. Proposition 1, attributed to McAfee, characterizes the proposer’s optimal price. These are economic results about a two-partner game, not results about complexity or algorithms.

The paper’s continuous valuation distribution \(F\) is also not a continuous population. It describes uncertainty about each of two partners’ private values. A continuum of separate ventures could be described by a distribution over valuation pairs, but that would aggregate independent copies of the game, not continuize the society in the problem the paper studies. The closest computational question—how to calculate the equilibrium from a representation of \(F\)—is not formulated or analyzed here.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strong because the paper supplies no computational-complexity result to mirror. The proponent names Theorem 4, Corollary 5, and Proposition 1, but they characterize equilibrium strategies, report ex-post efficiency, and characterize a proposer’s optimal price. None studies the complexity of a computational problem.

The paper’s distribution \(F\) is over the private valuation of each of two partners. It represents uncertainty inside a bilateral game; it is not a distribution of a population whose members can be treated as interchangeable mass. Theorem 4 is the strongest candidate for a computational reading, since its bidding function is given by an integral formula involving \(F\) and its quantile function. One could ask how to compute that function from a finite-support or otherwise encoded distribution. That would make equilibrium computation the subject of a new problem, while leaving the paper’s two strategic partners intact. It would not make the society continuous in the programme’s sense.

Replicating the game across many ventures also fails to supply the missing mirror. Each venture still has two pivotal owners, and aggregating the ventures gives a weighted average of their outcomes. Treating the owners themselves as an atomless population would require a new bargaining and ownership institution: the paper’s proposer, chooser, and buy-or-sell decision belong to each bilateral pair.

A related computational-equilibrium project could be worthwhile. The material here does not anchor it as a continuous-population mirror. That is a strong case against this paper’s eligibility, though it cannot rule out every broader research direction one might build from it.
