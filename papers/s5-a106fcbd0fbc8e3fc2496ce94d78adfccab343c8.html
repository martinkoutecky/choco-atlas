The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raises no named result with a continuous question, and that is the strongest part of their case. The paper has no theorem or proposition about the complexity of a computational problem. Its findings come from simulations of two query-selection algorithms, measured by how well each recovers one agent’s preferences. Those findings do not establish a computational result over a society distribution.

The most promising candidate would be to allocate a mass of queries across a population and minimize average prediction error. But that requires a new target: the paper evaluates recovery of each queried person’s preferences, while a mass of responses can reveal population-level behavior without revealing any particular person’s utility. If the continuous society’s types include each person’s full utility and response dynamics, the type masses disclose what the elicitation is meant to learn. If types omit those details, people grouped together may differ in precisely the preferences and response patterns the paper studies.

The three simulation themes do not resolve that problem. Instability depends on an individual’s sequence of earlier queries; model misspecification concerns the gap between that person’s utility and the learner’s hypothesis class; response noise concerns variation in that person’s answers. Pooling responses across mass changes the inference target in each case. One could study that new population-level elicitation problem, but the paper’s results do not anchor it.

So the negative case is strong for the paper as written: there is no formal computational result to mirror, and the plausible population formulation changes its unit of learning and target. The universal claim that no worthwhile scenario exists is less secure; a large consultation with shared preference profiles could motivate a separate continuous model. The paper gives no basis for saying that scenario is already its problem.
