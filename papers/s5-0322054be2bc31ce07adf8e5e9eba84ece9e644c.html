The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper gives no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 4 and 5 characterize locally consistent networks under egalitarian utilities; Proposition 9 gives a sufficient condition for groupwise consistency. None gives an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a problem. Although the introduction calls the search demanding, it specifies no computational task or input encoding. That is a substantial gap for a programme whose central contribution is a complexity landscape.

The obvious rescue would be to ask whether a given network belongs to \(F(k)\), or to compute \(F(k)\). But those are new questions, not mirrors of the propositions’ claims. Their complexity would depend on how utilities are represented: a full table over the \(2^{\binom n2}\) networks is already exponential in \(n\), while a succinct formula could yield very different problems. A continuous version would need its own utility encoding and notion of feasible computation before it had a determinate complexity question.

The population is also hard to aggregate in the programme’s usual way. Players with the same utility primitives can still occupy different positions in the network, and link formation depends on which specific endpoints consent. A distribution over fixed types loses that incidence information; treating each changing neighborhood as a type makes the types endogenous to the network and its farsighted paths. A graphon or other continuum network could preserve relational structure, but a single link change then has measure zero. Preserving the paper’s local moves and payoffs would require a new continuum network model, rather than a high-multiplicity relaxation over type masses.

The paper’s own public-good example is the strongest candidate for a symmetric population story. Yet Proposition 5’s efficient networks are connected trees with \(n-1\) links. In a dense graph limit, their edge density tends to zero, so the limit loses both the links’ cost and the connectivity condition that drives the result. Sparse graph limits might preserve more of that structure, but they would still leave a new analytic characterization to turn into a computational problem.

So I would vote against a ChoCo mirror on the material given: there is no computational theorem to anchor one, and the most plausible continuous settings either lose the paper’s relational structure or require substantial new modeling. The universal claim that no scenario could be worthwhile is weaker. A deliberately block-structured continuum network game might be sensible, but its value would come from questions and assumptions the paper does not supply.
