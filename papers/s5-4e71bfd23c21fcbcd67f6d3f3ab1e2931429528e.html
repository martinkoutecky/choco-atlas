The case FOR (proponent)

This paper contains no named computational complexity result, so there is no qualifying anchor for a continuous mirror under the requested criteria.

Theorem 1, proved here, says that under the paper’s catastrophic freeloading assumptions, if the payoffs satisfy \((m+1)\alpha>\nu>m\alpha\) and \((m+1)\nu>\pi>m\nu\), the first \(m\) workers’ dominant strategy is to propose and the remaining workers’ is to vote. Propositions 2–5 characterize related dominant strategies and payoff conditions. These are game theoretic results about worker behavior, not results that a computational problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. The experiments are empirical.

A large microtask crowd could plausibly be modeled as a distribution over worker types, with mass choosing among proposing, voting, and abstaining. But analyzing that model would be a new continuum game theory question, not a continuous-population mirror anchored to one of this paper’s computational results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s only plausible anchor is Theorem 1, but it does not state a computational result. It characterizes dominant strategies under a deliberately stark belief model. Propositions 2–5 do the same; the experiments report observed behavior. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational problem.

The strongest candidate mirror would put workers into types by their beliefs or confidence, then ask how much mass proposes, votes, or abstains under a given payoff schedule—and perhaps which schedule best balances discovery and voting. That could be a sensible continuum game-theory project. It still changes the question from the paper’s result: the theorem reasons about successive individual workers, where one proposal adds a new candidate to the agenda. A mass description of open-ended answers does not naturally preserve that event. Grouping answers into fixed categories makes the voting calculation more aggregate, but takes the model away from its central feature: workers discover alternatives that were not fixed in advance. Keeping the sequential, agenda-changing structure preserves individual actions as pivotal, so the continuum game is no longer a straightforward aggregate-population mirror.

A large, anonymous microtask crowd is a plausible high-multiplicity regime; lack of repeated worker types is not a sound objection. But that does not supply the missing computational result. Turning the incentive-tuning idea into an optimization problem could be worthwhile new work, yet it would not mirror a computational problem established in this paper. The negative case is strongest on that eligibility gap; it cannot prove that no related continuum computation could ever be interesting.
