The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of category rankings, rational type masses \(\mu_t\), and rational per-capita category supplies \(\kappa_h\), for each integer scale \(q\) clearing their denominators create \(q\mu_t\) named agents of type \(t\) and \(q\kappa_h\) unit-capacity slots of category \(h\), with slots in each category tied in every ranking. Let \(Z_q\) count the popular matchings under the paper’s pairwise-vote rule, identifying matchings that differ only by permutations of slots within a category. Compute or approximate to additive error \(\varepsilon\) the entropy density \(\mathcal H(\mu,\kappa)=\limsup_{q\to\infty}q^{-1}\log(1+Z_q)\), over admissible scales.

The model it lives in

The society is the distribution \(\mu\) over complete category-ranking types, with per-capita supplies \(\kappa_h\). At scale \(q\), count popular assignments of the named agents to unit slots, quotienting permutations of equivalent slots; the objective is their asymptotic log-count per unit scale, \(\mathcal H(\mu,\kappa)\).

A type-level flow alone cannot encode the identity-sensitive pairwise comparisons; the entropy definition preserves them by retaining labeled finite agents, so it relies on its stated microstate and house-slot quotient conventions.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers HAT popular-matching counting as named in Theorem 1, whose hardness statement is attributed to Nasre. It leaves the paper’s FPRAS for HAT in Theorem 2 and its capacitated-house-allocation results in Theorems 3–5 without a stated mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the defining limsup always converge to a limit?
  • Can the entropy density be computed or approximated efficiently from \(\mu\) and \(\kappa\), perhaps through an aggregate characterization of popular assignments?
  • How does the finite-instance \(\#\mathrm P\)-hardness relate to the complexity of computing this entropy density?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 1: counting popular matchings in House Allocation with Ties (HAT)—unit-capacity houses and agents whose preference lists may contain ties—is \(\#\mathrm P\)-hard. The paper cites this result from Nasre [13]; it does not prove it here.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large public-housing allocation with many interchangeable units in a fixed set of housing categories. Applicants can share a type when they have the same complete ranking of those categories. For example, the input could give masses \(\mu_t\) of applicant types and per-capita supplies \(\kappa_h\) of category \(h\). The population grows while the number of categories and preference types stays much smaller.

Here is a precise mirror: High-Multiplicity Popular-Allocation Entropy. Given the rational masses \(\mu_t\), category supplies \(\kappa_h\), and category rankings, choose any scale \(q\) that makes all \(q\mu_t\) and \(q\kappa_h\) integers. Form a finite HAT instance with \(q\mu_t\) applicants of each type and \(q\kappa_h\) unit-capacity houses in category \(h\); every applicant ties together the houses within a category. Let \(Z_q\) count the popular assignments of applicants to categories, treating permutations of identical house slots as the same assignment. Popularity uses the paper’s pairwise vote rule. The continuous society is the distribution \(\mu\); a type-level flow \(x_{t,h}\) records what fraction of type \(t\) receives category \(h\), with each unit of mass still receiving one house.

The question is to compute, or approximate to additive error \(\varepsilon\),

\[ \mathcal H(\mu,\kappa) = \limsup_{\substack{q\to\infty\\q\text{ clears the denominators}}} \frac{1}{q}\log(1+Z_q). \]

This asks for the entropy density of popular assignments as the population grows. It is a continuous-population version of the paper’s counting question: the input is a distribution over complete preference types, supplies scale with the population, and the output tracks how the number of popular assignments grows. Counting category assignments rather than permutations of equivalent physical slots gives a finite entropy density while preserving the allocation distinctions applicants can actually make.

I would initially expect this entropy problem to be Class A. With finitely many types and categories, the number of assignments realizing a given type-to-category flow has a multinomial entropy. If popularity admits a finite aggregate characterization, computing the limiting count may reduce to maximizing entropy over the asymptotically popular flows. The paper’s hardness result makes the question interesting, but does not by itself rule out that kind of continuum optimization. Useful follow-ups are whether the limsup is a limit, whether popular flows have a tractable aggregate characterization, and whether the entropy can be computed by a concave program.

This case covers Theorem 1’s HAT counting problem, with per-capita supplies as the co-modification needed to scale the population. Its weakest point is that the mirror replaces an exact finite count with an asymptotic entropy and identifies equivalent house slots. The paper’s \(\#\mathrm P\)-hardness result does not establish hardness—or tractability—of that entropy problem. Still, the finite instances are HAT instances with the same popularity rule, and the continuous question keeps the population as its central object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the strongest anchor, but the negative case is limited. A large housing programme with many applicants sharing rankings is a credible high-multiplicity regime, so I cannot dismiss the mirror on the ground that applicants are inherently individuated.

The sharper objection is that Theorem 1 counts assignments of named agents to houses. A type distribution and a type-to-category flow record how much mass receives each category, but not the identity-level changes used to compare two matchings. For example, two assignments can have identical flows while swapping which applicants receive the better category; their pairwise vote comparison depends on that correspondence. A continuum model can supply it by representing applicants as points in a measured type space, but then its matching objects are measurable assignments, not just mass flows.

The proposed entropy limit is the best repair: it retains the finite assignment counts and extracts their asymptotic growth. But it makes a consequential choice about which distinctions count. With fixed numbers of types and categories, the number of aggregate integer flow tables is at most \((q+1)^{\tau k}\), so counting only those tables gives zero entropy per capita. Nonzero entropy comes from retaining labeled assignments: for a flow \(x\), the number of ways to assign \(q\mu_t\) named applicants of type \(t\) across categories includes the factor
\[ \frac{(q\mu_t)!}{\prod_h x_{t,h}!}. \]
The proposed quotient removes distinctions among house slots while retaining distinctions among applicants. That may suit the housing story, but it is an additional convention about the counted object, not something determined by the continuous population distribution alone.

So the strongest case against this anchor is that its natural aggregate mirror loses the paper’s identity-sensitive comparison, while the entropy mirror restores the lost microstate distinctions and becomes an asymptotic counting problem over finite populations. The latter could still be worthwhile; the paper’s FPRAS for HAT does not establish a convergence rate for the proposed entropy limit, and finding one could be substantive work. I cannot honestly turn this into a universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists. The honest negative case is that the proponent has proposed a plausible new entropy question, but has not shown that it is the continuous-population version of the paper’s counting result.
