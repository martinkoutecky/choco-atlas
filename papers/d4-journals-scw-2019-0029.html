The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a computational follow-up to a natural high-multiplicity model. Imagine a national assessment with millions of students but only a moderate number \(\tau\) of reported ordinal score types. A type is a score level, and its mass is the fraction of students at that level. Given two finite-support distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) with rational masses, ask whether one is preferred to the other by the paper’s leximin-antileximax criterion. Using quantile functions, the paper’s variable-population formulation says this holds if they agree everywhere, or if there are \(0<s<t<1\) where \(Q_\mu(s)>Q_\nu(s)\), \(Q_\mu(t)<Q_\nu(t)\), and they agree outside \([s,t]\). The answer is a yes/no comparison; for finite support, sorting and comparing the quantile steps should make this a Class A problem. That is a genuine population continuization, and the paper’s discussion of Pisa scores makes the scenario plausible.

The closest result is Theorem 4.2, proved in the paper. For all profiles \(u,v\), it says these are equivalent: \(u\) can be obtained from \(v\) by finitely many permutations and Hammond progressive transfers; every anonymous social preference relation satisfying Hammond equity ranks \(u\) at least as well as \(v\); and \(u\) leximin-antileximax dominates \(v\). The additional material’s Theorem 1 extends this kind of characterization to variable population sizes using quantile functions. These results give good reason to think the authors would recognize the distribution-comparison problem as theirs.

But neither result asserts a computational complexity classification or gives an algorithm for the underlying problem. The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind, so it supplies no eligible anchor and no per-anchor continuous problem. The proposed comparison is a plausible new computational question derived from the paper’s normative results, not a mirror of a named computational result.

The weak point is also the main limitation: the paper is axiomatic, and its variable-population quantile formulation already comes close to describing the distribution model. A computational version may therefore be a straightforward algorithmic restatement of its supplementary theory, rather than a new complexity landscape.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is the one the programme’s own instructions identify as decisive: this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorems 4.1–4.4 and 6.1–6.2 characterize normative relations through anonymity, Hammond equity, Pareto conditions, and sequences of transformations. None studies an algorithmic problem or asserts a complexity result. The proponent acknowledges that gap. The additional Theorem 1 is a variable-population extension of the same kind of characterization, not a second computational anchor.

The proposed quantile-comparison question is the best direct version of a computational follow-up: given two finite-support rational distributions, decide whether one leximin-antileximax dominates the other. It is a sensible question, and the national-assessment example makes the high-multiplicity regime plausible. But it is derived from a criterion the paper already states, rather than from a computational result in the paper. With finite support, the quantile functions are step functions; sorting their breakpoints and scanning the intervals gives an exact test polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). That ease is not, by itself, a reason to reject the question. The narrower point is that this is essentially an algorithmic annotation of the paper’s characterization: it adds no choice, feasibility, or optimization structure that could open a broader complexity landscape.

A stronger computational project might ask for the least-cost mass intervention that achieves a desired dominance relation. But Theorem 4.2 supplies no intervention costs, feasible actions, or budget. Its Hammond transfers are uncosted steps in a normative characterization, and the attribute is ordinal, so the paper does not determine how changing its value should be priced. Adding such structure could produce a legitimate new problem, but the paper does not single out one version of it; the model would come from the extension, not from a computational question the authors already posed.

The case should not claim that high multiplicity is nonsensical here, that identities matter, or that an existing continuous axiomatic treatment collides with a computational result. The paper concerns anonymous distributions, and its quantile discussion supports a variable-population formulation. Those are points in favor of the model. The negative case is strong against calling this paper an anchored continuous-computational mirror. It is weaker against the universal claim that no worthwhile project could grow from it: a computational extension of its distribution comparisons is plausible, but the supplied paper does not provide the required computational anchor.
