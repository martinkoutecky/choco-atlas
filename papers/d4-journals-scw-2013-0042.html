The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: the paper suggests a plausible population-level research setting, but it has no qualifying computational anchor for a continuous mirror. Its numbered Propositions 1–5 are behavioral predictions—for example, Proposition 3 predicts allocation patterns under the accountability principle, and Proposition 5 predicts no difference between outcome and opportunity decisions. They make no claim about an algorithm, tractability, or complexity. The paper’s Results 1–6 are empirical findings, not named computational results. So none can honestly be restated as an anchor of the kind your brief requires.

A large organization that repeatedly allocates jointly earned bonuses among workers could provide a plausible high-multiplicity setting: workers might share types defined by effort, productivity factor, bonus, and relevant behavioral preferences. But the paper studies decisions within pairs. A population model would therefore need a distribution over *pair situations*, say \(\mu_z\), and an allocation choice \(r_z\in[0,1]\) for each situation. Aggregating those choices could describe average behavior. The paper supplies no objective for selecting the \(r_z\), however. Adding one—such as minimizing deviation from accountability-based allocations—would create a new planner’s problem, not mirror a computational problem Becker studies.

That is the central weakness, and it is decisive under the stated criteria: a more convincing many-agent scenario cannot supply a missing computational question or named complexity result. I therefore cannot give a valid continuous problem per anchor, predict whether it is tractable or hard, or claim that this paper’s reported findings fall into ChoCo’s complexity trichotomy. At most, the paper offers motivation for a future empirical study of allocation behavior across many repeated, similar cases.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

A plausible high-multiplicity setting exists: repeated cohorts of workers in a large organization, paired for joint production and sharing the same effort, productivity, bonus, and relevant preference types. The type should describe the whole pair situation, since the account and each worker’s share depend on both workers. The pair-based design therefore does not itself rule out a continuous population.

One could use the paper’s findings to motivate a continuous study of how allocation behavior varies across those pair types. For example, given a distribution \(\mu\) over pair situations and a response function \(r(z)\), one could compute the aggregate share allocated to oneself, or its gap from the accountability share. A more ambitious extension could seek the least costly bonus redesign that reduces that gap while holding the total budget fixed. Those questions have plausible workplace interpretations.

But the first is an aggregate empirical question, and the second adds a planner and an objective absent from the paper. Neither mirrors a computational result Becker reports. Propositions 1–5 are behavioral predictions; Results 1–6 are experimental findings. None states a computational problem or establishes its complexity. I can’t honestly turn any of them into a ChoCo anchor without changing the research question. So the pair setting weakens a blanket objection based on lack of multiplicity, but the case for a continuous *computational mirror of this paper* remains weak.
