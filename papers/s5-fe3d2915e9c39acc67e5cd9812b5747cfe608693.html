The case FOR (proponent)

The required kind of anchor is absent: the paper contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Its §§3.1–3.4 report a small case study: the two MA versions select alternative A8, and that choice persists when the approval limits change from medians to means. The authors also caution that the small sample cannot support generalization. Those are findings, but they are not named results about a problem being in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. I can’t honestly cite them as qualifying anchors.

The strongest plausible mirror is an extension of MA Version 1, which aggregates owners’ individual criterion rankings and approval votes. Consider many members of a forestry cooperative choosing among a shared set \(A\) of harvesting plans. An owner’s type \(t\) is their ranking of the common criteria \(J\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of owners with that ranking. As in the paper, let \(b_{aj}=1\) if plan \(a\) is approved on criterion \(j\), and \(0\) otherwise. For \(q=|J|\), let \(\operatorname{rank}_t(j)\) be criterion \(j\)’s position in type \(t\)’s ranking. The continuous version of the paper’s Version 1 score is

\[ S(a)=\sum_t \mu_t\sum_{j\in J} \bigl(q+1-\operatorname{rank}_t(j)\bigr)b_{aj}. \]

The question is: given \(A\), \(J\), the shared approval matrix \(b\), and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over owner types, find every plan \(a\) maximizing \(S(a)\). The expected answer is direct polynomial-time computation in the input size, including the number of types and the rational bit length. This lifts the paper’s sum over individual owners to mass over owner types while preserving its scoring rule.

The regime is plausible but not established by the case study: a large forestry cooperative or regional planning process could have many owners choosing among common plans, with relatively few recurring criterion-priority orders. The paper itself suggests group decision support in wood-procurement organizations and participatory planning of state-owned forests. I think its authors would recognize the extension as their group-choice problem. Its weakest point is that the demonstrated group has only three owners, and the paper gives no evidence that larger groups have a small, repeated set of types.

So this is a candidate mirror, not an anchor-backed case for the paper’s computational results. It covers the aggregation and selection step of Version 1; it does not mirror the derivation of harvesting alternatives or their criterion evaluations. Questions about sensitivity to changes in \(\mu\), or about what happens when types have different approval thresholds, would be natural follow-ups, but the paper does not establish complexity results for them.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that there is no computational result in this paper to mirror. It presents two MA procedures, applies them to 30 harvesting alternatives and three forest owners, and reports that A8 wins under the tested approval limits. It does not state or prove a complexity result for a computational problem. Its contribution is a practical evaluation of group decision support, not an analysis of how a problem’s complexity changes with population size.

The proposed Version 1 extension is the strongest candidate, and the owner-type model is plausible: a large cooperative could have many members with recurring criterion rankings. I would not reject it because the case study has only three owners, because owners might be grouped by type, or because population shares resemble normalized counts. Those are not sound objections. But the proposed question takes the paper’s score rule and supplies a distribution over rankings; it does not mirror a computational result the authors obtained. Giving owners different approval thresholds or other type-specific inputs would broaden that same aggregation exercise, not supply the missing result.

A more ambitious question about how the winner changes as the population distribution varies could be useful, but it would be new sensitivity analysis. Extending the alternatives to full forest plans would add a planning problem the paper does not study. Neither gives a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this article.

So the negative case is strong against the paper as an anchor for ChoCo’s complexity programme, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be asked about MA. The cooperative scenario is credible; what this paper lacks is the computational result that would make such a question a mirror of its contribution.
