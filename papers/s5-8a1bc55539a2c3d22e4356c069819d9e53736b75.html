The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(X\), an explicitly listed common menu domain \(\mathcal D\), complete choice correspondences \(c_t\) for \(t=1,\ldots,\tau\), positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational budget \(B\), decide whether there are finite books \(R_t\) of complete preorders such that every menu's choice under \(c_t\) is maximal under some preorder in \(R_t\), and \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t|R_t|\le B\).

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution over complete behavioral types \(c_t\), each specifying choices on every menu in \(\mathcal D\). Choose a book \(R_t\) of complete preorders for each type to minimize the mean book size \(\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t|R_t|\).

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a population-level version of Theorem 3.2, which the paper proves here by reduction from Partition into Cliques. The theorem says that RCP-D is NP-complete: given a finite alternative set \(X\), an arbitrary nonempty menu domain \(\mathcal D\subseteq 2^X\setminus\{\emptyset\}\), a choice correspondence \(c\) with \(\emptyset\ne c(A)\subseteq A\) for each \(A\in\mathcal D\), and an integer \(K\), decide whether there are at most \(K\) complete preorders \(\succsim_1,\ldots,\succsim_k\) such that every menu is rationalized by one of them: for each \(A\in\mathcal D\), some \(j\) has \(c(A)=M(A,\succsim_j)\). In the reduction, each graph vertex gives a menu whose chosen alternative is that vertex; menus assigned to one rationale force their vertices to form a clique. So the hardness lies in the structure of the choice behavior and menus, not in having many people.

Here is a continuous problem that preserves that object. Call it Population Rationale Burden. An instance consists of \(X\), a common explicitly listed domain \(\mathcal D\), behavior types \(c_1,\ldots,c_\tau\) over that domain, positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_\tau\) summing to \(1\), and a rational budget \(B\). A type is a complete choice correspondence: members of type \(t\) have the same selected set \(c_t(A)\) for every menu \(A\). Choose a finite book \(R_t\) of complete preorders for each type, with the requirement that for every \(t\) and every \(A\in\mathcal D\), some \(\succsim\in R_t\) satisfies \(c_t(A)=M(A,\succsim)\). Minimize the mean per-member book size
\[ \sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mu_t |R_t|. \]
The decision version asks whether books with mean size at most \(B\) exist. The input is given by the explicit choice tables and the rational masses; its relevant parameters include \(m=|X|\), \(\tau\), \(|\mathcal D|\), and the mass bit length \(L\). A solution is the books themselves, which can be checked against every listed menu and against the budget.

A plausible regime is a large panel, consumer platform, or policy study that repeatedly presents a standardized library of alternatives to many people. The types are stable behavioral profiles over that library; many respondents may share a profile, while the number \(\tau\) of distinct profiles is much smaller than the population. The mass is the fraction of respondents with each profile, not a probability of choosing an alternative. This makes the population the continuous object, without continuizing outcomes or time.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as a population extension of their problem: the per-type task is exactly their minimal-book rationalization, and the population question measures its average explanatory burden. The hardness prediction is Class B. With \(\tau=1\) and \(\mu_1=1\), setting \(B=K\) gives RCP-D exactly, so Theorem 3.2 transfers directly. For explicit input tables the population decision problem is also in NP: each type can be rationalized with at most one order per menu, and the resulting books have polynomial size. Thus the mirror is NP-complete unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). It does not claim that a continuum removes the difficulty; it identifies a sensible continuous population problem whose hard core survives even when everyone has the same type.

This mirror covers the paper’s minimal multiple-rationales result, not its WARP case or its other choice procedures. It also suggests follow-up questions: whether useful algorithms arise when each type has few \(c\)-maximal menus; whether the graph structure in the paper yields parameterized or approximation results for the mean burden; and whether a shared rationale library across types gives an interesting coupled problem.

The weak point is that the population objective decomposes by type: masses weight the average, but every positive-mass type must still be fully rationalized, and a one-type instance is already hard. The high-multiplicity scenario also requires many people to share a whole choice correspondence, not just one observed choice. I think the mirror remains credible as a population measure of the explanatory burden the paper studies, but its strongest case is that the question is well-posed and faithful—not that adding mass produces a new algorithmic advantage.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.2 is a genuine computational result, so the case against cannot be that the paper lacks an anchor. The problem is what its object represents. A choice correspondence \(c\) is one decision maker’s behavior across a whole domain of menus. The multiple-rationale “book” is an analyst’s explanation of that behavior; it is not something each person holds or consumes. In the proposed mirror, the population objective therefore assigns a per-person meaning to a quantity that belongs to the analyst’s model. And it decomposes exactly: if \(\kappa(c_t)\) is the minimum book size for type \(t\), the optimum is \(\sum_t \mu_t\kappa(c_t)\). Each profile is rationalized independently; the masses only weight the reported average. Repeating a profile many times does not create a collective choice or a population-level rationalization problem.

A large panel answering a standardized menu library makes repeated profiles imaginable, but it does not resolve that modeling issue. Each “type” must share an entire choice correspondence over the library, including menus an individual may never have faced. The panel story can support a study of average explanatory burden, but that is a new statistic about a researcher’s model, not an evident social-choice task from the paper. Pooling profiles under a shared rationale catalogue could give the catalogue an operational meaning, but that adds a model-compression problem whose value and assignment rules are not supplied by Theorem 3.2.

The more natural population repair is to record choice frequencies across menus and ask whether they come from a distribution of people with stable preferences. That makes population mass substantively relevant, but it changes the paper’s object: the paper lets different rationales explain different menus, whereas this model gives each person one preference and aggregates their choices. Moreover, population random-utility rationalizability already has computational work, including column-generation methods for testing models with unrestricted consumer heterogeneity and results establishing hardness of the testing problem ([Smeulders et al., *Nonparametric Analysis of Random Utility Models: Computational Tools for Statistical Testing*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/ECTA17605)). That is useful adjacent prior art, not a collision with Theorem 3.2; it does, however, make this repaired population question a less compelling new mirror of this paper.

So I would reject the proposed mirror as a weak fit for ChoCo: its cleanest population version averages independent analyses of individual behavior, while its more natural aggregate version becomes a different, already-developed computational problem. The negative case is not airtight. A controlled setting where researchers explicitly care about average explanatory burden across many exact behavioral profiles could make Population Rationale Burden worthwhile. The paper alone does not show that this is a meaningful population-level choice problem.
