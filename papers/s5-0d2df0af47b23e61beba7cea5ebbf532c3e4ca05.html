What the mirror covers

Theorems 1–4 characterize TTC under stochastic-dominance and ex-post conditions; none supplies a computational result for a continuous-population mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

There is no eligible computational anchor in this paper. Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes TTC on FPT domains using SD-Pareto efficiency, SD-individual rationality, and SD-top-strategy-proofness. Theorem 2 gives the analogous characterization on FTT domains using SD-pair efficiency. Theorem 3 replaces the first theorem’s efficiency and rationality conditions with their ex-post versions; Theorem 4 does the same for Theorem 2. All four are characterization results, not claims that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or belongs to another complexity class. The paper describes the TTC algorithm, but gives no numbered running-time or complexity result for it.

The most favorable candidate would be a large assignment market—say, a university reallocating many course seats—where mass represents the share of participants of each preference-and-endowment type, and a probabilistic assignment rule allocates object mass subject to feasibility. But the paper’s theorems concern axiomatic properties of rules, not computational questions. And its one-to-one endowments distinguish individual objects: if object identity is part of a complete type, increasing the number of participants may also increase the number of types. Treating many seats as interchangeable copies could make high multiplicity plausible, but it changes the setting the theorems characterize.

So I cannot write a continuous problem per anchor that meets the requested standard. The paper’s probabilistic outcomes are continuous in the outcome space; its society and preference profiles remain finite. An atomless extension might be an interesting separate axiomatic project, but it is not a computational mirror supported by a named result here. My weakest point is decisive: the paper offers no qualifying anchor, and its central one-to-one assignment structure also makes a convincing high-multiplicity regime difficult to preserve.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational anchor to mirror here. Theorems 1–4 characterize which probabilistic assignment rule satisfies specified axioms: under their domain conditions, it must be TTC. They do not claim a complexity bound for a decision, optimization, or search problem. The paper gives a TTC procedure, but turning that into a question about computing assignments would introduce a new computational task, not continuize one of the results. Likewise, asking whether an atomless TTC-like rule is uniquely characterized would be an axiomatic extension, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

The strongest proposed setting is a large market with repeated preference and endowment types. But in this paper each agent owns a distinct object, and preferences range over those named objects. Since type must include the endowment, the model as stated gives agents distinct types. Repeated object categories or interchangeable copies could make a high-multiplicity market plausible, but they would require reformulating the object and endowment structure—and still would not turn the paper’s characterization theorems into computational results. Fractional assignment outcomes do not change that: the population remains finite.

That is the strongest negative case the paper supports, though it does not prove that no useful project could be built nearby. One could study a continuous-population axiomatic characterization or define a new computational problem for a many-to-many assignment model. Those may be worthwhile, but neither is a continuous computational mirror of a named result in this paper.
