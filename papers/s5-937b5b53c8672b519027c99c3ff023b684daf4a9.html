The continuous mirror question

Given a finite-support society μ with rational masses over complete buyer and seller types, gross-substitute valuations, and the paper's bundle and endowment limits, split every type's mass equally between two halves. Compute each half's Walrasian prices, run MIDA at the opposite half's prices, and allocate type mass among feasible integral bundles so each good clears. What is the resulting GFT ratio to the optimum, and can a witnessing mass allocation be computed in time polynomial in τ and input bit length for fixed G and M?

The model it lives in

A continuum μ over complete trader types, with each infinitesimal trader still receiving or selling an integral bundle. Each half contains exactly half of every type's mass; prices are Walrasian prices for each half, and fractional type mass can be assigned across feasible integral choices while trades clear good by good.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the limiting GFT-efficiency question behind Theorem 2 and applies to Theorem 3's alternative efficiency bound; it does not establish Theorem 1's truthfulness or budget-balance claim, nor reproduce the finite-market sampling bounds.

Open questions for a prover

  • For fixed G and M, can the cross-price allocation and its GFT be computed in time polynomial in τ and the rational input bit length?
  • Does every such atomless MIDA allocation attain the maximum GFT, including under the paper's tie-breaking conventions?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow: this paper’s natural mirror is an atomless limit of its double-auction mechanism, where the population becomes continuous but each person still trades discrete bundles of goods. There is no numbered result classifying a problem as P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. The paper’s useful named anchors are mechanism guarantees, so this is a case about the algorithmic behavior of a high-multiplicity market, not a discrete-hardness result that might disappear in the continuum.

A type should include everything the mechanism uses: buyer or seller status, the valuation over bundles, initial endowment, and any restrictions on what the trader can sell. A society is a finite-support distribution of trader mass over such types. Mass means trader-equivalents: a type’s mass can be divided among outcomes, while every infinitesimal trader still receives or sells an integral bundle. A possible regime is a large exchange for a fixed menu of substitute goods, where many small traders submit one of a moderate number of standardized valuation schedules. Then the population size \(N\) is large relative to the number of types \(\tau\), each trader is limited to at most \(M\) units of each good, and the optimal trade in every good grows proportionally with \(N\). For the truthfulness result, the seller types must specialize in one good.

The lead anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It says that if all traders have gross-substitute valuations, MIDA’s expected competitive ratio is at least
\[ 1-\frac{18M^{2G+2}c\ln(10Gk_{\max})}{\sqrt{k_{\max}}} -o(1/k_{\max}), \]
where \(G\) is the number of goods, \(M\) is the per-trader unit bound, \(k_{\max}\) is the largest number of units of any good in the optimal trade, and \(c=k_{\max}/k_{\min}\) is the ratio between the largest and smallest such numbers. In particular, with \(G\), \(M\), and \(c\) bounded, the guarantee tends to one as \(k_{\max}\) grows.

I would mirror it with the following problem.

Atomless MIDA and maximum gain-from-trade (lead). The input is a finite set of buyer and seller types with rational masses, gross-substitute valuations over the paper’s discrete bundles, and seller endowments. For a scale \(\lambda\), type \(t\) has mass \(\lambda\mu_t\). The requested output is a price vector and a mass allocation among each type’s integral trading choices, clearing every good and maximizing total gain-from-trade. Equivalently, the question is whether the atomless limit of MIDA attains the optimal gain-from-trade, and how to compute an allocation witnessing that.

This is a population continuization: the bundles and goods stay discrete, while the mass assigned to each type and bundle can be fractional. In the atomless version, split each type’s mass equally between the two halves. Both halves then have the same type distribution and can use the same Walrasian equilibrium prices. Gross substitutes gives an efficient clearing allocation, so the continuous mechanism reaches the optimal gain-from-trade; its ratio is one. For fixed \(G,M\), the type-by-bundle allocation can be represented by a linear program with one variable per type and feasible bundle. I expect this mirror to be tractable, Class A, for fixed \(G,M\), with running time polynomial in the explicit type and valuation input. If \(G\) and \(M\) vary, that bound is not a uniform polynomial-time result. The finite-market sampling loss in Theorem 2 is then the correction for deviations from the exact type-by-type split.

A useful second anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that if each seller sells multiple units of a single good and all traders have gross-substitute valuations, MIDA is truthful and strongly budget-balanced, ex post for every randomization outcome. Its distinct mirror is:

Atomless truthful, balanced double auction. Given a finite-support atomless market of gross-substitute types, with sellers specialized in one good, compute a price-and-allocation mechanism that balances trade in each good, balances transfers between buyers and sellers, and gives no individual trader an incentive to misreport, for each realization of its random choices. The solution is a continuum version of MIDA: an individual has zero mass and cannot move the market price, while buyers choose their best available bundle and sellers choose their best supply at that price. I expect this to be tractable for fixed \(G,M\), using the equilibrium computation and type-level allocation. The incentive claim is explicitly about a unilateral infinitesimal deviation; it says nothing about a coalition controlling positive mass.

Theorem 2 is the stronger anchor for ChoCo: it describes how the paper’s mechanism approaches efficiency as a market grows, and the continuum model gives a precise limit for that behavior. Theorem 1 adds an incentive and budget-balance question in a narrower seller regime. I would not use Theorem 3 as a separate anchor: its alternative bound under a marginal-gain parameter \(h\) is useful evidence about the same efficiency story, but would produce a redundant mirror question.

The main weakness is that the continuum removes the finite-sample fluctuations that drive much of the paper’s analysis. Exact typewise splitting gives both halves identical populations; individual price-taking also makes the incentive argument much easier. The resulting LP and equilibrium computation may be a straightforward limit formulation rather than a new complexity landscape. Still, this is a plausible mirror of the paper’s own high-multiplicity regime: the finite theorem supplies the convergence result, while the continuous question asks what clearing and incentives mean once population mass itself is the object.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper gives ChoCo no clear complexity problem to continuize. Theorem 2 bounds the expected competitive ratio of a randomized mechanism; Theorem 1 establishes truthfulness and budget balance. Neither classifies the complexity of an input-output problem. Theorem 2 is the closest anchor, but its central object is finite-market sampling error.

For Theorem 2, the proposed atomless market is coherent: repeated valuation and endowment schedules in a large exchange are plausible, and a society can be represented by masses over those types. But replacing each half with exactly half of every type’s mass removes the finite sampling that the theorem analyzes. The two halves then have the same distribution and use the same equilibrium prices. Asking for a mass allocation that maximizes gain from trade shifts the task to computing a market-clearing allocation; it does not ask how MIDA’s random partition performs. Keeping the sampling fluctuations requires finite traders or a separate random-population model. The former stays discrete, and the latter changes the source of uncertainty. A new complexity question about type-level allocation is possible, but it is not the computational question posed by Theorem 2.

Theorem 1 has a similar problem. In a nonatomic market, an individual has zero mass and cannot change aggregate prices or supply. The natural truthfulness condition is therefore price-taking choice: given prices, each infinitesimal trader chooses its best bundle. That may be a sensible economic model, but it removes the finite queue and rationing setting in which the theorem establishes truthfulness. Asking whether a positive-mass coalition can misreport would retain strategic influence, but it becomes a group-incentive question with coalition size as a parameter, not a mirror of individual truthfulness. Neither version supplies a computational-complexity result from the paper.

I would not argue that a high-multiplicity market is implausible. Large exchanges with repeated valuation schedules are a credible regime, and an atomless, gross-substitutes market is a sensible object. That is where this negative case is weakest: a mechanism-design researcher could reasonably value the continuous model. My narrower claim is that the paper’s anchors do not yield a worthwhile *ChoCo computational mirror*: one is a finite-sampling guarantee, and the other is an incentive property that becomes price-taking behavior in the atomless limit.
