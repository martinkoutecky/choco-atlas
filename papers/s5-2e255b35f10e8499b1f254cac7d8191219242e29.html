The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible setting is a large system of health districts or providers allocating a scarce grant. Treat each district as a claimant; its type records its per-unit claim and every other feature relevant to allocation. Let \(\mu_t\) be the fraction of claimants of type \(t\), \(c_t\) their claim, and \(E\) the average available budget, with \(\sum_t\mu_t c_t>E\). A continuous \(\alpha\)-min allocation would return awards \(a_t\in[0,c_t]\) such that \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t=E\): if \(E\le c_{\min}\), set every \(a_t=E\); otherwise set
\[ a_t=c_{\min}+\lambda(c_t-c_{\min}),\qquad \lambda=\frac{E-c_{\min}}{\sum_s\mu_sc_s-c_{\min}}. \]
Many districts sharing a modest number \(\tau\) of complete claim types makes this a credible high-multiplicity version of conflicting claims allocation. Computing this rule is tractable by rational arithmetic, in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input bit length.

But that is an extension, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. The paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity result—no claim of polynomial time, hardness, or parameterized complexity. Its numbered items are equations defining allocation rules and criteria; its conclusions compare those rules on the Catalan data and report their axiomatic properties. The nearest result is the paper’s conclusion that \(\alpha\)-min is the only considered rule satisfying its equity, stability, and social-constraint criteria, summarized in Table 7. That is an axiomatic finding, not a computational anchor.

The proposed district model could be handed to a prover as a new problem, but it would ask about allocating grants among many repeated claimant types, not the computation studied in this paper. The paper’s actual instance has six distinct expenditure categories, and its budget is already infinitely divisible; that alone does not continuize the population. My weakest point is therefore also decisive under the requested standard: the most defensible positive angle changes the claimant regime, while the paper supplies no named computational result for it to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper gives ChoCo no computational result to continuize. Its equations define bankruptcy rules and evaluation criteria; its main conclusion is that \(\alpha\)-min meets the selected equity, stability, and social constraints and is preferred in the Catalan comparison. The tables apply those rules to six named budget categories over four years. The paper poses no algorithmic problem and makes no complexity claim.

The district or provider version is the best repair. A distribution over complete claim types with a per-capita endowment gives a coherent high-multiplicity claims problem, and \(\alpha\)-min can be extended to that setting. But the paper’s six claimants are distinct accounting functions—salaries, investment, transfers, and so on—not repeated units of a claimant population. Turning them into districts changes the instance regime and the object being studied. Preserving the paper’s Gini, power-index, and Borda comparisons would also require a new model of how those criteria apply across the new population. A continuum of stakeholders voting over rules would be another new problem: the paper’s Borda tally is over six budget categories, not a population of voters.

So the district model could be worthwhile as a separate high-multiplicity bankruptcy problem. It does not supply a computational mirror of this paper’s result. The honest limit of the negative case is that I cannot rule out every useful continuous claims-allocation project; the narrower point is that none of the paper’s findings provides the computational anchor ChoCo is looking for.
