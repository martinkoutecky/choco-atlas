The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named computational result of the kind ChoCo requires: it gives no complexity classification or algorithmic result for a computational problem. Its numbered results concern Bayesian equilibria, information aggregation, and welfare comparisons. In particular, “efficient” here means choosing the Bayes-best outcome given the voters’ signals; it does not mean computationally tractable.

The closest result to a possible lead is Proposition 3, proved in the paper. Under Assumptions 1 and 2, a voting rule implements the Bayes-efficient outcome in equilibrium if and only if it is a generalized flexible rule: there must be a vector \((\psi_i)_{i\in N}\), with each \(\psi_i\) in the interior of shareholder \(i\)’s ballot space and \(\sum_i\psi_i=0\). The related Proposition 2 gives the flexible-rule equilibrium strategy: each shareholder votes \(c\ln t_i+\kappa_i\), where \(t_i\) is their signal’s likelihood ratio, \(c>0\), and the constants satisfy \(\sum_i\kappa_i=0\). These are substantive analytic results, but neither is an eligible computational anchor.

The best population scenario I can see is a large public company with many retail shareholders, grouped by identical voting rights, proxy ballot options, and signal technology. A candidate extension would take a rational mass distribution \(\mu\) over those shareholder classes, give each shareholder a private likelihood-ratio signal \(t\), and ask whether there is a measurable ballot strategy \(x_a(t)\) for each class \(a\) such that the aggregate vote selects the Bayes-efficient outcome and the strategies form an equilibrium. A solution would give the strategies and prove both properties. The regime is plausible when the number of shareholders is large and the number of such classes is modest.

But this candidate loses the paper’s central strategic force in the continuum: an individual infinitesimal shareholder cannot affect the vote, so the pivotality that shapes the paper’s Bayesian equilibria disappears. Preserving it requires finite voters or positive-mass blocs, either of which changes the model. More fundamentally, the paper keeps a finite society of \(n\) shareholders; its continuous objects are the signal space and the ballot space, not the population. The authors might recognize the proposed extension as interesting, but I would not present it as a faithful mirror of their results.

So the strongest honest positive case is only a possible future extension built around Proposition 3—not a ChoCo mirror anchored in a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no computational result to continuize. Propositions 2 and 3 characterize Bayesian equilibria: the flexible rule’s equilibrium uses ballots \(c\ln t_i+\kappa_i\), and efficient implementation requires a generalized flexible rule. “Efficient” here means choosing the Bayes-best outcome given the signals; it says nothing about computational efficiency. A continuous version of those propositions would therefore continue an equilibrium analysis, not chart the computational complexity of a problem the paper studies.

The proposed shareholder-class mirror also loses the mechanism that gives those propositions content. In an atomless society, an individual shareholder’s ballot has zero effect on the aggregate vote. Their best response no longer selects an informative strategy; the equilibrium characterization behind Propositions 2 and 3 disappears. One can still use the aggregate score \(\int \ln t\,d\mu(t)\) to implement the Bayes decision, but that is an aggregate decision rule, not the paper’s strategic equilibrium result or a computational problem it poses.

The more faithful alternative is to preserve pivotality by treating positive-mass shareholder classes as strategic blocs. But then the strategic players are coordinated blocs, not many high-multiplicity shareholders. Their collective actions and incentives require a new game. Keeping the original individual players preserves the finite electorate; making them atomless removes their individual strategic influence. Neither route yields a population-continuous mirror of the paper’s central results.

The manager section does not repair this gap. Its selection-versus-voting comparison depends on the manager responding to the electorate’s error probabilities. A continuum model would need a new account of how aggregate signals, individual incentives, and proposal selection interact. One could invent computational questions about choosing ballot spaces or changing mass across signal types, but those introduce objectives and controls absent from the paper; they are new research directions, not mirrors anchored in its results.

So the negative case is strong on fit with ChoCo: there is no named computational anchor, and the most natural continuous version removes the strategic object the paper actually analyzes. It is weaker as a universal claim that no worthwhile extension could exist. A mass-level voting-design problem might be interesting, but this paper gives no reason to treat it as a computational mirror of its results.
