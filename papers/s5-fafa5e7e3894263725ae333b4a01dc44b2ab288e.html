The continuous mirror question

Given a finite item set \(M\), types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), explicit valuation tables \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\), and a fixed order of type cohorts, let each item \(j\) have a nondecreasing concave rational piecewise-linear cost \(f_j:[0,1]\to\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) with \(f_j(0)=0\) and \(\alpha f_j(y)/y\ge f'_{j,+}(0)\) for \(y>0\). Process mass in cohort order, assigning type \(t\) at current loads \(y_j\) a lexicographically tie-broken bundle maximizing \(v_t(B)-\sum_{j\in B}f'_{j,+}(y_j)\), and increase each load by the mass assigned that item. Compute the resulting bundle masses \(x_{t,B}\) and payment density; determine whether this has a polynomial event-driven implementation and guarantees per-capita social cost \(\pi_\infty(x)=\sum_j f_j(y_j)+\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}[v_t(M)-v_t(B)]\le\alpha\pi_\infty^*\u005c), where \pi_\infty^*\u005c) is the minimum of that objective over x_{t,B}\ge0\) with \(\sum_Bx_{t,B}=\mu_t\).

The model it lives in

Use a distribution over complete valuation types, with each unit of mass receiving an integral bundle. For a finite replication of size \(N\), item \(j\) has total cost \(Nf_j(|S|/N)\) when serving \(S\). The decision variables are bundle masses \(x_{t,B}\); the objective is the per-capita social cost \(\sum_j f_j(y_j)+\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}[v_t(M)-v_t(B)]\). The sequential rule prices an item at its current right marginal cost and outputs both the mass allocation and its payment density.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the social-cost approximation aspect of Theorem 4.8, with nonnegative payments and exact budget balance under the stated mass-scaled costs. It leaves continuous weak group-strategyproofness open, and does not mirror Theorem 3.4 or the applications in Theorem 4.9.

Open questions for a prover

  • Prove the event-driven procedure's running-time bound and \(\alpha\)-approximation, including behavior when a load crosses a piecewise-linear breakpoint.
  • Determine whether a meaningful weak group-strategyproofness notion for positive-mass coalitions holds in this model.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 4.8, proved in this paper. It says that with general nondecreasing valuations, if each item’s cost function is nondecreasing, subadditive, and \(\alpha\)-average max-bounded, the Sequential Mechanism is individually rational, has no positive transfers, is weakly group-strategyproof, exactly budget-balanced, and gives an \(\alpha\)-approximation to minimum social cost. The paper has no named NP-hardness result to anchor a hardness-dissolution question, and Theorem 4.8 does not claim polynomial running time. It is the strongest anchor for a continuous mirror because it gives a constant-factor guarantee for the general-valuation setting. Theorem 3.4 is a possible algorithmic lead, but its \(H_n\) approximation grows with population size.

A credible high-multiplicity regime is a large service provider processing recurring cohorts of customers. Each customer has a valuation over the same finite set of services; many customers share one of a small number of complete valuation types. The provider’s operating cost for each service depends on how many customers receive it. For example, the user base might be large while the number of distinct service-demand profiles stays small. A fixed order of cohorts is plausible when the Sequential Mechanism processes renewal or enrollment groups in that order. The relevant scale is \(N\) customers and \(\tau\) types, with \(N\gg\tau\). Each customer still receives an integral bundle of services; mass records how many customers of each type receive each bundle.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Sequential Cost Sharing (CSM\(_\infty\)). An instance has a finite item set \(M\); types \(T\) with binary-rational masses \(\mu_t\ge0\) summing to \(1\); a nondecreasing valuation table \(v_t:2^M\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) for each type; and a fixed order of type cohorts. For each item \(j\), the per-capita cost is a nondecreasing, concave, rational piecewise-linear function \(f_j:[0,1]\to\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\), with \(f_j(0)=0\). The cost for serving a subset \(S\) of a replicated \(N\)-person population is \(Nf_j(|S|/N)\). Assume, for some given \(\alpha\), that
\[ \alpha\,\frac{f_j(y)}{y}\ \ge\ f'_{j,+}(0) \qquad\text{for every }y>0\text{ and every }j. \]
This is the continuous form of the paper’s average max-bounded condition.

Process the population along \(r\in[0,1]\), in the specified cohort order. Let \(q(r)\) be the type at position \(r\), and let \(y_j(r)\) be the mass already receiving item \(j\). At position \(r\), the mechanism assigns the lexicographically tie-broken bundle maximizing
\[ v_{q(r)}(B)-\sum_{j\in B} f'_{j,+}(y_j(r)). \]
The instance asks for the resulting mass allocation \(x_{t,B}\), where \(x_{t,B}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(B\), and the associated payment density. A solution must give this allocation and show that its per-capita social cost is at most \(\alpha\) times
\[ \pi_\infty^*= \min_{\substack{x_{t,B}\ge0\\\sum_Bx_{t,B}=\mu_t}} \left[ \sum_j f_j(y_j)+ \sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}\bigl(v_t(M)-v_t(B)\bigr) \right], \qquad y_j=\sum_{t,B:\,j\in B}x_{t,B}. \]
The payment density at \(r\) is \(\sum_{j\in B(r)}f'_{j,+}(y_j(r))\); its integral equals the total per-capita cost for piecewise-linear \(f_j\).

I expect this stated version to be Class A. Between breakpoints of the \(f_j\), marginal prices stay constant, so each cohort’s chosen bundle stays constant. Since served mass only increases, each cost breakpoint is crossed at most once. An event-driven algorithm can advance to the next cohort boundary or cost breakpoint, update prices, and recompute the best bundle from the explicit valuation table. Its work is polynomial in the table size and the number of encoded cost breakpoints. The approximation claim has a direct bridge to Theorem 4.8: in every \(N\)-person replication, \(c_j(S)=Nf_j(|S|/N)\) is nondecreasing and subadditive, and the displayed condition implies the discrete \(\alpha\)-average max-bound. The theorem therefore gives an \(\alpha\)-approximation for each replication; dividing by \(N\) and taking the mass limit gives the stated social-cost guarantee. Budget balance passes through the same limit.

The paper’s other relevant named result, Theorem 4.9, is proved here and says that the Sequential Mechanism is \(\alpha\)-approximate for its listed combinatorial cost functions: Set Cover with \(\alpha=d\), Vertex Cover with \(\alpha=k\) for maximum degree \(k\), bipartite Matching with \(\alpha=k\), and general-graph Matching with \(\alpha=(5k+3)/4\). Those are useful application anchors, but I would not add them as separate mirrors: the symmetric mass-cost model above is the cleaner, more defensible lead.

The weakest point is the incentive guarantee. Theorem 4.8 proves weak group-strategyproofness for finite agents; that statement does not automatically become the same claim for positive-mass coalitions in an atomless population. I would treat its continuous version as a further question, alongside extending the cost model beyond symmetric, piecewise-linear functions. The proposed mirror still preserves the paper’s main computational object: sequential bundle choice under marginal cost, measured against the same social-cost objective, with the same approximation factor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.9 is the weakest part of the proponent’s case. In its Set Cover, Vertex Cover, and Matching examples, players are elements or edges whose incidence determines the cost. Replicating an element or edge produces a weighted or multiset variant, and the paper’s constant approximation parameters can deteriorate with the replication factor: the cost may stay fixed while the number of players grows. Rescaling costs to avoid that changes the combinatorial problem. So these applications do not independently establish a convincing high-multiplicity mirror.

Theorem 3.4 is a better alternative, but its continuous version has a serious limitation. Its \(H_n\) guarantee grows with population size, so it gives no constant guarantee in an atomless limit. For flat public-good costs, keeping the original total cost makes per-capita cost vanish; scaling the cost to keep it nontrivial produces a discontinuity at zero mass. Linear costs avoid that degeneration, but the approximation bound still grows with \(n\). This weakens the case for a useful continuum result, though it does not rule out asking one.

Theorem 4.8 is the real obstacle to a negative verdict. Its conclusion is an approximation guarantee, not a complexity classification, and the paper does not specify a compact input model for general valuations. The proponent’s event-driven algorithm also works in a restricted regime: anonymous, mass-scaled piecewise-linear costs and a fixed order of type cohorts. Those are meaningful limitations, and the continuous weak group-strategyproofness claim remains open. But they do not invalidate the mirror. In that regime, the sequential allocation process is well-defined, its cost breakpoints give finitely many events, and the finite-population guarantee transfers to the mass limit. A large service population with repeated valuation types and costs depending on the fraction served is a plausible scenario.

So I can defeat Theorem 4.9 as a distinct anchor, and I can argue that Theorem 3.4 gives a poor limiting guarantee. I cannot honestly defeat the Theorem 4.8 mirror. The negative case is therefore weak on the central question: it identifies a narrow and partly inherited continuous result, but that result is still a worthwhile population-continuous computational problem under the programme’s stated criteria.
