The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible setting is a large course allocation: many students share the same complete valuation over course bundles, and the number of students is large relative to the number of valuation types. Let \(\mu_t\) be the population share of type \(t\), and let \(q_o\) be the per-capita supply of seats of course \(o\). A candidate continuous model assigns mass \(x_{t,S}\) of each type to each integral bundle \(S\), with \(\sum_S x_{t,S}=\mu_t\) and course-use totals matching supply. One could ask whether such an assignment exists that is EFX\(^{\pm}\) for every pair of positive-mass type-and-bundle groups and Pareto-optimal against feasible mass reallocations. This keeps the population continuous while each student still receives an indivisible bundle.

But I cannot honestly give that question a qualifying anchor from this paper. Its numbered theorems and corollaries assert existence or incompatibility of fairness and efficiency properties; none asserts a complexity result such as membership in P or NP-hardness. The closest result is Theorem 4, proved here: with general but identical valuations, the leximin allocation satisfies EFX\(^{\pm}\) and PO. That is an axiomatic existence result, not a computational result, and it does not establish a complexity claim for the proposed mass-allocation problem. Theorem 6, also proved here, says that some instances with arbitrary items and nonidentical general valuations have no allocation satisfying both EF1 and PO; it likewise gives no complexity result.

So the paper offers a credible high-multiplicity setting and suggests a continuous allocation question, but it provides no named computational result to anchor the requested mirror. The candidate question is a new computational formulation inspired by its axiomatic results, not a continuous version of a computational problem this paper studies.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The course-allocation example is a credible high-multiplicity setting. Students with the same complete valuation can form types, seats can scale with population, and each student can still receive an integral bundle. I would not reject that model on grounds of anonymity, indivisibility, or the need to scale supplies.

The problem is that it does not mirror a computational result in this paper. Theorem 4 proves an axiomatic guarantee for a finite leximin allocation under identical valuations; it gives no complexity result for finding that allocation. A mass version could ask whether a suitably defined leximin assignment still has EFX\(^{\pm}\) and PO, or ask how to compute one. The first is another axiomatic question; the second introduces a search problem the paper never studies. Theorem 6 is likewise an axiomatic incompatibility witness for two agents and four items. Cloning its valuation profiles and scaling supplies could motivate a new question about mass allocations, but it does not continue a computational result from the paper.

The proposed \(x_{t,S}\) model is therefore a plausible research direction, but it must supply its own computational problem: group-level EFX\(^{\pm}\), Pareto improvement over mass reallocations, and any leximin objective all need continuous definitions. Those choices may be sensible; they do not create a computational anchor in the paper.
