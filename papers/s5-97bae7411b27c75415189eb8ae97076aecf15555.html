The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\), \(N\ge m\), and an empirical distribution \(\mu\) with \(\mu_u=n_u/N\) over finitely many normalized utility types \(u:M\to[0,1]\), let \(J^*\) sample a type according to \(\mu\), then choose its top candidate with probability \(1/2\) or uniformly among its \(\lfloor m^{1/3}\rfloor\) top candidates otherwise. Is \(J^*\) truthful when one voter’s report transfers mass \(1/N\) from their true type to a reported type, and does it guarantee expected per-capita welfare at least \(c m^{-2/3}\max_{c\in M}\sum_u\mu_u u(c)\) for some universal \(c>0\)?

The model it lives in

A society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over complete normalized cardinal utility vectors, with masses \(\mu_u=n_u/N\). The mechanism samples a type according to its mass and applies the paper’s \(J^*\) choice rule; each voter’s deviation transfers mass \(1/N\). The objective is its expected per-capita welfare relative to \(\max_{c\in M}\sum_u\mu_u u(c)\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the explicit mechanism and welfare guarantee of Theorem 4. It leaves Theorem 3’s ordinal-mechanism performance bound and broader synthesis of welfare-optimal truthful mechanisms aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the same welfare guarantee extend to arbitrary binary-rational type masses outside the \(N\)-voter grid, with a specified finite mass for each strategic agent?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of truthful cardinal voting. A type is a complete normalized utility vector \(u:M\to[0,1]\), with minimum \(0\) and maximum \(1\). The society is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over such types, where \(\mu_u\) is the fraction of voters with utility vector \(u\). For a population of \(N\) voters, a single voter’s misreport moves mass \(1/N\) from \(u\) to a reported type \(u'\). A mechanism maps each such distribution to a lottery over candidates; its objective is the worst-case ratio of expected aggregate welfare to the welfare of the range-voting winner.

That is a plausible setting for a large consultation or electorate with many voters but relatively few recurring cardinal-rating patterns: for example, millions of voters represented by hundreds of distinct utility vectors. The paper’s mechanism \(J^*\) can be applied from the distribution by sampling a type according to its mass, then sampling a candidate from that type’s top choices. Keeping the \(1/N\) report change preserves the paper’s individual incentive constraint in the high-multiplicity representation.

The paper’s closest anchors are not computational-complexity results in the sense required here. Theorem 4, proved in this paper, states that \(J^*=\tfrac12J_{1,1}+\tfrac12J_{1,\lfloor m^{1/3}\rfloor}\) is truthful and has welfare ratio \(\Omega(m^{-2/3})\) as \(n\ge m\) and \(m\to\infty\). Theorem 3, attributed to [5], states that for sufficiently large \(m\) and sufficiently larger \(n\), every truthful ordinal mechanism on the no-ties domain has welfare ratio \(O(m^{-2/3})\). Together they give a matching asymptotic welfare guarantee for the proposed mechanism within the paper’s setting. They are meaningful mechanism-design results, but neither establishes that a problem is in P, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or otherwise computationally classified.

An induced question would be: given \(m,N\), does there exist a truthful mechanism on all \(N\)-voter empirical distributions whose worst-case welfare ratio is at least \(\rho\), and can such a mechanism be produced? A solution is a mechanism mapping each distribution to a lottery and satisfying the one-voter \(1/N\)-mass incentive inequalities. Theorem 4 supplies a candidate with ratio \(\Omega(m^{-2/3})\); Theorem 3 supplies a matching bound for the stated ordinal, no-ties restriction. Whether optimizing or computing such mechanisms has an efficient algorithm is a further question, not a result established by those theorems.

The weakest point is that an exactly atomless population makes individual reports change zero mass, so ordinary strategy-proofness becomes vacuous. Retaining the \(1/N\) mass quantum keeps the authors’ incentive question intact, but makes this a high-multiplicity family rather than a fully atomless limit. And because the paper contains no named computational-complexity result of the requested kind, this is a plausible continuous-population mirror, but it cannot supply a qualifying anchor under your stated rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that neither anchor is a computational result of the kind ChoCo is meant to mirror. Theorem 4 gives a welfare guarantee for an explicit truthful mechanism; Theorem 3 bounds the guarantee of a class of mechanisms. They concern what truthful mechanisms can achieve, not the complexity of an input/output problem. Theorem 3 is also attributed to earlier work. The paper supplies no complexity classification or algorithmic question whose input is a society and whose solution is bribery, control, campaigning, or robustness.

The best population model is still coherent: use a finite rating scale, let \(\mu\) record the fractions of voters with each complete utility vector, and let one voter’s deviation transfer mass \(1/N\). That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime. But it does not turn these theorems into computational ones. Truthfulness constrains one global mechanism across every possible profile and deviation; it is not a task that can be solved independently for a supplied \(\mu\). The paper’s \(J^*\) can be applied directly from the type masses by sampling a type and then one of its top candidates. Optimizing over all truthful mechanisms would be a new global mechanism-synthesis problem, not an algorithmic result mirrored from Theorem 4.

The proponent’s proposed decision question—whether there exists a mechanism for all \(N\)-voter empirical distributions with a given worst-case ratio—does make that extension explicit. It could be worth studying, especially with a finite rating alphabet. But its computational interest would come from adding a synthesis objective and analyzing its complexity. It is not an algorithmic question the paper poses or answers. Meanwhile, if the population is truly atomless, a single voter’s report changes no mass, so truthfulness becomes vacuous and range voting attains the benchmark. Keeping the \(1/N\) change preserves the strategic problem, but leaves a high-multiplicity family of finite profiles.

The continuous type-space variant also has an input problem: the paper permits arbitrary real-valued utility vectors, and truthfulness quantifies over every alternative report. A finite-support distribution does not make that uncountable report domain finite or specify a bit representation. Restricting utilities and reports to a rating grid can fix the encoding, but it narrows the model and still leaves the new global synthesis problem.

So the negative case is strong under ChoCo’s computational gate: neither Theorem 3 nor Theorem 4 anchors a continuous computational mirror of this paper. It is weaker as a claim that no valuable research could grow from the setting. A high-multiplicity study of truthful mechanism synthesis is plausible; the point is that it would be a new computational project, rather than a mirror of the paper’s named results.
