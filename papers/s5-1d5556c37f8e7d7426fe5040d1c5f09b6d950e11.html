The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of the Bertrand oligopoly result. The paper’s closest anchor is Proposition 4.4, proved in this paper. For its finite game with \(n\) firms, it says the projective core of the grand coalition is nonempty if and only if, for every \(s=h,\ldots,n-1\),
\[ \frac{d_{c,q}(s)}{s}\leq\frac{\pi^*}{n}, \qquad d_{c,q}(s)=\max_{0\leq p\leq c(n-s)}q(p)(p-c(s)), \]
where \(\pi^*\) is monopoly profit, \(h=(n+1)/2\) for odd \(n\), and \(h=n/2+1\) for even \(n\). In the paper’s model, \(c\) decreases with coalition size, \(q\) is nonincreasing, and the price constraint reflects the second-largest coalition’s cost. This is a precise characterization of when the grand coalition is stable against projective deviations.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a market with a very large cohort of near-identical small producers. Each firm has the same technology, cost schedule, market access, and ability to join a consortium; firms differ only in their consortium membership. The society is the unit interval with Lebesgue mass, all of one complete type. Mass \(x\) represents a fraction \(x\) of the firms. A measurable set of firms may deviate together, while the rest keep their original coalition, as in the paper’s projective reaction. The economics is recognizable: many similar firms coordinate, and consortium size affects production cost. The premise is strongest for markets with many small suppliers and weaker where firms’ individual identities or scale are essential.

One concrete follow-up is the Atomless Bertrand Projective-Core Problem. Its input is a rational piecewise-linear demand function \(q\) and a rational piecewise-linear, nonincreasing cost function \(c\) on coalition mass, with positive monopoly profit
\[ \pi^*=\max_{p\geq 0}q(p)(p-c(1)). \]
The question is whether there is a stable payoff density \(u\in L^1([0,1])\). A solution must satisfy \(\int_0^1u(a)\,da=\pi^*\) and, for every measurable proper coalition \(S\), must give its members at least the worth they can obtain by deviating. Writing \(x=\lambda(S)\), that worth is
\[ v(x)= \begin{cases} \displaystyle\max_{0\leq p\leq c(1-x)}q(p)(p-c(x)),&x>1/2,\\[4pt] 0,&x\leq 1/2. \end{cases} \]
Thus stability requires \(\int_Su(a)\,da\geq v(x)\) for every such \(S\). A solution is the density \(u\), not a coalition partition or an encoding of the problem.

By symmetry, the natural candidate is equal division, \(u(a)=\pi^*\). In this atomless setting, the existence question reduces to checking whether
\[ x\pi^*\geq\max_{0\leq p\leq c(1-x)}q(p)(p-c(x)) \quad\text{for every }x\in(1/2,1). \]
The threshold \(1/2\) is the continuum counterpart of the paper’s \(h\): only a uniquely largest deviating coalition can earn positive profit. I would expect this version to be tractable for the stated piecewise-linear input, since the remaining check is a finite collection of low-dimensional optimization problems. That is a conjectured direction for the new problem, not a complexity result in the paper. Natural next questions are whether the finite tests converge to this condition under a specified scaling of \(c\), and how the test changes when there are several firm types.

The important qualification is that Proposition 4.4 is not itself a named complexity result. The paper gives a necessary-and-sufficient stability condition, but no complexity classification, and it does not specify computational encodings for its general demand and cost functions. So under the requested anchor rule, this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor; the atomless problem is a research question suggested by its result, not a continuation of a hardness result whose hardness might dissolve. The mirror covers the Bertrand grand-coalition stability result only. Its weakest modeling step is treating consortium size as continuous mass: that is plausible for a large cohort of similar small firms, but it does change the paper’s finite-player market into an atomless one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Proposition 4.4 is a necessary-and-sufficient condition for a Bertrand game’s grand-coalition projective core to be nonempty. It does not state an algorithm or classify the complexity of an encoded problem. The same is true of the paper’s other propositions: they characterize stability, rather than ask computational questions. There is therefore no result here whose complexity might change when the population becomes continuous.

Proposition 4.4 is the proponent’s only anchor, and the proposed atomless version is coherent as a new question. But making it computational requires choices absent from the paper: for example, rational piecewise-linear encodings for \(q\) and \(c\), and a rule relating finite coalition costs \(c_n(s)\) to costs at normalized mass \(s/n\). One might set \(c_n(s)=c(s/n)\), but the paper does not specify that scaling, or how market demand should scale as the number of firms grows. Those choices are allowed co-modifications; they do not invalidate the mirror. They do mean that its computational problem is supplied by a new model and encoding, rather than inherited from the paper’s result.

A stronger version could study a cohort of identical small firms, or several firm types with different technologies, and ask about stable payoff densities under mass deviations. Identical firms are a plausible high-multiplicity regime: identity need not matter, and deviations larger than half the market do not disappear in the limit. So I would not reject the proposal on grounds of multiplicity, identity, or atomlessness. The difficulty is narrower: even its best version is a computational project built on this paper’s stability characterization, not a continuous mirror of a computational result in the paper.

That makes the negative case strong under ChoCo’s stated anchor standard, but weak as a universal claim that no worthwhile continuous question can be built from this model. A reader who accepts new algorithmic problems motivated by non-computational results could reasonably keep the atomless Bertrand question. The paper itself, however, supplies no computational anchor for such a mirror.
