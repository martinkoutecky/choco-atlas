The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying computational anchor in this paper, so I can’t make a proper anchor-based case for a continuous mirror. The closest results are characterizations: Theorem 3.5, proved here, says that a choice function is representable by the two-stage threshold model with known \(f\) exactly when \(xW_fy\) implies \(\neg[yH_fx]\). Theorem 3.12, also proved here, gives the corresponding condition when \(g\) is known. These are axiomatic results about one agent’s choice function; neither asserts a complexity classification or presents a computational formulation that the paper uses to solve the underlying problem. Theorem 2.5 is a characterization cited from earlier work, and Proposition 2.6 says every single-valued choice function has a representation. Checking the stated conditions may be computationally straightforward, but the paper does not make or analyze that complexity claim.

A possible follow-on would study a large market with many consumers choosing from the same finite menus. A type would be a consumer’s complete TST profile, including any known structural variable; its mass would be that type’s population share. Given observed choice shares \(q_A(x)\) for each menu \(A\) and alternative \(x\), one could ask whether a distribution over types reproduces all those shares while every type’s choices satisfy the relevant TST restrictions. The solution would be a feasible distribution over types. This is concrete, and a large population with relatively few profile types is conceivable.

But that question replaces the paper’s individual choice data with population-level mixtures. The paper has no population, mass, or support-size parameter; its real-valued structural variables do not make the society continuous. The proposed extension is therefore not a convincing mirror of a named result. It covers none of the paper’s results as a continuous analogue, and there is no anchor-linked question here to classify as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard. The weak point is the identity of the problem itself: the authors could reasonably regard the population version as a new mixture-rationalizability question, rather than their partial-knowledge question continuized.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive problem is that this paper gives no computational result to continuize. Theorem 3.5 characterizes compatibility with known \(f\); Theorem 3.12 does so with known \(g\). Both are statements about one chooser’s complete choice function across menus. They specify when suitable unobserved variables exist, but pose no algorithmic problem, complexity classification, or optimization task. With explicit choice data, their conditions reduce to checking relations for cycles or consistency. Turning those checks into algorithms would be useful implementation work, but it would not supply the missing computational question the ChoCo programme is meant to chart.

The proposed market of consumers is plausible, but it changes the object being studied. The relations in those theorems depend on choices by the same person across multiple menus. Menu-by-menu population shares erase which choices belong together. In the unrestricted single-valued model, Proposition 2.6 makes the loss especially stark: every deterministic choice function is TST-representable. Any set of aggregate menu shares can therefore be coupled into a distribution over deterministic choice functions, each allowed by the model. Those aggregate observations cannot test the paper’s unrestricted TST claim. Observing persistent individuals or their full choice patterns restores the cross-menu links, but then the evidence is individual choice data again.

One can make a richer population model by recording each person’s known structural variable, such as \(g\), and asking whether the observed choice shares can be generated by a distribution over compatible individual profiles. That could be a real mixture-rationalizability problem. It still does not mirror Theorem 3.5 or 3.12: its central object is a distribution matching aggregate observations, and its constraints depend on what profile information and cross-menu data are supplied. The paper neither formulates that problem nor offers a result whose computational content it continues. Adding mass-transfer costs, shared resources, or population interventions might make an optimization problem, but those additions would be the source of the computational question, not a continuization of a result here.

The same issue applies to nearby results such as Theorem 3.9 and Theorem 3.18: they refine the individual characterization as more variables become known. Cloning the chooser adds mass without adding a new decision problem or a population-level constraint. Heterogeneous consumers with linked profiles could motivate a worthwhile follow-on, but the case for it would rest on a newly specified economic model and its aggregate data, rather than on a computational anchor in this paper.

So the negative case is strongest on scope, not on impossibility: a continuous consumer-population extension is conceivable, but no named result here yields a worthwhile computational mirror in the programme’s sense. The proposed aggregate-share version either loses the cross-menu structure that gives these theorems content or must add a new latent-population problem to recover it.
