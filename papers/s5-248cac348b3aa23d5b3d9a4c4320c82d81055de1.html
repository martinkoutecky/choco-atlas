The continuous mirror question

Given \(d\) weighted thresholds, \(\tau\) voter types with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), weight vectors \(w_t\), and quotas \(q_r\), decide whether every pair of distinct types is comparable under the following relation: \(a\succeq_\infty b\) if, for every \(0<\delta\le\min(\mu_a,\mu_b)\) and every feasible background \(x\) with \(x_a,x_b\le\mu_{a,b}-\delta\), victory after adding \(\delta\) of type \(b\) implies victory after adding \(\delta\) of type \(a\). Here \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\), and \(x\) wins exactly when \(\sum_t w_{rt}x_t\ge q_r\) for every threshold \(r\).

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over voter types, each type carrying a vector of weights across \(d\) thresholds, defines a binary threshold game on coalition masses \(x\). For each ordered type pair, a witness to failure consists of a feasible background \(x\) and mass slice \(\delta>0\) such that \(x+\delta e_b\) wins and \(x+\delta e_a\) loses; the decision asks whether any pair is incomparable. There is no optimization objective.

The objection that survived

The relation compares positive-mass blocs rather than individual voters, so its answer need not determine whether individual desirability remains ordered at mass scale \(1/N\).

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 5.3’s linearity decision for multiple weighted voting games and leaves the paper’s power-index, representation, and other desirability results untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the LP witness tests be assembled into a polynomial-time algorithm that also outputs the complete type desirability ordering?
  • How does type-level linearity relate to individual-player linearity in finite high-multiplicity instances as their coalition-mass lattices become dense?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead mirror is a continuous version of Proposition 5.3. The paper proves this result itself, by reduction from PARTITION: it is NP-hard to decide whether a multiple weighted voting game is linear, meaning whether every pair of players is comparable under the desirability relation. In the paper’s definition, \(a\) is at least as desirable as \(b\) if replacing \(b\) by \(a\) preserves victory for every coalition containing neither. The proposed mirror keeps that question and lifts its coalitions from sets of players to masses of voter types.

Consider a large federation whose voting rule requires a proposal to meet each of \(d\) separate weighted thresholds. Many members share the same voting rights across all \(d\) tests. A type \(t\) is the complete vector of those weights; \(\mu_t>0\) is its fraction of the membership, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). The regime is one with many members but relatively few such vectors: \(N\gg\tau\), where \(\tau\) is the number of types. A coalition is described by \(x_t\), the fraction of the whole membership in type \(t\) that joins, with \(0\le x_t\le\mu_t\). It wins when \(V(x)=1\), where \(V(x)=1\) exactly when \(\sum_t w_{rt}x_t\ge q_r\) for every threshold \(r\). The weights \(w_{rt}\) are contributions per unit mass, and \(q_r\) is the quota for test \(r\).

Call this problem Fractional-MWVG Linearity. For distinct types \(a,b\), define \(a\succeq_\infty b\) if, for every \(0<\delta\le\min(\mu_a,\mu_b)\) and every background coalition \(x\) satisfying \(x_a\le\mu_a-\delta\) and \(x_b\le\mu_b-\delta\), victory after adding a mass \(\delta\) of type \(b\) implies victory after adding the same mass of type \(a\): \(V(x+\delta e_b)=1\Rightarrow V(x+\delta e_a)=1\). The question is whether every pair of types is comparable in at least one direction. A solution is YES or NO; a NO answer can provide an incomparable pair and mass backgrounds witnessing both failures. The rule’s outcome remains binary. What becomes continuous is the population and the coalition composition.

The mirror seems plausible to me because desirability is already a comparison of equal additions against every possible background coalition. Here the “player” being substituted is an equal-mass slice of a homogeneous type, and backgrounds range over fractional coalition profiles. That is recognizably the same structural question about influence in a coalitional voting game. In a finite election with \(N_t\) members of type \(t\), the coalition masses \(x_t=k_t/N\) recover its lattice of discrete coalitions; this version fills the gaps between those lattice points.

I expect this mirror to be Class A, tractable by linear programming. To find a witness that \(a\not\succeq_\infty b\), require all \(d\) tests to pass for \(x+\delta e_b\), and choose one test that fails for \(x+\delta e_a\). For each possible failed test, these conditions and the bounds on \(x,\delta\) form a rational linear feasibility problem, with a strict inequality for the failed quota. Such feasibility can be checked in polynomial time, including by maximizing a slack variable to handle strictness. Repeating this for ordered type pairs and candidate failed tests takes polynomially many LPs in \(\tau,d\), and the input bit length. Further questions include outputting the full desirability classes and finding a quantitative margin for an incomparable pair.

The mirror covers Proposition 5.3, not the paper’s results on power indices or compact representations. Its weakest point is that a mass-slice comparison is not individual-player desirability or Banzhaf power, and the paper’s PARTITION reduction can use a distinct weight vector for each player. The continuous question therefore does not claim to preserve the hard instances’ answers. It asks whether the same desirability question becomes tractable when coalitions may contain fractional mass of each type. That is precisely why this is a useful mirror to test: the hard reduction relies on discrete coalition choices, while the continuous witness search is linear feasibility.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 5.3 is the only anchor raised, and it is a genuine computational result. A large federation with many members sharing a small number of voting-rights vectors is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, so the negative case cannot dismiss that scenario.

The strongest objection is that the proposition orders individual voters: it asks whether replacing one named voter by another preserves victory in every background coalition. In an atomless population, adding one individual changes no mass, so that original comparison makes all individuals equally desirable. The proponent avoids this collapse by comparing equal positive masses \(\delta\). That gives a coherent question about substituting blocs, but it changes the object: a fixed \(\delta\) measures the effect of replacing a group, while a one-person change has mass \(1/N\) and vanishes as \(N\) grows. Preserving that one-person effect requires retaining the finite population scale and its quota-boundary details.

This objection also reaches the better version of the mirror: call the question *block desirability* and quantify over every feasible \(\delta\). That is a meaningful extension, but the paper’s individual-desirability result does not itself motivate why block desirability is the right target. The mirror now studies robustness to replacing a positive share of a type, rather than whether individual influence is totally ordered. The LP may solve that new problem cleanly; its tractability does not resolve the mismatch.

That is the strongest case against, but it does not establish that no worthwhile mirror exists. The federation scenario makes the block question plausible, and the paper’s desirability relation gives it a natural starting point. I cannot honestly defeat this anchor universally: the negative case is weak, and the mirror survives if the authors accept type-block influence as their problem.
