What the mirror covers

The proposed bloc question concerns sure weak-dominance manipulation under Borda and Baldwin; the paper’s remaining results concern individual manipulation, other method sets and dominance notions, and empirical profile counts.

The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered result establishing a problem as polynomial-time solvable, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. It does contain named structural results and findings from computer searches, but none is a computational-complexity result of the kind your anchor rule requires.

The nearest result is Theorem 3.6, proved in the paper: for three candidates and every voter count \(m\geq4\), each of \(\{\mathrm{Borda},\mathrm{Baldwin}\}\) and \(\{\mathrm{Borda},\mathrm{StrictNanson}\}\) eliminates sure weak-dominance manipulation. That means the full pair is not susceptible, while each singleton subset is susceptible. Theorem 3.7, also proved there, says the same for \(\{\mathrm{WeakNanson},\mathrm{Baldwin}\}\) and \(\{\mathrm{WeakNanson},\mathrm{StrictNanson}\}\). These are substantive anchors for a follow-up about manipulation, but they do not meet the required complexity-result test.

The most plausible positive-side follow-up I can offer is a mass-block version of Theorem 3.6. Consider a large membership organization voting on three candidates, with many members but only a few shared ranking types. A type is a complete ranking; its mass is its fraction of the membership. The chair will use either Borda or Baldwin, fixed before the ballots are cast but unknown to voters. Suppose a coordinated bloc all has true ranking \(t\). For an input society \(\mu\), type \(t\), and budget \(\beta>0\), ask whether there are \(q\in\mathbb{Q}\) and a distribution \(\rho\) over rankings other than \(t\), with \(0<q\leq\min(\beta,\mu_t)\), such that moving mass \(q\) from \(t\) according to \(\rho\) makes both methods’ winner sets strictly better under weak dominance for \(t\). The solution is \(q,\rho\), or a no-instance. Borda scores are mass-weighted; Baldwin repeatedly eliminates candidates with the lowest mass-weighted Borda score, using the paper’s convention when all remaining candidates tie.

This has a plausible high-multiplicity regime: for example, a large organization with many members and a small number of stable preference blocs. The output remains a winner set, while the society becomes the distribution over types. For the fixed three-candidate case, I would expect this budgeted decision problem to be tractable: there are only six ranking types, and its possible score comparisons can be handled by finitely many linear-feasibility cases. Natural next questions are the minimum bloc mass needed, whether the result changes for the other pairs in Theorems 3.6 and 3.7, and what happens when the candidate count varies.

The weak point is central: the paper studies one voter changing one ballot, whereas this mirror gives a positive-mass bloc a coordinated deviation. A single voter has zero mass in a nonatomic society, so the bloc is what keeps manipulation consequential—and that makes this a coalition extension, not a direct continuization of the theorem. The paper’s theorems motivate the question, but they do not anchor a claim about its complexity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the paper offers no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 3.6 and Theorem 3.7 prove that certain pairs of methods eliminate sure weak-dominance manipulation for three-candidate profiles; they do not establish an algorithmic complexity result. The computer searches and profile percentages are evidence about manipulability, not analyses of running time or complexity. By ChoCo’s anchor criterion, neither theorem is a computational anchor.

A direct continuous version also loses the paper’s strategic actor. A single voter has zero mass in a nonatomic society, so changing that voter’s ranking leaves the aggregate scores unchanged. Away from score and elimination ties, the winner sets are locally constant, and unilateral manipulation disappears. At a tie boundary, the outcome may change discontinuously, but that gives a boundary case, not a general population-scale analogue.

The proposed mass-block question repairs that problem, but it changes the question: \(q\) and \(\rho\) describe a coordinated coalition’s deviation, not one voter’s unilateral choice. One could define the minimum mass of a type needed to make both methods yield better outcomes. That is a plausible computational social-choice problem, especially for a large organization with a coordinated bloc. But Theorems 3.6 and 3.7 say nothing about that threshold. Making it more ambitious by varying the candidate count or adding report costs would produce a new coalition-manipulation project, rather than a continuization of the paper’s results.

This is a strong case against a direct mirror, but it does not establish the universal claim. The bloc-threshold question may be worthwhile on its own; the negative case is weakest precisely where the proposed mirror replaces individual agency with a plausible collective one.
