The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive intuition is that the paper’s market could have a sensible large-cohort regime: many firms and applicants, with agents grouped by side, quality, and a small number of fit classes. Mass would represent each group’s share of the market; early and late offers and acceptances would become mass flows. A continuous game could ask which equilibrium flows produce qualitywise-inefficient early matches. That is a plausible population analogue of the model.

But this paper has no named computational result to anchor the requested ChoCo case. The closest is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: a qualitywise-inefficient early matching can occur in a subgame-perfect equilibrium only in the comparable-demand-and-supply case \(n_A>n_F>n_A^h\). That is an equilibrium characterization, not a claim that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classified. Lemma 1 and Propositions 1–4 likewise characterize equilibria or comparative statics; none supplies a computational anchor.

One could formulate a new problem about deciding whether a continuous mass market admits an inefficient early-matching equilibrium. But that would be a new analytic equilibrium question, not a continuous mirror of a numbered computational result in this paper. So the market setting offers a plausible population model, while the paper itself cannot support an anchor-compliant positive case.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only concrete anchor is Theorem 1, and its best continuous mirror is easy to state: replace firm and applicant counts by population shares, let early contracts be mass flows, and ask whether a subgame-perfect equilibrium can produce qualitywise-inefficient early matching. The threshold \(n_A>n_F>n_A^h\) becomes the corresponding condition on shares. That is a coherent population model; I cannot honestly argue that mass has no meaning here or that the continuum necessarily degenerates.

But it is an equilibrium-characterization question, not a computational result. Theorem 1, Lemma 1, and Propositions 1–4 describe equilibria and comparative statics; the paper poses no decision, optimization, or algorithmic problem whose complexity a continuous mirror could chart. Asking how to compute or decide properties of equilibria in the mass game would create a new computational project. It would not mirror a computational result in this paper. The paper’s own references even include a continuum-agent model of unraveling, which reinforces that the natural continuous route belongs to analytic matching theory.

So the strongest case against is specifically a case against this paper as a ChoCo contribution: its natural continuous question leaves the programme’s computational scope. That does not establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The mass-market reinterpretation is plausible, and could be worthwhile to equilibrium theory; the honest negative case is that it supplies no computational anchor for this programme.
