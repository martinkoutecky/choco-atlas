What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror is motivated by Proposition 5’s optimal weakly collusion-proof mechanism in the weak-correlation regime; it does not cover the paper’s other propositions or its results on strong ratifiability.

The case FOR (proponent)

The positive case is narrow. This paper has no numbered result asserting a complexity class, and it gives no LP, MILP, or other computational formulation that it solves. Strictly under the anchor rule, it has no qualifying computational result. The best arguable lead is Proposition 5, proved in the paper: it solves a named optimization problem by characterizing the optimal mechanism in the weak-correlation regime. That is an analytic optimization result, not a complexity theorem, so the reader may reasonably reject it as an anchor.

Proposition 5 says that the symmetric optimal weakly collusion-proof mechanism has a strictly decreasing output schedule, \(\bar x_c>\hat x_c>x_c\). It has no distortion at the top, \(\bar x_c=\bar x^*\), and downward distortions in the other states relative to the no-collusion optimum. The high-valuation agent’s Bayesian incentive constraint always binds; when correlation is positive, both downward coalition incentive constraints bind strictly; the low-valuation agent’s participation constraint binds; all remaining constraints are strictly satisfied; and the high-valuation agent receives positive informational rent. The proposition gives first-order conditions for the two lower output levels. These are results about the optimal mechanism, not about the complexity of computing it. It is proved there, rather than cited.

A plausible continuous mirror would be Continuous Dyadic Collusion-Proof Public-Good Design. Imagine a continuum of local public-good projects, each administered to a pair of residents, such as repeated neighborhood service projects. Each pair has private valuations from a finite set \(T\), and the pair’s types have a specified joint distribution \(\mu\). The mass \(\mu_{tu}\) is the fraction of project-pairs in which the two residents have types \(t,u\); \(\tau\) is the number of positive-mass pair types. A type records the resident’s valuation, while the joint distribution supplies the correlation and conditional beliefs. There may be very many residents and projects, but few distinct types.

The instance also specifies a rational cost-of-public-funds parameter \(\lambda\), a convex public-good cost \(c\), and an outside option for each resident. The decision is a common direct mechanism \(G\): for every pair of reports, it sets a nonnegative project level \(x\) and taxes \(z_1,z_2\). Each pair can also use a side mechanism that changes the reports sent to the principal and imposes balanced side transfers, \(y_1+y_2=0\). The question is to find a welfare-maximizing \(G\) that is individually Bayesian incentive compatible and individually rational, and weakly collusion-proof: the null side contract is optimal for the coalition intermediary and is unanimously ratified under the paper’s passive-belief continuation equilibrium. A solution is the mechanism itself, with its allocation and tax schedule. The objective is the population-weighted expected welfare, with each pair’s contribution
\[ (v_t+v_u)x-(1+\lambda)c(x)+\lambda(z_1+z_2). \]

This is meant to preserve the paper’s central problem—designing a public-good mechanism when privately informed agents can collude—while making the population of repeated local projects continuous. The discrete bridge is a finite collection of such project-pairs whose type counts approximate \(\mu\). The relevant regime is many pairs over a small support of valuation types, not a claim that the paper’s two-agent example already has a large electorate.

I would expect this mirror to be tractable for a finite type set and a rational piecewise-linear convex cost: the welfare objective is concave in the allocation variables, while individual incentive and participation conditions are linear. The key unresolved computational step is to give an efficient finite characterization of the null side contract’s optimality as the type support grows. Proposition 5 makes the small-type optimization credible, but it does not prove that generalization or its running time. Further questions include whether the continuous optimum gives an additive approximation for finitely many repeated pairs, how complexity depends on \(\tau\) and input bit length, and whether the two-agent coalition condition remains efficiently checkable for larger coalitions.

The weakest point is the replication story. The paper has exactly two agents, and the proposed continuum is a mass of dyadic projects; if the authors regard that as a routine replication rather than a meaningful population-level version of their problem, the mirror loses force. It also does not turn Proposition 5 into a complexity result. So this is a defensible but modest positive case: an analytic anchor and a specific computational question motivated by it, with the formal-anchor limitation stated plainly.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The case against is strongest at the anchor gate: this paper contains no result about the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 5 characterizes an optimal mechanism in a fixed two-agent, two-valuation model. It gives first-order conditions for three output levels and identifies which incentive constraints bind; it gives no algorithm, complexity bound, or hardness result. Recasting “find the optimal mechanism” as an optimization problem does not supply the missing computational result. In the stated model there are only four joint states and a few allocation variables, and the paper solves that problem analytically.

The proposed continuum of local public-good projects does not repair this gap. It makes the *pairs of agents* the units whose masses are distributed across joint types. If each pair gets its own project and mechanism, copies of the paper’s problem are independent: repeating them scales welfare but adds no population-level computational structure. If all pairs use one common mechanism, their masses weight expected welfare, much like the prior over the four joint states in the original model. That prior is not itself a continuous population, and treating it as one would confuse uncertainty about two agents’ types with multiplicity of agents in a society.

A more ambitious version could put everyone under one shared public-good decision and allow coalitions to form across the population. That would create genuine interactions between groups, but it would also require specifying which coalitions may deviate, how large they must be to matter, and how side contracts and Bayesian incentives work for measurable groups. In an ordinary nonatomic model, a single agent or finite coalition has no effect on aggregate reports; preserving the paper’s collusion stakes therefore requires a new model of positive-mass coalitions. This may be a worthwhile research direction, but it is not a continuization of Proposition 5’s result: it replaces the paper’s two-agent coalition structure and asks for new results.

There is an honest limit to this negative case. The authors explicitly say their methods could extend beyond two agents, so a population-wide public-good model with collusion is not obviously alien to their interests. I cannot establish that no such descendant would be worthwhile. But the dyadic-project mirror offered here either reduces to repeated applications of the original problem or needs a substantially new coalition model; Proposition 5 provides no computational result whose continuous mirror it could be.
