The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t find a named computational result to anchor a positive case. Theorem 1, proved in this paper, is the closest candidate: under anonymity, strategy-proofness, responsiveness, and positive participation costs, both versions of the game have a unique refined equilibrium with exactly one participant, whose peak is either the highest or the lowest. That is a structural equilibrium result, not a claim that a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise classified. Proposition 1 and the appendix lemmas are also equilibrium results, not complexity results.

A possible continuous model would describe a population by a distribution \(\mu\) over peak-and-cost types and ask which mass of each type participates. But the paper’s result turns on a single voter being pivotal. In an atomless population, one voter has zero mass and cannot move the outcome, so positive participation costs lead to abstention; allowing whole types to coordinate would change the game being studied. The paper also assumes distinct peaks and explicitly targets settings with more alternatives than voters, which is a poor fit for a high-multiplicity regime.

So I can’t give an honest continuous problem per eligible anchor. The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 is an equilibrium theorem; Proposition 1 and the appendix lemmas are also equilibrium results. None asks for an algorithm or classifies the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 1 is the closest anchor, but that alone does not supply the computational question the programme is meant to study.

The best attempt would take a distribution over complete voter types—peaks, costs, and any other relevant attributes—and ask for an equilibrium of the mass-participation game. In a standard atomless model, though, one voter has zero mass and cannot change the outcome. Since participation still has a strictly positive cost, abstaining is better for that voter; all abstain is the degenerate equilibrium. The finite theorem’s central mechanism, one voter being pivotal, has vanished. A nontrivial limit would need to scale costs or payoffs with population size, or otherwise restore pivotality, changing the model’s assumptions.

One could instead let each positive-mass type cohort coordinate its members’ participation. That could yield an interesting game among cohorts, but it replaces individual participation decisions with collective ones and abandons the paper’s generic distinct-peaks setup. Likewise, minimizing the mass needed to move the outcome would be a new collective intervention problem, not a continuous version of Theorem 1’s equilibrium claim. Those may be worthwhile questions in their own right; the paper does not give a computational anchor for them.

This is a strong negative case for treating this paper as a continuous computational mirror, though not a proof that no related continuum participation model could ever be useful. The case is strongest at the boundary of the programme: without a computational result, and with the pivotal voter disappearing in the natural atomless model, the proposed mirror lacks both the target and the mechanism that made the paper’s main result distinctive.
