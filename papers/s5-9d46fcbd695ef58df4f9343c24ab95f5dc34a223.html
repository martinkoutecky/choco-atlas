Why no mirror

The ML–RaDiUS rule can be lifted coherently to a high-multiplicity society \(\mu\) over rankings, with the metric remaining universally quantified. However, the sole anchor, Theorem 5, states only a \(2.753\) distortion bound for a fixed rule. Under the computational-result gate, distortion bounds do not satisfy bit (a), so the continuous formulation cannot yield green.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror would cover the main \(2.753\) ML–RaDiUS rule-design theorem and its mass-based implementation, while leaving the \(2\sqrt{2}\) ML–RCB theorem, lower-bound examples, and auxiliary rederivations untouched.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the fixed ML–RaDiUS sampler and its Nash-equilibrium computation be represented exactly in time \(\operatorname{poly}(m,\tau,L)\)?
  • What is the optimal distortion of a robust lottery computed from \(\mu\), rather than the paper’s fixed mixture?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a direct high-multiplicity mirror of the paper’s randomized metric-distortion problem. It is not a new outcome-space relaxation: candidates remain discrete, and the only continuously represented object is the electorate.

Take a public-facility or civic-project setting with \(m\) candidate sites and a very large population. A type \(t\in T\) is a complete ranking of the candidates, so \(T=\mathcal L(C)\) and \(|T|=m!\). The society is an explicit rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of residents with ranking \(t\). The intended regime has \(n\) extremely large while \(K=|\operatorname{supp}(\mu)|\) is fixed or at least \(K\ll n\): for example, many residents belong to a small number of recurring neighbourhood, commuting, or ideological cells.

For \(i,j\in C\), define \(s^\mu_{i\succ j}=\sum_{t:i\succ_t j}\mu_t\), and similarly define \(s^\mu_{I\succ j}\) and the plurality mass \(\operatorname{plu}_\mu(i)\). A metric realization consists of a probability space of voters, candidate and voter locations in a common metric, and a measurable ranking map whose pushforward distribution is \(\mu\). Its social cost is \(\operatorname{SC}_\mu(c)=\int d(c,v)\,\dif\nu(v)\).

The paper’s randomized voting rule becomes a map \(F:\mu\mapsto p^\mu\in\Delta(C)\). The continuous problem is:

Continuous Metric-Distortion Lottery Design at \(2.753\). Given \(C\) and \(\mu\), compute a lottery \(p^\mu\) over candidates, using only \(\mu\), such that for every metric realization consistent with \(\mu\),

\[ \frac{\sum_{c\in C}p^\mu_c\,\operatorname{SC}_\mu(c)} {\min_{c\in C}\operatorname{SC}_\mu(c)} \leq 2.753. \]

A solution is the sampling procedure implementing \(p^\mu\), together with the universal guarantee above.

My lead anchor is Theorem 5 of the paper, proved there rather than cited. Its full content is that, with suitable constants \(p,B\), and density \(\rho\), the rule which runs Maximal Lotteries with probability \(p\), and otherwise samples \(\beta\in(1/2,B)\) according to \(\rho\) and runs \(\beta\)-RaDiUS, has metric distortion at most \(2.753\) on every election and every consistent metric. The proof chooses \(B\approx0.876353\), \(p=\left(1+\int_{1/2}^{B}(1-\beta^2)^{-1}\dif\beta\right)^{-1}\), and \(\rho(\beta)=p/((1-p)(1-\beta^2))\). This is the strongest anchor because it is already an explicit approximation rule, not merely an existence statement. The result is proved in the paper’s own framework; see the [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17838).

The continuous rule is literal. Maximal Lotteries is computed from the matrix \(s^\mu_{i\succ j}\) as a Nash equilibrium of the Condorcet game. For RaDiUS, construct the weighted covering relation using \(s^\mu_{i\succ j}\geq\beta\), form the weighted uncovered set \(U_\beta\), sample a ranking type \(t\) according to \(\mu\), and choose its favourite candidate in \(U_\beta\). Thus the paper’s “uniformly random voter” becomes a draw from the mass distribution \(\mu\). The output remains a lottery over indivisible candidates; only the voter population has been continuized.

The authors should recognise this as their problem. Their analysis already writes every relevant quantity as a fraction or probability of voters, and the proofs use no voter identities or one-voter operations. The finite averages become integrals, while the pairwise statistics and top-choice masses become sums against \(\mu\). The biased-metric argument is pointwise in the underlying metric and therefore survives unchanged under a continuum of voters. This is a genuine high-multiplicity reformulation, not merely saying that the finite theorem “already uses fractions.”

I would expect this mirror to be Class A for the explicit-type representation. Computing all \(s^\mu_{i\succ j}\) and top-choice masses is polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the Maximal Lotteries component is obtained from a polynomial-size zero-sum-game LP. RaDiUS requires only pairwise comparisons, the covering relation, and one pass over the type table. The natural complexity question is therefore polynomial-time computation in \((m,\tau,L)\), with the paper’s \(2.753\) guarantee inherited analytically.

Further questions are worthwhile: can the optimal continuous lottery be found by an LP or separation oracle rather than by the paper’s hand-designed mixture? Do the biased metrics give a complete finite constraint system for optimal continuous rule design? And does the optimum over irrational \(\mu\) equal the supremum over rational high-multiplicity profiles?

The weakest point is that the paper does not itself state an NP-hardness, FPT, or polynomial-time theorem for a continuous population. Its named result is an approximation guarantee for a voting rule. Also, the observable type is only the ordinal ranking, while members of one ranking type may have different hidden cardinal distances in a metric realization. I would therefore present this as a strong Class A mirror of the paper’s robust rule-design problem, not as a hardness-dissolution result. The hidden metric is part of the adversarial evaluation model, exactly as in the paper, rather than an additional input attribute of the voter type.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed anchor is not a computational result of the kind the programme is meant to continuize. The paper’s \(2.753\) theorem is a distortion guarantee for a fixed, explicitly specified voting rule. It does not define a decision, optimization, hardness, or approximation problem. Computing its lottery from \(\mu\) is immediate: replace voter fractions by \(\mu\)-weighted sums, solve the same Maximal Lotteries game, construct the same covering relation, and sample a ranking type. This is an implementation of the existing rule, not a new computational question about a continuous society. That is a category objection, not the claim that the continuous guarantee happens to have the same numerical answer.

There is also a more fundamental problem with the proposed type space. A ranking is not a complete type for this paper’s objective. The rule observes only rankings, but the quantity being optimized is \(\mathrm{SC}(c)\), which depends on cardinal distances in the latent metric. Two voters with the same ranking \(a\succ b\) can have completely different distance vectors to \(a\) and \(b\), and those differences affect both the social costs and which candidate is optimal. Thus \(\mu\) over rankings describes the distribution of reports, not the population relevant to the objective. Saying that the metric is merely an adversarial evaluation model does not repair this: it leaves an unspecified cardinal distribution conditional on each ranking type.

The strongest repair would be to make a type include a complete metric realization—say, a ranking together with its distance vector, under a fixed candidate geometry—and take a distribution over those types. But that is no longer the proposed \(m!\)-type high-multiplicity model. The type space is generally continuous and instance-dependent, metric feasibility couples all types globally, and giving the rule those distances changes the paper’s ordinal-information model. Hiding the distances restores the paper’s information model but returns to the incompleteness above. Restricting attention to finitely many repeated metric cells is possible, but it introduces a new structured metric-social-choice problem rather than mirroring the paper’s named theorem.

One could still study the optimal robust lottery over such metric-cell populations. That may be interesting, but it is a new rule-design problem, not a continuous version of the paper’s \(2.753\) result. I cannot honestly claim that every such repaired model would be worthless: repeated civic or facility-location cells are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The negative case therefore defeats the anchor as stated—its type space is not complete and its “algorithm” adds no computational problem—but it does not establish the universal claim that no carefully enriched metric-population formulation could be worthwhile.
