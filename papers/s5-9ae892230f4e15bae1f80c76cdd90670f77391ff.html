What the mirror covers

The proposed application uses Proposition 4’s \(A\)-admissibility test and leaves the paper’s other decision criteria and results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive mirror is Population A-Admissible Policy Selection, anchored on Proposition 4. The proposition is proved in this paper. It gives an LP test for whether an act \(X_z\) is \(A\)-admissible in a finite decision system: there must exist a utility representation \(u\in U_A\) such that, for every compatible probability measure \(\pi\in M\) and every available act \(Y\), \(X_z\) has expected utility at least that of \(Y\). The LP encodes the preference system’s ordinal and cardinal constraints, and adds those expected-utility comparisons at every extreme point of the polyhedral credal set. The proposition proves that \(X_z\) is \(A\)-admissible exactly when the LP’s optimal value is strictly positive. The paper does not state a complexity-class label, but with finite rational data and the credal set’s extreme points explicitly listed, this is a polynomial-time test by standard linear programming.

Here is a continuous-population problem that uses that test. A large public is evaluating the same finite set \(G\) of policy acts \(X:S\to A\), under a common finite state space \(S\) and outcome set \(A\). Each voter type \(t\) consists of a complete partial preference system \(\mathcal A_t=[A,R_{1,t},R_{2,t}]\) and a credal set \(M_t\). These specify everything relevant to how members of that type assess the acts. The input gives the \(\tau\) types present and their rational population masses \(\mu_t\), with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For an act \(X\), set \(I_t(X)=1\) if type \(t\) regards \(X\) as \(A\)-admissible against all acts in \(G\), and \(I_t(X)=0\) otherwise. The decision is to choose
\[ X^\star\in\arg\max_{X\in G}\sum_t\mu_t I_t(X). \]
The solution is a maximizing act and its supporting population mass; ties return all maximizing acts. Each type’s admissibility is precisely the paper’s underlying problem, not a continuous encoding standing in for it.

A plausible regime is a large stakeholder population assessing a shared set of public plans under a small set of economic or environmental scenarios. A structured elicitation process could produce many repeated complete preference-and-belief profiles: for instance, hundreds of thousands of respondents but only hundreds of distinct types. Mass then means a fraction of the population, not an individually targeted vote. This is a regime claim, not an assumption that all public consultation populations have few types; only exact matches on all relevant information belong to the same type.

The mirror is tractable under Proposition 4’s explicit input regime. For each pair \((t,X)\), solve its LP; \(X\) receives type \(t\)’s mass exactly when the optimum is positive. Then sum the rational masses and compare the totals across the explicitly listed acts. This takes at most \(\tau|G|\) LPs, each polynomial in the explicit descriptions of the preference relations, acts, and credal-set vertices. Thus the expected class is tractable. A further computational question is whether the extreme points can be avoided when \(M_t\) is given by inequalities or a separation oracle; the proposition’s vertex-list formulation does not by itself settle that.

This case covers Proposition 4 and its \(A\)-admissibility criterion, not the paper’s other criteria or its broader theory. The paper supplies no named hardness result to anchor a “hardness dissolves in the continuum” claim. The positive case is instead that a specific named admissibility computation has a natural population-level use, where type masses determine which policy wins. Further questions include whether a mass-maximizing endorsement rule is the right collective rule, what happens under an endorsement quota, and how discrete populations map to or approximate the rational-mass version.

The weakest point is that the paper studies one decision maker, not a society or an aggregation rule. The authors would recognize the type-level computation as exactly their \(A\)-admissibility problem; they might regard selecting the act with greatest admissible population mass as a new social-choice wrapper rather than their own problem. The case therefore stands or falls on whether that group application is an acceptable continuous analogue. It is deliberately limited to this one result rather than claiming the whole paper has a continuous mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Proposition 4 is the right anchor, and its computation is clear: for one decision system, test whether an act is \(A\)-admissible by solving the stated LP. The proposed population model can batch that test across types and sum their masses. Its weak point is the step that makes this a social-choice problem: the paper does not define a population of decision makers or a rule that selects the act with the most type mass finding it admissible.

That rule also gives “admissible” a new role. In the paper, \(A\)-admissibility means there exists a compatible utility representation under which the act maximizes expected utility for every compatible probability measure. It does not mean the decision maker actually chooses or endorses the act. Since a type can find several acts admissible, counting admissibility as a vote is an additional collective-choice convention. It might be defensible, but the paper neither motivates nor studies it. The LP then serves as a subroutine for a new aggregation problem; it does not itself provide the continuous mirror.

A more faithful population version would still face that gap. One could ask which act is admissible for the greatest mass, meets a support threshold, or belongs to the admissible sets of some specified fraction of the population. Each is well-defined, and each can use Proposition 4 type by type. But choosing among them requires adding a social objective the paper leaves open. Once that choice is made, the population contributes a weighted sum of independent LP outcomes; it does not change the admissibility question or create a new optimization structure in the paper’s model.

The regime itself is plausible: repeated decision makers could share the same complete preference system and credal set. Exact matching may be demanding, but that is not a decisive objection, and high multiplicity would be sensible if those profiles recur. So the negative case is narrow. It does not show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists; it shows that this anchor becomes a mirror only after adding an externally chosen social-choice rule. If the authors would recognize that rule as a natural extension of their decision framework, the case against loses its main force.
