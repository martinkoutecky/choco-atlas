For example, Theorem 1, proved here, says that with two slots, a unique efficient allocation, arbitrary values and click-through rates, and a specified tie-breaking rule, an efficient pure-strategy equilibrium without overbidding exists. Theorem 2, also proved here, characterizes when a two-slot, three-bidder efficient, globally envy-free equilibrium exists. Theorem 4 proves that certain nonseparable click-through rates admit values for which the VCG result cannot be supported. These are substantive results, but none analyzes the complexity of deciding or computing the stated outcomes. So the set of eligible anchors is empty, and there is no continuous problem to state *per anchor* without stretching your criteria. The obvious population mirror—grouping many advertisers into types \((v,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\) with masses \(\mu_t\)—would also require scaling slot capacity across repeated auctions. That changes the paper’s fixed-slot auction setting, and its equilibrium results do not establish what happens in that aggregate model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent raised no qualifying anchor: Theorems 1, 2, and 4 are equilibrium existence, characterization, and impossibility results, not stated complexity results. That is the strongest case against a mirror under ChoCo’s stated remit.

Theorem 1 is the best candidate for an algorithmic reinterpretation: given bidders’ values and click-through rates, compute an efficient equilibrium. But its proof already gives the bids by formula once the efficient two-slot allocation and a maximizing losing bidder are found; those can be computed by checking \(O(n^2)\) assignments. Theorem 2’s condition is a single inequality, and Theorem 4 is an existence construction. One could study these as computational problems, but the paper does not present them as unresolved algorithmic questions.

The population limit also fits this auction poorly. With a fixed number \(m\) of slots, at most \(m\) advertisers win. If a type has positive limiting mass, eventually it supplies enough identical bidders to fill any number of those slots; counts beyond \(m\) stop mattering. The limiting masses therefore carry little information about allocation. Meanwhile, equilibrium prices and deviations are determined by individual bids: an advertiser may set a price or change who wins. In an atomless population, that individual influence disappears. The continuum would alter the strategic problem, not simply represent the same advertisers in mass terms.

A stronger alternative is to repeat auctions while scaling impression inventory with the advertiser population. That gives masses meaningful roles, but it changes the model into a population-scale allocation market. Its equilibrium questions could be worthwhile, yet they would not mirror these theorems about a finite-slot iterated second-price auction. This leaves the negative case strongest for the paper’s actual results and weaker as a universal claim: a convincing repeated-auction scenario is conceivable, but it would need a new model and a new computational question.
