The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t give a qualifying, result-anchored case for a continuous mirror: the paper contains no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. Its findings are experimental, reported in tables. They include evidence that the KL metric is informative and that the Small rule performs well, but those are not eligible anchors under your criteria. The anchor set is empty.

The strongest plausible mirror I can see is therefore only an unanchored research question. Consider a large combinatorial exchange with many buyers and sellers who repeat a small set of complete types: each type specifies the agent’s role, endowment or demand set, and valuation function. Let \(\mu_t\) be each type’s share of the market, with supplies scaled to the population. Given a finite-support distribution over such market environments and a strategyproof VCG reference, ask for a budget-balanced payment rule—among rules using the same efficient allocation—that minimizes the KL distance between the \(\mu\)-weighted distributions of normalized truthful payoffs under that rule and under VCG. The output is the payment rule and its objective value. This preserves the paper’s payoff-distribution criterion and gives a concrete high-multiplicity scenario.

The serious weakness is that the paper studies incentives for individual agents in a finite-agent game. In a nonatomic market, one agent’s report may have zero effect on the outcome; VCG marginal contributions and the normalized payoff metric may then collapse. Treating a positive mass of agents as deviating instead risks changing the question from individual incentives to coalition behavior. So this is a plausible direction the authors might recognize, but I would not present it as a mirror of a result in this paper or assign it a complexity class without further work.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is decisive but narrow: the paper has no result to anchor a continuization on. Its reported findings are experimental correlations, equilibrium approximations, and mechanism selections—not a theorem about the complexity of a computational problem. The exact equilibrium computation is described as intractable, and the paper uses restricted best-response searches instead. So there is no named computational result here whose continuous counterpart could complete the programme’s complexity map.

The best version of the proposed exchange mirror would be a new problem: take a mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete buyer and seller types, scale supplies with the market, and choose a budget-balanced payment rule minimizing the KL distance between its payoff distribution and a VCG benchmark. That is a plausible high-multiplicity setting; dismissing it for lack of repeated types would be a weak objection. But with the paper’s finite menu of payment rules, the task is just to evaluate the metric for each rule and choose the minimum. If the rule class is enlarged to make the optimization computationally interesting, its representation and constraints must be supplied by the new work; that complexity question is not already in this paper.

There is also a deeper modelling cost. The paper’s metric is meant to say something about individuals’ incentives in a finite-agent game. In an atomless population, one person’s report has zero mass and does not change aggregate quantities. The usual VCG marginal externality for that person therefore vanishes; the strategyproof reference and the payoff comparison that motivate the metric need a new definition. Rescaling payoffs per capita can avoid the metric’s numerical collapse, but it does not restore the individual’s effect on the market. Giving a positive mass of a type the ability to deviate restores an effect, but turns the question into one about cohort or coalition deviations. Keeping finite-agent effects instead requires a finite-population sequence or a derivative benchmark. Each may be worth studying, but each changes what the paper is measuring.

Thus the opponent’s proposed mirror is better understood as a possible new project than as a mirror of a result in this one. That is the strongest case against spending the continuization programme’s time on this paper. The broader claim that *no* continuous-population version could be worthwhile is weak: the large-exchange setting is credible, and a carefully defined new benchmark might be valuable. What the paper cannot support is a result-anchored complexity mirror.
