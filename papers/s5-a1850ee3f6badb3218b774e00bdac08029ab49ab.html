The continuous mirror question

Given rational type masses \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\) and \(\min_t\mu_t\ge\delta\), additive values \(v_t(z)=\sum_{g=1}^m a_{tg}z_g\), and rational per-capita supplies \(b_g\), allocate mass \(x_{t,z}\ge0\) among integral bundles \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\), subject to \(\sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_g=b_g\). Require EF1 for every positive-mass type-bundle pair \((t,z)\) and every positive-mass bundle \(y\): either \(y\) is empty or some \(g\) with \(y_g>0\) satisfies \(v_t(z)\ge v_t(y-e_g)\). Let \(\mathrm{OPT}\) be maximum welfare without EF1 and \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{EF1}}\) maximum welfare subject to it. Determine the sharp worst-case ratio \(\sup \mathrm{OPT}/\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{EF1}}\) as a function of \(\delta\), and whether an EF1 allocation achieving that asymptotic guarantee can be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the binary input length.

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population has \(\tau\) complete additive valuation types with rational masses \(\mu_t\). Type mass is split among integral bundles through \(x_{t,z}\); each good category has rational per-capita supply \(b_g\). Welfare is \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}v_t(z)\), and EF1 compares every bundle in the allocation’s support.

The objection that survived

The proposed \(1/\delta\) lower bound depends on a rare type and large differences in cross-type utility scales, so its relevance depends on those unscaled valuations being meaningful in the target allocation setting.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the unscaled EF1 result. Theorem 1 says the price of EF1 is \(O(\sqrt n)\) for scaled subadditive valuations and \(O(n)\) for unscaled subadditive valuations, with both bounds tight even for additive valuations. The paper proves the upper bounds and gives polynomial-time algorithms. The scaled lower bound is due to Bei et al. (2019); the paper constructs the unscaled lower bound in its proof. I would anchor on that unscaled branch: it has a particularly clean high-multiplicity interpretation. The paper’s cited hardness statements are not comparable numbered results of its own, so an algorithmic anchor is appropriate here.

I would call the mirror Mass-EF1-Price(\(\delta\)). A unit-mass population has \(\tau\) complete additive valuation types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). There are \(m\) good categories, each with rational per-capita supply \(b_g\); a person’s bundle is still an integral vector \(z\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\), with value \(v_t(z)=\sum_g a_{tg}z_g\). An allocation is a finite-support distribution \(x_{t,z}\): the mass of type \(t\) receiving bundle \(z\). It must satisfy \(\sum_z x_{t,z}=\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}z_g=b_g\) for every category \(g\).

Require EF1 pairwise across the bundles that actually occur: for every positive-mass type-\(t\) bundle \(z\) and every positive-mass bundle \(y\), either \(y\) is empty or some good unit \(g\) in \(y\) satisfies \(v_t(z)\ge v_t(y-e_g)\). Welfare is \(\sum_{t,z}x_{t,z}v_t(z)\). The problem is to determine the sharp worst-case ratio of unconstrained maximum welfare to maximum EF1 welfare, over such instances with \(\min_t\mu_t\ge\delta\), and to give an algorithm producing an EF1 allocation with the sharp asymptotic guarantee. A proposed solution would establish whether the price is \(\Theta(1/\delta)\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the binary input length \(L\).

The lower-bound family is very close to the paper’s construction. Take one good category with supply \(1\), a high-value type of mass \(\delta\) valuing each unit at \(1/\delta\), and a low-value type of mass \(1-\delta\) valuing each unit at \(\delta\), for reciprocal \(\delta\). Without fairness, each high-type recipient can receive \(1/\delta\) units, giving welfare \(1/\delta\). Under EF1, any two bundle sizes in positive-mass support must differ by at most one: each type’s value is proportional to bundle size, so either type would otherwise envy the larger bundle after one unit was removed. Since the average bundle size is \(1\), everyone receives one unit. Welfare is then \(1+\delta(1-\delta)\), giving a price of \(\Theta(1/\delta)\). This remains a high-multiplicity scenario: with \(\delta=1/K\) and population size \(K^2\), there are \(K\) high-type agents and roughly \(K^2\) low-type agents.

A plausible regime is a large public distribution of standardized goods to households with a modest number of recurring demand profiles. The number of recipients is much larger than the number of profiles; type mass is the share of households in each profile. Each household still gets an integral bundle, and the fairness test still compares realized bundles. The aggregate distribution \(x\) allows a type’s mass to split among different whole bundles. That makes the society continuous while leaving indivisibility and EF1 intact.

I would expect a Class A answer for this additive finite-catalog version: a sharp \(O(1/\delta)\) guarantee with an algorithm based on mass-allocation optimization, perhaps column generation. The bundle-support constraints in EF1 are the likely pricing obstacle. The continuum allocation has more ways to split a type among bundle outcomes than the discrete instance, so hardness does not automatically transfer; that is part of what the problem should test.

This mirror covers the unscaled, additive EF1 branch of Theorem 1. It does not claim to mirror the scaled \(\Theta(\sqrt n)\) branch, Prop1, or MMS.

My weakest point is the per-capita supply model: it co-modifies the goods inventory as population mass is normalized, and the exact price may depend on more than the smallest type mass. Still, the integral-bundle condition prevents this from becoming fractional-goods allocation, and the paper’s own unscaled lower bound already uses only two valuation profiles. That makes the proposed question a credible continuation of the authors’ EF1 problem, rather than a change of subject.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a real computational anchor: it gives polynomial-time algorithms and tight price bounds. I can’t dismiss it for lacking a computational result, and cake-cutting is not a collision because it continuizes the goods, not the population. High-multiplicity work also supports the regime.

The strongest objection is to the proposed lower-bound family. With unit per-capita supply, masses \(\delta\) and \(1-\delta\), and per-good values \(1/\delta\) and \(\delta\), its price grows as \(1/\delta\). That is a coherent model, but the growth comes from a rare type with a very different cardinal utility scale. If the type shares stay bounded below as the population grows, this family’s price does not grow with population size; the operative parameter is the rare type’s share. And if valuations are normalized so each type values the whole catalogue equally, this particular construction loses its gap. That weakens its use as evidence that the paper’s unscaled \(\Theta(n)\) result is a robust high-multiplicity phenomenon. It does not invalidate the paper’s result, or rule out settings where those utility scales are meaningful.

A scaled, additive EF1 mirror with per-capita supply is the natural repair. I do not see a fundamental obstruction to it: people still receive whole bundles, and mass can describe how many people receive each bundle. But the paper’s scaled lower-bound family may not survive a fixed, small type space, and the proposed model does not yet establish that its support-dependent EF1 constraints yield the claimed optimization approach. Those are reasons to narrow the claim, not to reject the research question.

So the negative case is weak against the best version of the mirror. The proposed unscaled example has a fragile motivation, but I cannot honestly argue that no plausible population regime produces a worthwhile continuous EF1 problem.
