The continuous mirror question

Given \(n\) alternatives and a finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) over report types \((S,\pi)\), where \(S\subseteq[n]\) and \(\pi\) ranks the alternatives in \(S\), with each pair appearing in reports of total mass at least \(p\), find a complete ranking \(\sigma\) maximizing \(\sum_{(S,\pi)}\mu_{S,\pi}\left|\{\{i,j\}\subseteq S:\sigma\text{ and }\pi\text{ order }i,j\text{ identically}\}\right|\). For fixed \(\beta>0\), this is the weighted selective-Mallows likelihood objective up to terms independent of \(\sigma\).

The model it lives in

A continuum of respondents has mass \(\mu_{S,\pi}\) for each observed subset-and-ranking type \((S,\pi)\). Choose a complete ranking \(\sigma\) maximizing total mass-weighted pairwise agreement; the Mallows normalizers depend on \(S\), not on \(\sigma\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers maximum-likelihood ranking in Theorem 4.1, including its likelier-than-nature variant. It leaves the sample-complexity results for recovering the central ranking and top-\(k\) ranking, and the experiments, aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • What exact, approximation, or parameterized algorithms are possible for the weighted incomplete-ranking problem as functions of \(n\), support size \(\tau\), and mass bit length \(L\), beyond the known-hard complete-ranking restriction?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is the exact maximum-likelihood ranking result, Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that for a \(p\)-frequent selective Mallows sample profile of length \(r\), with \(n\) alternatives, spread \(\beta>0\), and \(\alpha>0\), there is an algorithm that returns a ranking at least as likely as the true central ranking with probability at least \(1-n^{-\alpha}\), in time
\[ O\!\left(n^2+n^{1+O((2+\alpha)/(r\beta p^2))}2^{O(1/(p\beta))}\log^2 n\right). \]
It also gives an algorithm that returns an exact maximum-likelihood ranking with probability at least \(1-n^{-\alpha}\), in time
\[ O\!\left(n^2+n^{1+O((2+\alpha)/(r\beta p^4))}2^{O(1/(p^3\beta))}\log^2 n\right). \]
I would mirror the second result. The first is part of the same theorem, but exact maximum-likelihood estimation is the clearer computational question.

Here is that continuous problem. Fix \(n\) alternatives and \(\beta>0\). A type is a pair \(t=(S,\pi)\), where \(S\subseteq[n]\) is the subset a respondent ranked and \(\pi\) is a complete ranking of \(S\). The input is a finite-support distribution \(\mu\) over these types, with rational masses summing to \(1\). Require that each pair of alternatives appears in sets of total mass at least \(p\): for every \(i\ne j\),
\[ \sum_{(S,\pi):\,i,j\in S}\mu_{S,\pi}\ge p. \]
The decision variable is a complete ranking \(\sigma\) of all \(n\) alternatives. Return one maximizing
\[ F_\mu(\sigma)= \sum_{(S,\pi)}\mu_{S,\pi} \left|\{(i,j)\in S^2:\ i\succ_\sigma j\ \text{and}\ i\succ_\pi j\}\right|, \]
where each unordered pair is counted once. This is the continuous maximum-likelihood problem: the weighted log-likelihood under the selective Mallows model differs from \(\beta F_\mu(\sigma)\) by terms independent of \(\sigma\). A solution is any complete ranking attaining the maximum.

A plausible regime is a large recommendation service whose users rank subsets of a fixed catalogue. Assume the service has balanced exposure so every pair is ranked by a substantial fraction of users, and that its common-noise model is reasonable for a user cohort. A respondent’s type for this task is their exposed subset and reported order; people with the same report are interchangeable in the objective. With the catalogue fixed, the number of possible report types is finite, so as the number of respondents grows it is plausible to have population size \(R\) much larger than the number \(\tau\) of report types with positive mass. The Mallows noise remains part of the model; the continuum is the population measure over reports, not a replacement of the population by a probability law.

The theorem makes this a recognizable mirror of the authors’ problem: it optimizes the same central-ranking likelihood, and aggregating repeated reports into rational masses preserves that objective. Its guarantee, however, is for finite random samples drawn from a selective Mallows model. It does not establish an algorithm for every rational \(\mu\) in the problem above. For that unrestricted exact problem I expect hardness transfers (Class B). Set every \(S=[n]\), so \(p=1\), and the objective becomes the weighted discrete Kemeny ranking objective; rational masses can be scaled to integer multiplicities. The paper itself notes that computing a median ranking is NP-hard via weighted Feedback Arc Set in tournaments. That hardness is driven by the alternatives and their pairwise ordering, so allowing fractional population masses does not remove it.

The continuous question therefore covers the paper’s maximum-likelihood ranking result, not its sample-complexity bounds for recovering the center or the top-\(k\) ranking. It also leaves a useful, narrower question: under what explicit concentration or planted-model promise on \(\mu\) can the high-probability algorithmic guarantee of Theorem 4.1 extend to arbitrary rational masses, and with what dependence on \(n,\tau\), and the mass bit length?

The weakest point is that the theorem’s algorithmic guarantee is distributional, while the continuous optimization problem I have stated is worst-case. That gap prevents claiming the theorem already solves the continuous problem. It does not weaken the mirror itself: the same likelihood objective is well-defined over population masses, and the planted or concentrated version is a natural follow-up to the paper’s result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Theorem 4.1 as a *continuous mirror* is that the proposed mass-vector problem does not preserve the theorem’s promise. The theorem gives a high-probability algorithm for samples drawn from a \(p\)-frequent selective Mallows model. If \(\mu\) is an arbitrary rational distribution over reports \((S,\pi)\), it need not come from that model or have any underlying central ranking. The objective is still defined, but the theorem’s guarantee says nothing about it.

There is a useful trilemma. If \(\mu\) is an empirical distribution from \(r\) reports, it is a normalized sample profile, and the theorem applies only when that profile has the required random generation. If \(\mu\) is arbitrary, the problem becomes unrestricted weighted aggregation of partial rankings. If \(\mu\) is the exact report law under the Mallows model, its pairwise marginals point toward the central ranking, so recovery is a straightforward population-level task. None of these cases transfers Theorem 4.1’s particular combination of a short random sample and a high-probability exact-MLE guarantee into an algorithm for arbitrary continuous societies.

That is a real limitation, but it does not defeat the best mirror. A recommendation service with a fixed catalogue and a bounded menu of exposure subsets can have a large population with repeated report types and meaningful rational masses. Maximizing likelihood over those masses is a coherent computational problem. Its reduction to weighted Kemeny aggregation, and the possibility that hardness persists, are not objections under the programme’s criteria; they could make it a Class B result.

So I cannot honestly make the requested universal negative case. The objection shows that the paper’s theorem does not itself establish a worst-case continuous guarantee, but the proposed population problem survives as a worthwhile mirror. The negative case is weak at its decisive point.
