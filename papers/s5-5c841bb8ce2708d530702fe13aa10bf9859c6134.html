The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has named algorithmic results, but none is a computational-complexity anchor of the kind this task requires. Theorem 1, proved here, says that if an allocation of regional caps makes DA produce a weakly stable but inefficient matching, adapted allocations exist under which DA produces an efficient, weakly stable matching and makes every doctor weakly better off. Corollary 1, also proved here, says an allocation yielding an efficient, weakly stable matching exists. Theorem 2, proved here using prior results on FDA, says that FDA’s matching is exactly the matching DA produces when hospital capacities are set to FDA’s resulting hospital counts. None classifies a problem as tractable, hard, FPT, or otherwise.

Theorem 2 suggests a possible continuous-population question: with a distribution of doctor types and fractional hospital and regional capacities, does an appropriately defined continuum FDA outcome coincide with continuum DA under capacities adapted to that outcome? A plausible regime would be large cohorts of doctors sharing preference types and hospital-side evaluations, with relatively few types. But the paper does not define such a continuum model, and the proposed question would extend its algorithmic equivalence rather than mirror a named complexity result.

So I can’t give a compliant anchored case for a continuous mirror from this paper. The algorithms and their equivalence may motivate a separate continuization study, but presenting them as complexity anchors would overstate what the paper proves.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no ChoCo computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 establish that suitable regional-cap allocations yield efficient outcomes; Theorem 2 establishes that FDA’s outcome equals DA’s under capacities taken from that outcome. These are claims about which matching a mechanism produces, not about the complexity of a computational problem. Naming and describing algorithms does not by itself supply a complexity result.

The strongest proposed mirror is to ask whether continuum FDA agrees with continuum DA under capacities adapted to FDA’s outcome. But the paper’s exact equivalence concerns individual assignments. In its model, every hospital strictly ranks doctors, so any two doctors differ in at least one hospital’s ranking. A complete type must capture those differences. For \(n\) doctors, that means \(\tau=n\): the model has no high-multiplicity compression. A distribution over types that omits hospital rankings would merge doctors the mechanism distinguishes.

One could instead group doctors into priority classes, allow ties, and compare the resulting mass assignments. That may be a coherent research question, but it changes the paper’s strict-priority model and its individual matching outcome. More fundamentally, it remains a structural equivalence question, not a complexity question. To bring it into ChoCo’s remit, someone would need to add a computational task—such as computing a mass matching or optimizing a capacity-design objective. The paper supplies neither such task nor a complexity bottleneck; adding one would start a new project rather than mirror a named result.

So the negative case is strong for this paper under the programme’s criteria: none of Theorems 1, 2, or Corollary 1 survives as a computational anchor. The universal claim that no related continuum matching model could be worthwhile is harder to defend; large cohorts with shared preferences and priority classes could motivate separate work. That possibility does not make this paper a good continuous mirror.
