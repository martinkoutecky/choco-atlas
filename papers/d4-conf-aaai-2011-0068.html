What the mirror covers

No mirror covers Theorems 1, 3, or 5; they address equilibrium structure, welfare, and nonexistence of separation, respectively.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is that this paper has no qualifying computational anchor for a ChoCo mirror. Its named results are about equilibrium properties, not whether a computational problem is tractable or hard. For example, Theorem 1 proves that, when \(Y_H\ge Y_T\), the specified strategies and beliefs form a separating PBE; Theorem 3 says that for convex \(Q\), any separating PBE maximizes social welfare among all PBE; and Theorem 5 rules out separation between Alice’s \(H\)-signal/no-outside-payoff type and her \(T\)-signal/outside-payoff type under its stated conditions. These are substantive results, but none is a complexity result or an algorithm for an underlying computational problem. So there is no anchor here for which I can honestly state a prover-ready continuous computational question.

The apparent continuity is on the wrong axis for this programme. The reports \(r_A,r_B\in[0,1]\) are continuous market probabilities, and \(\alpha\) is the probability that one Alice has an outside incentive. Neither makes the population continuous. The modeled society remains two strategic participants, and the central phenomenon is signaling between them. Replacing Alice with a mass of traders would require specifying how their reports combine and how each trader’s deviation affects Bob’s beliefs; that could define a new continuum game, but it would change the problem this paper analyzes. Thus the plausible positive direction is a future extension about equilibrium behavior in a market with large cohorts—not a continuous mirror of a named computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper has no computational result to continue. Theorem 1 characterizes a separating PBE; Theorem 3 compares welfare across equilibria; Theorem 5 rules out a particular separation. They are results about a two-player signaling game, not complexity or algorithmic results for a computational problem. The paper also fixes two binary signals and two roles, and gives no input-size regime. Even \(Q\) is allowed to be any continuous function, with no finite encoding specified. A computational question can be built from these results, but it would need extra choices about what is input and what is to be computed.

The best candidate is to ask whether a separating equilibrium exists, or to compute one, in a market with a continuum of traders. That does not preserve the mechanism behind Theorem 1. Its costly report matters because Alice is a pivotal first mover whom Bob observes and interprets. In a nonatomic population, an individual trader has measure zero and cannot change the market probability enough to pay a signaling cost to influence Bob. If a whole positive-mass cohort moves together, that cohort acts as a strategic principal; the population is then a parameter of a finite-player signaling game, not the continuum of strategic individuals. A shared market with many independently acting traders could be interesting, but it introduces a different interaction and information structure.

Theorem 3 has the same obstacle. One could integrate payoffs across a population of traders and ask for a welfare-maximizing equilibrium. But if the continuum consists of repeated copies of Alice and Bob, that is a population of separate games. If everyone trades in one shared market, the relevant deviations and beliefs differ from those in this paper. Neither construction supplies a continuous-population version of the theorem’s comparison among equilibria of Alice and Bob’s game.

Theorem 5 is especially easy to misread as a population result. Here \(\alpha\) is the prior probability that one Alice has an outside payoff. Replacing it with the fraction of traders who have outside incentives changes uncertainty about a player’s private type into known heterogeneity across a population. If the types remain hidden, individual traders in a continuum still lack the pivotal influence that makes Alice’s deception consequential. If a cohort’s mass can move the market as a block, the model again depends on a strategic bloc rather than atomless agents.

A plausible larger-market project does remain: specify many traders, a shared LMSR market, a distribution of signals and outside incentives, and a finite representation of that distribution; then study equilibrium computation or information aggregation. But that would be a new mean-field market game motivated by this paper, not a continuous mirror of one of its named results. The distinction matters under ChoCo’s scope: analytic equilibrium questions are not the computational complexity questions the programme is charting.

I would not claim that no imaginable population model could be worthwhile. The negative case is strong at the programme’s scope gate, but the universal claim is too strong to establish: a carefully specified many-trader model could be a worthwhile computational project in its own right.
