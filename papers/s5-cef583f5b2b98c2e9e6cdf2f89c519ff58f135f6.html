The continuous mirror question

Input a finite set of delegation roles \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational transition probabilities \(q_{su}\) with \(\sum_u q_{su}\leq 1\), a rational selected share \(\kappa\), and a rational threshold \(c\). Choose \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t x_t=\kappa\). For each selected type \(t\), define the first-hit probabilities by \(h_{st}=q_{st}x_t/\mu_t+\sum_u q_{su}(\mu_u-x_u)h_{ut}/\mu_u\), and let \(b_t=x_t+\sum_s(\mu_s-x_s)h_{st}\). Decide whether some \(x\) satisfies \(b_t\geq c x_t\) for every \(t\) with \(x_t>0\).

The model it lives in

A society consists of exchangeable delegation-role types with masses \(\mu_t\). An unselected voter delegates with probability \(q_{su}\) to a uniformly chosen member of type \(u\), and selected mass suspends delegation. The decision variable is the selected mass vector \(x\); its first-hit flows \(h\) determine captured vote masses \(b\). At fixed total selected share \(\kappa\), maximize the minimum per-capita power \(b_t/x_t\) among selected types.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest case is for the paper’s general-graph peer-selection hardness, not for a continuous version of every result in the paper.

Theorem 7 states that, given a directed graph \(G\) and a slate size \(k\), computing \(\absorbdecay(G,k)\) is NP-hard. The underlying problem is to choose \(S\subseteq V\), \(|S|=k\), maximizing the least power of a selected node after deleting the outgoing edges of all selected nodes. In the paper’s \(\alpha\to1\) formulation, that power is PageRank in the graph with those edges removed. The theorem is proved in this paper: Appendix A.7 reduces Independent Set to its decision version. In outline, the construction makes meeting a threshold equivalent to every selected node retaining an incoming edge, and encodes independent sets through edge gadgets. The result applies to general directed graphs, not just functional ones.

I would mirror it as Role-Flow Decide-AbsorbDecay\(_\infty\). The society has a finite set \(T\) of delegation-role types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(s\) has a rational delegation vector \(q_{su}\): a type-\(s\) voter delegates with probability \(q_{su}\) to a uniformly chosen member of type \(u\); the remaining probability is a direct vote. Thus \(\sum_u q_{su}\leq1\). A type describes the voter’s full profile for this model: the same delegation vector, direct-vote probability, and eligibility for selection. The regime is a large platform with many members in a modest number \(\tau=|T|\) of genuinely exchangeable roles, such as recurring governance roles in a large DAO. It is a poor fit for a small committee or a network where individual links are the relevant distinctions.

A slate is a mass vector \(x\), where \(0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t\) is the selected fraction of type \(t\), and \(\sum_t x_t=\kappa\) for a specified rational population share \(\kappa>0\). Selected mass suspends its outgoing delegations and votes directly. For an unselected voter of type \(s\), let \(h_{st}(x)\) be the probability that their delegation walk first reaches the selected cohort of type \(t\). It is the least nonnegative solution to

\[ h_{st}(x) = q_{st}\frac{x_t}{\mu_t} + \sum_{u\in T}q_{su}\frac{\mu_u-x_u}{\mu_u}h_{ut}(x). \]

The first term is the chance of delegating directly to selected mass of type \(t\); the sum covers delegation to unselected members who continue the walk. The total vote mass captured by selected type \(t\) is

\[ b_t(x)=x_t+\sum_{s\in T}(\mu_s-x_s)h_{st}(x). \]

The decision problem takes a rational threshold \(c\) and asks whether there is such an \(x\) for which \(b_t(x)\geq c x_t\) for every selected type \(t\) with \(x_t>0\). A witness is the mass vector \(x\); \(b_t(x)/x_t\) is the per-capita power of the selected members of that type. The corresponding optimization problem maximizes the minimum of those per-capita powers. This keeps the paper’s central operation—selected voters absorb delegation—and its max-min representation objective, while making the slate proportional to population size. That scaling has some support in the paper’s own proportionality discussion, which measures entitlement using \(k/n\).

I would provisionally expect continuum-specific hardness (Class C) for unrestricted type graphs: the mass choices interact with the type-to-type hitting probabilities, so the resulting optimization is not obviously an LP. But Theorem 7 does not establish that prediction. Its Independent Set reduction relies on choosing particular vertices, and fractional mass choices may defeat that encoding. A convex flow formulation could instead make some or all of this mirror tractable. The useful follow-up questions are whether acyclic or functional type graphs admit such a formulation, whether the problem is tractable when \(\tau\) is fixed, and whether unrestricted type graphs retain hardness despite fractional selection.

The weakest point is the aggregation itself. Theorem 7’s hardness lives in correlations among individual edges, and this mirror replaces those edges with exchangeable, type-level delegation probabilities and replaces \(k\) named candidates with a selected population share. The paper’s own lottery interpretation of delegation, its expected-voting-weight/RWD equivalence, and its proportional-selection framing make this a credible population-level extension. They do not prove that the authors would regard it as the same problem. This case therefore covers the general-graph \(\absorbdecay\) peer-selection objective of Theorem 7; it does not claim continuous mirrors for the axiomatic results or for Theorem 5’s polynomial-time result on functional graphs.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 7 is a real computational anchor: it proves NP-hardness for selecting a peer slate on a general delegation graph. So the negative case cannot rest on the paper having no algorithmic result.

The proposed Role-Flow problem changes a consequential part of that input. In the paper, each delegation edge names a particular person; the lottery is over whether that person delegates or votes directly. In the mirror, a voter delegates to a uniformly chosen member of a destination type. The hitting-probability equation therefore describes a stochastic role network, not the fixed individual network on which \(\absorbdecay\) is defined. It may be a sensible new model, but Theorem 7 does not anchor it merely because both models involve delegation and selection.

For an exact high-multiplicity version of the paper’s problem, a type would have to preserve the individual’s relevant network position, including whom they delegate to and how the graph connects them to other voters. On a general graph, those positions can be unique, leaving \(\tau\) close to the number of voters. Theorem 7’s reduction makes its choices among distinct original vertices with distinct incidences to edge gadgets. Averaging those relations into role-to-role probabilities discards the structure the slate-selection objective uses. That mismatch is the objection; whether the resulting fractional problem is easier or harder is not.

A stronger proposal might keep a finite set of named delegates or candidates while aggregating large cohorts that delegate to them. That is plausible for some platforms, but the selection problem then depends on the named core’s network. If the core stays small, the slate choice is small; if it grows, the identities and links that matter for selection grow with it. This makes a useful weighted-network model conceivable, but it does not rescue the specific claim that Theorem 7’s general-graph hardness has a direct population-continuum mirror. The proponent’s proportional slate also changes the paper’s practical setting of choosing a manageable number of participants into selecting a population-sized committee. That is a possible co-modification, but it needs its own case that the authors would recognize as their peer-selection problem.

I would reject the stated Role-Flow formulation as a faithful mirror of Theorem 7. But the universal negative is weak: a platform with large cohorts delegating to a growing set of named proxies could support a meaningful continuous selection problem, and I cannot rule out the authors recognizing it as a worthwhile extension. The strongest case against is therefore about the gap between this paper’s fixed, identity-bearing network and the proposed exchangeable role model—not a convincing demonstration that no continuous mirror exists.
