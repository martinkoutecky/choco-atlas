What the mirror covers

The proposed source-population interpretation could compute meet or join from the positive-mass partition types, but it covers none of the paper's axiomatic characterization results as computational claims.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive reading is very thin. A plausible high-multiplicity setting would have many data sources, each reporting a partition of the same finite set of entities. A type would be a complete partition; its mass would be the fraction of sources using it. If there were many sources but relatively few distinct partitions, the grand meet or grand join could be computed from the support of that distribution. Theorem 1, proved here, characterizes join aggregators; Theorem 5, also proved here, characterizes meet aggregators.

But neither theorem is a computational result. Theorem 1 says that neutrality, unanimity, and disjunctive separability characterize the join aggregators. Theorem 5 says that unanimity and conjunctive separability characterize the meet aggregators. The paper proves axiomatic characterizations; it does not assert that an underlying computational problem is in P, hard, or in another complexity class. So there are no named computational anchors to restate, and I can’t honestly give a prover-ready continuous problem for each anchor.

The source-population scenario is also a weak mirror: duplicate partition types do not affect meet or join, so masses have no role beyond indicating which types have positive support. Computing the aggregate would be a natural adjacent task, but it would not mirror a computational result in this paper. The strongest honest positive case is therefore a possible high-multiplicity interpretation of the model, without the required computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The decisive objection is that Theorem 1 and Theorem 5—the proponent’s two anchors—are axiomatic characterizations, not computational results. They characterize maps \(F:\mathcal E^n\to\mathcal E\) satisfying specified axioms; neither asks for an algorithm nor establishes a complexity bound. There is therefore no computational claim in either theorem to translate to a continuous society.

The strongest rescue is to model many interchangeable data sources as a population whose types are partitions of the same set, with mass \(\mu\) over those types. That is a coherent high-multiplicity scenario. One could define meet and join from the positive-mass types, but that poses a new computational task; the paper’s theorems do not establish its complexity. Adding an objective or constraints to make the task more substantive would add modeling content absent from the paper. And in the paper’s own examples, attributes such as employment and religion are dimensions of classification, not a population of agents.

So the negative case is strong against a computational mirror of this paper’s named results, but weak as a universal claim about future computational work on equivalence relations. The source-population interpretation may be worth studying; it does not supply the missing computational anchor here.
