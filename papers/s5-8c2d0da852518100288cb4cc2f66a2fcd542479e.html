What the mirror covers

Proposition 2.3 and Theorems 4.2 and 4.5 concern equilibrium correspondences or analytical characterizations; the paper's other core and blocking results likewise provide no algorithmic or complexity result.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive premise is natural: a large exchange economy can have many consumers but relatively few complete economic types. A type would include the consumer’s preferences, information partition, prior, and state-contingent endowment; \(\mu_t\) would be the fraction of consumers of type \(t\). A mirror could ask about feasible reallocations of consumption across types. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the paper’s model gives it a close relative.

But the paper contains no named computational result asserting tractability, hardness, or an algorithmic classification. So it supplies no eligible anchor for the requested continuous computational problem.

The nearest result is Proposition 2.3, proved here. Under assumption (A3), a non-trivial Walrasian expectations quasi-equilibrium \((x,\pi)\) of the finite economy corresponds to one of the associated equal-treatment continuum economy, with \(f(t,\cdot)=x_i\) for \(t\in I_i\); conversely, a continuum equilibrium corresponds to the finite one with \(x_i=n\int_{I_i} f(t,\cdot)\,d\mu(t)\). The proposition establishes an exact discrete–continuum equivalence, but it gives no algorithm or complexity bound. Theorems 4.2 and 4.5, also proved here, characterize Walrasian expectations allocations through Aubin non-domination and private non-domination in perturbed-endowment economies. They are analytical characterizations, not computational results.

The regime is especially plausible because the paper itself constructs a continuum of agents with equal treatment: every consumer in a type’s interval shares the same relevant characteristics. That is good evidence that this kind of continuum model makes sense for the economic problem. It is also the main limitation of a novelty claim: the paper already defines and studies the continuous-population version of its core and equilibrium questions. A computational study of finitely encoded instances might be a worthwhile next step, but the paper does not specify how to encode its general Banach-lattice commodity spaces or utilities as inputs.

So the honest positive case is that the population model is natural and already explicit in the paper. The weakest point—and the decisive one for this task—is that the paper provides no named computational anchor, while its existing continuum results are already results about a continuous population.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to mirror. Proposition 2.3 and Theorems 4.2 and 4.5—the proponent’s three anchors—establish an equilibrium correspondence and analytical characterizations of Walrasian allocations. The other named results concern core equivalence, blocking coalitions, and ex-post efficiency. None defines an algorithmic problem, gives a complexity bound, or supplies an encoded input on which such a bound could be stated. Under ChoCo’s criteria, that is decisive against treating this as a mirror of the paper’s results.

Proposition 2.3 is especially close to a continuous model already: it maps a finite economy to an equal-treatment continuum economy and back. A stronger computational proposal could generalize the construction to arbitrary rational masses over complete economic types, then ask whether a proposed allocation is in the core or is a Walrasian allocation. But the paper’s general model allows arbitrary Banach lattices and utility functions, with no finite input representation. Making that proposal computable would require choosing a new encoding and substantially restricting the model. That could motivate a separate computational-economics project; it would not be a computational mirror of Proposition 2.3.

The same problem affects Theorems 4.2 and 4.5. Their mass-based versions might ask whether some coalition can block an allocation, or whether an allocation satisfies an equilibrium characterization for every perturbed endowment. Those are plausible questions once types, commodities, preferences, and endowments have finite encodings. But the theorems themselves do not pose them computationally, and choosing those encodings creates the algorithmic object from scratch. The paper studies exchange and competitive equilibrium, rather than a voting or collective-choice rule, so the resulting work would also need a case for why it belongs in this programme.

The continuum population is plainly sensible here: the authors construct it explicitly, and its equal-treatment types are a natural high-multiplicity regime. That supports the model; it does not supply the missing computational target. Nor does the existing continuum analysis by itself settle a later computational question. So the negative case is strongest as a scope and anchoring argument: none of the proposed anchors survives as a computational mirror, and the best replacements are new encoded problems rather than results of this paper. The case is weaker as a claim that no worthwhile related project could be devised; finite, encoded versions may be worth studying, but this paper does not provide the computational question that would make them ChoCo mirrors.
