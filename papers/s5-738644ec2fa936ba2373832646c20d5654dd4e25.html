The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot make an anchor-compliant case for a continuous mirror of this paper. Its named results are economic characterizations, not computational results: for example, Proposition 1 gives the surplus-maximizing shares under symmetric information, \(\alpha_L^{\mathrm{sym}}=\gamma_L^2/(\gamma_L^2+\gamma_H^2)\) and \(\alpha_H^{\mathrm{sym}}=\gamma_H^2/(\gamma_L^2+\gamma_H^2)\). Proposition 3 characterizes shares that maximize the range in which both agents disclose, and Proposition 5 characterizes the surplus-maximizing shares across parameter ranges. None states a complexity classification or an algorithmic result for a computational problem. They therefore cannot serve as the required anchors.

The most promising positive intuition is the paper’s setting of professional partnerships, including larger medical practices. One could imagine a high-multiplicity extension with many partners but few types, where a type records productivity and information-acquisition characteristics, and mass is the fraction of partners of that type. A planner might choose type-level revenue shares to maximize expected surplus subject to disclosure and effort behavior. That would make the population continuous.

The weakness is decisive for this task: the paper models exactly two agents, each with a distinct productivity role, and its results characterize their incentives and optimal shares. A many-partner model would need new assumptions about production, share scaling, information disclosure, and project choice. It might be a related research project, but the paper supplies neither a computational anchor nor enough structure to present that extension as the continuous version of one of its results. So there are no valid anchors here, and no per-anchor continuous problems I can honestly offer.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Propositions 1, 3, and 5 characterize economic optima: respectively, shares that maximize surplus under symmetric information, shares that maximize the scope for full disclosure, and surplus-maximizing shares under the paper’s information and adaptation constraints. They do not classify the complexity of a computational problem or give an algorithm. The paper’s task is to explain incentives and characterize optimal contracts, not to compute winners, control outcomes, or solve a social-choice problem.

The proponent’s best extension is a large partnership with a small number of productivity and information types. That is a plausible high-multiplicity economic setting; the fact that the paper models two partners does not make large practices implausible. But it does not supply a computational mirror of Proposition 1. If we replicate independent two-person teams, their surplus adds: with a common sharing rule, the aggregate objective is a scalar optimization; with team-specific rules, it separates into the paper’s original problem. If instead we put many partners in one team, we need a new production function, a revenue-sharing rule that scales with team size, and a model of how information affects the joint project. Those could be worthwhile choices, but they define a new model rather than continuize the proposition’s computational content.

Proposition 3 is a stronger-looking anchor because disclosure changes project choice. In the paper, however, the disclosure decision belongs to one individually informed partner and affects the whole team. In a continuum of partners, one person has zero mass, so their disclosure cannot affect an aggregate decision. Giving a positive mass of agents the information, or adding a collective disclosure rule, restores an effect only by changing the information structure and strategic game. Proposition 5 inherits the same issue. Computing the paper’s stated optimum is already reduced to its parameter thresholds and share characterization; a harder optimization over many types would arise from the added model, not from a computational question the paper poses.

These extensions may support useful continuous team-theory research. The negative case is weaker if read as the literal claim that no valuable continuous economic study could ever be built from this paper. It is much stronger under the programme’s criterion: neither a larger-partnership continuum nor the named propositions yield a computational social-choice mirror without introducing a different problem.
