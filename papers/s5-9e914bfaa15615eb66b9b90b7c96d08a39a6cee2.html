What the mirror covers

The proposed LP would compute the downstream incremental solution from prefix welfare values; it does not cover the paper's other three axiomatic characterizations or its comparisons of solutions.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest plausible population mirror is an ordered mass of standardized irrigation users. Imagine many users served at a finite set of river intake points. Users at the same point with the same per-capita water right and benefit curve are interchangeable. A type is \((\text{intake position},\text{per-capita inflow},\text{benefit curve})\); its mass is its share of the users. There may be millions of users but only \(\tau\) such types. Total inflow scales with mass, while each user’s benefit remains a benefit from their own water consumption. Water is already continuous in the paper, but here the *population* is continuous too.

The closest named result is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that a solution \(f\) is the downstream incremental solution \(d\) if and only if it satisfies efficiency, the lower bound property, the weak aspiration level property, and independence of downstream benefits. In the paper, \(d_i=v_i-v_{i-1}\), where \(v_i\) is the maximum welfare attainable by the first \(i\) upstream agents. This is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result. The paper contains no named theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a complexity bound or hardness result; arbitrary continuous concave benefit functions are not given a computational encoding. So Theorem 4.1 is not a valid anchor under the requested standard.

If I were turning the paper’s allocation primitive into a ChoCo question anyway, I would call it High-Multiplicity Downstream-Incremental Water Allocation. An instance has ordered types \(t_1,\ldots,t_\tau\), positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita inflows \(e_i\), and rational piecewise-linear concave benefit functions \(b_i\). For each \(k\), let \(V_k\) be the maximum of \(\sum_{i\le k}\mu_i b_i(x_i)\), subject to \(x_i\ge0\) and \(\sum_{i\le \ell}\mu_i x_i\le\sum_{i\le \ell}\mu_i e_i\) for every \(\ell\le k\). The requested solution is an efficient allocation \(x^*\) and per-capita payoffs \(z_i=(V_i-V_{i-1})/\mu_i\), with \(V_0=0\). Transfers \(p_i=z_i-b_i(x_i^*)\) implement those payoffs and satisfy \(\sum_i\mu_i p_i=0\). Under this finite input format, the welfare maximizations are linear programs, so I would expect this candidate to be tractable. It preserves the river-flow constraints and the downstream incremental welfare rule; it does not establish that any discrete hardness from this paper dissolves, since the paper gives no such hardness result.

The soft spot is that this candidate computes a rule whose characterization is the paper’s contribution, rather than continuizing a computational problem the authors studied. The paper’s agents are usually a small number of countries, cities, or firms, and its central questions are axiomatic. The mass-user setting is credible for pooled irrigation users, and the authors should recognize the water-allocation object, but whether they would accept its computational version as a mirror of their contribution is uncertain. The result’s continuous water quantities alone do not make it a population-continuity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4.1 is an axiomatic characterization, not a computational result. The paper’s other numbered theorems characterize solutions the same way; none states a complexity bound or hardness result for a computational problem. And the paper gives no input encoding for its arbitrary continuous concave benefit functions. The proponent correctly identifies this gap, but it means Theorem 4.1 cannot anchor a continuous mirror under the standard set for this task.

The strongest repair is to ask for an algorithm that computes the downstream incremental solution in a population of pooled users. That is a coherent new problem if users at each intake point share a position, per-capita inflow, and benefit curve. But it changes the paper’s unit of agency: its agents are ordered river jurisdictions, and the prefix constraints and axioms apply to those jurisdictions. Replicating people at one site requires a pooling model and a rule for distributing water and transfers among them. If users at the same site have different benefit types, the theorem also supplies no order among those types or rule for their within-site allocation. Aggregating them into a site-level benefit function is a reasonable repair, but then the computation returns site-level welfare and transfers; the population-level allocation rule is an extra modeling choice.

That repair could support useful work on water allocation. Its weakness as a mirror is that it creates a computational question around an axiomatically defined rule rather than continuizing a computational problem studied in this paper. There is no discrete complexity result here whose high-multiplicity version the paper invites us to compare. The proposed LP’s likely tractability is not an objection; the mismatch is what the paper studies.

So the negative case is strong against treating Theorem 4.1 as a ChoCo anchor, but it cannot honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous question exists in any scenario. The pooled-irrigation setting is plausible; it looks like an adjacent algorithmic project, not a continuous mirror of this paper’s results.
