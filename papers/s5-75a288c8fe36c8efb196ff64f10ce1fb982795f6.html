The continuous mirror question

Given an ordered sequence of \(m\) items, matroid types \(T\) with rank oracles, rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and per-item supply \(\eta=1/N\), each arriving item may be assigned to mass at most \(\eta\) of currently eligible facilities. Each facility receives an integral bundle independent in its type's matroid, while the aggregate mass assigned may be fractional. What is the best worst-case competitive ratio achievable online against the offline optimum for the same population and item supplies, by an algorithm polynomial in \(m\), \(\lvert T\rvert\), and the input bit length?

The model it lives in

A unit-mass population of facilities is partitioned among matroid types with masses \(\mu_t\). Each arriving item has supply \(\eta=1/N\); an online decision transfers eligible facility mass from bundle \(S\) to \(S\cup\{j_k\}\), subject to total assigned mass at most \(\eta\). Individual bundles remain integral and matroid-independent, and welfare is the final mass-weighted sum of ranks.

The objection that survived

The evolving distribution may require exponentially many bundle-state coordinates, so polynomial-time implementation in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length is unestablished; the proponent flags compact representation as an open question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1.2 for online welfare with matroid-rank utilities. It leaves the general fractional SAP guarantee, the small-bids result, and the water-level machinery outside this anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does a fluid analogue of Matroidal Ranking achieve competitive ratio \(1-1/e\) with a compact implementation polynomial in \(m\), the number of types, and the input bit length?
  • When the horizon scales with \(N\), do discrete lower bounds persist in the fluid limit?
  • Can the evolving mass distribution over bundles be represented or updated without explicitly tracking exponentially many states?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I see is Matroid-OSWM\(_\infty\), for the paper’s matroid-rank welfare problem. Its anchor is Theorem 1.2, proved in Section 6: for online submodular welfare maximization with indivisible items arriving one at a time and agents whose utilities are matroid rank functions, the paper gives a randomized polynomial-time algorithm whose expected welfare is at least \(1-1/e\) times the offline optimum. The paper also describes an extension to weighted matroid ranks, but the theorem itself states the unweighted result, so I would anchor only that result. The paper has no numbered hardness result for this problem; its cited hardness barrier concerns the broader class of general submodular utilities.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large service network with many facilities but only a few operating blueprints. A blueprint specifies a facility’s matroid over the arriving job types: for example, which combinations of jobs the facility can handle independently. Facilities sharing a blueprint have the same utility in every respect relevant to the problem. With \(N\) facilities and \(\tau\) blueprints, \(N\) can be very large while \(\tau\) stays small. This is a population regime, not a claim that every application of online welfare has many repeated agents.

Here is a precise continuous problem. Let \(J=(j_1,\ldots,j_m)\) be the ordered item sequence, and let \(T\) be a finite set of matroid types on \(J\), with rank functions \(r_t\). The atomless facility population has rational type masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Set \(\eta=1/N\), the per-capita supply corresponding to one item per \(N\) facilities. After item \(j_k\) arrives, let \(\nu_{t,S}\) be the mass of type-\(t\) facilities currently holding bundle \(S\subseteq\{j_1,\ldots,j_{k-1}\}\). The policy chooses transfers \(y_{t,S,k}\) with \(0\le y_{t,S,k}\le\nu_{t,S}\), \(y_{t,S,k}=0\) unless \(r_t(S\cup\{j_k\})=r_t(S)+1\), and \(\sum_{t,S}y_{t,S,k}\le\eta\). It moves that mass from bundle \(S\) to \(S\cup\{j_k\}\). Thus each facility receives whole items and has an integral bundle; the aggregate recipient mass can be fractional. The objective is to maximize final welfare \(\sum_{t,S}\nu_{t,S}r_t(S)\), compared with the offline optimum over the same population and item supplies.

The question to hand to a prover is: what is the best worst-case competitive ratio achievable by an online algorithm for Matroid-OSWM\(_\infty\), with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the bit length of the rational masses and supply, given matroid rank oracles? In particular, does a compact fluid version of Matroidal Ranking achieve \(1-1/e\)? A natural candidate assigns each type a continuum of priority levels and, on each arrival, serves up to mass \(\eta\) from the highest-priority currently eligible facility states. I expect this question to be tractable, with \(1-1/e\) the right target, but the theorem alone does not establish that bound for the continuous problem. Further questions are whether the discrete lower bound survives the fluid limit, whether the fluid optimum matches the limit of finite high-multiplicity optima, and whether the evolving state distribution admits a compact representation.

This is a credible mirror because the paper already studies exactly this welfare problem: its agents become the population, and their matroid ranks remain the individual utility functions. Items still arrive in discrete time, and the problem retains the matroid independence decisions at its core. The mirror covers Theorem 1.2 only; I would not extend it to the paper’s general fractional SAP result on the strength of this argument.

My weakest point is the scaling step. In the paper, each arriving item is assigned to one named agent; here its per-capita supply is \(\eta\), so it can reach a fractional mass of a continuous population. That co-modification is needed to make high multiplicity meaningful, but it makes the proposed problem a fluid analogue rather than an identity with the paper’s finite instance. Also, Theorem 1.2’s polynomial running time is polynomial in the number of agents, so simply simulating it takes time depending on \(N\), not just on the compact type description. The continuous algorithm and its compact implementation are therefore genuine questions, rather than consequences already proved in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 1.2: Matroid-OSWM has a clear computational result, and a large population of facilities sharing a matroid blueprint is a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The type grouping is exact, since facilities with the same matroid are indistinguishable for the problem. That makes this a difficult mirror to argue against.

The strongest objection is that the proposed scaling becomes trivial in the most direct population limit. With \(m\) arrivals and per-capita supply \(\eta=1/N\), at most \(m/N\) of the population receives anything. If \(m\) is fixed while \(N\) grows and every type has positive limiting mass, there are eventually enough facilities of each type to give every non-loop item to a fresh facility. Each such assignment adds one to rank, so the online and offline welfare coincide. The limiting population has erased the interactions that make the matroid problem interesting.

To retain those interactions, one could let the number of arrivals grow with \(N\), or give each arrival a larger per-capita supply. But then the first option requires matroids over a growing set of distinct jobs, while the second changes the arrival units. In either case, a policy may need to track how mass is distributed across exponentially many bundles. The paper’s theorem does not provide a compact representation or a polynomial-time algorithm for that fluid state.

That is a real cost to the mirror, but it does not defeat the stronger version. Scaling the horizon with the population is a coherent online regime; exponential state may be the computational question, not a reason to dismiss it. A fluid analogue of Matroidal Ranking could have a compact implementation, and the paper’s theorem gives reason to investigate one. I cannot make the universal negative case stick: the fixed-horizon limit degenerates, but the natural way around that degeneration leaves a well-posed continuous-population problem. My case against this anchor is therefore weak.
