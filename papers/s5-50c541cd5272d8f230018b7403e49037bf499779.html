The continuous mirror question

Given a choreography DAG, rational masses \(\mu_t\) of provider types with per-capita service capacities and costs, unit demand, and budget \(p\), let \(C(y)\) be the minimum cost of routing one unit of \(s\)-to-\(t\) flow using capacity supplied by coalition mass \(y\), and set \(v_p(y)=\max\{0,\min(p,C(\mu-y))-C(y)\}\). Under measurable positive-mass objections and counter-objections, compute an anonymous stable payoff density \(x_t\) for the type masses, or determine that none exists.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic provider game with finite type support: type \(t\) has mass \(\mu_t\) and a service portfolio, per-capita capacity, and operating costs; a coalition of mass \(y\le\mu\) routes unit demand at minimum cost and earns value against the cheapest outside option capped by \(p\). The output is a type-level payoff density stable against positive-mass objections and counter-objections.

What the mirror covers

The stable-allocation question mirrors Theorem 1, while the proposed payment-limit question extends Theorem 3. The proposal leaves the paper's threshold characterization, empty-core example, and coalition-detection algorithm without a continuous counterpart.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can stability and counter-objection constraints be separated or optimized over efficiently as a function of the type support?
  • Under what conditions is the continuous stable imputation unique, and when does Theorem 1's formula have an analogue?
  • For a precisely defined replicated capacity market, does the total VCG payment converge to the continuous minimum stable budget?

The case FOR (proponent)

This paper has no named theorem that classifies a problem as polynomial-time or NP-hard. Its algorithmic contribution is instead carried by named structural results that it computes with. The strongest case for a mirror rests on Theorem 1 and Theorem 3, both proved in this paper; neither is cited as an external result.

The regime I would use is a large market for repeated, standardized cloud or managed-service workflows. There are many providers, but relatively few complete provider types: a type records the services a provider can supply, its operating costs, prices, and capacity. Providers of the same type are interchangeable for the market. The choreography DAG remains the paper’s object. As the provider population grows, capacity grows with it, while demand is normalized to one unit of executions. This is a high-multiplicity regime for vendors, not a claim that every network-routing instance is naturally nonatomic.

Here is a concrete continuous model. Let \(T\) be the finite support of provider types, with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to one. A unit of type-\(t\) mass supplies capacity \(q_{tw}\) for service \(w\), at operating cost \(c_{tw}\). For a coalition with mass vector \(y\), define \(C(y)\) as the minimum operating cost of serving one unit of demand using an \(s\)-to-\(t\) path flow in the DAG. This is a min-cost-flow LP: path flow through each service must equal the amount supplied there, and type \(t\)'s supply at \(w\) is at most \(y_tq_{tw}\). Set \(C(y)=\infty\) if the coalition cannot serve the demand. For budget \(p\), give the coalition value

\[ v_p(y)=\max\{0,\min(p,C(\mu-y))-C(y)\}, \]

with \(\min(p,\infty)=p\). This retains the paper’s surplus logic: the coalition earns value by offering a cheaper feasible enactment than the providers outside it, subject to the user’s budget.

For stability, coalitions are measurable portions of the provider population. An anonymous imputation assigns each type a payoff per unit mass \(x_t\), with \(x_t\ge 0\) and \(\sum_t\mu_tx_t=v_p(\mu)\). Use the paper’s objection and counter-objection idea over measurable coalitions: an objection reallocates a coalition’s value so its members do at least as well as under \(x\), with a positive-mass slice doing strictly better; a counter-objection must include a positive-mass slice of the objected-to side, exclude a positive-mass slice of the objectors, and afford members of the overlap at least what the objection promised. Stability means every objection has such a counter-objection. This makes the continuum version of bargaining-set stability explicit.

Theorem 1 is my lead anchor. It says, for \(A=\{j\in S:c_j\le p\}\), that the paper’s game has a unique stable imputation \(x\) under these conditions:

  • \(x_j=0\) for every \(j\in S\setminus A\);
  • for every \(j\in A\),

\[ x_j=\frac{v(S)}{|A|}+ \left(c^{-j}-\frac{\sum_{k\in A}c^{-k}}{|A|}\right); \]

  • for every \(j\in A\),

\[ c^{-j}\ge \frac{c_{SP}-p+\sum_{k\in A}c^{-k}}{|A|}. \]

Here \(c^{-j}\) is the cheapest route cost when player \(j\) is excluded, and \(c_{SP}\) is the shortest-path cost. The paper proves this result. Its continuous mirror is Continuous Stable-Alliance Allocation: given the DAG, finite provider-type support and masses, capacities, costs, and budget \(p\), find an anonymous imputation in the continuous bargaining set, or report that none exists; when the set is a singleton, return its payoff density \(x\). The solution is the stable payoff allocation itself, not an encoding of it.

Theorem 3 supplies a second, distinct question. In the paper’s finite lowest-cost routing setting, it states that the total payment of the truthful lowest-cost-path mechanism equals the minimum user budget \(p\) for which a stable coalition exists. The proof expresses that payment as
\[ \sum_{v\in P^*}c^{-v}-(|P^*|-1)c_{SP}, \]
where \(P^*\) is a shortest path and \(c^{-v}\) is the cheapest path cost avoiding \(v\). The paper proves this identity. Its continuous mirror is Continuous Stable-Budget / VCG Payment: on the same mass-capacity instance, compute
\[ p_{\min}=\inf\{p:B_p\ne\varnothing\}, \]
where \(B_p\) is the continuous bargaining set just defined, and compare it with the limit of total VCG pivot payments in finite replications of the market. In a replication with \(N\mu_t\) providers of type \(t\), scale each provider’s capacity and operating cost by \(1/N\); report whether the payment limit exists and whether it equals \(p_{\min}\). The solution is \(p_{\min}\), the payment limit if it exists, and the equality or gap between them.

I expect both questions to be Class A candidates. The route-cost subproblem is an LP, and the finite support gives a plausible route to separation-based algorithms for the continuum coalition constraints. That is a direction to prove, not a result already in the paper: if bargaining-set objections resist efficient separation, either question could instead expose continuum-specific hardness. Follow-up questions include how the answer depends on support size \(\tau\), whether the finite-provider solutions converge with useful error bounds, and which graph or capacity restrictions make the stability tests tractable.

The authors should recognize these as mirrors of their problem: they preserve the choreography DAG, cost-based coalition value, bargaining-set stability, and the minimum-budget question. The capacity scaling is a substantive co-modification, but it has a direct foothold in the paper’s conclusion, which identifies service capacities and multitenancy as future work. The argument also does not sell population continuization as novel: the gap is the computational study of stable alliances and their payments when the provider society is represented by type masses.

The weak point is that bargaining-set objections in the paper can turn on excluding a particular pivotal provider. In a nonatomic model, excluding one provider has zero mass; the mass version must decide what positive-mass deviations and counter-objections mean. The capacity model also changes a single chosen path into service flow for a normalized demand cohort. Those choices may affect the answer, and the paper’s finite formula should not be assumed to survive unchanged. Still, they are visible, problem-relevant changes that the authors’ own capacity and multitenancy agenda makes plausible—not a change of subject.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that the relevant population here is the vendors, not the users. A vendor is a player because it owns a particular bundle of service nodes; that bundle determines which routes its coalition can offer and which routes remain available outside it. A large number of users does not create high multiplicity in this game. A plausible high-multiplicity regime would need many small vendors with repeated portfolios and costs, and would likely need aggregate demand and service capacity to scale with their number. That regime can be imagined, but it is an extra market model, not a consequence of the paper’s routing model.

Theorem 1 is especially difficult to carry over faithfully. Its bargaining-set argument is built around individual providers: an objection is made by one player against another, and the formula uses each player’s exclusion cost \(c^{-j}\) and the count \(|A|\). In an atomless population, excluding one provider removes zero mass. Excluding a positive-mass slice can change the available routes, but then the theorem’s individual exclusion costs and payoff formula no longer describe the stability problem. The proponent’s capacity model makes that new problem coherent; it does not show that Theorem 1’s result has a natural continuous counterpart. A model that treats each type as one player would preserve finite coalition logic, but would discard the ability of only part of a type’s population to join an alliance.

Theorem 3 has a similar fidelity problem. Its equality relates the budget threshold to the sum of pivotal payments for vertices on one discrete shortest path. With replicated providers supplying fractional capacity, procurement becomes a flow-allocation problem. Individual pivot effects may vanish as the market grows, and the total-payment limit depends on how the replicated market and its allocations are defined. Comparing that limit with a continuum bargaining threshold could be a worthwhile question, but it compares newly specified objects; the paper’s identity does not itself supply the continuous model.

Those objections do not defeat the best scenario. Many small providers offering standardized service bundles, with capacity and demand scaled to market size, is plausible for the paper’s large-scale service setting. Its conclusion explicitly points toward capacities and multitenancy, which gives the proponent’s flow model a foothold the paper’s framing alone might not suggest. Types can preserve the complete relevant provider description, including portfolio, costs, and capacity. So identity and type aggregation are not decisive objections.

My negative case is strongest on fidelity, not on whether a worthwhile continuous question can be posed. The proposed versions would study a nonatomic capacity-allocation game whose bargaining stability and VCG payments need their own definitions. That makes them extensions of the paper’s path game rather than direct continuizations of its theorems. But the paper’s future-work direction makes that extension recognizable, and I cannot honestly rule out a plausible vendor regime. The universal claim that no worthwhile mirror exists is therefore weak; Theorem 1 is the more vulnerable anchor, while the capacity-based version of Theorem 3 remains a credible mirror candidate.
