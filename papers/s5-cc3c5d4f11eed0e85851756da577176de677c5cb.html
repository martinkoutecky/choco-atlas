The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is narrow: this paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational result. So it supplies no qualifying anchor under your rule, and I can’t honestly claim that a cohort problem below is a mirror of one of its named theorems.

The closest computational result is the minimax-regret formulation in Eqs. (10)–(11). The paper defines a feasible product \(x\)’s maximum regret against all feasible alternatives and utility functions consistent with the elicited constraints, then minimizes that maximum regret over feasible products. Under local constraints on GAI parameters, it gives a linear MIP formulation using auxiliary variables; it handles the large constraint set by iteratively generating violated constraints. The authors compute with these formulations in constrained configuration and product-database examples. This is a real algorithmic anchor in the informal sense, but it is not a numbered theorem or a complexity classification.

A candidate population mirror is Cohort GAI Minimax-Regret Configuration\(_\infty\). Consider a large customer cohort choosing from a common finite product catalog \(F\), such as a rental service selecting one standard vehicle configuration to recommend to its customer base. A type \(t\) is a group sharing the same latent GAI utility and the same elicitation information; the platform represents that utility by a rational polytope \(U_t\) of semantically normalized GAI parameters, as in the paper. The input lists the \(\tau\) supported types with rational masses \(\mu_t\), where \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), along with their GAI models and the catalog or its feasibility constraints. The number of customers may be large while \(\tau\) is small.

The decision variable is one common recommendation \(x\in F\). For each type, let
\[ \rho_t(x)=\max_{u\in U_t}\left(\max_{z\in F}u(z)-u(x)\right). \]
The task is to find an \(x\in F\) minimizing \(\sum_t\mu_t\rho_t(x)\): the population-weighted average of each type’s worst-case regret. A solution is the recommended catalog item. A prover could be asked to classify exact and additive-approximation complexity in the number of attributes, factor scopes, support size \(\tau\), and input bit length, with \(F\) represented either explicitly or by constraints.

This mirrors the paper’s regret-based choice from a constrained configuration space, rather than its query-selection strategy. My expectation is hardness inherited from the underlying minimax-regret choice problem, if that finite-outcome problem is hard: the restriction to one supported type, \(\mu_1=1\), is exactly the paper’s MMR decision problem. The paper itself establishes no hardness result, though, so that prediction is not proved by an anchor here. The question also leads to natural extensions: allocate query effort across types, or allow type-specific recommendations under limited product supply.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper studies one decision maker’s preference uncertainty, not a population choosing collectively. A shared recommendation and a mass-weighted welfare objective add an inter-user aggregation choice that the authors may regard as a different problem. The paper’s normalized utility scale makes the average-regret objective definable, but does not establish that this is the right way to compare regret across types. So this is a plausible, concrete extension of its MMR computation—not a strong claim that the paper already contains a population result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection comes before modeling: this paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing a computational complexity result. Equations (10)–(11) give MIP formulations, and Section 5 reports experiments, but neither supplies the kind of complexity result the proposed mirror was supposed to anchor. The proponent acknowledges this. Their candidate therefore extends the paper’s computational technique rather than continuizing one of its named results.

The best version of that extension is a cohort choosing one shared product from a finite catalog. Give each of \(\tau\) utility types a rational mass \(\mu_t\) and an elicited utility set \(U_t\), then choose \(x\) to minimize \(\sum_t \mu_t \rho_t(x)\), where \(\rho_t(x)\) is that type’s worst-case regret. This is a well-posed population problem. But the paper’s uncertainty set represents one user’s incomplete knowledge of their own utility. For a homogeneous cohort, the masses have no effect on that problem. To make population composition matter, the model must add heterogeneous users and a rule for aggregating their regrets. Average regret is one possible rule; the paper’s normalization of each user’s utility to \([0,1]\) does not establish that those utilities are comparable across people. Other aggregation rules would pose different computational questions.

Alternatives do not preserve the paper’s central problem. Giving each type its own recommendation makes the decisions independent unless one adds shared supply constraints; adding those constraints turns the task into a resource-allocation problem. Aggregating utilities before computing regret still requires a social objective that the paper does not specify. These may be worthwhile research questions, but they introduce the collective decision problem that a continuous mirror would need to inherit from the paper.

So the case against is strong for this paper, though it cannot rule out every adjacent cohort model: the sole candidate is a plausible new population extension, not a continuous version of a named computational result here.
