The continuous mirror question

Given a set of alternatives \(A\), distinct complete rankings \(\succ_t\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(B\), decide whether there is a linear order \(\succ\) such that \(\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathrm{KT}(\succ_t,\succ)\le B\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete rankings of \(A\), each carrying mass \(\mu_t\); the decision variable is one output ranking \(\succ\), and the objective is its expected Kendall–tau distance from the population.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s \(\mathrm{NP}\)-completeness result for three voters and the corresponding fixed-voter hardness implication. It leaves the paper’s further implications for other aggregation rules and winner determination outside this anchor.

Open questions for a prover

  • Which restrictions on the alternatives or support rankings make exact aggregation tractable?
  • What approximation guarantees are achievable for rationally weighted societies?

The case FOR (proponent)

The cleanest mirror is Kemeny rank aggregation with the electorate represented directly by a distribution over complete rankings. A ranking is a type because it contains all the information this objective uses; its mass is the fraction of the population holding that ranking. The output remains one complete ranking. The new input object is the population distribution.

Call the decision problem Continuous Kemeny Rank Aggregation. An instance consists of alternatives \(A\), distinct rankings \(\succ_t\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and a rational threshold \(B\). It asks whether there is a linear order \(\succ\) such that \(\sum_t \mu_t\,\mathrm{KT}(\succ_t,\succ)\le B\). A solution is such an order; the optimization version minimizes this expected Kendall–tau distance. Its natural parameters include \(m=|A|\), support size \(\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\).

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: KEMENY SCORE with three voters is NP-complete. The instance is a set of alternatives, three linear orders, and an integer \(b\); the question is whether some output order has \( \mathrm{Kemeny}_2 \) score at most \(b\). Here that score charges each pair the majority margin when the output order contradicts the majority. The paper proves hardness by reduction from SIMPLE MAX CUT with long even edges.

The continuous problem contains those instances exactly. Give each of the three rankings mass \(1/3\). With three voters, every pair has a \(2\)-to-\(1\) majority, so the sum of Kendall–tau distances is \( \binom{m}{2}+\mathrm{Kemeny}_2(\succ) \). Thus the continuous threshold \(B=(\binom{m}{2}+b)/3\) gives a yes-instance exactly when the paper’s instance does. The mirror is NP-hard even with \(\tau=3\), so an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) would imply \( \mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP} \).

A plausible regime is a large ranking platform or membership organization in which people rank the same expanding slate of options and fall into a few recurring, complete ranking patterns—for example, three stable policy-priority blocs. As the population grows, the number of people can greatly exceed the number of distinct rankings: take \(N=3q\) people, with \(q\) in each bloc. Their empirical society has masses \(1/3,1/3,1/3\), and its normalized Kemeny objective has exactly the same minimizers as the three-voter objective. The reduction’s particular rankings are constructed gadgets, as one expects in a worst-case proof; the proposed population regime is the many-people, few-ranking-types setting those instances represent.

This is a credible mirror of the authors’ problem: the input preferences, aggregation objective, and single-ranking output are preserved, while the population is expressed in proportions. The result points to Class B: the hardness transfers directly from the discrete problem, and comes from the growing agenda rather than population multiplicity. It also raises useful follow-up questions: how do algorithms behave when \(\tau\) is bounded beyond three; which restrictions on the alternatives or rankings make the problem tractable; and what approximation guarantees hold for rationally weighted societies?

The soft spot is that this case demonstrates a faithful high-multiplicity formulation and transferred hardness, not a new continuum-driven algorithmic phenomenon. The hard instances already use just three types with equal masses, so the positive case rests on the naturalness of the population model and the exact embedding—not on fractional masses making the problem easier.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this mirror adds no new optimization structure: it is Kemeny aggregation with ballot weights normalized to sum to one. The paper’s hard instances already have three ranking types, each with mass \(1/3\). For three voters, every pair has a \(2\)-to-\(1\) majority, so
\[ \sum_{t=1}^{3}\frac13\,\mathrm{KT}(\succ_t,\succ) =\frac{\binom{m}{2}+\mathrm{Kemeny}_2(\succ)}{3}. \]
Thus the paper’s threshold \(b\) maps directly to \(B=(\binom{m}{2}+b)/3\). Replicating each ballot \(q\) times gives the same society as a population of \(3q\) people in three equal blocs. The theorem therefore supplies hardness for the continuous formulation without any new continuous machinery or population-sensitive effect.

That criticism does not defeat the anchor under ChoCo’s stated standard. Three large blocs with shared complete rankings are a coherent high-multiplicity regime, and the output ranking aggregates their preferences. The mirror has population as its continuous object; it does not depend on fractional outcomes or on tracking individuals. Calling the result a weighted restatement, or observing that its hardness comes from the agenda, does not make the question unworthy by the programme’s rules.

The stronger version—allowing arbitrary rational masses and asking for exact Kemeny aggregation—survives as well. The three-type instances already rule out an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\) unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). That leaves room to study restrictions, approximations, and other parameter regimes, even if the general exact problem has an immediate Class B answer.

So the honest negative case is weak: the mirror may yield a modest result because the paper’s theorem transfers almost by rescaling, but I cannot make a sound case that no worthwhile mirror exists. Theorem 1’s anchor survives.
