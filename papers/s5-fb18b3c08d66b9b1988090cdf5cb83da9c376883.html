The continuous mirror question

Given finite agent types \(A\) and item types \(O\), masses \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), common weak preferences and priorities, and a mass matching \(p\) with row sums \(\alpha\) and column sums \(\beta\), decide whether \(p\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal S_\infty)\), where \(\mathcal S_\infty\) consists of type-incidence limits of weakly stable perfect matchings in compatible \(H\)-copy markets as \(H\to\infty\); if yes, produce the finite lottery decomposition.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity two-sided matching market with agent and item types carrying masses \(\alpha_\theta\) and \(\beta_\omega\). The input is an aggregate coupling \(p_{\theta\omega}\); the decision variables are lottery weights \(\lambda_\ell\) and limiting stable type-incidence matrices \(M^\ell\), with objective feasibility of \(p=\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell M^\ell\).

The objection that survived

The type-mass matrix \(p_{\theta\omega}\) does not uniquely determine individual support, so the chosen block-symmetric lift may not preserve every support-sensitive ex-post property of the finite problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers weak ex-post stability and the hardness phenomenon of Theorem 3.8 in a high-multiplicity population. It leaves the paper's robust and strongly stable variants, the integer-programming optimization result, and the finite identity-level support variants untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Characterize \(\mathcal S_\infty\) and determine whether it has a finite-dimensional polyhedral description.
  • Decide whether type-mass feasibility is equivalent to ex-post stability of the canonical block-symmetric lift.
  • Determine whether the X3C integrality obstruction disappears under high multiplicity or whether stability preserves continuum-specific hardness.
  • Develop algorithms or separation procedures and finite-\(H\) rounding guarantees.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s weak ex-post stability problem. I would call it \(\mathrm{HM\text{-}ExPostStable}_\infty\).

There are finite sets \(A\) and \(O\) of agent types and item types. An agent type \(\theta\in A\) is a complete preference profile: every agent of that type has the same weak preference over item types. An item type \(\omega\in O\) is likewise a complete priority profile over agent types. Let \(\alpha_\theta\) and \(\beta_\omega\) be their masses, with \(\sum_\theta\alpha_\theta=\sum_\omega\beta_\omega=1\). To mirror Theorem 3.8, every preference and priority list may have length at most three and have at most two indifference classes.

The population consists of \(H\alpha_\theta\) copies of each agent type and \(H\beta_\omega\) copies of each item type, for arbitrarily large compatible \(H\). Thus \(H\) is the population size while \(|A|+|O|\) is the number of distinct types. The intended regime is \(H\gg |A|+|O|\): for example, a large school-choice or resident-matching market with many students sharing the same three acceptable institutions and many seats sharing the same coarse priority profile.

The input target is a mass matching \(p\), where \(p_{\theta\omega}\geq 0\) is the mass of type-\(\theta\) agents assigned to type-\(\omega\) items. It satisfies \(\sum_\omega p_{\theta\omega}=\alpha_\theta\) and \(\sum_\theta p_{\theta\omega}=\beta_\omega\). This is not merely an outcome lottery over named individuals: it is the aggregate assignment of a continuous population. Expanded to \(H\) copies, it corresponds to a block-symmetric random matching in which a type-\(\theta\) agent receives type \(\omega\) with conditional probability \(p_{\theta\omega}/\alpha_\theta\).

For each \(H\), take an ordinary deterministic weakly stable matching of the \(H\)-copy market and record its normalized type-incidence matrix \(M^H\). Let \(\mathcal S_\infty\) be the set of all limits of such matrices as \(H\to\infty\). The continuous question is:

\[ p\in \operatorname{conv}(\mathcal S_\infty)? \]

Equivalently, does there exist a finite lottery \(\{(\lambda_\ell,M^\ell)\}_{\ell=1}^L\), with \(M^\ell\in\mathcal S_\infty\), \(\lambda_\ell\geq 0\), \(\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell=1\), and \(p=\sum_\ell\lambda_\ell M^\ell\)? A solution is such a decomposition; the objective is feasibility. By Carathéodory, one needs at most \(|A||O|+1\) components. Crucially, \(M^\ell\) must be limits of actual weakly stable matchings, not merely matrices satisfying a newly invented fractional stability condition.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3.8, proved in this paper:

Deciding whether a random matching \(p\) is ex-post stable is NP-complete, even if all preference and priority lists have length at most three and are dichotomous.

The theorem is proved by reduction from Exact Cover by 3-Sets, with every element occurring in exactly three sets. Proposition 3.9 establishes the forward direction: if the constructed \(p\) is ex-post stable, then the X3C instance has an exact cover. Proposition 3.10 establishes the reverse direction: if an exact cover exists, then \(p=\frac{1}{3}(M_1+M_2+M_3)\) for three deterministic weakly stable matchings. The \(G\)-gadgets switch between covering and idle states, while the \(H\)-gadgets enforce that all three positions of a set are selected together.

The mirror is faithful in exactly the way the programme requires. It retains the two-sided market, weak stability, indifferences, short lists, and ex-post implementability. The only systematic change is that each complete type has many interchangeable copies and the target assignment is stated in mass rather than person-by-person notation. The paper’s own school-choice motivation makes this recognizable: students with the same coarse priority and preference profile are exchangeable, as are seats with the same priority profile.

I would provisionally expect this mirror to be Class A, although proving that is an open problem. The reason is visible in the paper’s X3C gadget. In the finite construction, a set is either selected or not selected in a stable matching. With \(H\) interchangeable copies, different copies of the same set type can occupy the two states. If \(q_j\) is the mass of copies of set \(C_j\) in the covering state, the exact-cover constraints relax to

\[ 0\leq q_j\leq 1,\qquad \sum_{j:a_i\in C_j}q_j=1 \]

for every element \(a_i\). Since each element occurs in three sets, \(q_j=\frac13\) for every set satisfies these equations, even when no integral exact cover exists. The integrality obstruction exploited by Propositions 3.9 and 3.10 therefore appears to dissolve under population continuization. The local gadget states and their mass-conservation constraints look suitable for a configuration LP or flow formulation.

The further questions are substantial: characterize \(\mathcal S_\infty\); determine whether it equals an appropriate fractional-stability polytope; obtain a polynomial-time separation or optimization algorithm; and quantify how solutions round back to finite markets. One could also study the maximum implementable stable mass when \(p\notin\operatorname{conv}(\mathcal S_\infty)\), paralleling the paper’s integer-programming formulation.

The weakest point is that the reduction’s incidence structure still grows with the X3C instance. It is possible that global synchronization across type classes preserves a different, continuum-specific hardness even after the \(0/1\) selector becomes fractional. Nor is it yet proved that every fractional selector can be realized by stable high-multiplicity matchings. But that is a genuine research question about the continuous population model, not a reason to reject the mirror. The mirror emphatically covers Theorem 3.8 and its weak ex-post stability problem; it does not claim to continuize every result in the paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 3.8 is the only convincing anchor, but the proposed mirror does not preserve the paper’s defining object.

The paper asks whether a particular random matching \(p\) lies in

\[ \operatorname{conv}\{M:\ M\text{ is weakly stable and }\operatorname{supp}(M)\subseteq\operatorname{supp}(p)\}. \]

Thus ex-post stability depends crucially on the support of \(p\) over individual agent–item pairs. A type-mass matrix \(p_{\theta\omega}\) does not contain that information. The same type masses can be lifted to a fully mixed random matching, in which every agent of type \(\theta\) may receive every item of type \(\omega\), or to a sparse block matching permitting only a few permutations. Those lifts can have different ex-post decompositions. Choosing the fully mixed lift is a substantive relaxation, not a consequence of continuization.

The obvious repair is to retain the full coupling—some measurable kernel describing which individual agents can receive which individual items. But then the finite type space no longer represents the input: the kernel can encode arbitrarily fine identity-level combinatorics. If one suppresses that information to retain a finite type description, one has changed the problem from ex-post implementation to a quotient assignment problem.

The proponent’s Exact-Cover argument does not repair this. The equations

\[ 0\le q_j\le 1,\qquad \sum_{j:a_i\in C_j}q_j=1 \]

show only that a fractional selector would satisfy the obvious mass-balance constraints. They do not show that a sequence of \(H\)-copy matchings realizing those \(q_j\) is weakly stable. In the finite proof, the “selected or unselected” conclusion is enforced by blocking pairs. With \(H\) copies, a covering copy and an idle copy of the same nominal type may themselves create cross-copy blocking pairs. Establishing that such mixtures are realizable is precisely the missing theorem. If they are not realizable, the proposed relaxation does not dissolve the gadget; if they are, that is because stability has effectively been replaced by a mass-flow condition.

Indeed, the natural mass-flow condition is already the paper’s fractional stability notion:

\[ \sum_{\omega'\succeq_\theta\omega}p_{\theta\omega'} + \sum_{\theta'\succeq_\omega\theta}p_{\theta'\omega} - p_{\theta\omega} \geq \alpha_\theta. \]

The paper explicitly distinguishes this from ex-post stability when ties are present. Thus there is a forced choice. Either the mirror uses these linear mass inequalities, in which case it is the already-studied fractional matching problem rather than a mirror of Theorem 3.8; or it retains the distinction between fractional stability and ex-post implementability, in which case the type-mass input is insufficient and the proposed finite-type formulation is incomplete.

The school-choice interpretation does not remove this tension. If students and seats really are interchangeable within types, aggregate type masses are the honest object, but the individual lottery-support constraint largely disappears. If individual priorities, lottery numbers, eligibility, or correlations matter to implementation, those features must be included in the complete type, and the purported high-multiplicity compression loses its force.

So the proponent has identified a plausible research direction, but not yet a faithful continuous mirror of the paper’s central theorem. The negative case is not a proof that no carefully designed high-multiplicity matching problem could be worthwhile. Its stronger point is that this paper’s hardness result is about support-sensitive decomposability of a finite assignment, whereas the proposed continuization either forgets that information or reduces to fractional stability already present in the paper and its cited literature.
