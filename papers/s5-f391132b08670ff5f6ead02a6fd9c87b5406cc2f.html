The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is a computational follow-up, not a mirror anchored in a computational result of this paper. There are no named results here establishing that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifying its computational complexity. Theorems 1 and 2 and Corollaries 5.3 and 6.2 are statistical generalization results; Theorems 3 and 4 are axiomatic impossibility results. The paper itself lists computational tractability as future work. So none qualifies as the required computational anchor.

The closest named result is Corollary 6.2, proved in the paper. It says that for finite \(I\) and any bounded scoring rule \(s\), the mechanism \(f_s\) that selects a candidate profile maximizing average score on the sample satisfies
\[ \Pr\!\left[U^s_{D_I,M}(f_s(S))\ge -2\epsilon+\max_{C\in\mathcal C}U^s_{D_I,M}(C)\right]\ge 1-\delta \]
whenever
\[ |S|\ge \frac{c}{\epsilon^2}\left(|I|N\log N+\log\frac1\delta\right) \]
for a constant \(c>0\). This supports the relevance of optimizing a representative profile, but it says nothing about the time needed to compute that profile.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a recurring legislative docket: millions of constituents, a finite set of issues, and many residents sharing the same complete preference profile over the docket. Let each such profile be a type \(t\in\mathcal T\), with rational population share \(\mu_t\), and let \(D_I(i)\) be the saliency of issue \(i\). Then \(n\) can be much larger than the support size \(\tau=|\mathcal T|\). A type records every preference distinction used by the problem, so people with different relevant preferences belong to different types.

The follow-on problem I would put to a prover is Continuous Representative Winner Determination. Its instance is a finite issue set \(I\), \(N\) outcomes per issue, a finite support \(\mathcal T\subseteq LO(N)^I\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\), a rational saliency distribution \(D_I\), and a bounded rational scoring rule \(s\). Give the feasible candidate space a succinct representation: a circuit \(P(x,i)\), for \(x\in\{0,1\}^d\), that returns the ranking \(C_x(i)\) of outcomes for issue \(i\). The question is to output some \(C_x\) maximizing
\[ U_s(C_x)=\sum_{i\in I}D_I(i)\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\mu_t\,s\bigl(t(i),C_x(i)\bigr). \]
The relevant input parameters include \(|I|, N, \tau\), the rational bit length, and the circuit size. This is the direct population-score counterpart of the sample-scoring mechanism in Section 6.1.

For that unrestricted succinct policy representation, I would expect hardness to transfer from the policy or issue-search component: the combinatorics can remain when \(\tau=1\), so population continuization alone need not remove it. Structured candidate classes might instead admit tractable optimization. Natural next questions are whether additive approximation is easier, which policy structures enable efficient optimization or pricing, and how runtime depends on \(\tau\) and the policy description.

This case covers only the scoring-mechanism branch around Corollary 6.2. Its strongest point is that the authors explicitly identify computational tractability as open, and their framework already supplies population utility and a scoring objective. Its weakest point is decisive under the requested anchor rule: the optimization problem above is a proposed research question, not a computational result proved in the paper. Also, the paper already models the population as a distribution, possibly over an infinite set of profiles; continuization itself is not new here. The defensible positive claim is that a computational complexity study of this population-based optimization is a natural gap—not that this paper contains a named complexity result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Corollary 6.2 does not give a computational result to mirror. It bounds the generalization error of a scoring mechanism; it does not classify the cost of finding that mechanism’s output. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are learning bounds, and Theorems 3 and 4 are axiomatic impossibility results. None supplies the required complexity anchor.

The proposed winner-determination problem changes the setup in a consequential way. Corollary 6.2’s mechanism receives the sample \(S\); the proposed problem receives the true population distribution \(\mu\) and maximizes population score. If instead \(\mu\) is the empirical distribution of \(S\), weighted optimization gives exactly the same objective as sample-score maximization. Grouping repeated observations is then lossless compression, not a new continuous model. Either version could motivate research, but neither mirrors a computational result in this paper.

The circuit representation for \(\mathcal C\) also adds the main computational ingredient. With a succinct policy class, complexity depends on how that class is represented and optimized; with an unconstrained candidate space, the paper gives no algorithmic description to analyze. A more structured class might yield a useful algorithm, but that would be a new computational study of representative social choice, not a continuous mirror of Corollary 6.2.

So the negative case is strong on the exercise’s anchor test: no proposed anchor survives as a mirror of a named computational result. It is weaker as the broader claim that no worthwhile study could arise in any scenario. The authors themselves flag computational tractability as future work, and a carefully chosen candidate class could make that follow-up valuable. I would argue against calling it a ChoCo mirror of this paper, while conceding that the paper may motivate adjacent computational work.
