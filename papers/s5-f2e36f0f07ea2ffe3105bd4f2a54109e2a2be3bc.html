The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses on finite sets of complete worker and firm types, contract templates joining those types, weak preferences over templates, and per-person laminar integral quotas, does there exist a polynomial-time \(3/2\)-approximation for maximum stable contract mass? An allocation gives each type a distribution over feasible integral multisets of template instances; for every template \(e=(a,b)\), the total mass of its worker-side and firm-side instances must agree and be realizable by coupling the atomless populations. Let \(r_{a,e}\) be the mass of type \(a\) whose bundle can add an instance of \(e\), or replace a strictly less-preferred instance with \(e\), while respecting its quotas. Stability requires \(r_{a,e}=0\) or \(r_{b,e}=0\) for every template. The algorithm should return a stable allocation with total contract mass at least \(2/3\) of the maximum, using time polynomial in the type and template counts, input bit length, and maximum nesting depth, with a sparse bundle-mixture output.

The model it lives in

An atomless population partitioned into complete types with rational masses; individuals receive integral feasible bundles, while each type’s mass may be distributed among bundles. Equal template-level contract mass is coupled across endpoints, blocking requires positive free mass at both endpoint types, and the objective is total matched contract mass.

The objection that survived

Equal template-level masses and positive free masses do not yet prove that integral bundles can be globally realized as matches, especially when a person can hold several contracts of one template; the output encoding also needs specification.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s \(3/2\)-approximation guarantee for maximum stable matching. It leaves Theorem 17’s worker-strategy-proof mechanism and the paper’s other results unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the laminar-matroid structure support a polynomial-time \(3/2\)-approximation for this mass-based stability model?
  • Can the maximum stable mass be optimized exactly, or does stability’s endpoint disjunction preserve hardness in the type and template structure?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for an instance of \(\textsc{max-smti-lc}\), the algorithm finds a stable matching \(M\) with \(|M|\ge \frac{2}{3}|M_{\mathrm{OPT}}|\) in \(O(k|E|^2)\) time, where \(k\) is the maximum nesting depth of the laminar constraints. It is a good anchor because it concerns the paper’s central computational problem: maximizing the size of a stable matching when agents have ties and laminar quotas. The proof’s three-copy construction and its projection back to the original instance give a concrete route to investigate in a population model.

A plausible regime is a large hiring or placement exchange with many applicants and many employer teams or sites. Applicants share a relatively small number of complete profiles: the same rankings, eligibility, and relevant quota data. Teams likewise share profiles, including rankings and nested hiring quotas by role, location, or credential. Contract templates connect these types and describe the role or project being matched. There might be millions of people but only hundreds of profiles. The template is instantiated between many distinct people; its mass is the fraction of the population matched through that kind of contract.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover: Continuous Maximum Stable Matching with Ties and Laminar Constraints. The input is a finite set \(T\) of worker and firm types, with rational masses \(\mu_a>0\) summing to \(1\); a finite set \(E\) of contract templates, each joining a worker type to a firm type; and, for each type \(a\), a weak preference order on its templates and a laminar family \(\mathcal L_a\) with integral per-person quotas \(q_a\). Assume a finite per-person capacity, represented by a quota on the full set of templates. A type is complete: its members share all preferences, quotas, and other data the problem uses.

A solution assigns each person an integral feasible bundle of contracts. For each type \(a\), let \(p_{a,B}\) be the fraction of that type receiving bundle \(B\); the bundle respects that type’s laminar quotas. Thus the population can split across bundles, but an individual’s assignment is not fractional. For each template \(e\), the worker-side and firm-side assigned masses must agree; call that common mass \(x_e\). A template is free for a person if they can add an instance of it feasibly, or replace a strictly less-preferred contract feasibly. Let \(r_{a,e}\) be the mass of type \(a\) for whom \(e\) is free. The allocation is stable exactly when, for every \(e\), at least one endpoint type has \(r_{a,e}=0\): otherwise positive masses on both sides could form more contracts of that template. The objective is to maximize \(\sum_{e\in E}x_e\). The algorithmic question mirroring Theorem 2 is whether one can return a stable allocation with mass at least \(\frac{2}{3}\) of the maximum stable mass in time polynomial in the number of types, templates, input bit length, and nesting depth, without expanding the population into named agents.

I expect this approximation question to be Class A. The paper’s guarantee comes from structure in the stability condition and laminar matroids, not from enumerating agents; per-person quotas scale naturally with type mass, and continuous optimization may make the type-level bundle mixtures manageable. That is a reason to investigate a \(3/2\)-approximation, not a claim that the paper’s algorithm already proves one for this model. The stability condition couples the bundle distributions on the two sides, so adapting the three-copy argument or finding an appropriate separation method is real work. Further questions include whether the continuous optimum is exactly solvable, whether the discrete high-multiplicity optima converge to it, and where the \(3/2\) guarantee stops improving.

The weakest point is the type regime: if preferences or quotas depend on named counterparts, the number of complete types may grow with the population and the computational benefit disappears. The mirror also deliberately treats a type’s mass as splittable across integral bundles; that is the population continuization, but it can change which stable allocations exist. I would cover Theorem 2’s approximation result only. The paper’s worker-strategy-proof result, Theorem 17, needs a separate account of individual misreports in a continuum and should not be smuggled into this mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Theorem 2 as an anchor is that its algorithm works on a finite graph of named contracts, while a type-level mirror must decide what a contract template means among many interchangeable people. If “free mass” at both endpoints is meant to imply a block, the model must also show that those masses can be paired consistently with everyone’s integral bundles. That is a real modeling and algorithmic step; the three-copy construction does not supply it.

But this does not defeat the stronger mirror the proponent gives. With complete types, a template connects interchangeable worker and firm types; bundle distributions record which fractions of each type receive each feasible integral bundle; and positive free mass at both endpoints can form a positive-mass block. This makes the objective total matched mass, with no need to track named individuals. It is a coherent population continuization, and the unresolved pairing and complexity questions are part of the research problem rather than reasons it is not worth asking.

The available objections do not carry further. Theorem 2 is explicitly a computational approximation result. Large placement markets with repeated worker and firm profiles are a plausible high-multiplicity regime. The objective does not depend on who specifically receives a contract, and positive-mass blocking avoids the zero-measure problem. The paper’s high-multiplicity neighbors support the regime; they do not already answer this continuous computational question.

So I cannot honestly make a strong universal negative case. The best criticism is that the proposed mirror needs a careful formal account of realizable pairings and output representation. That may narrow or reshape the question, but it does not show that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
