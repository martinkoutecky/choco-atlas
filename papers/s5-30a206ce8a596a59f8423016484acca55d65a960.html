The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result of the required kind, so I can’t give an honest continuous problem per anchor. Its numbered results characterize farsighted solution sets; they do not classify a computational problem as tractable or hard.

The closest candidate is Proposition 3, proved in this paper: for the pairs competition problem under the Knuth swapping effectiveness function, the largest consistent set is the singleton containing the positive assortative matching. Proposition 2, also proved here, says that in a particular three-pair example under the standard effectiveness function, the largest consistent set is the set of all matchings. Both are substantive results, but neither is a complexity result. Treating Proposition 3 as “in P” because one can sort abilities would be an inference, not an anchor the paper states.

A plausible population regime would be a large contest with many male and female participants drawn from a small set of ability types. A matching could be represented by masses \(\pi_{ij}\) paired across ability types \(i\) and \(j\), and the question might be whether the largest consistent set contains only the assortative allocation under a mass-based version of Knuth swapping. But the paper’s results concern deviations by individual pairs. In an atomless population, such a deviation moves zero mass; allowing positive-mass deviations instead changes the stability model. That is the main obstacle to a credible mirror, quite apart from the lack of a computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is about fit with ChoCo’s computational question. Propositions 2 and 3 are the proponent’s best anchors, but neither states the complexity of a computational problem. Proposition 2 establishes that, in one calibrated \(3\times3\) example, the largest consistent set is the set of all matchings. Proposition 3 characterizes that set for the paper’s contest model under Knuth swapping. The paper gives no algorithmic input-output task, complexity classification, or optimization problem whose computational landscape it charts. Calling Proposition 3 “in P” would add a problem the paper never asks.

The natural stronger mirror is easy to state. Suppose a contest has many participants in a few ability classes, with each class sharing all relevant payoffs. Represent matchings by a coupling \(\pi_{ij}\) between male and female ability classes, and define positive-mass swaps between its entries. One could ask whether the largest consistent set under this mass-based Knuth rule contains only the assortative allocation, or ask for an algorithm to compute that set. That is a coherent high-multiplicity scenario. The paper’s cited continuum-agent work also makes a blanket claim that these populations are implausible untenable.

But this stronger version does not simply carry over either proposition as a computational mirror. Keeping individual-pair deviations in an atomless population changes no aggregate state. Replacing them with positive-mass deviations fixes that problem, but changes the deviation primitive and gives LCS an uncountable state space of couplings. The paper’s results concern finite sequences of deviations among individual matchings; they do not specify how to encode or compute a mass-level LCS. A decision problem such as “is the mass-level LCS a singleton?” could be developed, but it would be a new computational project layered onto the paper, rather than a complexity question exposed by one of its results.

So the negative case is strongest as a reason to leave this paper out of a programme focused on computational mirrors: the paper’s contribution is a solution-set characterization, and a continuous version naturally asks for another characterization unless one supplies an independently motivated algorithmic task. It is not a strong universal case. The finite-type contest scenario is sensible, and mass swaps offer a plausible model authors might recognize. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario; I would say the paper itself does not provide the computational anchor needed to make one an evident ChoCo mirror.
