What the mirror covers

The proposed mass-average trajectory formulation tracks Theorem 1 only; it leaves aside Theorem 2's expected-welfare analysis, Lemma 3's interaction bound, and the broader non-modular discussion.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest lead is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for a graph \(G\) of maximum degree \(\Delta\), if each agent’s value for a good is drawn independently from a common distribution \(D_r\), with values distinct, then the expected length of that good’s trajectory is at most \(\Delta^2\). The paper’s proof supports blind strategies and the max strategy; its closing caveat warns against extending the result to every conceivable heuristic. This is a named result about the number of steps in a resource allocation process, though it is not a standard complexity classification such as NP-hardness or membership in P. The paper contains no named result of that kind, so there is no hardness anchor here.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large system made of many copies of the same small exchange neighborhood: for example, repeated local allocation groups in a distributed logistics system. Fix a finite graph \(H=(V,E)\) with maximum degree \(\Delta\), and let there be \(q\) copies of it. Each copy has one indivisible good, initially held by the same role \(v_0\). A copy’s profile \(\theta\) specifies the values of all its agents for that good. For a finite rational type model, take \( |V| \) distinct rational values and distribute them uniformly over the roles. This gives a finite set of profile types. Uniform random rankings have the same distribution as the rankings induced by independent draws from any atomless common value distribution, and the trajectory depends on these values only through their comparisons.

An agent’s complete type is \((\theta,v)\): the copy’s full valuation profile and the agent’s role \(v\) in \(H\). Including \(\theta\) retains the within-neighborhood information that determines which trades are rational. If the profile mass is \(\mu_\theta\), then each role type \((\theta,v)\) has agent mass \(\mu_\theta/|V|\). As \(q\) grows, the number of agents \(q|V|\) grows while this finite type support stays fixed. Each copy still allocates its good integrally; only the aggregate mass of copies at each profile and allocation state becomes fractional.

The resulting problem, which I would name Continuum Mean Trajectory Length, is: given \(H\), an initial holder \(v_0\), an admissible strategy \(\sigma\), and rational masses \(\mu_\theta\) over complete copy profiles, determine the mass-average number of transfers per good,

\[ \bar L=\sum_\theta \mu_\theta\,\ell_\sigma(H,\theta,v_0), \]

where \(\ell_\sigma(H,\theta,v_0)\) is the trajectory length in a copy with profile \(\theta\). Equivalently, one can ask the threshold question “is \(\bar L\le B\)?” for a supplied rational \(B\). The anchor’s distribution is the uniform mass distribution over distinct-value rankings; for that case, Theorem 1 gives \(\bar L\le\Delta^2\). This is my lead mirror because it preserves the paper’s object—the trajectory of an indivisible good through individually rational deals—while making the population itself the mass distribution over repeated local environments.

I expect the anchored bound question to fall on the tractable side: the paper proves the uniform bound by a structural argument about bounded degree and random rankings. It is not a hardness-transfer result, since the anchor supplies no discrete hardness to transfer. Exact evaluation for a compactly represented distribution, arbitrary correlations between agents’ values, and bounds for strategies beyond those justified in the paper are further questions; Theorem 1 does not settle them.

The mirror covers the trajectory-length result, not the paper’s full study of welfare or synergies. Its weakest point is the repeated-neighborhood regime: it assumes many local copies that do not trade across copy boundaries, and its complete types retain the joint profile of a neighborhood rather than only marginal frequencies of individual roles. That is a real restriction. Still, large systems built from repeated local exchange structures are plausible, and the construction does not fractionalize the good or change the trade rule. The original authors could reasonably recognize it as a population-level version of their trajectory question. The paper’s random-value expectation is not already a continuous population model: it randomizes values in a finite society. Here, the society itself is the distribution over complete local profiles.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the paper’s strongest anchor, but it is a probabilistic bound on the behavior of a negotiation process, not a complexity result for a computational problem. The paper does not ask an algorithm to choose an intervention, optimize a trajectory, or decide a threshold. The proposed “Continuum Mean Trajectory Length” supplies that algorithmic question after the fact.

For the proposed repeated copies of \(H\), the question reduces to a weighted sum of independent finite processes. Given the explicit profile support, simulate each profile’s trajectory and compute \(\bar L\) in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the size of \(H\). The masses never interact, and no continuous optimization or high-multiplicity algorithm is needed. This construction is coherent as an ensemble of local systems, but its tractability comes from averaging independent instances, not from making one interacting society continuous. The paper’s expectation over random values is not itself prior continuous-population work; the point is that replacing that expectation with explicit profile masses adds little computational content.

The stronger alternative—one growing, connected population with repeated agent types—runs into a tension with the theorem’s setup. The theorem assumes distinct values for each good, so a fixed finite set of repeated valuation types introduces ties. With strict individual rationality, a good cannot move between equal-valued agents; with \(k\) value levels, its trajectory can visit at most \(k\) levels. That may define a sensible mass model, but it no longer captures the paper’s random ordering of distinct values. Preserving distinct rankings as the population grows instead requires types that distinguish more and more agents, so the support grows with the population. If trajectories cross a network, masses of preference types alone also lose the adjacency information that determines which moves are possible. Adding enough network context to recover it risks encoding whole neighborhoods or whole instances as types.

A single indivisible good creates another limit: in a growing population its holder has zero population mass. Replicating goods avoids that, but in the modular case each good again traces its own trajectory, returning to an average over largely independent processes. Richer models with many goods, non-modular utilities, and population-scaled resources might yield worthwhile questions, but they would need new structure beyond this anchor.

I would not call the negative case airtight. The repeated-neighborhood construction is a plausible mass distribution, and an author could recognize it as a population-level version of the trajectory question. Its weakness is that the continuum only averages complete finite systems; the more connected version either changes the theorem’s distinct-value regime or needs substantially richer type and network data. On the evidence here, that leaves a possible mirror, but not a compelling continuization target for the programme.
