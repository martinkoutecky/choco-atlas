The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no named computational result in the complexity sense: no theorem or proposition classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar. So it has zero anchors that meet your stated rule. The strongest honest positive case is a new verification problem motivated by Theorem 1, proved in this paper. Theorem 1 says that, under ex-post canonical information, an assignment is incontestable exactly when it is individually rational, non-wasteful, and respects top-priority sets. A top-priority set is an acceptable preference prefix that cannot be filled using only students with higher priority at its schools; respecting it means assigning the student to a school in that set. This is a strong structural hook, but it is not itself a complexity result.

My lead candidate is Continuous Incontestability Verification. An instance gives finitely many schools \(S\), rational type masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), per-capita school capacities \(q_s\), and a proposed allocation \(x_{t,s}\) (including an outside option). Each type \(t\) has a complete preference ranking and a priority tier at each school; schools order the tiers, with members of a tier tied. The question is whether \(x\) is individually rational, non-wasteful, and respects every top-priority set. A solution is a yes/no answer; the objective is to verify the proposed allocation, not to optimize welfare.

This uses the natural mass version of Theorem 1’s conditions. A type’s top-priority set \(H\) is a preference prefix of acceptable schools for which no feasible assignment of higher-priority mass can fill every school in \(H\). The proposed allocation respects \(H\) when all mass of that type is assigned within \(H\). Non-wastefulness requires that if any mass of a type gets an outcome below school \(s\) in its ranking, then \(s\) is full. Capacities and allocations are fractional at the aggregate level; each student still receives one school.

I would expect this verification problem to be in P, though that is a proposed result, not one established by the paper. For each type there are at most \(m\) preference prefixes to check. For each candidate prefix, a rational max-flow computation can test whether higher-priority mass can fill its schools. Individual rationality and non-wastefulness are direct checks. The work would be to prove that this mass-level test captures a meaningful continuous version of appeal-proofness, not merely to run those checks.

A plausible regime is a large recurring school-district intake: perhaps tens of thousands of applicants, but only tens or hundreds of combinations of reported preference rankings and priority categories. Mass represents a fraction of the cohort, and capacities scale with the cohort. The tiered-priority assumption is a co-modification needed to make types genuinely repeat: within a tier, applicants share the same priority description. It is part of this mirror, rather than a claim that the paper already studies tied priorities.

The paper’s authors would likely recognize the question as an extension of their central assignment property. The scope is deliberately just Theorem 1; I would not claim a mirror of the paper’s incentive results or mechanism comparisons. Further questions include whether the mass characterization is equivalent to a direct mass-level definition of legitimate appeal, and whether one can optimize an objective over continuously incontestable allocations.

The soft spot is substantial: the paper assumes strict rankings of named students, so under a literal complete-type interpretation, priority ranks can make almost every student a distinct type. Priority tiers change that model, and the paper’s characterization may not carry over unchanged. Thus the positive case is credible for a tiered-priority school-choice regime, but it is not a continuous-population mirror of the paper’s exact strict-priority model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no named computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes incontestable assignments; Theorem 2 characterizes attainable outcomes; the later results concern mechanism properties and incentives. None states a complexity classification or analyzes an algorithm. “Continuous Incontestability Verification” is therefore a new problem built from Theorem 1, not a continuous version of a computational result in the paper. That does not rule it out by itself, but it puts the burden on the proposed model to preserve the theorem’s central object.

In the paper’s model, every school has a strict priority order over named students. If type means a complete description, it includes each student’s position in those orders. Since each position is unique, the strict-priority model has no repeated complete types: \(\tau=|I|\). Grouping applicants who share preferences while ignoring their priority positions drops information that determines the top-priority sets at the heart of Theorem 1.

The proposed tiered-priority repair changes that heart. For example, let one type have mass \(1\), rank school \(s\) first, and share a priority tier with everyone else; let \(s\)’s per-capita capacity be \(1/2\). Under the proposed definition, \(\{s\}\) is a top-priority set because no higher-priority mass can fill it. Requiring all of the type’s mass to be assigned within \(\{s\}\) is impossible. Yet with weak priorities, assigning half the type to \(s\) and half outside can be stable: the students outside have no strictly higher priority than those assigned to \(s\). So the proposed mass condition does not carry over the paper’s appeal-proof characterization. A corrected residual-mass condition might be possible, but it would need a new definition and a new theorem.

The stronger repair is to retain strict priorities by giving each applicant a rank or lottery position. That can preserve meaningful individual priority claims, but it removes finite type compression: exact ranks distinguish applicants, while a continuum of lottery positions requires a distribution over a continuous type space and a new way to encode its input. Discretizing those positions returns to priority tiers and the problem above. Either route changes the object the theorem characterizes. The paper’s own verification result does not resolve that modelling gap.

The remaining escape is a school-district study with shared priority categories and a newly designed appeal rule. That could be a sensible related project, but it is not yet a continuous mirror of this paper’s Theorem 1: it supplies a different priority model and must establish anew what counts as a legitimate complaint. I cannot rule out that such a project would be worthwhile; the negative case is strongest against the proposed anchor as stated, not against every conceivable school-choice continuization.
