The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) candidates and an event family studied in the paper, represent each ranking as a type in \(T=S_m\), and let \(\Delta(T)\) be the simplex of population shares \(\mu\). Compute exactly the normalized volume of the set of \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\) satisfying that event’s majority or score inequalities, with the paper’s tie conditions.

The model it lives in

The society is a mass distribution \(\mu\) over complete ranking types \(T=S_m\). The variable is \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\); for a specified event region \(E\), the objective is its uniform simplex measure, \(\operatorname{vol}(E)/\operatorname{vol}(\Delta(T))\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers exact volumes for the paper’s majority-event families, including Condorcet, plurality and runoff, majority-relation, and Borda events. It leaves finite-\(k\) Ehrhart series beyond their role in the limiting bridge, as well as Normaliz benchmarks and implementation details, outside the question.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow one: the paper already gives a continuous population model for its probability calculations, and its named Theorem 5 provides a bridge from discrete profile counts to that model. The potential new question is the complexity of extending those computations to variable numbers of candidates. There is an important qualification: the paper has no named result classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time, hard, FPT, or similar. Theorem 5 is the closest named computational anchor, but it is an exact counting identity, not a complexity result.

Theorem 5 (proved here) starts with linear forms \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_q\) on \(\mathbb R^d\) that vanish at \(\mathbf 1\). It defines the weak cone \(C=\{x\in\mathbb R_+^d:\lambda_i(x)\ge 0\}\), its strict counterpart \(D=\{x\in\mathbb R_+^d:\lambda_i(x)>0\}\), and the simplex slice \(P=\{x\in D:\sum_i x_i=1\}\). If \(P\) has dimension \(d-1\), the theorem shows that strict-cone lattice points are translates of interior lattice points of \(C\), and that the Ehrhart series of \(P\) and its closure satisfy Ehrhart reciprocity. It also gives the corresponding reversal formula for their numerator polynomials. In the paper, these series count finite anonymous election profiles.

A concrete continuous question supported by this anchor is Continuous Majority-Event Volume. Let the types be the complete rankings \(T=S_m\), and let \(\Delta(T)\) be the simplex of their population shares \(\mu\). The instance is a social-choice event specified by the paper’s kinds of majority or score inequalities, including their strict or weak tie conditions. The question is to compute exactly the normalized volume
\[ \frac{\operatorname{vol}_{m!-1}(E)}{\operatorname{vol}_{m!-1}(\Delta(T))}, \]
where \(E\subseteq\Delta(T)\) is the set of societies realizing that event. The rational number is the solution. For example, for the event that a specified candidate is the Condorcet winner, \(E\) is cut out by the pairwise-margin inequalities \(\lambda_{c^\star b}(\mu)>0\) for every other candidate \(b\).

The regime is a large anonymous electorate with relatively few possible ballot types: in the paper’s four-candidate experiments, the electorate can grow without bound while its rankings come from just \(24\) types. A society is the vector of type shares. Under the paper’s Impartial Anonymous Culture assumption, the uniform distribution over finite anonymous profiles converges to uniform volume on this simplex. The authors should recognize this question: computing precisely these event volumes is the paper’s stated task. The paper’s four-candidate calculations, including the Condorcet-winner probability, show that the continuous object is sensible and computationally meaningful.

For a variable-candidate version, my tentative expectation is Class C: exact volume computation may be hard for continuum-specific reasons as the profile simplex grows. That is a conjecture, not a consequence of Theorem 5; the article proves no such hardness. Its symmetry reductions also leave room for tractable families. The resulting questions include whether exact volume is hard for natural event families, whether approximation is efficient, and whether symmetry or parameters such as the number of inequalities yield tractable cases. This scope covers the paper’s event-probability volume computations, not all its Ehrhart data or its software benchmarks.

The weakness is central: for four candidates, the continuous probability question is already the paper’s own model and has already been computed. So this is not a new continuization of a finite-population problem in that setting. The honest positive case is that the finite-profile counts and continuous volumes are distinct objects connected by a proved counting theory, and that the complexity landscape of the volume problem as the number of candidates varies remains a plausible follow-on question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with a mismatch: the paper has no named result classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Theorem 5 proves an Ehrhart reciprocity identity for two related polytopes. The volume and series computations are substantial, but the reported running times are experiments at four candidates, not a complexity classification. So Theorem 5 is not itself a computational result whose continuous mirror could be classified.

The proposed “Continuous Majority-Event Volume” is a stronger candidate, but it exposes the main weakness in the negative case: this paper already studies that continuous object. Its event regions are subsets of the simplex of ranking shares; equation (2.2) identifies their volumes with limiting IAC probabilities, and the paper computes those volumes and event probabilities. The bridge to finite anonymous profiles is part of the paper’s mathematics, not a conjectured extension. This is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, with no evident identity dependence or degeneration.

One can still ask for the exact complexity of computing these volumes when the number of candidates varies. That is a well-posed computational question, and the fixed four-candidate experiments do not answer it. But it is a new complexity classification of a task the paper already performs continuously, not a mirror of a discrete complexity result in the paper. Calling it a worthwhile ChoCo follow-on is plausible; calling it a new continuization is much harder to defend.

So the negative case succeeds only on that narrow distinction: there is no named complexity result to mirror, and the clearest proposed continuous question is already in the paper. It does not establish that no worthwhile continuous research question exists here. On the substance, the proponent’s volume anchor survives: the paper itself gives a genuine discrete-to-continuous bridge and studies the resulting population simplex.
