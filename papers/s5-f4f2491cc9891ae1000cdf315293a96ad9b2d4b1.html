The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a plausible continuous problem, but this paper supplies no eligible anchor. It is a survey: it contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. May’s Theorem is cited for a characterization of majority rule, not a complexity result. The closest computational claim is the unnumbered observation that Generalized Approval procedures are “computationally demanding” because each possible committee must be scored separately. That motivates a question; it does not establish hardness or an algorithmic result.

The best candidate mirror is Continuous Generalized-Approval Committee Optimization. Given candidates \(C\), a fixed committee size \(k\), a representation sequence \(r\), and a list of approval types \(V\subseteq C\) with rational masses \(\mu_V\ge 0\) summing to \(1\), find a committee \(S\subseteq C\), \(|S|=k\), maximizing
\[ \sum_V \mu_V\,r(|V\cap S|). \]
A type is an approval ballot, and its mass is the fraction of the population submitting it. A plausible regime is a large recurring conference audience choosing \(k\) papers or menus, where many attendees share a ballot pattern and the number of distinct patterns \(\tau\) is modest relative to the audience. This tracks the paper’s Generalized Approval procedures directly: it keeps their committee objective and changes the electorate into a distribution over ballot types. The paper’s REP-1 (Chamberlin–Courant) is a natural example.

This is a credible object for the authors to recognize, but it is not a result the paper proves or classifies. A discrete profile maps to masses \(\mu_V=n_V/n\), preserving the maximizing committee, so an independently established discrete hardness result could transfer. The paper itself gives no such anchor, however, so I cannot honestly assign this proposed problem to a complexity class on the basis of its results. The soft spot is that computational complexity is incidental to the article’s survey; the mirror is well motivated by its subject, but it has no named computational finding here to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. It is a survey, and its examples illustrate how voting rules behave; it gives no theorem or other named result about the complexity of a computational problem. The remark that Generalized Approval procedures are computationally demanding because each committee must be scored motivates a question, but does not establish a complexity result. So the proposed mirror is not anchored in a computational finding of this paper.

The best version of that mirror is still a sensible problem: approval patterns are natural voter types, and a large conference audience choosing menus or papers is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. For REP-1, for example, one can maximize \(\sum_V \mu_V\,\mathbf{1}[|V\cap S|\ge 1]\) over committees \(S\) of size \(k\). But for a finite election this is the same objective with each \(\mu_V\) replaced by the count of voters of type \(V\); normalizing counts does not change the maximizing committee. The continuum adds a distributional representation, while the decision remains a search over candidate subsets.

That equivalence makes the mirror look modest, but it does not defeat it under the programme’s rules: high multiplicity is a valid subject, and complexity in the candidate set or bit length can still be worth studying. I can make a strong case that this paper has no eligible computational result to mirror. I cannot honestly make the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists: the proposed problem survives as a plausible new high-multiplicity question, even though this paper does not prove or classify it.
