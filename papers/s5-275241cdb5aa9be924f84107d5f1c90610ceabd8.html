The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a high-multiplicity version of Theorem 4. As stated in the paper and proved there, in the Silver Bullet model a maximum-weight strong-IR allocation can be computed in polynomial time even when there is an upper bound on the number of recipients given immunosuppressants. Strong-IR means that a patient-donor pair either stays out of the allocation or participates with the patient receiving a transplant. Silver Bullet assumes there are no inherently incompatible kidneys: a recipient given a suppressant can receive any kidney.

A plausible regime is a large regional or national exchange pool with many concurrent patient-donor pairs but substantially fewer complete clinical profiles. A type would specify everything the allocation problem uses: whether the recipient has a paired donor, the relevant recipient and donor characteristics, compatibility with each donor type, and the allocation weights. Thus a type is not merely a blood group; it must be refined until members have the same compatibility pattern and weights. Mass is the fraction of patients in that cohort. This could describe, for example, a scaled-up clearinghouse with thousands of pairs but many fewer recurring profiles. The paper’s experiments on pools of up to 256 nodes motivate exchange clearing, but do not establish that today’s pools already have this high-multiplicity structure.

I would ask the authors to study Typed Mass-Budgeted KEI Clearing. The input is an explicitly listed typed exchange graph, with rational mass \(a_v\) for each node, rational edge weights \(w_e\), and a suppressant limit \(\eta\), where total patient mass is normalized to \(1\). The graph uses the paper’s patient, donor, private, and dummy nodes; paired patient and donor cohorts have equal mass. In the Silver Bullet version, there are compatible edges \(E_C\), half-compatible edges \(E_H\), and no inherently incompatible edges. Find a mass allocation \(x_e\) maximizing \(\sum_e w_e x_e\), subject to \(x_e\geq 0\), \(\sum_{e\ni v}x_e=a_v\) for every type node \(v\), and \(\sum_{e\in E_H}x_e\leq\eta\). The private and dummy edges preserve the paper’s interpretation of pairs that do not participate and donors that go unused. The solution is a flow of mass between types. Each person still receives at most one integral transplant; a cohort is split into subgroups according to the flow.

This is a credible mirror of Theorem 4: it retains the same matching structure, strong-IR condition, suppressant budget, and weighted allocation objective, while replacing repeated individual vertices with their masses. The expected result is tractability: the type-level problem is a rational linear program with a number of variables polynomial in the explicitly listed type graph size, and hence in \(\tau\) and the input bit length \(L\). Its point is high-multiplicity scaling: a polynomial-time algorithm in the number of individual patients need not be efficient when cohort counts are encoded compactly as large binary integers. This is not a claim that the continuous version dissolves discrete hardness; Theorem 4 already gives a discrete polynomial-time result.

The paper’s strongest named anchors are algorithmic; it has no numbered hardness theorem to use here. I would keep the scope to Theorem 4’s Silver Bullet, unrestricted-cycle model. The bounded-cycle section’s NP-hardness discussion is not the anchor for this mirror, and limiting cycle length would add a different constraint.

The weakest point is the regime itself. The paper describes current exchange pools as ranging from dozens to a few hundred participants, and detailed compatibility profiles may be so varied that \(\tau\) approaches the number of patients. If so, the compression buys little. The proposed type system makes that limitation explicit: grouping by a convenient label such as blood type alone would not be a faithful mirror. Further questions are whether an \(n\)-fold finite replication of a typed society admits an allocation matching the continuous optimum, what rounding loss arises when it does not, and how the gap changes in the general model with inherently incompatible kidneys.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is a real computational result, and its mass version is easy to state faithfully. The difficulty is finding a kidney-exchange population where that version describes a meaningful high-multiplicity regime. A type cannot just mean blood group: it must preserve paired-donor status, compatibility with donor types, and any allocation weights the objective uses. In this paper, half-compatible edges are pair-specific; its experiments add them randomly between individual pairs. That makes full compatibility profiles likely to distinguish many participants, so the support size \(\tau\) may grow almost as fast as the pool. The paper describes real pools in the dozens to a few hundred, with larger exchanges reaching a few hundred—not a population where replacing individuals by a small set of masses is evidently useful.

The best repair is to define types by complete clinical and allocation profiles, including all compatibility and weight information. That avoids the objection that the model forgets relevant distinctions, but it also makes repeated types the demanding assumption. Shared clinical characteristics do not suffice if two recipients differ in which donors they can accept, or in the value of those matches. A national registry with many participants does not by itself supply many copies of the same complete patient-donor type. The paper gives no evidence that such repetition occurs at a scale that would make \(\tau\) small relative to the number of patients.

One could posit a large exchange made up of repeated exact-profile cohorts. Then the proposed mass-flow model is coherent: a cohort can be divided among different exchanges, and the type-level allocation can represent those divisions. But that scenario is not established by the paper’s application, and if exact cohorts are imposed by construction, the mirror is a model of that special pool rather than a continuization supported by the kidney-exchange setting here. The unrestricted-cycle assumption further separates Theorem 4 from practical clearing, where cycle length is constrained, though that alone does not invalidate the theorem’s mirror.

So the strongest case against is about the missing plausible regime of substantial compression, not about whether the LP is well-posed or what it would solve. This case is not airtight: if exact clinical-profile cohorts are common in a sufficiently large pool, Theorem 4 has a sensible continuous mirror. The paper does not show that they are, and its pair-specific compatibility model points the other way.
