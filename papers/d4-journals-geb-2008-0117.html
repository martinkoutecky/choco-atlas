The case FOR (proponent)

I cannot give a compliant computational anchor for this paper. It contains no numbered result about a problem being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally classified. Its numbered results concern equilibrium existence and stability.

The closest seed is Proposition 4, proved here: from the empty matching, every stable matching for the true preferences can be obtained with probability one as the outcome of an ordinal equilibrium in which firms follow their true preferences. Theorem 1, also proved here, says that when firms use preference strategies in an ordinal equilibrium, every matching in the outcome distribution’s support is stable for the profile consisting of those firm preferences and workers’ true preferences. These are substantial results, but neither analyzes computation.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting would be a large entry-level labor market: many employers and graduates grouped into types with identical rankings over partner types and unemployment, with \(\tau\) such types much smaller than the population. A natural continuous output problem is: given rational masses of employer and worker types and their rankings, find a nonnegative mass-matching matrix respecting those masses, individual rationality, and the condition that no pair of types has positive mass on both sides willing to match with each other. This is a sensible continuum matching question, and type-level deferred acceptance suggests it may be tractable.

But that question captures stable outcomes, not Pais’s central contribution: a randomized decentralized game and its ordinal equilibria. A faithful continuous mirror would need to define how proposals, random order, and unilateral deviations work for a nonatomic population. Those choices are consequential; with infinitesimal agents, a unilateral deviation may have zero effect on aggregate outcomes. I do not have a precise, justified continuum game to hand to a prover here, and treating the stable-mass problem as equivalent would overstate the case.

So the positive case is limited: Proposition 4 offers a natural theorem to investigate in large cohort markets, and the paper’s labor-market setting makes those markets plausible. The weakest point is that the paper supplies no computational result to anchor the required complexity question, while its equilibrium notion depends on finite-agent dynamics that do not pass directly to a continuum.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Proposition 4 shows that, from the empty matching, each stable matching can be implemented by an ordinal equilibrium with firms stating their true preferences. Theorem 1 constrains the outcomes of equilibria in which firms use preference strategies. Neither gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or a computational problem. They are results about equilibrium and stability.

The most plausible rescue is the entry-level cohort example in the case for: group employers and graduates by complete preference type, give each type a rational mass, and ask for a stable mass matching. That is a sensible high-multiplicity setting, and the paper’s labor-market story supports it. The objection is not that such a society is implausible. It is that computing a stable mass assignment drops the paper’s defining object: a randomized sequential game with strategic agents. It looks like a type-capacity version of deferred acceptance, with no computational obstacle or optimization question identified by this paper.

Neither anchor restores that missing link. Theorem 1’s stability conclusion is proved through individual deviations: a particular worker changes how they respond to offers from a particular firm, improving their own probability of a preferred assignment. In a nonatomic model, that deviation has zero effect on aggregate mass outcomes. A continuum equilibrium could still track each agent’s personal incentives, but then it must represent the histories and strategies that make those incentives meaningful. Those are not captured by the paper’s compact input of preference types and masses.

Proposition 4 has the same difficulty. Its implementation strategy lets each worker accept one designated partner and reject everyone else. A mass matching may split a preference type across several partners, so its implementation needs the corresponding strategy distinctions within that type. A clock process or an expanded strategy-type model might make this precise, but either choice adds dynamics and state that the paper’s result does not specify. It would be a new continuum game whose computational questions need their own motivation.

So the case against is strongest on fit: the only crisp mass problem suggested by these results is static stable allocation, while the results themselves concern finite-agent equilibria. The honest weakness is that this does not rule out a worthwhile continuous matching project; large cohorts make the regime plausible, and stable mass allocation could be worth studying independently. It does leave the case for a continuous computational mirror of this paper without a named computational anchor or a clear algorithmic target.
