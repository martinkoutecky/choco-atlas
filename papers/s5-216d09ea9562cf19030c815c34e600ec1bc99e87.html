The continuous mirror question

Given a finite hospital set \(H\), supported resident types \(T\), binary-rational type masses \(\mu_t\), binary-rational hospital capacities \(\kappa_h\), strict resident preference lists, and a feasible binary-rational mass matching \(x_{t,h}\) (including an unmatched option), do there exist at most two complete strict rankings of \(T\), assignable to the hospitals, such that the matching has no blocking type pair? A pair \((t,h)\) blocks if positive mass of type \(t\) is assigned to an option it likes less than \(h\), and \(h\) either has positive spare capacity or assigns positive mass to a type ranked below \(t\).

The model it lives in

A continuum population represented by a finite support \(T\) and rational masses \(\mu_t\); each type carries a resident preference list, \(x\) distributes its mass among hospitals or the unmatched option, and each named hospital has a mass-scaled capacity \(\kappa_h\). The decision variables are at most two shared strict rankings of the supported types and each hospital’s choice of ranking; stability is tested by positive-mass blocking pairs.

What the mirror covers

Covers the existence-of-witnesses decision problem in Theorems 2 and 3; leaves the first-fit algorithms and their experimental performance unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does Continuous 2-SHPL admit tractable cases under structural restrictions on its support-level precedence digraph, such as bounded treewidth?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is a high-multiplicity version of the paper’s existence-of-witnesses question. The residents are the continuous population; hospitals remain named programs with capacities that scale with that population. This keeps the paper’s decision problem intact: the matching is given, and the question is whether hospitals can use a small number of common ranking lists to make it stable.

In the mirror, a resident type \(t\) has a strict preference list over hospitals, and \(\mu_t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 0}\) is its share of the population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(x_{t,h}\) be the mass of type \(t\) assigned to hospital \(h\), including an unmatched option, and let \(\kappa_h\) be hospital \(h\)’s capacity in population-mass units. The input gives the preferences, masses, capacities, and matching \(x\). A hospital’s decision is a complete strict ranking of the supported resident types; each hospital must use one of at most \(k\) distinct rankings.

Call \((t,h)\) a blocking type pair if positive mass of type \(t\) is assigned somewhere it likes less than \(h\), and either \(h\) has positive spare capacity or it has positive assigned mass of a type it ranks below \(t\). The matching is stable when there is no such pair. The solution is a set of at most \(k\) rankings and an assignment of one ranking to each hospital that makes the given matching stable. There is no optimization objective: this remains a decision problem. Ranking types rather than individuals is the high-multiplicity assumption that members of a type are indistinguishable in the features the problem uses.

Lead: Continuous 2-SHPL. Its instance is the data above with \(k=2\); it asks whether two complete rankings over the supported resident types can be assigned to the hospitals so the given matching is stable. My anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: 2-SHPL is NP-complete even when every hospital has capacity one. The paper proves this by reduction from Acyclic 2-Coloring, whose NP-completeness it cites from [5].

The reduction carries directly to the mass model. For a graph with \(n\) vertices, make \(n\) resident types and \(n\) hospitals, give each type mass \(1/n\), give each hospital capacity \(1/n\), and assign each type’s mass to its corresponding hospital. A graph arc creates a precedence requirement: for the relevant hospital ranking to avoid a blocking pair, its incumbent type must precede the type that wants to move there. Hospitals using the same ranking therefore induce a union of precedence arcs that must be acyclic. Choosing between two rankings is exactly the two-coloring question in the reduction. Thus the mass formulation retains the NP-hardness; its solution certificate is polynomial in the support size, so this version is NP-complete. I expect hardness to transfer (Class B): the reduction’s combinatorics live in the type and hospital structure, not in the number of copies of each resident.

A second, broader question is Continuous \(k\)-SHPL, the same problem with at most \(k\) rankings. My anchor is Theorem 3, also proved here: for any fixed positive constant \(\epsilon\), \(k\)-SHPL is NP-complete for \(3\le k\le n^{1-\epsilon}\), even when every hospital has capacity one. Its proof reduces from \(k\)-Coloring, using the NP-completeness facts cited in [2]. In the mirror, take support size \(\tau=n\), masses \(1/n\), capacities \(1/n\), and \(3\le k\le\tau^{1-\epsilon}\). An edge creates opposing precedence requirements at its endpoint hospitals, so two endpoints cannot use the same ranking; the hospital ranking choices encode a proper \(k\)-coloring. This version also lies in NP and is expected to be NP-complete, again with hardness transferring. Theorem 2 is my lead because it already gives fixed-\(k\) hardness at \(k=2\); Theorem 3 shows that the same witness-existence question persists across a wider range of list counts.

This is plausible in the paper’s own domain. Large placement systems can have many applicants but a much smaller number of recurring complete profiles: the same ranked program list, qualification profile, and current assignment pattern. Hospitals may apply one of a few standardized ranking policies to those profiles. A regime with \(N=q\tau\) applicants and \(q\) members per type has many agents relative to its support, while still allowing \(\tau\) to grow in the complexity instance. Scaling each program’s capacity with its cohort is the per-capita supply adjustment this mirror requires. It asks exactly the paper’s question—whether a few common hospital rankings can witness stability—not a question about fractional outcomes or an encoding.

The mirror covers Theorems 2 and 3, not the first-fit heuristics or their experimental performance. It also generates nearby questions: what is the minimum number of distinct rankings that can witness stability, and do restrictions on the type-precedence graph yield tractable cases? A further, distinct extension would allow mass transfers between hospitals and ask for the least costly change that makes some set of rankings stable.

The weakest point is that the hardness constructions have support size \(\tau=n\): continuity does not remove the combinatorics when the number of types and hospitals grows. Also, this is a resident-side continuum, with named hospitals and scaled capacities. The case is strongest where applicants really do form substantial cohorts with shared profiles and ranking rules; if individual qualifications create a distinct effective type for almost every resident, it loses its high-multiplicity force.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 2 is a genuine computational anchor, and Theorem 3 repeats the same construction across a wider range of \(k\). The strongest version of the proposed mirror would give each graph vertex a resident type, make \(q\) identical residents of that type, assign the cohort to its own hospital, and scale that hospital’s capacity to \(q\). Then \(\tau\) types can represent a population of \(q\tau\) residents. This is a coherent high-multiplicity model; the negative case cannot dismiss it as an invalid literal limit.

The problem is what the matching question sees. Since the assignment is fixed, refine each type by its assigned hospital if necessary. At a full hospital, a desired move creates a blocking pair according to whether there is positive mass of an incumbent type ranked below the applicant type. At an undersubscribed hospital, the relevant fact is whether any positive mass wants to move there. The sizes of those masses do not affect either condition. In the paper’s reductions, all hospitals are full; each graph edge imposes a ranking constraint whenever its endpoint types have positive mass. Replacing each resident with \(q\) copies and scaling capacities changes none of those constraints.

So the continuous versions of both theorems reduce to coloring the same support-level precedence structure: a cohort of mass \(10^{-6}\) imposes the same constraint as one of mass \(1/2\). The mass values and their bit lengths contribute no population-scale question. The support must grow with the graph to encode the hardness, even if each supported type has arbitrarily many copies. This makes the proposed mirrors look like the paper’s finite digraph partitioning problem with normalized cohort weights, rather than questions that use the population’s continuous composition. A formal equivalence could still be useful, but it is a thin contribution for a programme aimed at opening computational problems to continuous optimization.

The same issue persists under the best repair to the proposed type definition: include the resident’s current assignment as well as their preference list, so agents grouped into a type are genuinely indistinguishable for stability. With finite hospitals and strict preference lists, there are only finitely many preference types. A distribution over them remains a finite support model, and the stability test uses the support and hospital fullness rather than the shares. Making scores or other personal attributes continuously varying could create a richer type space, but then the mirror also continuizes individual information and hospital priorities. If every distinct attribute profile has measure zero, the paper’s pairwise blocking notion needs a new definition to avoid vacuity. That is a different modelling project.

The application case is also strained at the point that matters for these anchors. The reduction gives each graph vertex its own preference cohort and hospital, with arbitrary graph-shaped acceptance relations. A large match with repeated applicants and standardized hospital ranking policies is conceivable, and it may be a sensible scenario to investigate. But it does not make the theorem’s key constraints depend on cohort size or proportions. The proposed scenario supplies multiplicity by cloning each vertex of a finite graph; it does not supply a population question that the theorem’s witnesses answer.

This is not an airtight argument that no worthwhile mirror exists. The theorems are real complexity results, and the programme may value the discrete-to-high-multiplicity equivalence itself. The negative case is that these particular anchors offer little beyond that equivalence: their strongest faithful mirrors are governed by positive support and fullness, while genuinely continuous type variation would require changing the problem more substantially.
