The continuous mirror question

Given a rational distribution \(\mu\) with support \(T\) of size \(\tau\), and a bounded non-atomic rational step-function valuation density \(v_t\) for each \(t\in T\), decide whether there is a labelled partition of \([0,1]\) using \(\tau-1\) cuts such that every type satisfies \(D_t \le \epsilon_0\), where \(D_t=\left|\int_{O^+}v_t(x)\,dx-\int_{O^-}v_t(x)\,dx\right|\) and \(\epsilon_0\) is the constant from Theorem 3.

The model it lives in

Types are complete valuation profiles with positive rational masses summing to \(1\). The decision variable is a cut-and-label partition; feasibility requires the same discrepancy bound for every type, regardless of its mass. The cut budget is \(\tau-1\), indexed by distinct valuation constraints.

The objection that survived

The paper ties its cut guarantee to the number of agents, while the mirror ties it to the number of distinct types; the proponent argues this is defensible but does not establish that the authors would accept that index shift.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorems 2 and 3 through support-indexed cut budgets; it leaves the \(n\)-cut PPAD-hardness result and the PPA containment result outside its scope.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the complexity change when the permitted cut count is \(\tau+k(\tau)\) rather than \(\tau-1\) or \(\tau+k\) for constant \(k\)?
  • Which mass-sensitive fairness variants, if any, retain a recognizable consensus-halving interpretation?

The case FOR (proponent)

I would lead with a type-compressed version of limited-cut consensus halving. A type is a complete valuation profile: everyone of type \(t\) has the same bounded, non-atomic value density \(v_t\) on \([0,1]\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) such types, with \(\mu_t>0\) and \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For example, take \(N=\tau^2\) conference participants, with \(\tau\) distinct valuation profiles and \(\tau\) participants of each profile; the input lists the \(\tau\) profiles and their masses, not all \(N\) people.

The decision variable is a labelled partition of the interval: choose cut locations and assign each resulting piece to \(O^+\) or \(O^-\). For type \(t\), its discrepancy is
\[ D_t=\left|\int_{O^+}v_t(x)\,dx-\int_{O^-}v_t(x)\,dx\right|. \]
Every person in a positive-mass type must meet the same discrepancy bound. That preserves the paper’s individual fairness requirement; it does not replace it with average fairness.

The co-modification is to measure the cut budget against the number of distinct valuation constraints, \(\tau\), rather than the number of named people, \(N\). Identical agents add population mass but no new fairness constraint. I think the authors could recognize that as a type-compressed version of their problem, though this is the hinge of the case.

My lead anchor is Theorem 3, proved in this paper by reduction from 3SAT. It states that for some constant \(\epsilon_0>0\), it is NP-hard, given \(n\) agents’ value-density functions, to decide whether a partition with \(n-1\) cuts exists in which every agent’s two portions differ in value by at most \(\epsilon_0\).

The corresponding question is Type \((\tau,\tau-1,\epsilon_0)\)-Consensus-Halving: given the rational type distribution and its valuation densities, decide whether there is a partition with \(\tau-1\) cuts such that \(D_t\le\epsilon_0\) for every \(t\in T\). The answer is yes or no; a yes-instance has such a cut-and-label partition as its witness. The source theorem supplies the hard instances: make each source agent a distinct type, then replicate each type to form a population with \(N\ge\tau^2\). I expect NP-hardness to transfer—Class B—because the shortage of cuts relative to independent valuation constraints remains. This is my strongest mirror.

A second anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. It states that for every fixed constant \(k\), there is a constant \(\epsilon_k>0\) such that finding an \(\epsilon_k\)-approximate consensus-halving partition for \(n\) agents using \(n+k\) cuts is PPAD-hard. The proof reduces from constant-error generalized circuits, whose hardness the paper cites as Lemma 1 from Rubinstein [29].

Its mirror is Type \((\tau,\tau+k,\epsilon_k)\)-Consensus-Halving: given the same type-distribution input, find a partition using \(\tau+k\) cuts with \(D_t\le\epsilon_k\) for every type. A solution is the cut list and labels. I expect PPAD-hardness to transfer, again Class B, even when the types represent large cohorts: repeated members of a type impose no new valuation inequality. This anchor extends the case beyond the exact \(\tau\)-cut threshold to a constant amount of slack. It does not claim that this problem is PPAD-complete.

The scope is these two limited-cut hardness results, not the paper’s full set of results. Further questions include whether the search mirror has a PPA upper bound for rational step densities, how the complexity changes when the extra-cut allowance grows with \(\tau\), and whether mass-sensitive variants—such as requiring only a specified fraction of the population to meet the discrepancy bound—have different complexity.

The weak point is that \(\mu\) records who is prevalent, but the paper’s fairness condition is imposed on every present type, so prevalence does not weight the constraint. More seriously, if the authors insist that the cut budget must scale with headcount, the proposed mirror loses its nontrivial regime: with only \(\tau\) distinct nonatomic valuation types, an exact solution exists with \(\tau\) cuts, so \(N-1\) cuts make the high-multiplicity instances automatically feasible when \(N\gg\tau\). The type-based cut budget is therefore a substantive modelling choice, not a consequence of Theorems 2 or 3. I still think it is defensible: it preserves the paper’s fairness question and treats duplicate agents as redundant constraints, while leaving the hardness combinatorics among distinct valuations intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mirrors make population mass irrelevant to the question. For Theorem 3, the \((\tau,\tau-1,\epsilon_0)\) problem is well-defined, and its reduction works: make each source agent a distinct valuation type, then replicate each type. But changing the masses while keeping the same support cannot change feasibility. Every positive-mass type must meet the same discrepancy bound, however small its share. The hard object is therefore a finite set of valuation constraints with a cut budget tied to its size. The replicated population supplies a cohort interpretation, but the theorem’s computational question uses only the support.

Theorem 2 has the same limitation. Its \((\tau,\tau+k,\epsilon_k)\) mirror preserves the generalized-circuit constraints by assigning each source agent a type and copying it. The extra-cut budget and fairness conditions still refer to types, not to how much population holds each type. So both anchors show that a type-compressed consensus-halving problem is hard; neither, by itself, makes the distribution over types computationally consequential.

There is a natural way to make mass matter: require that at least a specified fraction of the population be approximately balanced, or minimize the mass-weighted discrepancy \(\sum_t\mu_tD_t\). But those alter the paper’s defining guarantee that every agent is balanced. They may be worthwhile population-fairness questions, but the paper’s reductions do not establish their complexity, and they are not direct mirrors of Theorems 2 or 3. Keeping the every-agent guarantee while using a genuinely continuous type space would also require a new representation of the valuation distribution and its constraints; the paper’s finite list of step-function agents does not supply one.

That is a real scope concern, but it does not defeat the best version of the proposed mirror. A society with large cohorts of identical-valued agents is sensible, and measuring the cut budget against distinct valuation types is a defensible co-modification: duplicate agents add no new fairness constraint. Theorem 3’s \(\tau-1\) threshold is especially natural because \(\tau\) types admit the usual \(\tau\)-cut existence guarantee. I cannot honestly argue that this anchor fails the programme’s test if the authors accept that effective cut budget. The negative case is therefore weak: the mirrors leave mass unused, but they remain credible high-multiplicity questions rather than mere formal mistakes.
