The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a qualifying computational anchor in this paper. Its unnumbered “Theorem.”, proved here, says that for cost-saving games generated by one-machine scheduling with an additive and regular efficiency criterion, the \(\beta\)-rule gives a core allocation. Propositions 1 and 2 establish the structural route: superadditivity and \(\sigma_0\)-component additivity suffice for the rule, and these scheduling games have those properties. These are constructive game-theoretic results, but none asserts a complexity classification such as membership in P or NP-hardness.

The paper says the allocation needs values for \(2^n-1\) coalitions. It does not give a complexity bound for computing those values. So that observation cannot support a tractability anchor.

A continuum version of the \(\beta\)-core allocation might be a plausible research question, but presenting it as the continuous mirror of a named computational result would go beyond the evidence here. Under the requested anchor standard, there is no per-anchor prover question to give.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no computational result for one to mirror. Its “Theorem” says the \(\beta\)-rule gives a core allocation for a class of scheduling games. The rule uses values of about \(2n-1\) coalitions, but the paper gives no complexity bound for computing those values. In particular, its efficiency functions are arbitrary weakly increasing functions, with no finite input representation specified. Counting coalition values is not a complexity classification.

There is a plausible stronger setup: many recurring jobs grouped into cohorts with the same processing time and cost function, arranged in a finite sequence of blocks. That preserves meaningful high multiplicity; I would not reject it on population grounds. But asking whether a continuous \(\beta\)-allocation is in the core would extend the paper’s game-theoretic theorem. Asking how hard it is to compute such an allocation would define a new computational problem, requiring choices about how the ordered population and its cost functions are represented. Neither is a computational result from this paper carried into a continuous society.

So the negative case is strong for *this paper as a mirror target*, though it cannot rule out worthwhile adjacent work. The most promising repaired version is a continuous cooperative-scheduling project, but that would be a new project rather than the computational continuization the programme is looking for.
