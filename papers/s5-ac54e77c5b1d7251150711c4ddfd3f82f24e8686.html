The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case I see is one narrow mirror: Lead — Unknown-Mix Mass Bilateral Trade. Imagine a large platform matching sellers and buyers from stable, high-multiplicity pools. A seller’s type is her valuation and a buyer’s type is theirs; each type’s mass is its fraction of the relevant pool. In a hard family, the seller pool has mass \(1/2\) at \(0\) and \(1/2\) at an unknown \(x\), while the buyer pool has mass \(1/2\) at \(x\) and \(1/2\) at \(1\). Independent matching gives four encounter types, each with mass \(1/4\). The population can be enormous while the number of distinct seller and buyer types stays at two.

Here is the continuous problem. Fix a horizon \(T\) and an open interval \(I\) around \(1/2\). The unknown society is determined by \(x\in I\) and the two mass distributions above. Each round, a fresh seller and buyer are matched; the platform posts one price \(p_t\), then observes only whether each accepts. Its reward is \((B_t-S_t)\mathbf{1}\{S_t\le p_t\le B_t\}\), and its comparator is the best fixed price for that society. A solution is a pricing strategy based on past feedback and randomization; the question is whether any strategy can achieve sublinear worst-case expected regret, \(\inf_\alpha\sup_{x\in I}R_T(\alpha;x)=o(T)\).

The anchor is Theorem 12 in Appendix H, which restates the main-text Theorem 6. It is proved in this paper. Theorem 12 says that, in the realistic-feedback stochastic i.i.d. setting with independent seller and buyer valuations, the minimax regret is at least \(T/8\) for every horizon \(T\), with no bounded-density assumption. Its proof uses precisely the family above: the best price is \(x\), and any other price loses a constant amount of expected gain from trade. For any strategy, the proof selects an \(x\) the strategy hits with probability zero, yielding linear regret. Thus the restricted mass problem also has minimax regret \(\Theta(T)\): the theorem’s construction gives the lower bound, and the benchmark reward of \(1/2\) gives a trivial \(T/2\) upper bound.

I expect this to be continuum-specifically hard (Class C). The population has just four encounter types, so multiplicity is not doing the work. The obstruction is an unknown real-valued threshold \(x\), an exact-price optimum, and feedback that does not let a finite-horizon strategy identify it with certainty. Restricting possible \(x\) values to a known finite set would be a different learning problem; it removes the proof’s uncountable-target argument and raises the question of how regret depends on the number and precision of candidate values.

The mirror preserves the paper’s underlying question: online pricing against an unknown stochastic market, with the same reward, feedback, and fixed-price benchmark. It covers only the independent-valuation, realistic-feedback lower bound, not the paper’s full-feedback, correlated, adversarial, or weak-budget-balance results. Time remains discrete, and price remains the paper’s original action; the proposed move is to read the stable arrival law as the mass profile of a large market.

My weakest point is that this is close to the paper’s existing model: the paper already takes an i.i.d. distribution over valuations as its environment, so a referee could regard the mass-pool interpretation as a restatement rather than a distinct continuous mirror. There is also a category mismatch: the anchor is a minimax-regret lower bound, not a classical NP-hardness or parameterized-complexity result. I am treating it as an algorithmic learnability anchor because it bounds every online policy, but a programme requiring conventional complexity classes may not accept it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 12 (the main-text Theorem 6) is the only anchor, and its proposed pool interpretation is already built into the theorem’s model. The hard instance is exactly a pair of mass distributions,
\[ \mu_S^x=\tfrac12\delta_0+\tfrac12\delta_x,\qquad \mu_B^x=\tfrac12\delta_x+\tfrac12\delta_1. \]
Each round samples a seller and buyer from those laws. Calling them large pools with four possible encounter types gives a plausible story, but it does not continuize a discrete population: the paper already studies regret against unknown distributions over valuations, including atomic ones.

The theorem’s difficulty also depends on \(x\) being an unknown real parameter. The learner does not receive the mass profile as an input. If the mirror supplies the profile—including \(x\)—as a ChoCo society, it removes the uncertainty that drives the lower bound. If it keeps \(x\) hidden and preserves the paper’s feedback and online benchmark, it recovers the paper’s existing learning problem. Encoding \(x\) as a finite-precision input would pose a different problem again; the proof’s choice of a real \(x\) outside the strategy’s countable set of price atoms is specific to the uncountable, exact-price model.

A fixed, known high-multiplicity market could support a separate question about computing its best fixed price. But that is not the anchored regret result: it removes the unknown-law learning problem. Conversely, treating repeated random arrivals from fixed pools as the mirror preserves the result precisely because it preserves the paper’s distributional model. Neither move gives ChoCo a distinct computational question about a society supplied as masses, with complexity measured in support size and encoding length. The theorem is an information-theoretic regret lower bound over strategies, not a runtime or computational-complexity result.

That is the strongest negative case: this anchor is a collision of interpretations, not a new continuous-population mirror. I would not claim the universal case is airtight. Large markets with fixed cohorts, changing populations, or unknown finite type catalogues could motivate worthwhile research. But those require new models and questions; the proponent’s Theorem 12 mirror, as stated, is already the paper’s own unknown-distribution setting.
