The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an honest positive case that meets your anchor requirement. The paper’s strongest result proved here is Theorem 3: on cycle-free graph games, for every \(\epsilon\ge 0\), there is a unique solution satisfying component efficiency and \(\epsilon\)-parameterized fairness. That is an existence-and-uniqueness characterization, not a result about an algorithm or computational complexity. Theorem 1 (Myerson [7]) and Theorem 2 (Herings et al. [5]) are cited characterizations; Corollary 1 connects the new axiom to those earlier axioms. The numerical example gives a formula for one three-player game, but is not a named computational result. So there is no qualifying anchor here, and no per-anchor computational problem to hand a prover.

A plausible *new research direction* would use a finite tree of agent roles, with many interchangeable agents in each role. A type would be a communication role; its mass \(\mu_t\) would be the population share in that role; and \(x_t\) would be its per-capita allocation. One could ask for an allocation satisfying mass-weighted component efficiency and a mass-weighted version of \(\epsilon\)-fairness after deleting a link. For example, the roles might represent large cohorts in a tree-shaped production network. But the paper neither defines this continuum-player model nor proves that its axioms have a unique solution there. That would be a new axiomatic question, not a mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The weak point is decisive: the paper studies axioms for finite-player allocation rules, while the requested programme asks computational questions about a continuous population. A plausible high-multiplicity scenario cannot supply the missing computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper has no computational result for the programme to continuize. Theorem 3 proves existence and uniqueness of a solution satisfying component efficiency and \(\epsilon\)-parameterized fairness. Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are axiomatic characterizations cited from prior work; Corollary 1 identifies two endpoint cases. None states a complexity bound or analyzes an algorithm for a computational problem. The three-player example illustrates the solution family, but supplies no such problem either.

The best attempt to rescue an anchor would be to ask how efficiently one can compute the \(\epsilon\)-parameterized allocation. The theorem’s proof writes a linear system, but the paper does not define a computational task, an input representation for \(v:2^N\to\mathbb{R}\), or a complexity measure. Choosing those and then taking a high-multiplicity limit would create a new project rather than mirror a result in the paper.

The proposed finite tree of agent roles does not close that gap. A mass model needs a rule for extending coalition worths to groups of agents and for representing the communication links. Cloning players while preserving the tree structure changes the topology; deleting one individual link in a large cohort affects vanishing mass. Deleting all links between role groups would give the axiom a different operation. A continuum TU-game on role masses might be a sensible axiomatic project, but the paper’s result would not anchor a computational question about it.

So I would vote against treating this manuscript as a continuous mirror candidate. The case rests mainly on the missing computational anchor, and is strongest as a scope judgment: a related continuous cooperative-game project could still be worthwhile, but it would need its own question and motivation. I cannot establish the universal claim that no scenario could make such a project valuable; the paper simply gives no basis for one within this programme.
