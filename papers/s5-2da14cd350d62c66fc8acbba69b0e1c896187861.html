Theorem 4.1

  • Theorem 4.1 — survived: The opponent challenges transferring the guarantee but accepts that the continuous mass-market pricing question remains recognizable and worthwhile.

The continuous mirror question

Given \(m\) items of supply one, a population of mass \(N\), and \(\tau\) complete buyer types with rational masses \(N\mu_t\) and known subadditive valuations \(v_t\), does there exist an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\) that computes static anonymous item prices \(p\) so that sequential utility-maximizing demand along every fixed measurable arrival stream achieves welfare at least \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{cont}}/O(\log\log m)-\epsilon\)? Here \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{cont}}=\max_x\sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S)\), subject to \(\sum_Sx_{t,S}=N\mu_t\) and \(\sum_{t,S\ni j}x_{t,S}\le 1\); \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned the integral bundle \(S\), with empty bundles absorbing unallocated mass.

The model it lives in

A fluid online allocation market with item supply one, type masses \(N\mu_t\), and one complete valuation \(v_t\) per type. The offline benchmark is the configuration LP over mass assignments \(x_{t,S}\); the decision is a shared, fixed price vector \(p\), and the objective is to approximate the offline welfare under sequential demand.

The objection that survived

A type with mass \(\delta<1/m^2\) can make \(f(1/m^2)\) equal the whole continuous optimum, invalidating the proposed telescoping bound; the offline LP and its separation oracle also do not establish the online fluid guarantee.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 4.1's welfare-price computation in the fixed-type mass setting. It leaves the paper's general independent-draw setting, the revenue result in Theorem 5.1, and the paper's other results outside this mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does an \(O(\log\log m)\) static-price guarantee hold uniformly over rational type masses and fixed arrival streams, or can rare types rule it out?
  • If the guarantee holds, can it be computed in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L,1/\epsilon\) with demand queries under a precise fluid tie-breaking model?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead, and only anchor, is Theorem 4.1, proved in this paper. It says that for subadditive combinatorial auctions and any \(\epsilon>0\), a polynomial-time algorithm in \(n\), \(m\), and \(1/\epsilon\) can compute static anonymous item prices such that the resulting posted-price mechanism achieves expected welfare at least
\[ \frac{1}{\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf v}\!\left[\mathbf v(\mathrm{OPT}(\mathbf v))\right]-\epsilon, \qquad \alpha=O(\log\log m). \]
The theorem assumes demand-oracle access to the valuations in the support of the buyers’ distributions and sample access for estimating expectations. I would mirror its welfare-pricing problem, not its LP encoding.

Here is a continuous version I think the authors would recognize. There are \(m\) items, each with one unit of supply, and a population of total mass \(N\). The input gives \(\tau\) buyer types, with rational shares \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\); type \(t\) has a known normalized, monotone, subadditive valuation \(v_t\). Thus type \(t\) has mass \(N\mu_t\), and members of a type are identical in every respect the model uses. The population arrives in any fixed order. Each infinitesimal buyer receives an integral bundle; the aggregate mass assigned to each bundle can be fractional. In hindsight, the benchmark is
\[ \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{cont}} = \max_x \sum_{t,S}x_{t,S}v_t(S) \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_S x_{t,S}=N\mu_t,\qquad \sum_{t,S:\,j\in S}x_{t,S}\le 1, \]
where \(x_{t,S}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned bundle \(S\). Empty bundles absorb any unallocated mass.

Call the resulting question Continuous Subadditive Prophet Pricing: given this instance and demand-oracle access to each \(v_t\), compute a single nonnegative price vector \(p\in\mathbb{R}^m\), shared by all buyers and fixed throughout arrival, so that each infinitesimal buyer chooses a utility-maximizing bundle from the remaining items and the resulting welfare is at least
\[ \frac{1}{\alpha}\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{cont}}-\epsilon, \qquad \alpha=O(\log\log m). \]
The guarantee should hold for every fixed arrival order. A solution is the price vector; the sequential demand choices give its outcome.

I expect this problem to be tractable, Class A. The paper’s algorithm already covers the point-mass case of its valuation distributions, which is the case above. Its pricing argument also has the right structure for grouping repeated buyers: the constrained configuration LP has one capacity \(N\mu_t\) per type, and its dual has one variable \(u_t\) per type plus item variables \(y_j\). Separating a dual constraint amounts to finding a bundle maximizing \(v_t(S)-\sum_{j\in S}y_j\), exactly a demand query. This suggests an algorithm polynomial in \(m,\tau\), and the bit length of the masses, rather than in the expanded number of buyers. That multiplicity-compressed runtime is a proposed extension to prove; Theorem 4.1 itself states a runtime polynomial in \(n\).

The regime is a large market with many buyers sharing a modest number of exact purchasing profiles—for example, repeated procurement cohorts facing the same finite inventory of item categories. Here \(N\) is large relative to \(\tau\). This is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s subadditive allocation problem, and the mass-flow formulation keeps its central question intact: can static anonymous prices compete with an offline allocator who knows the whole population? The paper’s Bayesian valuation distributions do not already make the population continuous; they describe uncertainty about finitely many named buyers. This mirror instead replaces repeated buyers by type masses.

The scope is deliberately the welfare result in Theorem 4.1, in its complete-information special case. It does not claim to mirror the paper’s revenue result or to continuize the random valuation draws. Theorem 6.1 gives a useful boundary but is not a hardness anchor: it shows that the paper’s equal-marginal zero-sum strategy can lose a factor \(\Omega(\log\log m)\); it does not show that all continuous pricing methods must lose that factor.

The weakest point is the passage from the paper’s finite online sequence to atomless sequential depletion. The offline mass-allocation LP and the pricing separation problem extend naturally, but the paper does not explicitly define this continuous online dynamics or prove the grouped-mass runtime. A proof would need to formalize that passage and show that the guarantee survives fractional type masses. Still, the underlying allocation objective, item supply, demand behavior, and price-versus-prophet benchmark remain the paper’s own problem. This looks like a credible mirror, with a concrete tractability conjecture and a clear bridge to prove.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that the proposed mass-allocation LP does not by itself carry over Theorem 4.1’s online guarantee. In the finite proof, each item belongs to one buyer’s bundle in the offline optimum, which lets the argument assemble one shared price vector from the buyers’ separate key lemmas. In a fractional mass allocation, an item can be split across many types. The dual’s demand-query separation shows that the offline LP is tractable; it does not establish that a single static price vector achieves the claimed welfare in the sequential process.

There is a concrete break in the proof’s telescoping bound. It uses \(f(1/m^2)\leq \mathrm{OPT}/m\). In the proposed continuous model, let a type of mass \(\delta<1/m^2\) have valuation \(v(S)=V\) for every nonempty \(S\), and let all other types have zero value. Then \(\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{cont}}=\delta V\), while \(f(1/m^2)=\delta V=\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{cont}}\): the item-marginal cap still permits allocating the entire rare type’s mass. The finite-agent argument therefore gives no \(O(\log\log m)\) guarantee for this LP. A different analysis might succeed, but the proposed multiplicity-compressed runtime and LP dual do not supply it.

There is also a modelling tradeoff. If repeated buyers retain independent random valuations, an atomless population tends to replace the paper’s uncertain future draws with a deterministic flow of valuation types. If the model instead keeps the paper’s finite-agent stochastic uncertainty, it risks needing a separate type for each buyer and losing the compression. With fixed supply and population mass \(N\), at most \(m\) units of buyer mass can receive nonempty bundles, so the served share vanishes as \(N\) grows. Scaling supply with \(N\) avoids that, but moves the question toward a fluid multi-copy market.

These points weaken the proponent’s specific Class A claim. They do not defeat the anchor: Theorem 4.1 is a genuine computational result, repeated buyer types are plausible, and the continuous allocation question is one the authors could recognize. Per-capita supply scaling is allowed by the programme, and a new proof—or a negative result about that proof’s obstruction—could itself be worthwhile. So the honest negative case is limited: I cannot defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
