The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a named computational-complexity result here to anchor a continuous mirror. The closest is Theorem 2.6, proved in this paper. It says that a weighted plurality rule chooses from \(A\) with probability at least \(1-\epsilon\) when the weighted expected vote for every alternative in \(A\) exceeds that for alternatives outside \(A\) by \(\delta\), provided every voter’s effect is sufficiently small. Conversely, if \(f\) is not weighted plurality, there is a distribution \(P\) under which every voter is more likely to vote \(2\) than \(1\), yet \(f(X)=1\) with probability \(1\). The theorem’s following remark says this is constructive: an LP can find weights or a counterexample distribution.

That LP is a computational hook, but the paper does not state a complexity classification for a named problem. Calling recognition “in P” would add an input model—such as an explicit truth table for \(f\)—and a complexity conclusion not stated in the result. Under your anchor rule, I don’t think that qualifies.

The obstacle to a convincing population mirror is related: \(P\) is a probability distribution over profiles of \(n\) individually indexed voters, and the theorem’s hypothesis measures each coordinate’s effect. That is probability over elections, while the population remains finite. Replacing voters with types and mass would change what the effect condition means. One could formulate a new type-level analogue, but I don’t believe this paper’s named results anchor it as a continuous computational problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor is Theorem 2.6, and the proponent identifies its main weakness: it is a probabilistic characterization, not a complexity result for a computational problem on a society. The LP supplies certificates for whether a finite function \(f:[k]^n\to[k]\) is weighted plurality and, if not, a witness distribution. With \(f\) given by its full truth table, that LP is polynomial in the table size; with a succinct encoding, the computational question changes. The paper does not state a complexity landscape that a continuous mirror could carry forward.

The strongest proposed mirror would take an atomless electorate with types recording votes and weights, replace weighted sums by integrals, and ask for a low-influence analogue of the theorem. But the paper’s object is not just a distribution of voter types. \(P\) is an arbitrary joint law over individually indexed votes, and \(f\) can depend on voter identity. Type masses preserve neither that dependence nor that identity-sensitive rule. Keeping them requires a law over complete vote profiles and a rule on those profiles; that retains a distribution over elections rather than making the population itself continuous.

For aggregate rules on an atomless population, an individual has zero mass, so point influence is automatically zero. One could replace \(e_i(f,P)\) with sensitivity to moving positive mass and specify a model for correlations across the population. That might yield a sensible new analytic problem, but it changes the theorem’s influence notion and dependence model, and still supplies no computational task to solve. The mismatch is therefore more basic than a tie-breaking or integrality issue: the theorem’s central information is indexed dependence, while a continuous society records mass by type.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a ChoCo mirror: it offers no named computational problem, and its main theorem does not naturally survive the move to type masses. The broader claim that no scenario could make a related continuous computational question worthwhile is weaker; a new problem about mass perturbations or stochastic populations could be worth asking, but it would need its own motivation and formulation rather than being anchored by this paper.
