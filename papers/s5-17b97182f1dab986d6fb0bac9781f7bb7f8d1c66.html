What the mirror covers

The candidate mass-flow model would represent applicant and position types by masses and assignments by flows, but it does not carry over a computational result. The paper’s classical and tree-domain characterizations and impossibility results remain axiomatic; the example rule and domain-structure discussion do not add a computational anchor.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is that two-sided matching has a plausible high-multiplicity regime, but this paper provides no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror.

For example, consider a large regional placement market with many applicants and positions. Applicants may fall into a small number of preference types because they rank specialties or locations similarly; positions may likewise fall into a small number of employer types. A continuum model could give each type a mass, normalized so each side has total mass \(1\). Its decision variable would be a matching flow \(x_{ij}\): the mass of applicant type \(i\) assigned to position type \(j\), with the type masses as its marginals. Stability would rule out mutually preferred blocking assignments. The paper has no welfare or cost objective to carry over: its object is the existence of a matching rule with specified properties.

That is a reasonable population-level matching model. The difficulty is that the paper’s results concern axiomatic existence and impossibility, not computational complexity. For instance, Theorem 1 characterizes rich, anonymous, single-peaked domains: top dominance on at least one side is equivalent to the existence of stable strategy-proof rules and of stable weakly group strategy-proof rules; on such domains, one of the two deferred-acceptance rules is stable and weakly group strategy-proof. The paper states this classical result but skips its proof in favor of the tree generalization, proved in the paper. Corollary 1 says no stable strategy-proof rule exists on the maximal classical single-peaked domain. Proposition 1 and Theorem 2 show that for \(n\ge5\), no stable rule on such a domain is respectively non-bossy or strongly group strategy-proof. These are substantive results, but none asserts a complexity class, running time, or algorithmic hardness. The tree-domain results extend the same axiomatic findings.

A possible mathematical analogue would ask whether a rule from reported type masses to matching flows can satisfy continuum stability and suitably defined incentive properties, and whether top dominance still characterizes when such a rule exists. But that is an axiomatic question, and defining incentives for positive-mass cohorts changes the paper’s unilateral-report notion. In an atomless population, an individual report has zero mass and may leave the outcome unchanged. Compressing rankings over named partners into rankings over partner types also changes the original model.

So the positive case is limited: large placement markets make the population-continuum setting plausible, and matching flows give a natural candidate object. The weak point is decisive for this programme: the paper supplies no eligible computational anchor, and its central questions are precisely the kind of axiomatic questions the stated ChoCo scope excludes.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is strong as a verdict on this paper: it has no computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes when stable strategy-proof rules exist on certain domains; Corollary 1 gives an incompatibility result on the maximal domain. Proposition 1 and Theorem 2 rule out stable non-bossy and strongly group strategy-proof rules when \(n\ge5\). The tree-domain results repeat these axiomatic questions. None studies an algorithm’s running time, complexity, or approximation. The threshold in Theorem 2 is a condition for an impossibility result, not a complexity parameter.

A large placement market could make a continuous population sensible. One could represent applicant and position types by masses and a matching by flows. But that gives a matching at a profile; it does not mirror Theorem 1, which asks whether a rule defined across profiles can satisfy stability and incentive properties. In an atomless model, an individual report has no effect on the flow, so individual strategy-proofness may become vacuous. Defining incentives for positive-mass cohorts could address that, but it changes the question into a new axiomatic analysis. The same issue applies to the impossibility results: reinterpreting their coalitions or non-bossiness in terms of mass does not turn them into computational claims.

Deferred acceptance is described as an algorithm, but the paper makes no computational claim about it. A continuous algorithm for computing stable flows might be a worthwhile separate project. So might an algorithm for checking top dominance on an encoded domain. Neither is a continuous mirror of a result here: the former adds a computational question the paper does not ask, and the latter concerns the representation of preference domains rather than population continuization.

The universal claim that *no related continuous computation could ever be worthwhile* would be too strong; the matching-flow setting leaves room for new work. But the case against a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper is convincing: its central contributions are axiomatic existence and impossibility results, and making the population continuous does not supply the missing computational anchor.
