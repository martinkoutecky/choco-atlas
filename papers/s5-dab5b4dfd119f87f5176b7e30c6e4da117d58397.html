The continuous mirror question

Given a finite state space \(\Omega\), common prior \(\mathcal D\), target \(f:\Omega\to[0,1]\), finite set \(T\) of epistemic and network types with mass distribution \(\mu\), and a strongly connected directed graph \(H\) on \(T\), determine the least \(R\) for which a sequence of type-to-type broadcasts makes \(\sum_{t,u\in T}\mu_t\mu_u\,\Pr_{\omega\sim\mathcal D}[|E_{t,R}(\omega)-E_{u,R}(\omega)|>\varepsilon]\le\delta\). Here \(E_{t,R}\) is the type’s posterior expectation after the broadcasts; determine the worst-case scaling in \(|T|\), the diameter of \(H\), \(\varepsilon\), and \(\delta\).

The model it lives in

A distribution \(\mu\) over complete epistemic and network roles; every member of a type shares its information partition and receives the same type-level messages. A type-to-type broadcast counts as one message, and \(\mu\) weights the disagreement of a randomly sampled pair. The prior \(\mathcal D\) remains a distribution over world states, separate from the population distribution.

The objection that survived

The mirror counts one type-to-type broadcast as one message and weights disagreement by \(\mu_t\mu_u\), while the theorem counts individual-to-neighbor messages and guarantees agreement for every pair; population continuity does not uniquely force those replacements.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 10’s graph-based approximate-agreement guarantee for epistemic cohorts. It leaves aside the two-agent protocol comparisons, the smoothed simulation result, and their associated communication and sampling bounds.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the least broadcast count for mass-weighted agreement compare with the all-pairs \(O(|T|d^2/(\delta\varepsilon^2))\) guarantee obtained by applying Theorem 10 to the type graph?
  • Which message-cost model best represents repeated cohorts: one unit per type broadcast, or a cost that scales with the receiving mass?

The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest positive case is Theorem 10, proved in this paper. It says that for \(N\) Bayesian agents with a common prior, communicating on a strongly connected directed graph of diameter \(d\), the paper’s spanning-tree protocol makes every pair \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-agree after \(O(Nd^{2}/(\delta\varepsilon^{2}))\) messages. Here agreement means their expectations of the same \(f:\Omega\to[0,1]\) differ by more than \(\varepsilon\) with prior probability at most \(\delta\). This is the paper’s own result; the preceding exact-consensus theorem, Theorem 9, is cited from Parikh and Krasucki.

A continuous mirror would model a large organization or deliberative network with recurring information roles: for example, many analysts who share the same evidence access and communication role. Let \(T\) be a finite set of such epistemic types. Each type \(t\) specifies a complete information partition \(\Pi_t\) of a finite state space \(\Omega\), as well as its communication role. A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(T\), where \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of agents in role \(t\). All agents share a prior \(\mathcal D\) and want to estimate the same \(f\). A strongly connected directed graph \(H\) on the types specifies which cohorts can communicate. When type \(t\) sends a message to type \(u\), it broadcasts its current Bayesian expectation to that cohort; members of a type who have received the same messages update identically.

Here is the problem I would hand to a prover: given \((\Omega,\mathcal D,f,T,\mu,H)\) and \(\varepsilon,\delta\), find a sequence of type-to-type broadcasts minimizing the number \(R\) of messages, subject to
\[ \sum_{t,u\in T}\mu_t\mu_u\, \Pr_{\omega\sim\mathcal D}\!\left[ |E_{t,R}(\omega)-E_{u,R}(\omega)|>\varepsilon \right]\le\delta. \]
The solution is the broadcast schedule and its resulting posteriors. The pair is sampled from the population, so mass directly weights whose disagreement matters. This is a population-level agreement question: \(\mu\) is the share of society in each role, not a prior over world states or a noise model.

I expect the constructive message-complexity version to be tractable (Class A). Apply Theorem 10 to the \(\tau=|T|\) type representatives and graph \(H\). Its guarantee for every pair is stronger than the mass-weighted condition above, giving a schedule of \(O(\tau d^{2}/(\delta\varepsilon^{2}))\) type-level broadcasts, independent of how many agents the cohorts contain. The mirror therefore asks whether agreement among a large society can be achieved by communicating among its relatively few epistemic roles. The paper already studies agreement among \(N\) agents, so this population interpretation seems close to the authors’ problem. I am claiming a message bound in the paper’s protocol model, not a polynomial-time method for computing arbitrary Bayesian updates.

The soft spot is the regime assumption: many people must truly share both an information partition and a network role. That is plausible for repeated cohorts with common evidence access and broadcast channels, but if agents’ information or connections are mostly idiosyncratic, \(\tau\) will grow with the population and the high-multiplicity gain disappears. Further questions are whether a mass-weighted schedule can beat the all-pairs spanning-tree guarantee, and what happens when communication is described by a genuinely continuous interaction kernel rather than a finite type graph.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 10 is a real communication complexity result, so I can’t dismiss this anchor as having nothing computational to mirror. The strongest objection is that the proposed continuum changes what counts as a message.

In the paper, a message is sent from one named agent to one neighbor, and Theorem 10 counts total messages. Duplicating agents of a given role therefore duplicates transmissions; the mass vector \(\mu\) alone does not say how many there are or how their edges are wired. In an atomless limit, total transmissions are unbounded. Counting one type-to-type broadcast as a single message avoids that problem, but gives every sender a new broadcast operation. Counting delivery to each recipient preserves the paper’s resource, but makes the cost depend on population size and network structure, not just type masses.

The agreement criterion exposes the same tension. If the mirror retains Theorem 10’s requirement that every pair agree, the masses do not affect the guarantee: the type representatives and their graph determine it. If it instead weights disagreement by \(\mu_t\mu_u\), as the proponent proposes, the question becomes whether a randomly sampled pair agrees. That is a sensible aggregate objective, but it permits disagreement among sufficiently rare roles and is not the paper’s all-pairs criterion. Either way, with fixed finite support the posteriors and protocol dynamics still belong to a finite network; the continuous variable only changes the weighting or the cost convention. If the number of roles grows with the population, the proposed compression disappears.

That makes this a weak case against the best mirror, though. Large cohorts with shared information access and broadcast channels are plausible, and mass-weighted agreement is a reasonable social objective. I can argue that the proposed mirror needs a clearer resource model and moves away from the theorem’s exact guarantee. I cannot honestly argue that no worthwhile population-level question survives; this is one of the paper’s stronger anchors.
