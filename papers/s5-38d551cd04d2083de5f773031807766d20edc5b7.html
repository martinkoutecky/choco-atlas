The continuous mirror question

Given a finite set of interaction types \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric nonnegative rational kernel \(K_{ij}\), a rational \(\epsilon>0\), and initial and target opinion-mass distributions \(x^0_{i,a}\) and \(y_{i,a}\) over \(a\in\{0,1,2\}\), decide whether the synchronous process ever reaches \(x^t=y\). Define \(D_i=\sum_j K_{ij}\mu_j\) and \(p_{i,a}(x)=\sum_j K_{ij}x_{j,a}\). Each mass cohort of type \(i\) currently holding opinion \(a\) moves to \(b\), with \(c\) the remaining opinion, if \(p_{i,a}(x)\le D_i/2+\epsilon\), \(p_{i,b}(x)>D_i/2+\epsilon\), and \(p_{i,b}(x)>p_{i,c}(x)\); otherwise it stays at \(a\).

The model it lives in

A synchronous mean-field opinion process with finitely many interaction-role types, rational population masses, a shared interaction kernel, and deterministic mass-based exposure updates; the decision question is exact target reachability.

The objection that survived

For mixed opinions within a role, type-wide exposure erases the vertex-specific neighborhoods central to the paper's sparse graph dynamics; the hardness embedding uses unanimous roles and does not validate that fractional regime.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best mirror I see is a continuous reachability problem for strongly anti-conservative opinion dynamics. It keeps the paper’s central question—whether a synchronous opinion process ever reaches a specified state—and replaces each agent’s finite neighbor count with an exposure computed from the masses of interacting types.

My anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper. It states that for every \(\epsilon>0\), deciding whether a given unsigned, unweighted, undirected graph, starting from a specified \(\{0,1,2\}\)-configuration, ever reaches a specified target configuration under \(d_{\epsilon\text{-AM}}\) is PSPACE-complete. Here \(d_{\epsilon\text{-AM}}\) is the strongly anti-conservative rule with the constant stability requirement \(\sigma(u)=\epsilon\) at every node. The paper proves this by adapting its simulation construction; the underlying hardness of the binary majority reachability problem comes from the cited earlier work [35].

Call the mirror Continuous Strongly Anti-Conservative Reachability. An instance has \(\tau\) types, rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), a symmetric nonnegative rational interaction kernel \(K_{ij}\), an initial distribution \(x^0_{i,a}\), and a target distribution \(y_{i,a}\), where \(a\in\{0,1,2\}\) and \(\sum_a x^0_{i,a}=\sum_a y_{i,a}=\mu_i\). Type \(i\) is an interaction role: its members share the same interaction weights to every type and the same update rule. The variable \(x_{i,a}\) is the mass of type \(i\) currently holding opinion \(a\).

Define type \(i\)’s total exposure and its exposure to opinion \(a\) by
\[ D_i=\sum_j K_{ij}\mu_j, \qquad p_{i,a}(x)=\sum_j K_{ij}x_{j,a}. \]
Fix a rational \(\epsilon>0\). A mass cohort of type \(i\) currently holding opinion \(a\) moves to \(b\) when, for the remaining opinion \(c\),
\[ p_{i,a}(x)\le D_i/2+\epsilon,\qquad p_{i,b}(x)>D_i/2+\epsilon,\qquad p_{i,b}(x)>p_{i,c}(x). \]
Otherwise it stays at \(a\). All mass in a type/opinion cohort updates together; applying this rule synchronously defines the next distribution. The question is whether \(x^t=y\) for some \(t\ge 0\). A yes-instance has a finite trajectory to the target as its witness. This is a reachability problem, not an optimization problem.

There is a direct hardness embedding. Given a graph with \(n\) vertices, make one type per vertex, set \(\mu_i=1/n\), and set \(K_{ij}=n\) on edges and \(0\) otherwise. Then \(D_i\) equals the vertex degree, and on profiles where each type’s mass is entirely in one opinion, \(p_{i,a}\) equals the number of neighbors holding \(a\). Encode the paper’s initial and target configurations by putting mass \(1/n\) in the corresponding opinion of each type. The continuous trajectory then exactly reproduces the discrete one. Thus Theorem 4’s PSPACE-hardness transfers. The continuous problem is also in PSPACE: each of the at most \(3\tau\) initial type/opinion cohorts stays together, so its state can be tracked by its current opinion. There are at most \(3^{3\tau}\) such assignments, and rational exposures and target comparisons have polynomial-size representations. The expected classification is therefore PSPACE-complete, with hardness transferring.

A plausible regime is a large online community or institution with many participants in each recurring interaction role. People in a role receive the same standardized exposure from each other role, based on the opinion mass there. The number of people can be arbitrarily larger than the number of roles; a finite population approximation could have \(q\) people per type, with \(q\gg\tau\). In the hardness embedding, \(\tau\) grows with the graph, but each type still represents a continuum of interchangeable participants. The hard combinatorics live in the interaction pattern among roles, not in how many people occupy them. That is a meaningful class-B mirror, even though it does not show that continuization removes this paper’s hardness.

The mirror seems recognizable to the authors because it retains their three opinions, synchronous deterministic update, anti-conservative threshold rule, and target-reachability question. The model’s co-modification is to replace neighbor counts by type-mass exposures, with the kernel scaled so those exposures match counts on the embedded instances. Further questions include whether restrictions on the interaction kernel—such as bounded role degree or low rank—change the complexity, and how much genuinely mixed initial opinion mass changes the dynamics beyond the pure profiles used for the hardness embedding.

The weakest point is the choice of regime. Many vertices in the paper’s hardness construction may have distinct interaction roles, and their topology is essential; the continuous model preserves that structure in \(K\). A referee might regard the population as infinitely replicated graph positions rather than a convincing social population. The case survives only if role-based interaction is accepted as a sensible many-agent regime. The paper’s local update rule is already expressed entirely through weighted opinion exposure, which makes the integral replacement fairly faithful, but the scenario is less compelling than a case where the type count stays small while the population grows.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 4 is a real computational anchor: it proves PSPACE-completeness for a named reachability problem. The objection has to be about whether the proposed mass model is a continuous-population version of that problem, not about what its answer would be.

In the paper, each node updates from the opinions of its actual neighbors. Grouping nodes into interaction types preserves that process only when the group members’ relevant neighborhood behavior stays aligned—for example, when every member of a group has the same opinion. Then the continuous trajectory remains on an all-or-nothing subspace: each type’s mass holds one opinion, and the mass variables add no new state. The proponent’s embedding is exact on that subspace.

The proposed model becomes genuinely fractional when members of a type can hold different opinions. But its exposure \(p_{i,a}(x)\) gives every member the same pressure from the type-wide opinion masses. In the paper’s graph dynamics, two members with the same coarse role can have different neighbor opinions and update differently. The paper’s hardness construction uses bounded-degree graphs, so replicating each role does not make those local neighborhoods average out. It instead replaces individual contacts with homogeneous mixing across types. That may be a useful model, but it changes the update process.

A graphon-style model is the strongest repair: it can represent population-level interaction and keep heterogeneous opinions. For finite-step kernels, though, its state evolution reduces to the same type-level mean-field dynamics the proponent proposes. For sparse networks, preserving local neighborhoods requires tracking their correlations and evolving configurations; a finite mass vector generally does not close the dynamics. Retaining enough information to reproduce the paper’s exact node-level targets pushes the number of types up with the graph and leaves the continuous population as replicated copies of the original nodes.

That leaves a substantive new question about reachability in a mean-field opinion process. But the PSPACE-hardness embedding proves hardness using only profiles where every type is unanimous, so the continuous population contributes no fractional behavior to the hard trajectory. The mirror’s case therefore depends on whether the authors would recognize homogeneous mixing over interaction roles as their prediction problem. Given the paper’s focus on updates from graph neighborhoods, that recognition is uncertain.

This is the strongest objection I can make, but it is not airtight. If the authors accept the mean-field transition rule as a natural population version of their graph dynamics, Theorem 4 survives as a Class B mirror, which the programme explicitly allows. The negative case is strongest at the model-fidelity gate and weak there; I would not claim to have ruled out every worthwhile mirror.
