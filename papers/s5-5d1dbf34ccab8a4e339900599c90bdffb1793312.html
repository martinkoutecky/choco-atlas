The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify a valid computational anchor in this paper. The closest is Theorem 1.3 (proved here): a predicate \(P\) is \(\Phi\)-trivial for every arity if and only if it is \(\Phi\)-trivial for arity \(2\). Theorem 1.5 (also proved here) classifies which non-degenerate symmetric binary predicates are \(\Phi_{\mathrm{neg}}\)-trivial or \(\Phi_{\mathrm{id}}\)-trivial. These are structural classification theorems; neither asserts a computational problem is in P, hard, FPT, or otherwise gives a complexity result. The paper has no named result of the kind required for an anchor.

The most plausible population regime would have many agents, each with a feasible evaluation \(y\in P\), and type mass \(\mu_y\). But the paper’s polymorphisms \(f_i:\Sigma_i^n\to\Sigma_i\) can depend on which individual supplied each coordinate; the distribution \(\mu\) forgets those identities. Keeping identities leaves no high-multiplicity compression, while restricting to rules that depend only on type masses changes the class of aggregators being classified. A continuous preservation problem could be posed, but it would be a new algebraic question, without an objective or computational result from this paper to mirror.

So there are no eligible anchors and no honest per-anchor continuous problems or expected complexity classes to give. A positive case would require stretching the paper’s results beyond what they establish.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper has no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1.3 reduces an algebraic classification across all finite arities to arity two; Theorem 1.5 classifies which symmetric predicates have only specified kinds of polymorphisms. Neither studies an algorithmic problem whose input includes a society, nor gives a complexity or optimization result. Their numbered form does not make them computational anchors.

There is a plausible high-multiplicity regime: a large organization whose members share a finite set \(P\) of complete judgment types, with society represented by masses \(\mu_y\). That makes the population limit sensible, but it does not make it relevant to these theorems. The polymorphism condition quantifies over every feasible tuple of voter inputs. It is independent of how often each type occurs in a particular society. So the issue is not that repeated types are implausible, or that a mass model forgets information the paper needs; it is that the paper’s classification has no mass-dependent question to answer.

The best mirror would replace the finite voter list with an atomless population and classify issue-wise aggregation functionals on measurable voter signals that preserve \(P\). That is a coherent extension of the paper’s algebraic subject. But it remains a structural, axiomatic classification, outside ChoCo’s stated computational scope. To turn it into a computational problem, one would need to choose an encoding and restrict the allowed functionals—for example, to threshold rules or a particular circuit class. Those choices would define a new problem rather than continuize a result in this paper. Requiring deterministic output to vary continuously with a population distribution may even collapse the rule to a constant, since the output alphabet is finite; relaxing that requirement leaves a broad function-class classification.

So the proposed continuum algebra is the strongest nearby idea, and could be worthwhile on its own terms. It supplies no continuous computational mirror of Theorem 1.3 or 1.5. The proponent’s conclusion holds, though their concern about identity loss is not the decisive reason.
