The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a case that meets your anchor rule: the paper contains no named computational-complexity result. It proves structural results about unanimity and agendas, but does not classify a problem as being in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise computationally hard or tractable.

The closest results are structural. Theorem 2.2, proved in this paper, says that an aggregation space is robustly unanimity consistent exactly when its largest critical fragment has size at most three. Theorem 3.2, also proved here, says that the spaces of strict orderings, weak orderings, and equivalence relations are designably unanimity consistent. Theorem 3.4, proved here, says that an exposed critical fragment of size at least four rules out designable unanimity consistency. None is a computational-complexity result under your definition of an anchor.

There is a precise population-continuous model in the paper: take \(T=X\), the feasible complete views; let \(\mu_x\) be the fraction of society holding view \(x\); take the agenda \(\gamma\) as the decision variable; and ask whether sequential majority decisions preserve every unanimous issue. But this is already the paper’s model and question: it defines anonymous profiles as probability measures over \(X\) and states its results for those profiles. A continuous version would restate the paper, not provide a distinct mirror. The paper’s focus is also a structural consistency property, rather than the computational landscape ChoCo targets.

A plausible *new computational follow-up* would be: given a succinct representation of \(X\), decide whether there exists an agenda \(\gamma\) such that \(F^\gamma(\mu)\) respects unanimity for every \(\mu\in\Delta^*(X)\), and return such an agenda when one exists. Theorem 3.2 gives constructive agendas for three families, and Theorem 3.4 gives a structural obstruction. But this would be a complexity study of an existing agenda-design property—not a continuous mirror anchored in a named computational result of this paper.

So the strongest honest positive point is that the paper studies a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and agenda synthesis could be computationalized. The decisive weakness is that its population is already represented continuously, while it offers no qualifying computational anchor for a continuization programme to build on.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The main obstacle is that the paper has already made the population continuous in the relevant sense. Section 1.1 defines an anonymous profile \(\mu\) over feasible views and interprets \(\mu(x)\) as the fraction of voters holding view \(x\). That is already a mass vector over types; the paper’s results range over all such profiles, including fractional ones. A discrete electorate with counts \(n_x\) is just the special case \(\mu(x)=n_x/n\). So a continuous mirror of these results would not translate a finite-population theorem into a new population model. The source paper states them in that model already.

That disposes of the strongest proposed anchor, Theorem 2.2. Its characterization—robust unanimity consistency exactly when \(\kappa(X)\leq 3\)—already quantifies over distributions \(\mu\), not just integer voter counts. Theorem 3.2 likewise establishes agenda existence for every profile in the distribution model, and Theorem 3.4 rules it out under its exposed-fragment condition in that same model. These are structural theorems, not computational-complexity results, and changing the interpretation of \(\mu\) cannot turn them into such results.

The proposed agenda-synthesis follow-up is the best route to a computational question, but it changes the target. Given a succinct description of \(X\), deciding whether some agenda works for every profile asks for the complexity of recognizing a property of the feasible-view space. The profile is quantified away. For a fixed profile, agenda search can depend on its fractional masses, but the paper’s sequential rule already takes such profiles as input; any algorithmic challenge would come from the agenda and the representation of \(X\), not from introducing a continuous population. Either version could motivate separate work on agenda computation, but neither supplies a new continuous mirror of a result in this paper.

I would not claim that no useful algorithmic agenda problem can be built from these ideas. The narrower negative case is strong: the paper offers no named computational result to continuize, and its unanimity results already use the continuous-population model ChoCo would otherwise propose. That leaves little for a continuous mirror to add.
