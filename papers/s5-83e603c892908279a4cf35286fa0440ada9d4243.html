The case FOR (proponent)

There are no qualifying computational anchors in this paper. Theorem 1 characterizes when the distributional choice rule is the unique rule satisfying three axioms; Theorem 3 characterizes when it is path independent; and Theorem 5 characterizes the deferred-acceptance mechanism by market-design axioms. None asserts a complexity classification or analyzes running time. The polynomial-time claim in the related literature concerns Hafalir et al. (2026), not a result of this paper. So I cannot honestly give the required anchor-based case.

There is still a plausible *research lead*. Large-scale school admissions—the paper’s own setting—can involve a very large population spread across a small number of intersectional trait groups. A continuous version could represent each group and priority band by a mass \(\mu_{a,r}\), with school capacity \(\kappa\) measured as a population share. The decision is an admitted-mass vector \(x_{a,r}\), constrained by \(0\le x_{a,r}\le\mu_{a,r}\) and \(\sum_{a,r}x_{a,r}=\kappa\). A school’s distributional preference ranks the admitted trait masses \(z_a=\sum_r x_{a,r}\); among distributionally maximal vectors, the rule lexicographically favors mass in higher-priority bands. The computational question would be to find such an \(x\), given a compact representation of the preference and the population distribution.

That question connects naturally to the paper’s cohort frontiers, priority refinement, and deferred acceptance. For polyhedral preferences or matroid-base-polytope frontiers, I would expect a tractable route through linear optimization. It also raises questions about which preference representations admit efficient optimization, and whether path independence and deferred acceptance survive for mass allocations. But this is a proposed continuation, not a question anchored by a computational result in the paper.

The scenario is credible, but the mirror has a real modeling cost: the paper uses strict priorities over individual students. Aggregating applicants into finitely many priority bands suppresses distinctions the paper’s rule uses; preserving exact priority may require a continuous priority coordinate, so the finite-type advantage weakens. That is the strongest limitation of the positive case, alongside the absence of an eligible computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror of this paper is that it offers no computational result to mirror. Theorem 1 characterizes a choice rule, Theorem 3 characterizes path independence, and Theorem 5 characterizes a matching mechanism. None studies the complexity of a computational problem. The polynomial-time result mentioned in the related literature belongs to Hafalir et al. (2026), not to this paper. So the proponent raised no qualifying theorem anchors; the mass-allocation proposal is a new research lead, not a continuous version of one of the paper’s results.

That lead also needs more modeling before it becomes a computational question. The paper permits an arbitrary preorder over cohorts, but does not give it a computational representation. A continuous algorithm needs an input model for the distributional objective—such as a compact score function, polytope, or matroid representation. Choosing one supplies structure absent from the general results and makes the complexity question depend on that choice. Hafalir et al.’s polynomial-time work is already a close algorithmic neighbor for score-based distributional objectives, so a new proposal would need to identify its distinct problem precisely.

The stronger version would model a continuum of students by traits, school preferences, and priority information, then define continuum deferred acceptance. That is a plausible project, but it does not follow just by replacing counts with masses. Exact strict priorities may require a rank coordinate; finite priority bands can change which students the paper’s rule selects. And Theorem 5’s strategy-proofness concerns individuals’ reports. A mass-allocation model needs its own account of how an individual deviation affects the outcome and what strategy-proofness means there. Those choices amount to a new mechanism-design problem.

This makes the negative case strong on the programme’s stated criterion: there is no worthwhile continuous mirror anchored in this paper’s computational results, because there are no such results. It is weaker as the literal claim that no useful continuous-population project could arise in any scenario. Large-scale admissions with repeated student profiles remain plausible; the case against is that pursuing them would be a separate project, not this paper’s mirror.
