Equations (26), (27), and (31) define control optimizations; the stability claim in §3.4.3 is an analytic guarantee, and the performance and reporting claims come from simulations. None establishes the kind of complexity result this programme is set up to mirror.

The best candidate is reservation-based control in (27). A continuous society could distribute mass over approach, destination, value of time, and arrival window, then ask how to schedule that mass subject to headway and conflict constraints. But that would be a new traffic-scheduling problem, not a continuous counterpart to a computational result in this paper. If arrival and position are modeled finely enough to preserve the ordering and safety constraints, the support can grow with the individual vehicles. If they are coarsened into cohorts, the model gains a tractable aggregate structure by changing the scheduling problem.

The phase-switching and max-weight formulations are less promising as computational anchors. With a fixed set of phases, replacing sums over vehicles with type-weighted sums gives an aggregate objective, but the choice is still among the same finite actions; the paper gives no hard computational question about that step. The TU payment calculation is even more directly aggregative: its payoffs already depend on group totals, and the game is only \(2\times2\). Replacing vehicle sums with mass integrals would largely restate the mechanism.

There is a plausible high-density traffic scenario in which these models could be useful, so the universal claim that no scenario could produce a worthwhile mirror is not airtight. The honest negative case is narrower: this paper offers no eligible computational result to anchor one, and its most natural continuous reformulations either collapse to simple aggregation or open a new traffic-control problem. That makes the attached “no anchor” case persuasive under the programme’s stated criteria, even though it does not prove that every possible fluid-population project would be unworthy.
