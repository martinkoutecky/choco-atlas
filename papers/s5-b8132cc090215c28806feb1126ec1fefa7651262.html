The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive mirror is population-continuous PAV and Nash portioning. A voter type is an approval set \(A\subseteq P\); \(\mu_A\) is the fraction of the electorate with that type. The decision is a portioning \(r\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}^{P}\) with \(\sum_j r(j)=1\), and type \(A\)’s utility is \(u_A(r)=\sum_{j\in A}r(j)\). For a plausible regime—millions of residents expressing approvals over a modest set of parties, with many residents sharing ballots—the natural objective is weighted Nash welfare:
\[ \Phi_\mu(r)=\sum_A \mu_A\log u_A(r). \]
This makes society, rather than just the allocation, continuous.

The paper’s strongest foothold is Proposition C.13, proved here, though inspired by Peters [Pet25]. It says that for a fixed finite voter set, party set, and approval profile, a portioning \(r\) is a limit of PAV-optimal committees as committee size tends to infinity if and only if \(r\) maximizes Nash welfare among portionings. That result gives a direct reason to study the weighted objective above when ballots are aggregated by type.

A precise follow-on question is Continuous-Population Nash Portioning: given parties \(P\), a support of \(\tau\) nonempty approval types with positive binary-rational masses \(\mu_A\) summing to \(1\), and rational \(\epsilon>0\), return a rational portioning \(r\) such that \(\Phi_\mu(r)\ge \max_{x\in\Delta(P)}\Phi_\mu(x)-\epsilon\). I would expect this to be tractable by convex optimization, with running time polynomial in \(m,\tau\), the input bit length, and the requested precision. Natural follow-ups include exact solution and certificates for the optimality conditions and their price interpretation. This mirrors the paper’s PAV-to-Nash bridge, not its full network of axiomatic implications.

There is a decisive limitation under your anchor rule: the paper has no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a computational complexity result such as membership in P or hardness. Proposition C.13 is a limit characterization, not a complexity classification; the other numbered results are axiomatic implications. Algorithm 1 and the discussion of a polynomial-time procedure are the nearest computational items, but the text attributes that running-time result to prior work and it is not a numbered result of the required kind. So the problem above is a plausible new mirror question, but it cannot honestly be presented as a continuous counterpart to a qualifying computational anchor in this paper.

The soft spot is that the proposal extends the paper in two directions: the paper holds the voter set fixed while committee size grows, whereas this mirror also makes the population continuous and asks a new optimization question. Its connection to the paper’s own Nash-portioning result makes the extension recognizable, but it does not supply the missing computational anchor.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The anchor does not meet the paper’s computational-mirror criterion. Proposition C.13 is a limit characterization: with a fixed finite voter set and fixed approval profile, normalized PAV-optimal committees converge to Nash-optimal portionings. It asserts no complexity result. The paper has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition classifying the complexity of a computational problem; Algorithm 1’s polynomial-time result is unnumbered and attributed to prior work.

The stronger population version in the case for is easy to state, but its objective is exactly ordinary Nash portioning on an expanded finite electorate. For rational masses, choose a common denominator \(D\) and make \(D\mu_A\) voters have approval type \(A\). Then
\[ \sum_A \mu_A\log u_A(r) =\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\log u_i(r). \]
The factor \(1/D\) does not change the optimizer. Thus the proposal’s only new computational feature is encoding repeated voters by binary rational weights. That could motivate a separate succinct-input algorithm question, but it is not a computational mirror of Proposition C.13, and the paper’s own portioning section already supplies the Nash objective and its optimization framework.

There is a limit to this negative case: approval types are a plausible high-multiplicity model, and the weighted formulation is coherent. I would not claim that no worthwhile population-continuous problem could be built from this setting. The narrower conclusion is stronger: the proposed anchor does not survive the stated test, and the paper offers no named computational result from which another qualifying mirror can be drawn.
