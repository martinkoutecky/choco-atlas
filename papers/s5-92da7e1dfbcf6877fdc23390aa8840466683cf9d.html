The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant-type and position-type sets \(I,J\), compatibility relation \(A\subseteq I\times J\), rational masses \(\mu_i>0\) summing to \(1\), and rational per-capita supplies \(\sigma_j\ge 0\) summing to less than \(1\), with every supply jointly fillable, compute a feasible flow \(x_{ij}\ge 0\) supported on \(A\), satisfying \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\) and \(\sum_i x_{ij}=\sigma_j\), whose rates \(p_i=\sum_j x_{ij}/\mu_i\) give a lexicographically maximal population-weighted quantile function \(Q_p(z)\). Return the exact rational flow and a sampler for measurable one-to-one matchings realizing it, in time polynomial in \(|I|,|J|\), and the input bit length.

The model it lives in

A nonatomic applicant population partitioned into types \(i\in I\) of masses \(\mu_i\), with compatible position types \(j\in J\) supplied at masses \(\sigma_j\). The decision is a type-to-position mass flow \(x_{ij}\); each type’s matching rate is \(p_i=\sum_j x_{ij}/\mu_i\), and fairness lexicographically maximizes the population-weighted rate quantiles, with a measurable matching lottery realizing the flow.

The objection that survived

The precise mass-weighted quantile objective is not ordinary leximin over types, and the cited algorithms do not establish a running time polynomial in the binary-encoded masses; the proponent acknowledges that this version needs its own analysis.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest mirror is the paper’s one-sided matching result in a large placement market where applicants share a small number of exact eligibility profiles. For example, a large annual cohort might be matched to role categories using binary qualification rules. A type is a complete eligibility profile: two applicants have the same type exactly when the matching problem treats them identically. Positions are grouped by role and eligibility pattern as well. The regime has \(N\) applicants and vacancies, with \(N\) much larger than the number of applicant and position types, \(\tau+\kappa\).

Anchor: Theorem 7, proved in this paper. In its one-sided bipartite setting, applicants are the user side \(L\), every right-side vertex can be covered by a maximum matching, \(|R|<|L|\), and isolated vertices can be removed. The theorem says Algorithm 3 solves maxmin-fair matching exactly in expected time \(O((|V|^2+|E||V|^{2/3})(\log |V|)^2)\). Its output is a maximum matching drawn from a maxmin-fair distribution. The paper has no comparable hardness result to anchor instead.

Lead problem — Mass-Maxmin-Fair Matching. An instance has a finite applicant-type set \(I\), a finite position-type set \(J\), a compatibility relation \(A\subseteq I\times J\), applicant masses \(\mu_i>0\) with \(\sum_{i\in I}\mu_i=1\), and per-capita position supplies \(\sigma_j\ge 0\) with \(\sum_{j\in J}\sigma_j<1\). All masses and supplies are binary rationals. Assume the supplies can all be filled: for every \(B\subseteq J\), \(\sum_{j\in B}\sigma_j\le\sum_{i\in N(B)}\mu_i\). A feasible mass flow \(x\) satisfies \(x_{ij}\ge0\), \(x_{ij}=0\) outside \(A\), \(\sum_j x_{ij}\le\mu_i\), and \(\sum_i x_{ij}=\sigma_j\). Type \(i\)’s individual satisfaction rate is \(p_i=\sum_j x_{ij}/\mu_i\).

For fairness, sort these rates across the population, giving each type’s rate weight \(\mu_i\). Call the resulting weighted quantile function \(Q_p(z)\), for \(z\in(0,1]\). The problem is to compute a feasible flow whose \(Q_p\) is lexicographically maximal: maximize the lowest rate first, then the next population mass of rates, and continue. A solution is the exact rational flow and rates, together with a sampler for measurable one-to-one matchings that realizes the flow and gives every individual of type \(i\) matching probability \(p_i\). Each sampled individual is either matched once or unmatched; the fractional quantities describe mass and lottery probabilities.

This preserves the paper’s central question: which individuals get matched, and what is the strongest possible distributional guarantee for those individuals? In finite blow-ups where every applicant of a type has the same neighbors, symmetry and the paper’s uniqueness of maxmin-fair satisfaction probabilities make the common type rate the natural limit of the individual probabilities. The lottery is part of the matching problem; the continuization is of the applicant population and position supply. It does not replace the population with fractional recipients.

I expect this problem to be tractable in time polynomial in \(\tau,\kappa,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. Its constraints form a rational capacitated bipartite-flow problem, and the fairness profile suggests a sequence of bottleneck or min-cut computations. Theorem 7 motivates that expectation but does not establish it: its stated bound is for the individual-level graph, so the rational-mass version needs its own analysis.

The mirror covers Theorem 7’s one-sided bipartite matching result. It does not claim to continue the paper’s arbitrary-graph extension or its broader matroid characterization. Two follow-on questions are whether the continuous profile agrees exactly with finite high-multiplicity instances at rational masses, and how small an exact, auditable lottery sampler can be.

The weakest point is that the paper already gives a polynomial-time algorithm even when applicants have distinct neighborhoods. This mirror therefore does not show discrete hardness dissolving in the continuum; its contribution would be a precise high-multiplicity formulation and an algorithm parameterized by type support and rational bit length. The regime also requires genuinely few exact compatibility types. Where applicants’ eligibility patterns are mostly unique, the proposed compression has little force.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against Theorem 7’s mirror is that its type-level formulation is already a continuous flow-fairness problem in familiar clothes. Put applicant types on source arcs of capacity \(\mu_i\), position types on sink arcs of capacity \(\sigma_j\), and connect compatible pairs. The source flow for type \(i\), divided by \(\mu_i\), is its per-capita satisfaction rate. A matching lottery can then be recovered from the flow’s edge marginals using bipartite matching decomposition. For equal-mass types, the proposed leximin objective is exactly lexicographic maximization of source flows. Megiddo gave a polynomial algorithm for lexicographically optimal multi-terminal flows; later work gives polynomial algorithms for weighted max-min fair flow with variable routing, normalizing rates by demand. [Megiddo (1977)](https://theory.stanford.edu/~megiddo/pdf/lexflow.pdf), [Allalouf and Shavitt (2008)](https://www.eng.tau.ac.il/~shavitt/pub/ToN08maxmin.pdf). The paper itself cites earlier lexicographic-flow algorithms as applicable to its fairness computation.

That makes the mirror’s strongest possible contribution look like a compressed, matching-specific presentation of continuous fair-flow machinery. But it does not yet establish a novelty collision for the proposed problem as stated. Its \(Q_p\) objective counts each rate with weight \(\mu_i\); ordinary leximin over type rates, or demand-normalized weighted max-min flow, need not implement that frequency-weighted ordering. And an algorithm on an explicit clone graph does not by itself give the desired running time in \(\tau,\kappa,L\) when rational masses have large binary denominators.

Exact types may also make compression unhelpful in many hiring markets, where eligibility profiles can be numerous. That limits the range of the mirror, but it does not defeat the plausible standardized-cohort scenario the proponent gave. Per-capita position supply is a coherent co-modification, and the paper’s theorem is plainly a named computational result.

So the prior-art argument is a serious reason to check the weighted-flow literature before investing in this mirror, but I cannot honestly use it to rule out every worthwhile version. The precise binary-encoded, population-weighted leximin problem may still be a distinct computational question. On the material here, the negative case is weak at its decisive point: Theorem 7’s mirror survives.
