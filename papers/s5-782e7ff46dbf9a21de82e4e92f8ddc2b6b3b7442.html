The continuous mirror question

Given atomless doctor and hospital populations with rational masses on finitely many complete types, finite contract forms, and type-specific choice rules satisfying the paper's assumptions, find a measurable matching with integral portfolios for individual agents and at most one contract per doctor–hospital pair. Stability requires individual rationality and the absence of any nonempty finite blocking set of individual contracts. Find a doctor-greatest stable matching \(M^D\) such that, for every stable matching \(M\), \(C_d(M^D_d \cup M_d)=M^D_d\) for almost every doctor \(d\); give it a finite type or configuration representation.

The model it lives in

A two-sided atomless matching market described by rational masses over finitely many doctor and hospital types. Types specify complete choice rules and contract features; the mass describes the population, while each agent still holds an integral portfolio. The output is a measurable realization of the doctor-greatest stable matching, represented through type-level configurations.

The objection that survived

The proposed positive-mass blocking condition can ignore a null-mass blocking pair, so it may change the paper's stability concept; defining blocks as any nonempty finite set of individual contracts preserves that concept.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the procedural doctor-optimal selection in Theorem 1. It leaves the weakly hospital-quasi-stable lattice, hospital pessimality, maximality, and the rural hospitals result unaddressed.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the doctor-greatest stable matching be computed from a finite type or configuration representation, and what is the complexity in the support size and input bit length?
  • Under what conditions do doctor-greatest stable matchings of finite replica markets converge to the continuum allocation?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive anchor is Theorem 1, “Doctor-optimality of cumulative offers,” proved in this paper. Under Assumption 1—doctors’ choices are substitutable, unitary, and satisfy IRC; hospitals’ choices are unilaterally substitutable, unitary, and satisfy IRC—every doctor-proposing cumulative-offer trajectory terminates at the greatest stable allocation under the doctor Blair order. That allocation is the unique doctor-optimal stable allocation. The process proposes each contract at most once, so it uses at most \(|X|\) proposals. This is a genuine procedural result, though the paper does not analyze its complexity under a particular encoding of the choice functions or state an NP-hardness or polynomial-time classification.

Here is the continuous problem I would put forward for that anchor. Take finitely many doctor types \(I\) and hospital types \(J\), with rational masses \(\mu_i\) and \(\nu_j\), each summing to one. A type fully specifies the agent’s choice rule, preferences, capacity, and contract terms. Give each type an atomless population of that mass. Potential contracts connect individual doctors and hospitals and carry a contract form \(q\) from a finite vocabulary. Each individual retains an integral bundle of contracts, and each doctor–hospital pair can have at most one contract, as in the paper’s unitary domain.

A solution is a measurable, locally finite, contract-labeled matching between these populations. It is stable if agents choose their current bundles and there is no positive-mass set of new contracts that every endpoint chooses when offered alongside its current bundle. The question is to find the doctor-greatest stable matching \(M^D\): for every stable matching \(M\), almost every doctor \(d\) satisfies
\[ C_d(M^D_d \cup M_d)=M^D_d. \]
There is no cardinal objective in the paper to preserve; the objective is precisely this revealed-choice maximum among stable allocations. The continuous population is the mass of agents, while each agent’s bundle remains integral.

A plausible regime is a large staffing or placement market, such as locum clinicians and care facilities, with many agents but relatively few complete choice types: specialty, availability, capacity, ranking over contract forms, and so on. A type must include every feature used by the choice rule; if prices or preferences vary, they generate distinct types. This keeps the paper’s many-to-many relationships and contract-level choices while making the society itself continuous.

I would submit this as a low-confidence Class A candidate. With finite contract forms, finitely many types, and efficiently evaluable type rules, mass divisibility may permit an aggregate offer or configuration-optimization method. The theorem’s structural conclusion gives that effort a clear target: compute the greatest stable allocation, not merely some stable one. The questions it opens include whether an aggregate cumulative-offer process reaches that allocation, whether its output has a finite type-level description, and whether outcomes of large finite replica markets converge to it with useful approximation bounds. Whether the paper’s weakly hospital-quasi-stable lattice also survives in this setting is a separate question; I would not use it as another computational anchor.

The weakest point is that the paper’s process relies on a finite set of contracts. With continuum populations there may be infinitely many individual-to-individual contracts, and a type-level flow alone may fail to capture agents’ portfolios and blocking opportunities. A workable mirror needs a faithful aggregate representation and a termination or optimization argument for it. The manuscript supplies neither, and its \(|X|\)-proposal bound is not itself a complexity result for the continuous problem. The case is therefore for a credible question grounded in Theorem 1—not for a claim that the paper has already established tractability or that the continuous extension is automatic.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the only plausible anchor, and its main result is a correctness and existence theorem: every cumulative-offer trajectory ends at the doctor-greatest stable allocation. It does not analyze the complexity of an encoded problem. The process makes at most \(|X|\) proposals, but the paper gives no representation or cost model for evaluating the choice functions. That proposal bound is not a complexity classification for the continuous problem. The lattice and rural-hospitals results are further structural results, not computational ones.

The strongest proposed mirror would use finitely many doctor and hospital types, rational masses, and finite contract forms, then ask for the doctor-greatest stable mass allocation. That is a plausible matching model. But the paper’s Blair order compares bundles for the same named doctors. Contract-mass totals alone do not record which integral portfolios doctors hold or how those portfolios are paired with hospitals. A faithful aggregate version therefore needs distributions over complete portfolios and a way to realize their contract flows. It also needs an order on those distributions. Defining these objects could produce a worthwhile configuration-optimization problem, but the configuration representation, its pricing problem, and the aggregate order are new ingredients; Theorem 1 supplies no algorithmic result about them.

So my case against is that this paper does not provide a computational result for ChoCo to continuize. The aggregate matching problem may be worth studying, but it is better described as a new continuous matching project motivated by the paper’s structural theorem than as a mirror of a complexity result in the paper.

That is the strongest negative I can make, and it has a limit: I cannot honestly rule out a worthwhile continuous project in every scenario. If “an algorithmic structural theorem gives a useful target” is enough to count as an anchor, this one may survive.
