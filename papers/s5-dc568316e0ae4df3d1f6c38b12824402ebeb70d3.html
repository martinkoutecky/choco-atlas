The continuous mirror question

Given a finite catalog \(G\), types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and nonnegative additive values \(v_t(g)\) normalized by \(\sum_{g\in G}q_gv_t(g)=1\), define \(M_t\) as the maximum \(z\) for which a finite-support distribution \(p_t\) over integral bundles \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}^{G}\) has \(\sum_b p_t(b)=1\), \(\sum_b p_t(b)b_g\le q_g\) for every \(g\), and \(v_t\cdot b\ge z\) for every bundle in its support. Find finite-support masses \(y_{t,b}\ge 0\) with \(\sum_b y_{t,b}=\mu_t\), \(\sum_{t,b}y_{t,b}b_g\le q_g\) for every \(g\), and \(y_{t,b}>0\) only when \(v_t\cdot b\ge \frac{2}{3}M_t\).

The model it lives in

A finite type set carries population mass; each type receives mass across integral bundle patterns, subject to per-capita resource limits. Type-level MMS is the best minimum bundle value achievable by a distribution over integral bundles with the same per-capita inventory.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the \(2/3\)-MMS guarantee and its ordered-instance route for arbitrary additive valuations. It leaves aside the paper's discussion of MMS nonexistence, earlier approximation algorithms, and stronger guarantees from other work.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can \(M_t\) and a feasible mass allocation be computed in time polynomial in \(|G|\), \(|T|\), and the input bit length?
  • Does the bundle-pattern optimization admit an efficient pricing or separation procedure?
  • Is the problem fixed-parameter tractable for fixed catalog size \(|G|\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s main result, Theorem 11, proved here. For an ordered instance with additive valuations, Algorithm 3 returns an allocation in which every agent \(i\) receives a bundle worth at least \(\frac23\mu_i\), where \(\mu_i\) is that agent’s maximin share. The paper’s full algorithm applies this guarantee to arbitrary additive instances by first converting to an ordered instance and then converting the allocation back. That route uses Proposition 6 and Theorem 7 from Barman and Murthy [2]; the paper cites those results and includes a proof of Theorem 7. I would use only Theorem 11 as my anchor. The supplied text has no comparably named hardness result to build a second mirror around.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large annual allocation of standardized, indivisible resources. Let the catalog of resource categories be fixed, and let the number of copies of each category grow with the population. A person’s type is their complete additive valuation vector over the catalog. There are \(n\) people but only \(\tau\) distinct types, with \(n\) much larger than \(\tau\); each type’s mass records its population share. Each person still receives an integral bundle. Mass becomes continuous because a large cohort of indistinguishable people can be divided among different bundle assignments. Per-capita supply must scale with population here: write \(q_g\) for the number of copies of category \(g\) per person.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Call it Mass-\(\frac23\)-MMS Allocation. The input is a finite catalog \(G\), a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\geq0\) with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\), rational per-capita supplies \(q_g\), and nonnegative rational additive values \(v_t(g)\). Normalize each type so that \(\sum_{g\in G}q_gv_t(g)=1\), as in the paper’s normalized instances. An individual bundle is still an integer vector \(b\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^{G}\), and its value to type \(t\) is \(v_t\cdot b\).

Define type \(t\)’s continuous maximin share \(M_t\) as the largest \(z\) for which there is a finite-support probability distribution \(p\) over integral bundles with \(\sum_b p_b b_g\leq q_g\) for each \(g\), and every bundle with \(p_b>0\) has \(v_t\cdot b\geq z\). This is the high-multiplicity version of partitioning the per-capita inventory into bundles and asking what value a type can guarantee as its least-valued bundle.

The requested solution is a finite-support set of rational masses \(y_{t,b}\geq0\). These must satisfy \(\sum_b y_{t,b}=\mu_t\) for every type, \(\sum_{t,b}y_{t,b}b_g\leq q_g\) for every category, and \(y_{t,b}>0\) only when \(v_t\cdot b\geq\frac23M_t\). Thus the task is to find a population allocation giving every person at least two-thirds of their type’s continuous MMS. The fractional quantities are masses of people assigned to bundles; they do not make any individual’s bundle fractional.

This is a direct high-multiplicity reading of the paper’s problem. At scale \(r\), take \(r\mu_t\) agents of type \(t\) and \(rq_g\) copies of category \(g\), whenever those counts are integral. A finite MMS partition induces a bundle-frequency distribution with probabilities in multiples of \(1/r\); the continuous benchmark frees those frequencies. For rational input, the benchmark can be represented by finite rational-support witnesses, so sufficiently large compatible scales recover it. Applying the paper’s guarantee at such a scale yields a mass allocation with the same \(\frac23\) guarantee. This is an existence bridge; it does not establish a running time polynomial in the bit length of \(\mu\) and \(q\).

For the unrestricted version, I would initially expect continuum-specific hardness, with tractability a plausible target when the catalog size is fixed or has additional structure. The mass-allocation formulation is an LP over bundle patterns, but the patterns must remain integral. Pricing can therefore involve finding a low-cost integer bundle meeting a value threshold; computing the continuous MMS benchmark has a related bundle-selection obstacle. The paper’s polynomial algorithm for explicitly listed agents and items does not settle whether these operations can be done efficiently on the compact mass input. Natural follow-up questions are whether the benchmark and allocation have polynomial-time algorithms in \(|G|\), \(\tau\), and input bit length; whether pricing admits an efficient separation oracle; and whether the result has an FPT algorithm for fixed \(|G|\).

The soft spot is the benchmark definition. MMS is originally phrased using a finite number of bundles, so the continuous version must specify how that partition behaves as population and inventory scale. The per-capita supply model and bundle-frequency limit make that choice concrete, and preserve indivisible bundles for each person, but the authors may still regard it as a meaningful new formulation rather than an already canonical extension. That seems like a real modeling question to resolve, not a reason to dismiss the mirror: it keeps their types, additive utilities, MMS benchmark, and two-thirds fairness target intact.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The one real concern is that Theorem 11 guarantees fairness to every named agent, while a continuous allocation can guarantee it only to positive mass. Those quantifiers need not survive the limit. With one type, one item valued at \(1\), and per-capita supply \(q=1\), the continuous MMS is \(1\). But \(n\) agents with \(n-1\) such items have discrete MMS \(0\), even though their per-capita supply \((n-1)/n\) tends to \(1\). A vanishing supply shortfall can therefore destroy the paper’s individual guarantee while disappearing from the population description. The mass mirror answers a meaningful but weaker question when rare exceptions matter: what can be guaranteed to the population represented by positive mass?

That limitation also applies to a stronger formulation than the proponent’s. Keeping bundles integral and requiring every positive-mass bundle assignment to meet the threshold does not protect a single agent whose mass tends to zero. One could track a minimum cohort size or explicit exceptions, but then the model must retain a finite-scale parameter to express what an ordinary continuum forgets.

Still, this does not defeat the proposed mirror. Its exact-scaling regime avoids the example: when type masses and per-capita supplies are rational, compatible finite instances can preserve them exactly. The paper’s guarantee then has a plausible bridge to the mass problem, and asking for an algorithm polynomial in the compact input is a real computational question. The bundle patterns remain integral, so the resulting optimization need not be trivial. Calling this “already done continuously” would also be wrong.

So the strongest case against is that the mirror may misrepresent a theorem whose defining promise is person-by-person fairness, especially when supply ratios fluctuate by sublinear amounts. That is a serious limit on its scope and interpretation, but it does not rule out a worthwhile mirror in a stable, exactly replicated resource regime. I cannot honestly make the universal negative case the prompt demands: Theorem 11 survives the stronger mirror.
