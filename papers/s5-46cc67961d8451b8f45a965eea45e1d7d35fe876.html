The continuous mirror question

For a campaign of \(N\) contributors with rational type masses \(\mu_t\), type \(t\) has maximum precision \(u_t\) and maximum-effort cost \(c_t(u_t)\). Given prior precision \(\xi_x\) and a target global precision \(\xi_x+N\delta\), choose recruited masses \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) to minimize \(N\sum_t q_t c_t(u_t)\), subject to \(N\sum_t q_t u_t\geq N\delta\); determine whether a peer-payment mechanism can support truthful reporting and maximum effort for recruited contributors in equilibrium.

The model it lives in

A society of \(N\) agents has type distribution \(\mu\), with each type specifying its measurement technology, maximum precision, effort cost, and peer-payment parameters. Each selected person still makes an integral maximum-effort choice; the decision variable \(q_t\) is the mass selected from type \(t\). A population-scaled precision requirement \(\xi_x+N\delta\) keeps the selected share positive, and the allocation objective is fractional bounded knapsack, solvable by ordering types by \(c_t(u_t)/u_t\); the peer-payment implementation requires a separate equilibrium proof.

The objection that survived

With a fixed aggregate precision increment, the recruited fraction vanishes as the population grows; the proponent’s stated formulation does not specify a population-scaled target, and peer-payment incentives still need a proof for an atomless population.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 2’s maximum-effort selection and, under convex costs, Proposition 1’s variable-effort allocation. It leaves the incentive proof for a continuum and the paper’s dynamic-estimation extension open.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can random peer matching over recruited type masses implement truthful reporting, maximum effort, and individual rationality in an atomless population?
  • What finite-campaign rounding guarantee relates the mass optimum to selecting individual contributors?

The case FOR (proponent)

A defensible mirror is the estimator’s effort and participation allocation. Consider a large crowdsensing campaign with \(N\) eligible contributors and a finite set \(T\) of sensor/task types. Type \(t\) comprises contributors with the same effort cost function \(c_t\), maximum precision \(u_t\), and measurement technology. Let \(\mu_t\) be that type’s fraction of the population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). A plausible regime is a city-scale campaign with many contributors using standardized devices and task protocols: \(N\) is large while the number \(\tau=|T|\) of distinct profiles is modest. The real measurement \(x\) is already continuous in the paper; the mirror continuizes the *population*.

My lead is Theorem 2, proved in the paper. It claims that when condition (9) does not hold, the estimator can select agents who then exert maximum effort and report truthfully in a Nash equilibrium, with expected payment equal to each selected agent’s maximum-effort cost. The selection is obtained by minimizing \(\sum_i \phi_i c_i(\xi_i^u)\), subject to \(\xi_x+\sum_i\phi_i\xi_i^u\geq\Sigma_t^{-1}\), where each \(\phi_i\) is a binary selection decision. The paper says the resulting knapsack problem is NP-hard and solvable in pseudo-polynomial time, citing [35]. To be precise, that complexity claim is prose accompanying the theorem’s formulation; no numbered result in this paper asserts a complexity class.

The continuous question is Mass Max-Effort Procurement. Given the rational type masses \(\mu_t\), the precision caps \(u_t\), maximum-effort costs \(c_t(u_t)\), prior precision \(\xi_x\), and target \(\Sigma_t\), choose \(q_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), the fraction of the whole population recruited from type \(t\). Every recruited person still gets an integral assignment—maximum effort—and everyone else gets zero effort. Minimize total expected compensation \(N\sum_t q_t c_t(u_t)\), subject to \(\xi_x+N\sum_t q_t u_t\geq\Sigma_t^{-1}\). A solution is the mass allocation \(q\), its minimum cost, and an incentive-compatible peer-payment rule for the recruited mass.

This is a fractional bounded knapsack problem. With rational inputs, it can be solved by ordering types by cost per unit of precision, \(c_t(u_t)/u_t\), and taking mass in that order, possibly recruiting only part of the last type. I expect Class A: the discrete subset-selection hardness can disappear when agents are interchangeable within type and the chosen quantity is mass. This is exactly the relevant co-modification: each contributor still supplies either zero or maximum effort, while a fraction of a type’s population can be recruited. Follow-up questions include the bit-complexity in \(\tau\) and input length, and how well the continuous optimum rounds to a finite campaign.

A second, separately useful anchor is Proposition 1, proved here and implemented by Theorem 1. If condition (9) holds, the estimator can induce its chosen effort levels and truthful reports in equilibrium, while paying each selected agent that agent’s effort cost. Its associated optimization is to minimize \(\sum_i\phi_i c_i(\xi_i)\) subject to \(\xi_x+\sum_i\phi_i\xi_i\geq\Sigma_t^{-1}\), with effort bounded by each agent’s cap. For convex costs, a type-symmetric continuous version asks for effort \(e_t\in[0,u_t]\) per member of each type, minimizing \(N\sum_t\mu_t c_t(e_t)\) subject to \(\xi_x+N\sum_t\mu_t e_t\geq\Sigma_t^{-1}\). Convexity makes equal effort within a type no worse than splitting that type across effort levels. With rational piecewise-linear convex costs, this is an LP, so I expect Class A for that stated regime. The solution includes the effort allocation and a peer-payment mechanism that supports truthful reporting and the target effort in equilibrium.

The authors could plausibly recognize both questions: they preserve the same sensing task, effort costs, strategic reports, global MSE target, and expected-compensation objective. The mass-selection question especially closely mirrors their maximum-effort fallback. The scope is limited to these estimator-side allocation results; it does not continuize time, outcomes, or the paper’s real-valued measurements.

The weak point is that the paper’s results are for finite strategic agents, so the peer-payment equilibrium needs a fresh proof for an atomless population. There is also a concrete issue in Theorem 2: as printed, equation (29) appears to give expected payment \(-c_i(\xi_i^u)\), not the claimed \(+c_i(\xi_i^u)\), given the payment rule in (27). The mass-selection optimization remains a valid mirror, but I would not claim its incentive implementation follows from that formula without correcting and rechecking it. A separate regime risk is type compression: if contributors have substantially different cost curves, \(\tau\) may grow nearly as fast as \(N\), leaving little computational gain.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against starts with a scaling problem. The paper’s named Theorems 1 and 2 establish incentive and payment guarantees; neither states a complexity bound. The knapsack complexity claims appear in the surrounding prose. So a continuous mirror would extend the optimization problems attached to those results, rather than continuize a named complexity result.

For Theorem 2, that extension also depends on the accuracy regime. Let \(\Delta=\Sigma_t^{-1}-\xi_x\) be the required extra precision. With a fixed finite set of types and fixed maximum precisions, a fixed \(\Delta\) requires only a bounded number of maximum-effort participants as the eligible population \(N\) grows. Their share of the population tends to zero. A mass allocation then acts mainly as a fractional relaxation of selecting individual sensors; it does not describe a positive mass of contributors. The peer-payment guarantee also relies on comparisons with another recruited agent, which the zero-mass limit does not capture directly. The sign issue in equation (29), noted by the proponent, further weakens the theorem’s stated implementation, though it does not by itself invalidate the allocation question.

Proposition 1 has a related limit. With \(N\) identical agents, quadratic cost \(c(\xi)=\ell\xi^2\), and fixed \(\Delta\), the optimum spreads effort at \(\xi_i=\Delta/N\), giving total cost \(\ell\Delta^2/N\). As \(N\) grows, per-person effort and total compensation both vanish. The limiting mass problem loses the finite target that made the allocation interesting.

Those objections do not defeat the stronger mirrors. The authors could instead study campaigns whose required precision scales with population, \(\Delta_N=N\delta\). Then a positive share of the population is recruited or exerts nonzero effort, and the maximum-effort problem becomes a genuine fractional knapsack over type masses. The convex-cost problem remains a continuous resource-allocation problem. Both are plausible estimation objectives, and the paper’s crowdsensing setting makes populations with many contributors sharing a small number of cost and sensor profiles credible. The resulting questions would be computationally simple, but simplicity is not a reason to reject a well-posed mirror.

I therefore cannot make a convincing case that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The negative case is strongest for a literal infinite-population limit with fixed aggregate accuracy, where the recruited mass or per-person effort disappears. It weakens substantially once the accuracy target is scaled with population. Theorem 2’s mass-selection question, in particular, survives that better formulation.
