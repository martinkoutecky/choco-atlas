What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror uses Theorem 1’s separated-claims equilibrium and Pareto comparison; it leaves the three-person equilibrium tables, Theorem 2, and the concessionary-division correspondence outside the mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive case is narrow: the paper has no named NP-hardness, polynomial-time, or parameterized-complexity result. Its strongest usable anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It explicitly characterizes an equilibrium, so it supports an algorithmic mirror, though the paper itself does not state a complexity classification.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large pool of creditors divided into \(\tau\) classes: many creditors share each class’s marginal utility \(\alpha_i\), and a trustee negotiates for that class. Let class \(i\) have population mass \(\mu_i>0\), with \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and let \(1=\alpha_1<\cdots<\alpha_\tau\). The mass records how much of the population belongs to the class. With a unit of divisible asset per unit of population, a per-capita allocation \(x_i\) gives the class aggregate utility \(\mu_i\alpha_i x_i\); feasibility is \(\sum_i\mu_i x_i\le1\). Thus, writing \(v_i=\mu_i\alpha_i x_i\), the undistorted feasible utility set is \(\sum_i v_i/\alpha_i\le1\), the paper’s simple bargaining set. The mass also determines the resulting per-capita payoff, \(u_i/\mu_i\).

This yields a specific question: Cohort KS Pre-donation Equilibrium. Given binary-rational \((\mu_i,\alpha_i)_{i=1}^{\tau}\), with the ordering above, each class’s trustee chooses \(\lambda_i\in[0,1]\), where \(\lambda_1=0\) and class \(i\) commits to pass a fraction of its utility to class \(i-1\). Apply the paper’s pre-donation transformation to the class aggregate utility set, take its comprehensive closure, and apply the KS solution. The question is to compute a Nash equilibrium \((\lambda,u)\): the donation rates and resulting class aggregate payoffs, such that no trustee can increase its own class payoff by changing its rate alone. A solution is any such equilibrium and payoff vector.

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved here: if \(\alpha_i\ge2\alpha_{i-1}\) for every \(i\ge2\), then \(\lambda_i^*=\alpha_{i-1}/\alpha_i\), \(u_1^*=1\), and \(u_i^*=\alpha_i-\alpha_{i-1}\) for \(i\ge2\) form a pre-donation equilibrium. If all those inequalities are strict, its outcome strongly Pareto-improves on the undistorted KS outcome. In the cohort version, the same theorem gives an explicit equilibrium in that separated-claims regime. Computing it takes \(O(\tau)\) rational operations, with polynomial bit complexity in the input length; the population size itself does not enter. I would expect this restricted problem to be tractable. The anchor does not establish tractability for arbitrary claim ratios, uniqueness, or a way to compute all equilibria.

The mirror covers that equilibrium result and its Pareto comparison. It does not cover the paper’s full equilibrium analysis. Useful next questions would be how to compute an equilibrium for unrestricted \(\alpha\), whether mass-sensitive transfer rules change the result, and what happens when class members choose donations individually.

The soft spot is the strategic unit. This mirror gives each homogeneous class a trustee who chooses one class-wide contract. That is plausible for creditor classes represented in bargaining, and preserves the paper’s KS and pre-donation mechanism. But it changes the paper’s individual-agent Nash game. In a genuinely nonatomic population where each creditor acts alone, one creditor may have negligible influence on the bargaining set, so the equilibrium incentives could be quite different. Mass also affects per-capita incidence here without changing the theorem’s class-total equilibrium. A referee could therefore accept this as a mirror for representative creditor classes while rejecting it as the continuous limit of decentralized individual donations.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s anchor is Theorem 1, but it does not assert the complexity of a computational problem. It gives an equilibrium in a parameter regime and its payoff formula. In the proposed cohort model, computing that equilibrium means evaluating \(\lambda_i^*=\alpha_{i-1}/\alpha_i\) and \(u_i^*=\alpha_i-\alpha_{i-1}\) for each class. The claimed \(O(\tau)\) algorithm is just the theorem written as a procedure; the masses do no work. That is a valid continuous restatement, but it does not open a complexity question of the kind ChoCo is meant to chart.

The mass interpretation also changes the strategic unit. In the paper, each agent chooses a donation rate that affects a bargaining set over individual payoffs, and donates to the agent immediately below them in marginal utility. If many people share one \(\alpha_i\), there is no unique immediate predecessor within that type. The paper notes a possible tie extension, but that does not make a cohort choose one \(\lambda_i\): having a trustee choose for the whole cohort turns individual agents into collective players. In the proponent’s aggregate-payoff formulation, the masses cancel from feasibility, \(\sum_i v_i/\alpha_i\leq1\), leaving the same finite game over \(\tau\) classes.

The stronger candidate would use a distribution of marginal utilities and let each agent choose a donation policy. But as the types become dense, “the agent immediately below” has no direct counterpart; replacing it with a transfer rule over lower types requires specifying a new mechanism. And in a nonatomic population, one person’s choice has zero effect on the aggregate bargaining set and KS outcome. Positive donations then lose their strategic rationale at the individual level. Giving a positive-mass cohort control over a contract restores strategic influence, but returns to the representative-class model rather than giving a limit of the paper’s individual game.

So the creditor-class story is plausible as a separate model, and that is the negative case’s main weakness. But it does not rescue this anchor: with class trustees, Theorem 1 already supplies the answer, and the population masses are inert under the proposed normalization. The paper’s other results are also equilibrium characterizations and comparative statics, not complexity claims. I see no worthwhile *computational* continuous mirror here; the most credible extension would be a new continuum game whose strategic and transfer structure the paper does not specify.
