The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many alternatives at rational positions on a line and, for each of \(k\) districts, a finite-support rational probability measure \(\mu_d\) over voter locations, let \(q_d\) be its left \(\alpha\)-quantile. Each district selects the alternative preferred by a voter at \(q_d\), using the paper's tie-breaking, and the mechanism returns the rightmost district representative \(w\). Does every such society satisfy \(\max_d \int |z-w|\,d\mu_d(z) \le \max\{\frac{3-\alpha}{1-\alpha},\frac{2}{\alpha}-1\}\min_{x\in A}\max_d\int |z-x|\,d\mu_d(z))\? In particular, does the bound become 2+\sqrt{5}\) at \(\alpha=(3-\sqrt{5})/2\)?

The model it lives in

A type records its district and location, which determine its preferences and costs; its mass is its fraction of that district's population. The society is the list of district measures \((\mu_d)_{d\in D}\). The rule chooses each representative from the voter's type at the district's left \(\alpha\)-quantile, then returns the rightmost representative. The objective is \(C_\mu(x)=\max_d\int |z-x|\,d\mu_d(z))\); the computational task is to return the rule's winner with the stated uniform distortion guarantee.

The objection that survived

The mechanism only sorts support locations and accumulates masses, so the mirror adds little to ChoCo's complexity landscape; the proponent acknowledges this limitation, but it does not defeat the specified approximation question.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 3.2 for max-of-average cost. It leaves Theorem 2.1, average-of-max cost, other objectives, and general metric spaces aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can any ordinal distributed rule achieve a smaller worst-case distortion for this continuous-population model?
  • Does the earlier discrete lower bound transfer to district measures, making \(2+\sqrt{5}\) tight here?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved in this paper. It says that for max-of-average cost, the \(\alpha\)-Leftmost mechanism—each district chooses the favorite alternative of its \((\alpha n_d)\)-th leftmost agent, then the mechanism chooses the rightmost district representative—has distortion at most
\[ \max\left\{\frac{3-\alpha}{1-\alpha},\frac{2}{\alpha}-1\right\}. \]
Its Corollary 3.2, also proved here, sets \(\alpha=(3-\sqrt5)/2\) and gives an upper bound of \(2+\sqrt5\). These are algorithmic approximation guarantees, not complexity classifications: the paper gives no named NP-hardness, membership-in-P, or parameterized-complexity result. Its introduction says the bound matches a lower bound from earlier work, but that lower bound is not a numbered result in the text provided.

A direct mirror is Continuous Max-of-Average Quantile Voting on a Line. An instance has a finite set \(A\) of alternatives at rational positions on the line and \(k\) districts. For each district \(d\), give a finite-support probability measure \(\mu_d=\sum_j p_{dj}\delta_{z_{dj}}\) over rational voter positions, with positive rational masses summing to one. A type is a district and a position, including the ranking induced by distance and the paper’s consistent tie-breaking. The mass \(p_{dj}\) is that fraction of the district’s population. Equivalently, the overall society measure gives each district mass \(1/k\), with conditional distribution \(\mu_d\) inside it.

For an alternative \(x\), define its max-of-average cost by \(C_\mu(x)=\max_d\int |z-x|\,d\mu_d(z)\). Given \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), let \(q_d\) be the left \(\alpha\)-quantile of \(\mu_d\). The rule picks as \(y_d\) the alternative preferred by the type at \(q_d\), then returns the rightmost representative \(w\). It uses mass proportions to find quantiles, rankings to choose representatives, and the alternatives’ order to choose the winner; it need not see cardinal distances. The question for a prover is whether, for every such society, this rule satisfies
\[ C_\mu(w)\le \max\left\{\frac{3-\alpha}{1-\alpha},\frac{2}{\alpha}-1\right\} \min_{x\in A}C_\mu(x). \]
At \(\alpha=(3-\sqrt5)/2\), the target bound is \(2+\sqrt5\). A solution is the rule’s winning alternative together with a proof of the uniform guarantee. On an explicit finite support, the rule is computable by sorting types and accumulating their masses.

This is a credible high-multiplicity regime for the paper’s problem: for example, a large population in each of several electoral districts, where voters are represented by a modest number of shared location or preference types. Think of residents grouped into modeled census cells along a corridor, with a finite set of proposed service sites. Each cell contributes a large mass, while the number of district-by-location types stays small relative to the number of residents. The individuals in a type share the same position, ranking, and costs, so grouping them preserves every feature the model uses. The continuous object is the society; alternatives remain finite and winners remain indivisible.

The authors should recognize this as their problem’s continuous form. Their within-district average becomes an integral, the order statistic becomes a mass quantile, and the district maximum and rightmost-representative step stay intact. The line geometry and ordinal access are preserved. The proof’s counting arguments have natural mass counterparts: the portions to either side of a quantile replace counts of agents, while average distances become integrals. The mirror therefore looks Class A: an efficient rule with the same bound, supported by a direct measure-based version of the argument. The discrete profiles embed as rational atomic measures; conversely, rational mass vectors can be realized by finite populations after choosing a common denominator.

I would keep the scope to Theorem 3.1 and its parameter choice. Theorem 2.1 for average-of-max has a less direct mirror: its rule takes an order statistic across the finite set of district representatives, and continuizing voters within districts does not continuize that set. I would not stretch this case to the paper’s other objectives or to arbitrary metric spaces.

The main weakness is that this is a distortion result, not a complexity result, and the continuous proof may be a relatively direct extension. It may not open a new complexity landscape. Also, the high-multiplicity benefit depends on the scenario: if nearly every voter has a distinct modeled position, the support size grows with the population. A further question is whether the earlier lower bound still applies when mechanisms see normalized masses rather than absolute district sizes. If it does, the continuous guarantee is tight too; if not, the best continuous distortion may differ. Other useful questions are whether the bound holds for general probability measures beyond explicit finite support, and whether any ordinal distributed rule can improve on \(2+\sqrt5\) for this continuous society model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is about fit with ChoCo’s stated computational gap, not about whether Theorem 3.1 has a coherent continuous mirror. It does. Give each district a rational finite-support distribution over voter positions, use its mass quantile in place of the \((\alpha n_d)\)-th voter, and replace the within-district average by an integral. A district-by-position type preserves the information the model uses. That is a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and the existing work on continuous alternatives does not already answer it: that continuizes outcomes, not the population.

What weakens the case for spending ChoCo’s effort here is that the theorem studies distortion of a specified voting rule, not the complexity of a computational problem. With support size \(\tau\), the mirrored rule just sorts positions within each district, accumulates masses to find quantiles, and selects among the representatives. There is no hard search problem, exponentially large formulation, or pricing question for continuous optimization to resolve. The interesting question is whether the distortion guarantee extends to a measure-valued society; that is primarily a mechanism-analysis question, and it may leave the programme’s complexity landscape untouched.

A stronger proposal would optimize over all ordinal distributed mechanisms for the continuous-population model, rather than mirror this one bound. But that changes the task into a minimax mechanism-design question. It would need a specified mechanism class and information model before it became a well-defined computational problem; in its natural form, it is still about distortion guarantees, not the complexity of computing an intervention or outcome.

That is the best negative case I can make, and it does not defeat the mirror’s well-posedness. If ChoCo includes algorithmic mechanism analysis as part of its remit, Theorem 3.1 remains a viable anchor and my case is weak. I would not claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario.
