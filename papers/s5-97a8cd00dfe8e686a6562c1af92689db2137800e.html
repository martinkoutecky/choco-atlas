The continuous mirror question

Given finite types \(T\) with masses \(\mu_t>0\), item types \(J\) with per-capita supplies \(\sigma_j\), utilities \(u_{tj}\), rankings, and \(\varepsilon>0\), compute a mass allocation \(X\) satisfying \(\sum_j X_{tj}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t X_{tj}\le\sigma_j\). For \(v_t(X)=\sum_j u_{tj}X_{tj}/\mu_t\), require \(v_t(X)\ge\sum_j u_{tj}X_{t'j}/\mu_{t'}\) for every pair of positive-mass types \(t,t'\), and require that no feasible \(Y\) satisfies \(v_t(Y)\ge(\mathrm e^{1/\mathrm e}-\varepsilon)v_t(X)\) for every \(t\), with strict inequality for some type.

The model it lives in

A high-multiplicity random-assignment market with type masses \(\mu\), per-capita item supplies \(\sigma\), mass variables \(X_{tj}\), type-level linear utilities \(v_t\), and the convex objective \(\sum_t\mu_t\log v_t(X)\), together with envy-freeness and supply constraints.

The objection that survived

The proposed problems may add little algorithmic novelty because denominator clearing and symmetrisation reproduce them as finite repeated-agent assignment instances; the proponent acknowledges this but cannot eliminate that limitation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the PS maximum-Nash-welfare and approximate Pareto-efficiency results, together with the envy-free approximate-efficiency algorithm for random and submodular assignment. It leaves the general Fisher-market result, the chores result, and auxiliary existence statements untreated.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the normalized PS analysis yield a tight capacity-sensitive guarantee depending on the supply profile \(\sigma\), rather than on the number of named agents?
  • What is the complexity of exact envy-free Pareto efficiency when both agents and item supplies have finitely many repeated types?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a high-multiplicity course- or seat-allocation mirror. Imagine millions of students, but only finitely many complete preference/cardinal-utility types: the same programme choices, priorities, and valuations. Let \(T\) be these types, with mass distribution \(\mu\), \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). Let \(J\) be course or seat types, with per-capita supplies \(\sigma_j\), \(\sum_j\sigma_j=1\). A finite realization with \(N\mu_t\) students of type \(t\) and \(N\sigma_j\) copies of item type \(j\) is exactly the corresponding high-multiplicity instance.

The continuous allocation variable is \(X_{tj}\), the mass of type \(t\) receiving item \(j\). Thus \(\sum_jX_{tj}\le\mu_t\) and \(\sum_tX_{tj}\le\sigma_j\). A member of type \(t\) receives the per-capita lottery bundle \(x_{tj}=X_{tj}/\mu_t\), with utility \(v_t(X)=\sum_j u_{tj}x_{tj}\). The lottery remains part of the paper’s original random-assignment model; what is continuous is the population mass and the supply scaled with it.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1.1, proved in this paper. It states that, for cardinal utilities, the Probabilistic Serial mechanism approximates maximum Nash welfare within a factor \(\ln(n)+1\), and consequently is \((\ln(n)+1)\)-approximately Pareto efficient. The paper proves the tighter Theorem 2.6: the factor is \(H_n\), where \(\ln n\le H_n\le\ln n+1\).

The corresponding problem is Continuous PS–Nash Welfare. Given rational \((T,\mu,J,\sigma,u,\succ)\), run simultaneous eating on type masses: each type consumes its most-preferred currently available item at unit per-capita speed, and an item disappears when its supply \(\sigma_j\) is exhausted. Let \(X^{\mathrm{PS}}\) be the resulting mass allocation. The problem is to compute \(X^{\mathrm{PS}}\), compute the maximum weighted Nash welfare \(\operatorname{WNW}^*=\max_Y\exp(\sum_t\mu_t\log v_t(Y))\), and determine the approximation ratio \(\operatorname{WNW}^*/\operatorname{WNW}(X^{\mathrm{PS}})\), to prescribed accuracy.

I expect this problem to be Class A. The PS trajectory has only finitely many events, one when a currently consumed item is exhausted. The benchmark is a convex programme over the transportation polytope. More importantly, the paper’s \(n\) should not be naively replaced by the number of types \(\tau\). In the continuum, the natural parameter is item granularity: if \(Q_k\) is cumulative supply through the \(k\)-th exhausted item, the proof yields a harmonic/logarithmic bound in the \(Q_k\)’s, typically \(1+\ln(1/\sigma_{\min})\). Equal supplies \(\sigma_j=1/m\) recover an \(H_m\)-type bound, while millions of students with fixed \(m\) recurring seat types need not cause the guarantee to grow. This is a genuine population-continuum question, not merely a restatement of fractional assignment.

The second anchor is Theorem 1.3, proved in this paper and formalized as Theorem 3.4. It says that, for random assignment and the more general submodular assignment model, there is a polynomial-time algorithm which, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), finds an envy-free allocation that is \((\mathrm e^{1/\mathrm e}-\varepsilon)\)-approximately Pareto efficient. The formal theorem shows that an optimum of the envy-free Nash-welfare programme is \(\mathrm e^{1/\mathrm e}\)-approximately maximum-Nash-welfare and hence \(\mathrm e^{1/\mathrm e}\)-approximately Pareto efficient; the approximation can be computed with polynomially many arithmetic operations and value-oracle queries.

Its mirror is Continuous Envy-Free Assignment. The instance is the same type-mass and per-capita-supply data. A feasible \(X\) must satisfy the mass and supply constraints above. It is envy-free when, for every positive-mass types \(t,t'\), \(v_t(X)\ge\sum_j u_{tj}X_{t'j}/\mu_{t'}\). The task is to output such an \(X\) for which no feasible \(Y\) gives every type at least \((\mathrm e^{1/\mathrm e}-\varepsilon)\) times its utility under \(X\), with a strict improvement for some type.

This is also Class A in the stated finite-type regime. The direct programme is \(\max_X\sum_t\mu_t\log v_t(X)\), subject to the linear feasibility and envy-freeness constraints. With ordinary item supplies it is a finite convex-optimization problem; with the paper’s submodular supplies it uses exactly the separation-oracle machinery the authors invoke. The type formulation is faithful because agents of one type are indistinguishable in every parameter used by the problem, so a symmetric allocation loses no relevant information.

The authors should recognise both mirrors immediately: they preserve the paper’s cardinal utilities, ordinal rankings, simultaneous eating rule, matching constraint, Nash-welfare objective, and Pareto comparison. The only substantive change is replacing repeated named agents and item copies by their masses and per-capita supplies. A rational continuous instance also rounds back exactly to a finite repeated instance after choosing a common denominator.

The case is limited in two ways. First, this paper contains no named NP-hardness or PPAD-hardness theorem of its own; the PPAD-hardness discussed in the introduction is cited from elsewhere. Thus these anchors support a tractable continuous-population formulation, not a claim that discrete hardness dissolves here. Second, the paper already gives polynomial-time algorithms for its approximate finite-agent problems, so the mirror does not by itself reveal a new complexity collapse. Its value is instead the distributional formulation, the removal of irrelevant population granularity, and the new questions it opens: whether the capacity-sensitive PS bound is tight, whether exact envy-free Pareto efficiency remains PPAD-hard with finitely many repeated types, and which submodular supply structures preserve the Class A picture.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both proposed anchors are computationally too routine to justify a ChoCo mirror.

For Theorem 1.1, the type-mass formulation is a valid high-multiplicity quotient, but it introduces no new algorithmic bottleneck. With \(X_{tj}\) denoting assigned mass, Probabilistic Serial has at most \(m\) exhaustion events, and the benchmark is the standard convex programme
\[ \max_X \sum_t \mu_t \log\!\left(\sum_j u_{tj}\frac{X_{tj}}{\mu_t}\right) \]
over a transportation polytope. For rational masses and supplies, clearing denominators produces exactly a finite instance with repeated agents and repeated item copies. Conversely, symmetrising identical agents cannot reduce Nash welfare, by concavity of \(\log\). Thus this is not a new population-driven computational problem: it is the same fractional assignment problem with repeated rows and rescaled capacities. The proposed \(H_m\)- or \(\log(1/\sigma_{\min})\)-bound is a useful reformulation, but merely re-running the paper’s proof after normalization.

Theorem 1.3 is even more vulnerable. Its proposed continuous programme,
\[ \max_X \sum_t\mu_t\log v_t(X) \]
subject to type-level envy-freeness and feasibility, is precisely the finite theorem after replacing \(n_t\) identical agents by mass \(\mu_t\). The same convexity and separation-oracle argument applies without any population-specific phenomenon. A stronger formulation requiring individual-level envy-freeness does not rescue the objection: allocations among identical agents can be averaged within each type, preserving feasibility and envy-freeness while weakly improving Nash welfare.

One could instead ask whether exact envy-free Pareto-efficient allocation remains PPAD-hard under repeated types. That would be a genuinely interesting high-multiplicity question, but it is no longer an anchor supplied by this paper: the PPAD-hardness is cited from other work, and the paper proves only an approximation algorithm. Nor can one rule out such a mirror merely because the approximate version is already polynomial.

So the negative case ultimately rests on research value rather than validity: the proposed mirrors are sensible, recognisable, and computationally well-defined, but largely exact type-compressions of an already fractional assignment model. That is a real reason to regard them as modest rather than central. It is not, however, a fundamental obstruction. Under ChoCo’s stated standard, I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists.
