What the mirror covers

No result is mirrored. The targeting and seeding comparative statics and the cutoff characterization remain in the paper’s existing unit-mass population model.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best positive foothold is concrete: a large consumer cohort can be described by degree types \(k\), with mass \(P(k)\), and a marketer can choose the fraction \(x_k\) of each type to target. In the paper’s targeted word-of-mouth model, \(\theta(x)=\sum_k P(k)x_k\), and the marketer maximizes
\[ \sum_k P(k)\bigl[1-(1-x_k)(1-\theta(x))^k\bigr]-\frac{\alpha}{2}\theta(x)^2. \]
That is a plausible many-agent, few-types scenario. It also exposes a useful computational question: given rational \(P(k)\) and \(\alpha\), find a profit-maximizing targeting vector \(x\).

But the paper has no named computational-complexity result to anchor that question. Its numbered propositions give economic comparative statics. For example, Corollary 5.1, proved in the paper, says optimal targeted advertising is a decreasing cutoff strategy in degree, while optimal seeding is an increasing cutoff strategy. That is useful structure, but it does not assert that an optimization problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise classify its complexity. So there are no qualifying anchors, and no compliant per-anchor mirror or A/B/C prediction to report.

There is a further limit: the paper already models a unit-measure population and a distribution over degree types. Presenting that model itself as a continuous-population mirror would restate the paper’s starting point. Adding voters and a winner condition could create a campaigning problem, but that would add the social-choice problem the paper does not study. The honest positive case is therefore that its model suggests a natural optimization problem for a computational follow-up; it does not supply an anchored continuous mirror for ChoCo.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative is that this paper offers no named computational result to mirror. Its numbered propositions and Corollary 5.1 concern economic comparative statics and the shape of optimal targeting. None classifies the complexity of a computational social choice problem. The appendix lemma does not do so either.

The proposed word-of-mouth optimization is the closest candidate, but the paper already formulates it over a unit-measure population, with degree types of mass \(P(k)\) and fractional targeting \(x_k\). It also studies the resulting optimization and proves a cutoff structure. Asking how efficiently to compute an optimum would be a computational follow-up to an already continuous economic model, rather than a population continuization of one of this paper’s computational results. The cutoff theorem is a plausible anchor for further analysis, but it does not supply the required computational result.

A campaigning version could add rankings and require the target candidate to win. That may define a sensible research problem: types could combine preferences and degree, and the campaign could exploit diffusion. But the winner condition and preference aggregation supply the central social-choice problem; they are additions to the paper’s model, not a continuous mirror of a result it proves. The paper discusses firms, governments, and political parties as potential users of influence strategies, but it never models ballots, winners, or campaign complexity.

So the negative case is strong against a *mirror of this paper*: its population is already continuous, and its results are not computational classifications. It cannot establish that no worthwhile ChoCo project could ever build on the framework. A new election-diffusion model might be valuable, but that would need to stand on its own rather than count as a mirror anchored in this paper.
