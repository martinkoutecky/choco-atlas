What the mirror covers

The proposed bot-follower allocation problem is a possible new study; it does not mirror a named computational result in the paper. The paper's convergence propositions and simulation and regression findings remain without an anchored computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best case for a continuous mirror is that the paper’s setting has a natural population scale: in a large platform cohort, “15% are bot followers” may be more meaningful than a count of 600 users. A plausible type would have to capture everything relevant to updating: initial beliefs, user or follower status, and a role in the network’s interaction pattern. Mass would be the fraction of users of each type.

One possible follow-up would represent interaction by a type-to-type mixing rule and ask how to allocate a limited amount of bot exposure across types to maximize long-run misinformation or polarization. That keeps the paper’s subject—how bot exposure affects aggregate opinions—but it is a new optimization problem. The paper studies randomly assigned bot followers and reports simulation results; it does not formulate that optimization problem.

Propositions 1 and 2, proved in Appendices E.1 and E.2, are analytical convergence results: with a strongly connected network and no bot influence, society becomes wise; Proposition 2 allows links to activate stochastically with \(\rho\in(0,1)\). Both conclude that misinformation and polarization converge in probability to zero. Neither asserts a computational complexity result for an underlying problem. The simulations and regressions are not numbered complexity results either, so there is no anchor here for which to state a continuous problem and its expected complexity class.

The weakest point in the positive case is the network. Individual topology and centrality drive the paper’s findings, and grouping users by a finite set of interaction types may lose exactly those effects. A type-based continuous model is a plausible research direction, but this paper supplies no qualifying computational result to anchor it.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper has no named computational result to mirror. Propositions 1 and 2 are convergence results: without bot influence, the society learns the true state and misinformation and polarization vanish. The paper’s results about bot centrality, flooding, and threshold rules come from simulations and regressions on a fixed synthetic network. It poses no algorithmic problem and gives no complexity, approximation, or parameterized result. The proponents name Propositions 1 and 2, but explicitly acknowledge that neither supplies the required computational anchor.

A stronger follow-up than the one they suggest would use a population divided into network roles, with a fixed interaction pattern between roles, and ask how to allocate a fixed mass of bot followers to maximize long-run misinformation or polarization. That could be a sensible platform policy problem. But it adds a planner, a decision variable, and an optimization objective absent from the paper. It is a new computational study, not a continuous mirror of one of this paper’s results.

There is also a gap between that role-based model and the paper’s network. Its outcomes depend on where followers sit in a sparse graph and on how influence travels along particular paths. A finite type-to-type mixing rule would impose a block structure that the paper does not assume. A more faithful continuum might track a distribution of network neighborhoods and belief states, but then the computational question would need to be formulated from scratch. The paper gives no finite computational problem whose continuous version such a model would answer.

So the negative case is strong for this paper: the proposed mirror lacks a computational result to anchor it, and the plausible optimization version changes the research question. The broader claim that no useful continuous study could arise in any scenario is weaker. A continuous network intervention problem might be worthwhile as a separate project, but the paper itself does not establish it as a mirror.
