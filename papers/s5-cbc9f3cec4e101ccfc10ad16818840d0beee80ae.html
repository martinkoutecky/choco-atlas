The case FOR (proponent)

I find no qualifying anchor in this paper. It has no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational complexity result. Equations (4)–(17) define rationing rules, and Tables 2–7 calculate examples; neither is a named complexity result. The cited result that random priority corresponds to the Shapley value and the Talmud method to the nucleolus is attributed to earlier work, and is not a computational result of this paper. So there is no honest anchor to restate, or anchor-indexed prover question to supply.

The strongest positive case is an unanchored extension of the paper’s underlying rationing problem. Imagine a large water or energy quota scheme with \(n\) households or businesses, where the rules assign the same claim to everyone in each of a small number \(\tau\) of permit classes—for example, \(n=100{,}000\) claimants and \(\tau=20\) classes. A type is a claimant’s demand \(d\); mass \(\mu_d\) is the fraction of claimants in that class. In the paper’s rationing model, demand is the only feature used by the rules, so claim size suffices as a type. If the application uses other relevant features, they belong in the type too.

One precise continuous problem would be Continuous Claim Rationing. Its input is rational masses \(\mu_d>0\) over finitely many rational claim sizes \(d\), with \(\sum_d\mu_d=1\); a rational per-capita resource supply \(q\), where \(0\le q\le\bar d=\sum_d\mu_d d\); and a selected rule from the paper’s proportional, uniform-gains, uniform-losses, or Talmud methods. Its output is the per-capita allocation \(a_d\) for every type, satisfying \(0\le a_d\le d\) and \(\sum_d\mu_d a_d=q\), as prescribed by that rule. For example, proportional allocation gives \(a_d=qd/\bar d\); uniform gains gives \(a_d=\min\{\lambda,d\}\), with \(\sum_d\mu_d\min\{\lambda,d\}=q\). This is a computation problem, but the paper supplies no objective to optimize: the chosen rationing rule determines the allocation.

This mirror would cover the deterministic rationing model and rules in equations (1)–(17), not the paper’s coalition formation and stochastic cooperative-game analysis. I expect the stated computation to be tractable: sort the claim sizes and find the relevant threshold, using rational arithmetic. That is a plausible extension of the paper’s generic rationing model, which is presented as applicable to resources beyond Arctic claims. It is also clearly a population continuization: masses of claimants replace a finite list, while the paper’s resource was already divisible.

The weakest point is substantial: the paper’s concrete Arctic example has eight sovereign claimants, not a high-multiplicity population, and it proves no complexity result that this mirror could clarify. The large permit-class scenario is plausible for the paper’s general rationing framework, but it is not the paper’s motivating country-allocation instance. Its cooperative-game results also do not automatically carry over when the player set becomes a continuum. So I see a credible continuous form of the rationing model, but no anchored case that this paper demonstrates a computational landscape—or that continuization changes one.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

There is no computational result in this paper to anchor a mirror to. Its equations define rationing rules, and its tables apply them to an eight-country example. The Shapley-value and nucleolus connection is attributed to earlier work. The paper proves no complexity, algorithmic, or approximation result. So the proponent is right that there is no anchor to restate; under the requested anchor test, none survives.

The strongest extension is the proposed high-multiplicity rationing of households or permit holders grouped by claim size. That is a coherent population continuization, and the paper’s general rationing model gives it a natural basis. But it takes an explicit rule \(r(I,t,y)\) and asks for its evaluation on a distribution of claims. It does not mirror a computational question the paper formulated or a result it obtained. Turning its formulas into an input-output problem would add a computational wrapper, not reveal a computational contribution of this paper.

The stochastic cooperative-game discussion is a better candidate for a new research question, but the paper does not specify one computational task there. A mirror would have to choose what to compute—an expected allocation, a game solution, or something about coalition formation—and how to represent the random claims. With the Arctic application, the players are a fixed set of countries whose territorial and political circumstances distinguish them; treating them as interchangeable mass types loses the setting’s structure. A large population of firms or quota holders might support a separate high-multiplicity game, but that would be a new model and problem, not a mirror of a result here.

The honest limit of this case is that the household-rationing extension is mathematically sensible, and the authors might recognize it as an extension of their framework. If any plausible continuous question were enough, that example would weaken the negative verdict. But it supplies no paper-specific computational result to examine, and the more ambitious game-theoretic directions require inventing the computational question. I would argue against spending the programme’s effort on this paper as a continuous-complexity mirror.
