What the mirror covers

The case covers only the MNW incentive-welfare trade-off and the connected-piece envy-free lower bound; it leaves the constructive MNW and PA mechanisms, randomized truthfulness, interpolation results, and two-agent mechanism unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

Strictly under the ChoCo anchor rule, this paper has no eligible computational-complexity anchor: no numbered theorem says NP-hard, polynomial-time, W[1]-hard, FPT, and so on. Its results are mechanism-performance guarantees and impossibility bounds. The strongest honest positive case is therefore a near-case, anchored to those named lower bounds rather than pretending they are complexity classifications.

My lead is Theorem 5.4, proved in this paper (Appendix B). It states that, for every \(c\in[0,1]\), no deterministic mechanism for homogeneous divisible items can simultaneously have incentive ratio at most \(2^{1-c}\) and guarantee an MNW approximation ratio strictly greater than \(2^{-c}\). The proof actually rules out a slightly stronger finite-\(n\) guarantee, approaching this bound asymptotically.

A natural high-multiplicity mirror is:

\[ \textsc{HM-MNW-Incentive-Tradeoff}. \]

There are \(m\) divisible items, each with unit capacity, and a finite set of complete valuation types \(\Theta=\{\theta^1,\ldots,\theta^\tau\}\), where \(\theta^t\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge0}^m\). The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\in\Delta(\Theta)\); \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of agents of type \(t\). This represents, for example, millions of households or institutions with one of a small number of standardized priority vectors, with \(\tau\ll n\).

A type-level allocation is \(x=(x_{tj})\), where \(x_{tj}\) is the per-capita fraction of item \(j\) assigned to type \(t\), subject to

\[ \sum_t \mu_t x_{tj}\le 1 \]

for every item \(j\). Type \(t\)'s utility is

\[ u_t(x)=\sum_j \theta^t_jx_{tj}, \]

and social Nash welfare is

\[ \operatorname{NW}_\mu(x) =\exp\!\left(\sum_t\mu_t\log u_t(x)\right). \]

A deterministic continuum mechanism supplies a menu \(x_t\) indexed by reported type. Its price-taking incentive ratio is

\[ \max_{t,r} \frac{\theta^t\cdot x_r}{\theta^t\cdot x_t}. \]

The problem is to compute the largest achievable ratio

\[ \frac{\operatorname{NW}_\mu(x)} {\operatorname{NW}^{\star}_\mu} \]

among feasible menus whose incentive ratio is at most \(2^{1-c}\), where \(\operatorname{NW}^{\star}_\mu\) is unconstrained optimal MNW. Equivalently, decide whether a feasible menu with incentive ratio \(2^{1-c}\) achieves MNW approximation greater than \(2^{-c}\), and output the menu or a certificate that none exists.

This is recognisably the authors’ problem: the same homogeneous divisible resources, MNW objective, deterministic mechanism, strategic reports, incentive ratio, and welfare trade-off remain. Only the society changes from a list of agents to a distribution over complete valuation types. Divisibility of the resources is not being passed off as the novelty; it is the paper’s existing setting, while population continuization is the new operation.

For an explicitly listed \(\Theta\), I would expect this per-profile mirror to be Class A. The incentive constraints are linear, feasibility is linear, and maximizing weighted logarithmic welfare is a convex-optimization problem. The interesting question is whether the paper’s impossibility trade-off survives a stronger, global version in which one mechanism must work across all distributions \(\mu\). The finite proof relies on one agent’s report changing the relevant instance; in an atomless population, one individual has zero effect on \(\mu\). That makes the transfer nontrivial rather than vacuous.

The second useful anchor is Theorem 6.2, also proved here (Appendix C). It states that the incentive ratio of every envy-free cake-cutting mechanism subject to the connected-pieces constraint is \(\Theta(n)\). The upper bound is \(n\), while the paper constructs instances with \(n=8k-3\) agents in which some agent has truthful value at most about \(1\) but can misreport to obtain value approaching \(k=\Theta(n)\). The result remains true with free disposal.

Its population mirror would be:

\[ \textsc{HM-EF-Connected-Incentive}. \]

The type set consists of piecewise-constant valuation densities \(f^1,\ldots,f^\tau\) on \([0,1]\), with society distribution \(\mu\). A type-level allocation is a measurable share function \(a_t(x)\in[0,1]\), with

\[ \sum_t\mu_t a_t(x)\le1 \]

for every \(x\). Each \(a_t\) must have connected support, and type \(t\)'s per-capita utility is

\[ u_t(a)=\int_0^1 f^t(x)a_t(x)\,dx. \]

To preserve the paper’s strategic content, a deviation is made by a positive mass \(\delta\) of type \(t\), which reports type \(r\). The distribution becomes

\[ \mu'=\mu-\delta e_t+\delta e_r. \]

Given a deterministic rule \(M\) assigning an allocation to each distribution, the objective is to minimize

\[ \sup_{t,r,\delta} \frac{\int_0^1 f^t(x)a^{\mu'}_r(x)\,dx} {\int_0^1 f^t(x)a^\mu_t(x)\,dx} \]

subject to envy-freeness, feasibility, and connected support at every reachable distribution. A finite input version supplies a finite set of rational distributions and allowed deviations, making this a concrete decision or optimization problem.

Here I would expect the answer to depend sharply on the regime. If \(\tau\) is fixed and each type is repeated many times, the paper’s \(\Theta(n)\) construction may dissolve: its lower-bound instance appears to rely heavily on many distinct valuation patterns. If \(\tau\) grows with \(n\), the construction may survive. With fixed \(\tau\), connected-support constraints could still produce Class-C, continuum-specific difficulty because the cut order and interval geometry remain nonconvex.

The weakest point is strategic. Literal unilateral manipulation degenerates in an atomless population: one agent cannot change the aggregate distribution. The menu formulation or positive-mass-cohort deviation is therefore a necessary co-modification, not a theorem already contained in the paper. An author might reasonably say that this is a new continuum mechanism-design problem rather than their exact problem. The case survives only because the paper’s central objects—valuation types, MNW, envy-freeness, connected allocations, and incentive guarantees—remain intact, while the proposed regime is genuinely plausible: large populations with many repeated, fully specified preference types and \(\tau\ll n\).

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I would reject both anchors. The decisive point is that this paper has no eligible computational-complexity result. Theorem 5.4 is a mechanism-design impossibility theorem, and Theorem 6.2 is an incentive-ratio lower bound. Neither defines a computational input, output, decision problem, or algorithmic complexity class. Turning either theorem into an optimization problem would therefore be inventing a new problem inspired by the paper, not continuizing one of its computational results.

Theorem 5.4 also does not survive the high-multiplicity transfer in the way proposed. Its proof relies on a particular individual’s report changing the entire profile: in Appendix B, agent \(i\) can report differently and turn the \(n+1\)-st instance into instance \(i\). Formally, the theorem has the shape

\[ \not\exists M\;\forall v: \bigl(\operatorname{IR}(M,v)\le 2^{1-c} \ \wedge\ \operatorname{NW}(M(v))>2^{-c}\operatorname{NW}^{\star}(v)\bigr). \]

The proposed per-distribution problem instead chooses a menu separately for each \(\mu\), effectively changing the quantifiers to something like

\[ \forall \mu\;\exists x^\mu. \]

That is not the same mechanism-design problem. The cross-profile coupling used by the proof has disappeared.

The menu formulation is the best possible repair, but it confirms the mismatch. A single agent of type \(t\) has zero mass, so reporting \(r\) cannot change \(\mu\); it merely selects the already-existing menu entry \(x_r\). The resulting constraints,

\[ \theta^t\cdot x_r\le R\,\theta^t\cdot x_t, \]

are a price-taking screening problem, not the finite mechanism in Theorem 5.4, whose report changes everyone’s allocation. For explicitly listed types, the proposed welfare maximization is a routine finite convex program. It is potentially a legitimate new problem, but it is not a high-multiplicity relaxation of the theorem’s argument.

The alternative—allowing a positive mass \(\delta\) to deviate—restores aggregate effects only by replacing unilateral manipulation with coalition or cohort manipulation:

\[ \mu\longmapsto \mu-\delta e_t+\delta e_r. \]

That requires a new strategic model: who coordinates the cohort, how \(\delta\) is chosen, and whether utility is evaluated per person or per cohort. It may be interesting, but none of it is in the paper, and the finite proof does not transfer merely by replacing counts with masses. If one repeats every type \(q\) times, one individual changes only a \(1/q\)-fraction of that type, so the key profile-switching operation vanishes. If instead each distinct role has positive mass, the deviation is no longer unilateral.

Theorem 6.2 has an even more direct obstruction. Its lower-bound construction uses \(n=8k-3\) agents with location-specific valuation supports. The agents \(c_i\) act as separate boundary markers, while the \(a_{ij}\) and \(b_{ij}\) agents have distinct shifted patterns. The connected-piece argument needs each of these named agents to impose an independent geometric constraint.

With finitely many repeated types, those constraints collapse: copies of the same type are interchangeable, and the number of independent barriers is governed by the number of distinct types and breakpoints, not by the population size. Repeating every type \(q\) times makes the population arbitrarily large without creating \(q\) new cuts or barriers. To preserve the construction, one must let the type space grow with \(k\), so \(\tau=\Theta(k)\), eliminating the high-multiplicity gain.

A continuum of location-indexed valuation types does not solve this cleanly. The paper’s envy-freeness and connectedness are individual properties. A marginal function \(a_t(x)\) records the fraction of type \(t\) receiving \(x\), but it does not record whether the same individual receives all points in one connected interval. Connectedness is a correlation constraint across \(x\), which requires a distribution over intervals or an explicit individual-level allocation. Likewise, envy-freeness in a continuum needs an essential-supremum or cohort interpretation; the paper’s pairwise comparison of named bundles is no longer defined. Those are not cosmetic formalities: they change the object whose incentive ratio is being measured.

The honest caveat is that a separate paper on price-taking menus or positive-mass strategic cohorts could be worthwhile. But it would be a new continuous mechanism-design problem motivated by this paper, not a continuous mirror of either named result. Under ChoCo’s computational anchor rule, the negative case is therefore strong: there is no computational result here to continuize, and the two proposed repairs either erase the paper’s strategic mechanism or abandon high multiplicity in order to preserve its geometric lower bound.
