What the mirror covers

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous-population mirror here, but the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor under your rule. None of its numbered results says that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity.

The closest results are Proposition 8 and Lemma 9, both proved in the paper. Proposition 8 says that when the minimum-weight winning coalitions \(W^*(q,w)\) are weakly balanced, and proposer probabilities are proportional to voting weights—\(\theta_i=w_i\) and \(\theta_i^S=w_i/\sum_{j\in S}w_j\)—there is an SSPE in which only coalitions in \(W^*\) are proposed, ex ante payoffs equal \(w_i\), and conditional expected payoffs are \(w_i/q^*\). Responders’ actual payoffs are \([p/q^*+1-p]w_i\), with the proposer taking the residual. Lemma 9 characterizes that balancedness condition: \(W^*\) is weakly balanced exactly when \(w\) solves the paper’s LP minimizing maximum coalition excess. These are equilibrium and structural results, not complexity results. The LP characterization makes a computational question tempting, but the paper does not classify its complexity.

The strongest prospective mirror is a high-multiplicity bargaining limit. Take a large assembly with a fixed set \(T\) of delegate types. Type \(t\) has population fraction \(\mu_t\), and its delegates share the same voting weight, proposer probability, material preferences, and other features relevant to bargaining. Let \(a_t\) be their normalized per-person voting-weight density, with \(\sum_t\mu_ta_t=1\), and let \(q\) be the quota. A coalition is described by masses \(x_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) and wins when \(\sum_t a_tx_t\ge q\). Its budget division is a type-level payoff density \(u_t\), with \(\sum_t x_tu_t=1\).

To preserve the paper’s sequential bargaining game precisely, define this as a limit of finite replicas: for \(k d\) delegates, where \(d\) clears the denominators of \(\mu\), each type has \(kd\mu_t\) members and each member of type \(t\) has voting weight \(a_t/(kd)\). Keep the quota and breakdown probability fixed, and use the paper’s proportional proposer protocols. The named candidate problem is High-Multiplicity Stationary Bargaining Limit: given \((T,\mu,a,q,p)\), compute the accumulation points of type-level stationary equilibrium outcomes as \(k\to\infty\). A solution is a limit payoff density \(u_t=\lim kd\,y_{t,k}\) and a limit distribution over formed-coalition mass vectors \(x\), arising from stationary equilibria of those finite games.

Proposition 8 suggests a tractable branch of this problem. In the divisible limit, \(x_t=q\mu_t\) is a minimum-weight winning coalition profile, and every individual has the same probability \(q\) of belonging to it. This is the continuous counterpart of weak balancedness. It suggests proportional ex ante payoff densities \(u_t=a_t\), with conditional densities \(a_t/q\); as breakdown vanishes, responders’ actual divisions approach proportionality too. That points toward Class A for computing this proportional-equilibrium branch, while leaving open whether every limiting equilibrium is proportional. The candidate is not an anchored complexity result of this paper.

The regime would be a large chamber or assembly whose members are delegates with only a few complete bargaining types, so the number of agents grows while the number of types stays small. The paper’s parties are themselves the strategic players, however, and real party counts are usually small. Treating delegates as the bargaining parties is plausible for some assemblies but is a substantive modeling choice.

That is the weakest point: in an atomless population, an individual proposer or responder has zero mass, so their strategic role may change. Defining the mirror through finite replicas keeps the original game in each instance, but the paper does not show that its equilibria converge or that the limiting computation is tractable. So there is a concrete research question the authors might recognize as a high-multiplicity version of their game, but there is no named computational result here to anchor a continuous-complexity claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Proposition 8 is an equilibrium-existence and payoff characterization; Lemma 9 characterizes a balancedness condition through an LP. Neither classifies the complexity of a computational problem. The proposed “High-Multiplicity Stationary Bargaining Limit” is therefore a new problem built from the paper’s model, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.

The strongest version of that proposal—a large assembly with a fixed set of interchangeable party types—has a plausible high-multiplicity story. But its finite games depend on individually selected proposers and sequential, unanimous responses. In an atomless population, each individual has zero mass, so those actions no longer have the same strategic role. Replacing the game with a mass-based bargaining protocol would require new rules for who proposes and how a continuum of agents accepts. That may be a worthwhile model, but the paper does not determine which such rules preserve its game. Keeping the original protocol instead means studying a sequence of finite games and the convergence of their equilibria, rather than computation on a continuous society.

The proposed limit problem also lacks a finite computational specification: it asks for all accumulation points of equilibrium outcomes across an unbounded sequence of games, without a finite representation or approximation criterion for that set. Restricting attention to the proportional branch avoids that difficulty, but Proposition 8 already identifies that branch; in the divisible coalition model, the profile \(x_t=q\mu_t\) gives each type the same participation probability \(q\). That observation does not make a dull answer objectionable. It does show that the proposed computational task either needs substantial new equilibrium and output definitions, or is merely a limit version of an existence result.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchored ChoCo complexity mirror. It is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous extension exists: a large assembly of replicated party types is not an absurd regime, and a carefully specified asymptotic bargaining project could be valuable. But that would be a new project prompted by the paper, not a computational question the paper already puts on the table.
