What the mirror covers

The proposed query covers only Corollary 5’s natural-threshold criterion; it leaves Theorem 3’s seat-allocation assumptions, the IAC density results, the moment formulas, and the empirical comparisons aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is narrow. The natural regime is a large national electorate—millions of voters—with a small number \(N\) of parties. A type is a voter’s party choice, since that is the information this model uses; \(\mu_i\) is the fraction of the electorate voting for party \(i\). If districts matter to the type description, include district as part of the type. Many voters then share each type, while the number of supported types is small relative to the population.

The closest result to anchor a mirror on is Corollary 5, presented in this paper as a consequence of Corollary 4. Under the paper’s no-statutory-threshold condition, the \(k\)-th largest party is relevant exactly when its vote share renormalized over the \(k\) largest parties satisfies
\[ p_k^{\downarrow} =\frac{v_k^{\downarrow}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}v_j^{\downarrow}} \ge \frac{1}{2m+k}, \]
where \(m\) is mean district magnitude. The paper’s Theorem 3 supplies the seat-allocation model behind this criterion: under assumptions A1–A3, party \(j\)’s seats are \(s_j=p_js+p_jcn/2-c/2\). Theorem 3’s proof is attributed to Flis et al. (2018), rather than proved here.

A precise candidate mirror is Natural-Threshold Party Count. Given rational masses \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_N\) summing to one and a rational mean district magnitude \(m\), sort the positive masses as \(\mu_{(1)}\ge\cdots\ge\mu_{(r)}>0\). Return the largest \(k\) satisfying
\[ \frac{\mu_{(k)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{(j)}}\ge\frac{1}{2m+k}, \]
and identify the top \(k\) parties. This is a direct population-mass version of the paper’s relevance criterion: the inputs, question, and answer all concern its underlying party-relevance model. The expected direction is tractability: sort the \(r\) masses and make exact rational comparisons, in time polynomial in \(r\) and the input bit length. There is no intervention variable or objective in this mirror because the paper asks which parties count as relevant, not how to change the electorate. Further questions could study how the answer changes with \(m\), or how to include statutory thresholds and district-level variation.

That gives a plausible high-multiplicity *problem*, but it does not meet your stated anchor requirement. The paper contains no named result asserting a complexity classification such as membership in P, NP-hardness, or fixed-parameter tractability. Corollary 5 gives a criterion, not a complexity result. Theorem 3 is a seat formula; Theorems 7 and 8 give vote-share order-statistic densities. The paper’s expected-number calculations concern a probability law over vote-share vectors. They are analytical results, not named computational-complexity results.

This distinction also limits the scope of the positive case. The IAC model’s limiting distribution on the party-share simplex is close to a continuous electorate, but the paper’s main question there is probabilistic. Under the programme’s boundary, that probability model is not itself a continuous-population computational mirror. Natural-Threshold Party Count avoids that scope problem by making population shares the input, but it is a modest, readily tractable restatement of the criterion.

So my positive case is that Corollary 5 has a credible continuous-population interpretation in a large-electorate, few-party regime. Its weakest point is decisive under your anchor rule: the paper supplies no qualifying computational result to mirror, and the candidate question is already settled by its formula.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is prior to the choice of type space: this paper states no computational problem and proves no complexity result. Corollary 5 gives a criterion for relevance, Theorem 3 gives a seat formula under assumptions, and Theorems 7–8 give distributions. None asks for an intervention, an optimum, or a complexity classification. That leaves no named computational result here for a continuous mirror to carry forward.

For Corollary 5, the proposed “Natural-Threshold Party Count” takes the paper’s vote shares, now read as masses, and applies its relevance criterion. That is a sensible electorate model, but the paper already expresses the criterion in shares; evaluating it amounts to sorting parties and checking rational inequalities. It does not expose a computational question that the paper leaves open. A stronger proposal—minimum voter mass to switch so a target party becomes relevant—could be worthwhile as a separate campaigning problem. But it needs a switching rule and objective that the paper does not supply. The authors explicitly set aside voter psychology, and their electoral-engineering discussion concerns changing institutional parameters, not moving voters between parties.

Theorem 3 does not provide a stronger computational anchor. A mass-transfer problem aimed at changing which party receives a seat majority under its formula would add an intervention objective to a conditional seat estimate. The formula assumes, among other things, suitable multipliers and no relevant district anomalies; it does not model how voter mass can be moved or priced. Adding district-specific types and intervention constraints might produce a well-posed problem, but it would be a new model rather than a continuization of the theorem’s result.

The IAC results are an even clearer boundary case. The paper already takes the discrete grid of vote shares to its continuous-simplex limit and derives the order-statistic densities and expected number of relevant parties. That is continuous analysis already present in the paper. A new complexity question about altering that distribution or optimizing over it would require choices absent from the paper; simply computing the stated expectation follows its explicit formulas.

The negative case should not deny that large electorates with few parties are a natural high-multiplicity regime. They are. Its weakness is that a new voter-switching or electoral-engineering problem could still be a good ChoCo project. The narrower conclusion is stronger: this paper offers continuous population shares and useful analytic criteria, but no named computational result to mirror. The most promising computational extensions are new questions suggested by the paper, not continuous mirrors of results it proves.
