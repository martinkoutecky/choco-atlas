The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make an anchor-based positive case for this paper: it contains no named computational result asserting that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or any comparable complexity class.

The closest results are Theorem 6, proved in this paper: an inconsistent preference base has no acceptable solutions; and Theorem 7, also proved here: changes to a preference base require a new consistency check. Both are semantic claims. Neither classifies the complexity of deciding consistency or finding acceptable solutions.

A possible extension would treat types as stakeholder groups with identical modal preference bases and \(\mu_t\) as each group’s share of a large engineering program. One could ask whether a single preference structure over the design alternatives satisfies every group’s base. But this is not a sound mirror of a computational anchor here: the model’s population is a new addition, and the answer depends only on which types have positive mass. Their proportions do no work. Weighting groups’ satisfaction to make mass matter would change the paper’s consistency question.

The weakest point is therefore the central one: the paper studies a stakeholder’s preference base over a finite solution space, not computational problems over a population of agents. I don’t see a precise continuous problem per anchor that would meet the stated standard without inventing a result or changing the underlying question.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right to decline the requested anchors. Theorem 6 is the closest candidate: an inconsistent preference base has no acceptable solutions. But that follows from the paper’s definitions; it does not state an algorithm or classify the complexity of deciding consistency. Theorem 7 says a changed base needs another consistency check, without specifying an incremental computational problem. Neither supplies a computational result for a continuous mirror to preserve or investigate.

The strongest reconstruction would treat stakeholder groups with identical preference bases as types, assign each type a mass, and ask whether there is a common acceptable design. That is a coherent high-multiplicity setting, but the paper’s consistency condition requires satisfying every preference in the base. For a collective base formed by taking the union of the groups’ statements, only which types have positive mass matters. Their proportions do no work, so the question is a finite consistency check over the support.

To make mass matter, one could instead ask for a design satisfying the largest share of stakeholders, or for a largest jointly consistent share of their preference bases. That may be a worthwhile new collective-preference problem. It requires a new rule for handling disagreement and partial satisfaction, though; the paper defines neither. Its authors mention multiple stakeholders as future work, but do not study how to aggregate them or prove a result about doing so. This proposal is therefore an extension of the paper’s subject, not a continuous mirror of one of its results.

The other results do not offer a better anchor. Theorems 2–5 concern existence or structural effects in a finite solution space; continuing that space would make the outcomes continuous, while the programme’s target is the population. Theorems 6 and 7 remain the most promising hooks, and their natural population extension either ignores the masses or needs a new collective objective.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as a source of continuous computational results. It is weaker as the universal claim that no worthwhile related question could ever be devised: a computational theory of mass-weighted stakeholder compromise could be valuable in its own right. The paper does not establish that this would be a mirror its authors would recognize, or give ChoCo a named computational result to anchor it.
