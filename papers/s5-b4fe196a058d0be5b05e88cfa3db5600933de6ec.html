The continuous mirror question

Given a finite alternative set \(C\) and a rational distribution \(\mu\) over at most \(\tau\) complete rankings of \(C\), compute the final set of ordered pairs certified by Iterated 3MOT, using \(\mu\)-weighted ranking frequencies in its inequalities. Does the resulting set certify that each reported pair appears in that order in every minimizer of \(D_\mu(\rho)=\sum_{\sigma}\mu_\sigma d^3_{\mathrm{KT}}(\rho,\sigma)\)?

The model it lives in

The population consists of ranking types \(\sigma\), each carrying rational mass \(\mu_\sigma\). The chosen consensus remains a ranking \(\rho\), minimizing expected 3-wise Kendall–tau distance; Iterated 3MOT computes pairwise order certificates from the type masses.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the 3-wise order certificates of Theorems 14 and 25. It leaves aside the 3/4-majority classification, the separate 2-wise result, other 3-wise criteria, and the simulations and applications.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does exact 3-wise median computation admit useful algorithms parameterized by support size \(\tau\), beyond the pairwise certificates?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is a high multiplicity population ranking a shared, finite set of alternatives. A type is one complete ranking \(\sigma\); agents of that type are indistinguishable for the paper’s distance objective. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over rankings, with \(\tau\) rankings in its support and each mass giving a fraction of the population. The action remains a single consensus ranking \(\rho\), chosen to minimize expected 3-wise Kendall–tau distance:
\[ D_\mu(\rho)=\sum_{\sigma}\mu_\sigma d^3_{\mathrm{KT}}(\rho,\sigma), \qquad M_3(\mu)=\arg\min_{\rho}D_\mu(\rho). \]
For example, this fits a large survey or platform population ranking a modest shortlist, where many respondents give the same ranking and \(\tau\) is much smaller than the number of respondents. The ranking stays discrete; it is the society that becomes continuous.

My lead anchor is Theorem 25 (Iterated 3MOT), proved here. It starts with \(U_{-1}=\varnothing\), then adds ordered pairs \((x,y)\) satisfying its two inequalities, using the transitive closure of pairs already established. In the paper’s notation, those inequalities are \(Q_{x,y,z}\ge 0\) for every \(z\notin L_y(U_{k-1})\cup\{x,y\}\), and
\[ 2\delta_{xy}> \sum_{z\in X_k}\max\!\left( 0,\, -P_{x,y,z}+ \sum_{t\in Y_k\setminus\{z\}}\max(0,S_{y,x,z,t}) \right), \]
where \(X_k=C\setminus(L_y(U_{k-1})\cup R_x(U_{k-1})\cup\{x,y\})\) and \(Y_k=C\setminus(L_y(U_{k-1})\cup\{x,y\})\). The theorem says \(U_k\subseteq U_{k+1}\), and every pair in any \(U_k\) has \(x\) before \(y\) in every 3-wise median.

The corresponding continuous problem is Continuous Iterated 3MOT certification: given \((C,\mu)\), compute the final set of ordered pairs certified by those iterative inequalities, with the guarantee that every reported pair occurs in every ranking in \(M_3(\mu)\). Define the paper’s vote counts as masses, for example \(n_{xyz}(\mu)=\sum_{\sigma:\,x\succ_\sigma y\succ_\sigma z}\mu_\sigma\), and use those quantities in its definitions of \(\delta,Q,P,S\). This is a precise preprocessing problem; it does not claim to find every pair that happens to be fixed across all medians. Each iteration can test all pairs with finite sums, and at most \(n(n-1)\) pairs can be added, so the continuous version is polynomial in \(n,\tau,L\), where \(L\) is the input bit length. This is my strongest anchor because it is the paper’s improved, iterative result.

The base anchor is Theorem 14 (3MOT), proved here, with Lemma 15, proved here, giving the corresponding algorithm’s runtime. Theorem 14 says that if \(x\) is ahead of \(y\) in a majority of votes, \(Q_{x,y,z}\ge0\) for every other alternative \(z\), and
\[ 2\delta_{xy}> \sum_{z\ne x,y}\max\!\left( 0,\, -P_{x,y,z}+ \sum_{t\ne x,y,z}\max(0,S_{y,x,z,t}) \right), \]
then \(x\) precedes \(y\) in every 3-wise median. Lemma 15 states that the algorithm determining such relative orders runs in \(O(n^4m)\) time on a profile of \(m\) votes.

Its mirror, Continuous 3MOT certification, takes \((C,\mu)\) and returns exactly the pairs passing those tests after replacing vote counts by masses. The output has the same all-medians guarantee. The paper’s \(O(n^4m)\) computation scans an explicit vote list; on a support representation, the needed aggregates can instead be computed from the \(\tau\) rankings and their rational masses, giving polynomial bit complexity in \(n,\tau,L\). Theorem 25 extends this base certification by using earlier certified relations to tighten later tests.

Both anchors are algorithmic results proved in this paper. There is no numbered NP-hardness theorem to use as an anchor: §2.1 states that the decision version is NP-complete for every \(k\ge3\), citing Gilbert et al. [14]. That cited result also gives the right expectation for the full continuous median problem. A discrete profile with \(N\) voters maps to \(\mu_\sigma=n_\sigma/N\), and its median rankings are unchanged because \(D_\mu\) is the discrete total-distance objective divided by \(N\). Conversely, a rational \(\mu\) can be scaled to integer multiplicities without changing its medians. Thus exact 3-wise median computation inherits the cited discrete hardness: it is Class B, while the two continuous certification problems are Class A. Further questions include whether support size \(\tau\) yields useful parameterized algorithms for exact median computation, and how much the certified pairs can reduce the remaining search in that model.

This covers the paper’s 3-wise Major Order results, not its 3/4-majority classification for at most five alternatives, its separate 2-wise result, or its simulations and applications. It continuizes only the population.

The soft spot is that the paper already represents an election by a finite list of possibly repeated rankings, and its objective depends on their multiplicities. Passing from that list to a rational distribution does not change the medians; it changes the input representation and makes the high multiplicity parameters \(\tau\) and \(L\) explicit. I think the authors would recognize the mirrors as their same space-reduction questions for a compressed, continuous population, but I would not present them as a new mathematical objective or claim that continuization removes the hardness of finding a full median.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest skeptical case is that Theorems 14 and 25 certify some fixed pairwise orders among the 3-wise medians; they do not solve the median problem itself. Their tests use only finite sums of vote counts. For a rational distribution over rankings, one can replace each count by its mass, and clear denominators to recover a finite profile with the same tests and medians. That makes the proposed mirrors look like a change of input representation, with no new continuous optimization technique. The full median problem also remains hard under this translation.

That case does not defeat the anchors under the programme’s criteria. A change from an explicit voter list to support size \(\tau\) and binary rational masses is precisely the high-multiplicity question: the paper gives a runtime in the number of listed votes, while the mirror asks for complexity in \(n,\tau,L\). The paper neither takes a continuous society as input nor proves the relevant support-and-bit-length complexity result. And the full problem’s inherited hardness is a valid Class B outcome, not an objection. Large surveys or platforms ranking a shared shortlist also provide a plausible regime with many agents per ranking type.

So I cannot honestly make a strong case that no worthwhile mirror exists. Both certification anchors survive; the negative case is mainly that their continuous versions may be immediate extensions with modest methodological novelty. Under the stated standard, that is not enough to reject them.
