The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t identify an eligible anchor in this paper. Its named objects—the Maximal SSQ hypothesis and its three variations—are coalition-formation theories, not numbered theorems, lemmas, corollaries, or propositions asserting a computational complexity result.

The paper reports empirical predictive results: for example, Maximal SSQ matches \(8\) of \(65\) elections in the de Swaan data and \(0\) of \(18\) Israeli elections (Table 1). Those findings are not claims that an underlying problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise computationally classified. The paper gives no named computational result of the kind required for a continuous-mirror case.

The strongest possible angle would be to study computational optimization of the leader’s Shapley–Shubik index over majority coalitions. But the paper supplies no eligible result to anchor that question, and its model treats a small set of parties as indivisible coalition members. Turning them into continuous masses would risk changing the coalition-formation problem itself. So there is no defensible anchor-specific continuous problem or expected complexity class to give from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent identifies no eligible anchor, and that is the strongest objection here. The paper’s named objects—Maximal SSQ and its closed, minimum-size, and minimum-range variants—are predictive hypotheses. Its numbered-looking findings are empirical counts, such as \(8\) matches in \(65\) elections and \(0\) in \(18\), not results about computational complexity. The authors use coalition calculations to test those hypotheses; they do not claim an algorithmic classification for a problem.

The best possible rescue is to define a computational problem around the calculation: given party seat weights, a formateur, and a quota, find a majority coalition containing the formateur that maximizes its Shapley–Shubik index. But a faithful continuous version has to preserve the party as the unit of coalition membership. Parties are the actors whose agreement forms a government, and the index measures a named party’s pivotality among those actors. Treat parties as a population of interchangeable types and coalition choice becomes a choice of *fractions* of each type. Treat legislators or voters as the population and party cohesion—the feature that makes the paper’s game a game among parties—disappears. Keeping the formateur as one distinguished party leaves the central actor outside the high-multiplicity population.

A model of many interchangeable factions could make a sensible new optimization problem. The paper gives no indication that this is the object its authors mean: its empirical cases concern a small set of distinct parties and actual coalitions among them. So that possibility does not rescue a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The negative case is strongest on the absence of an eligible result and weaker as a universal claim: a separately motivated continuum of factions might be worth studying. I would not count that possibility as an anchor raised here or as a mirror of this paper.
