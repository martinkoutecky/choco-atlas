The continuous mirror question

Given \(p\in[-\infty,1]\), \(\lambda>0\), and a nonnegative finite-mean law \(\mathcal D\), let \(N\) statistically identical agents arrive sequentially and let \(M_N=\lfloor\lambda N\rfloor\) indivisible items have independent values from \(\mathcal D\); determine the limiting optimal online and offline ex-ante welfare \(W_p(u)\), their competitive ratio, and an attaining causal policy as \(N\to\infty\), where \(u(s)\) is expected utility at normalized arrival position \(s\in[0,1]\) and \(W_{-\infty}(u)=\inf_s u(s)\).

The model it lives in

The population has mass measure \(\mu_t\,ds\) over finite valuation or cohort types \(t\) and normalized arrival position \(s\in[0,1]\); \(M_N/N\to\lambda\) supplies indivisible items, finite agents receive integral bundles, and the limiting decision variable is a causal allocation kernel. The objective is the generalized mean \(W_p(u)\), compared between online and clairvoyant offline policies.

The objection that survived

The opponent's strongest point is that exact arrival rank is a complete problem-relevant type, so \(s\) may be a continuized stopping horizon rather than high multiplicity, while the hard laws in Theorem 3.1 vary with \(N\); the proponent does not fully establish a fixed-type formulation preserving the original pointwise objective.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Proposition 4's homogeneous ex-ante many-item identity and Theorem 3.1's egalitarian limiting guarantee, with the equalizing policy as the algorithmic witness. It leaves the heterogeneous \(\mu\) extension, the joint ex-post \(N,M_N\) regime, and a formal complexity classification unresolved.

Open questions for a prover

  • Does the joint regime \(N,M_N\to\infty\) with \(M_N/N\to\lambda\) have a nontrivial ex-post ratio or a \(\lambda\)-dependent phase transition reconciling Propositions 5 and 6?
  • Can the heterogeneous finite-type version with masses \(\mu_t\) be reduced to a finite-dimensional optimization or a tractable policy class?
  • Can finite-\(N\) integral policies be shown to converge to a causal kernel while preserving the pointwise egalitarian objective \(W_{-\infty}(u)=\inf_s u(s)\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a population-scaled version of the paper’s many-item fair-division problem. A bare continuum of agents competing for one indivisible item would be a poor mirror: the item has zero per-capita supply, and almost every agent receives zero utility. The natural co-modification is therefore to let the number of indivisible items scale with population size.

Take a finite set of substantive agent types \(T\), with population distribution \(\mu\). A type records an agent’s valuation law, arrival cohort, and any other parameter relevant to allocation. In the paper’s exact homogeneous subcase, there is one valuation type: every agent’s value for every item is an independent draw from the same finite-support distribution \(\mathcal D\). For \(N\) agents, use \(M_N=\lfloor \lambda N\rfloor\) indivisible items, where \(\lambda>0\) is the item supply per unit population. Agents arrive sequentially; on arrival, an agent’s values for all remaining items are revealed, and an online rule irrevocably assigns an integral bundle. An offline rule sees the entire valuation matrix.

For a type or normalized arrival position \(s\), let \(u(s)\) be the agent’s expected utility in the limit. The welfare objective is the generalized mean \(W_p(u)\), with \(W_{-\infty}(u)=\inf_s u(s)\). Aggregate allocations may become fractional in the limit, but every finite-\(N\) agent still receives an integral bundle. Thus this is genuinely population continuization, not merely fractionalizing outcomes.

My lead anchor is Proposition 4, proved in the paper. It states that, for every \(n\ge2\), \(m\ge1\), and distribution \(\mathcal D\), the sets of expected-utility profiles achievable by online and offline rules in the \(m\)-item problem are exactly \(m\) times the corresponding sets in the single-item problem. Consequently, for every \(p\in[-\infty,1]\), \(\ALG^{\mathrm{ea}}_{n,m}(p;\mathcal D)=m\ALG_n(p;\mathcal D)\), \(\OPT^{\mathrm{ea}}_{n,m}(p;\mathcal D)=m\OPT_n(\mathcal D)\), and \(\CR^{\mathrm{ea}}_{n,m}(p)=\CR_n(p)\).

The corresponding continuous problem is:

\(\mathsf{Fluid\text{-}ExAnte\text{-}Prophet}(p,\lambda,\mu)\). Given \(p\), \(\lambda\), the finite type distribution \(\mu\), and the valuation laws of the types, compute the optimal limiting online value \(W_p(u)\), the offline value, and their ratio as \(N\to\infty\) with \(M_N/N\to\lambda\). A solution is a causal allocation policy, represented either as a convergent family of finite-\(N\) policies or as its limiting measurable allocation kernel, together with the resulting utility field \(u\).

In the homogeneous case, Proposition 4 gives the exact finite-\(N\) identity
\[ \frac{\ALG^{\mathrm{ea}}_{N,M_N}(p;\mathcal D)} {\OPT^{\mathrm{ea}}_{N,M_N}(p;\mathcal D)} = \frac{\ALG_N(p;\mathcal D)} {\OPT_N(\mathcal D)}. \]
So the continuous problem is not an invented relaxation: it is the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s own fair-division problem, and its online/offline comparison is inherited exactly at every finite resolution. I would expect the homogeneous finite-support version to be Class A: itemwise decomposition reduces the problem to the single-item threshold problem, while the continuum supplies a natural mass-level representation. The heterogeneous \(\mu\)-version is a further optimization problem rather than something already solved by the paper.

A second, sharper anchor is Theorem 3.1, proved here. Define \(\rho(a,b)=\bigl((1-\frac{b}{a+b}e^{-a})(1+\frac{1-e^{-b}}a)\bigr)^{-1}\) and \(\Gamma=\inf_{a,b>0}\rho(a,b)\approx0.70591334\). The theorem states that, for every \(n\ge2\), \(\CR_n(-\infty)\ge\Gamma\), and that \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\CR_n(-\infty)=\Gamma\).

Its continuous mirror is:

\(\mathsf{Fluid\text{-}Egalitarian\text{-}Prophet}\). Determine the worst-case limiting competitive ratio
\[ \lim_{N\to\infty} \inf_{\mathcal D} \frac{\ALG^{\mathrm{ea}}_{N,\lfloor\lambda N\rfloor}(-\infty;\mathcal D)} {\OPT^{\mathrm{ea}}_{N,\lfloor\lambda N\rfloor}(-\infty;\mathcal D)}, \]
where the infimum ranges over nonnegative finite-mean valuation laws. The solution consists of the limiting value and a family of online policies. By Proposition 4, this ratio equals \(\lim_{N\to\infty}\CR_N(-\infty)\), so Theorem 3.1 gives the answer \(\Gamma\), independently of \(\lambda>0\). The paper’s backward equalization rule supplies the natural candidate policy: each arrival position receives the same expected utility, and the same rule is applied independently to every item.

This is plausibly recognizable to the authors as their problem. The agents remain sequential, values remain stochastic, bundles remain integral, and the objective remains the generalized mean of expected utilities. Only the population and item supply are scaled so that a limiting per-capita welfare problem exists. A plausible application is a large platform allocating a proportional supply of indivisible scholarships, training slots, housing units, or compute credits to a stream of statistically similar users.

The mirror does not cover the paper’s ex-post results as settled facts. Proposition 5 shows that when \(m=n\) and \(p\le0\), the worst-case ex-post ratio is \(O(\log n/n)\), while Proposition 6 gives convergence to the single-item ratio only for fixed \(n\) as \(m\to\infty\). Their joint \(N,m\to\infty\) behavior is therefore an important further question: does a positive item-density threshold separate a tractable fluid regime from a continuum-specific impossibility regime?

The weakest point is that arrival position matters to the paper’s fairness objective. In the continuum it becomes a type coordinate \(s\), so one could object that this is continuization of arrival time rather than population. The defence is that \(s\) records an agent’s place in an ordered population; the population measure, not the dynamics, is what is being continuized. A finite-\(K\) cohort version gives a stricter finite-type model, with \(K\) fixed while \(N\) grows, though it no longer captures every position-level distinction exactly.

A second caveat is formal: the paper has no NP-hardness, P-membership, or parameterized-complexity theorem. These are competitive-ratio and structural optimization anchors. Thus I would present this as a strong substantive continuous mirror, with a credible Class A direction, but not claim that the paper itself has already established a full continuous-computational complexity classification.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative case is that both anchors are really the same asymptotic optimal-stopping result, not computational results about a high-multiplicity society. The paper contains no theorem about NP-hardness, polynomial-time solvability, approximation, parameterized complexity, or an algorithm whose complexity depends on an encoded population. Proposition 4 is an exact welfare identity, and Theorem 3.1 is a sharp competitive-ratio and variational theorem. That omission is decisive under ChoCo’s stated remit: there is no named computational result here whose population-continuous counterpart would expose a new complexity landscape.

Proposition 4 does not repair this. For fixed \(n\), it says that \(m\) independent items replicate the single-item utility profile by a factor of \(m\). Setting \(m=\lfloor\lambda n\rfloor\) and then taking \(n\to\infty\) therefore adds a replication parameter and an asymptotic horizon; it does not turn the agents into a population of repeated computational types. The normalized value is still governed by the original one-item stopping problem.

More seriously, the paper’s agents are not interchangeable in the sense required by high multiplicity. Agent \(i\)’s arrival position is relevant both to feasibility and to the fairness objective: the objective compares \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\), and the equalizing rule deliberately assigns different conditional treatment to different positions. Thus two agents with the same valuation law but different arrival ranks are different problem-types. In the finite instance there are \(n\) such relevant positions, each with multiplicity one. The distribution \(\mu\) over valuation laws is merely a point mass and forgets the central structure.

Replacing \(i\) by a normalized position \(s=i/n\) does not solve this. It produces a continuum-time stopping or fluid-control problem whose type coordinate is arrival time, not a finite population distribution over substantive agent types. Each exact \(s\) has zero mass, while the paper’s egalitarian objective is a worst-case guarantee over every arrival position. If one uses an essential infimum, one has changed the objective; if one retains pointwise guarantees, the zero-mass points remain individually distinguished. Both are legitimate new models, but neither is the high-multiplicity mirror of Proposition 4.

The proposed finite-cohort fallback changes the result rather than preserving it. Taking the minimum over cohort-average utilities is not the paper’s \(p\)-mean over agents, especially at \(p=-\infty\). Requiring guarantees for every position within each cohort reintroduces the continuum-time problem. Consequently, one can obtain either a genuinely finite-type population model or the paper’s position-sensitive fairness objective, but not both without adding a substantial new model whose relevance is not established by the paper.

Theorem 3.1 has the same defect in sharper form. The proposed mirror is

\[ \lim_{N\to\infty}\inf_{\mathcal D} \frac{\ALG^{\mathrm{ea}}_{N,\lfloor\lambda N\rfloor}(-\infty;\mathcal D)} {\OPT^{\mathrm{ea}}_{N,\lfloor\lambda N\rfloor}(-\infty;\mathcal D)}. \]

By Proposition 4, this is simply the already-defined sequence \(\CR_N(-\infty)\), whose limit the theorem computes as \(\Gamma\). That equality is not objectionable merely because the answer is unchanged; a continuous identity can itself be valuable. The problem is that the displayed object is not a continuous society instance. It has no fixed finite type distribution, no encoded input population, and no mass-transfer or separation problem. It is the asymptotic number of arrival stages in a prophet inequality.

There is also no fixed valuation law behind the worst case supplied by Theorem 3.1. Its hard distributions vary with \(N\): one value is of order \(N\), another has probability of order \(1/N\), and the limiting argument takes \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\) before \(N\to\infty\). This is an adversarial sequence used to determine a worst-case asymptotic constant, not a single continuous society whose computational welfare problem is being solved. For a fixed \(\mathcal D\), the theorem does not say that the continuous problem has value \(\Gamma\); for the worst-case infimum, it gives a scalar extremal analysis rather than an algorithmic problem.

The ex-post results do not provide a better anchor. Proposition 5 might suggest a population-scaled matching mirror with \(m=n\), but its random bipartite graph gives each agent an essentially unique valuation vector over all items. Treating that vector as the type produces a type space whose dimension and effective support grow with \(n\), with negligible multiplicity. Replacing it by a fixed compatibility kernel or a continuum transport problem would be a new stochastic matching model, not a continuization of the paper’s agent types. The vanishing ratio is driven precisely by individual-level perfect matching and the last agent’s private edge; a mass description risks erasing the event the proposition studies.

A serious fluid-limit paper could certainly be written about sequential allocation with a proportional item supply. It would belong naturally to online allocation, stochastic control, or mean-field matching. But on the programme’s standard, neither proposed anchor supplies a worthwhile continuous computational-social-choice mirror: one scales the number of independent items, the other lets the stopping horizon grow, while the agents’ population types never acquire meaningful high multiplicity. The negative case is not an impossibility theorem about every imaginable reformulation; it is that every faithful reformulation either remains a time-horizon limit or changes the paper into a different fluid-control problem.
