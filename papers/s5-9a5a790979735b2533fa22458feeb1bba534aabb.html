The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is a continuous population mirror of the paper’s deterministic line results. It is a mechanism-design mirror, though, not a complexity-trichotomy result: the paper has no named theorem saying a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or the like. Its named results concern strategyproofness and approximation guarantees.

Take a finite-support population distribution
\[ \mu=\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}\mu_j\delta_{x_j}, \]
with rational masses summing to one and rational locations \(x_j\). A type is a resident’s ideal facility location; people of the same type have the same distance cost. The mass \(\mu_j\) is that type’s share of the population. In a plausible regime, a city serves a very large population concentrated in a much smaller number \(\tau\) of neighborhood or access-location types, and a public forecast \(\pi\) predicts where the facility should go.

For a facility at \(y\), define
\[ R(\mu,y)=\max_{x\in\operatorname{supp}(\mu)}|x-y|. \]
If \(a=\min\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\) and \(b=\max\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\), the optimum is \(o(\mu)=(a+b)/2\), with cost \(\operatorname{OPT}(\mu)=(b-a)/2\). A direct mechanism \(F\) takes \((\mu,\pi)\) and returns \(y\). To give truthfulness a nonvacuous meaning for a continuous population, I would use mass strong strategyproofness: no positive-mass coalition can change its reported locations so that almost all its members weakly benefit and a positive mass strictly benefits. This is a deliberate co-modification; the paper already studies strong group strategyproofness, and MinMaxP is claimed there to satisfy it on the line.

My lead anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for every prediction-error bound \(\eta\ge 0\), no deterministic strategyproof mechanism can achieve an approximation ratio below \(1+\min(1,\eta)\). The matching upper bound is attained by MinMaxP, which clips the prediction to the interval between the extreme reported locations. The continuous mirror is: for each \(\eta\), determine the best worst-case ratio over mass-strong-group-strategyproof rules on rational finite-support populations with prediction error at most \(\eta\), and give a rule attaining it. The expected answer is the same frontier, \(1+\min(1,\eta)\), attained by
\[ F(\mu,\pi)=\max(a,\min(b,\pi)). \]
The lower bound should transfer because the finite profiles used in the paper are special rational population distributions; extending the upper bound requires checking the paper’s group-strategyproofness argument for divisible-mass coalitions. This is an impossibility-transfer question, analogous to Class B in spirit, but it is not a computational-hardness result.

A second, related anchor is Theorem 2, also proved here. For every \(\epsilon\in(0,1]\), MinMaxP is the only deterministic strategyproof mechanism with \((2-\epsilon)\)-consistency and bounded robustness. Its continuous mirror asks for the characterization of mass-strong-group-strategyproof rules on these populations with consistency strictly better than \(2\) and bounded robustness. The expected answer is again the clipping rule: the paper’s uniqueness theorem applies to the discrete subinstances, while MinMaxP is the candidate to verify for divisible-mass coalitions. This is a rule-characterization problem, rather than a claim about runtime.

The scope is deliberately narrow: deterministic mechanisms on the line for the maximum-cost objective. It does not mirror the paper’s randomized results, tree or \(\ell_p\) settings, or \(L_p\)-norm social cost.

The weakest point is that maximum cost depends only on the support’s two extreme locations; the population shares themselves do not affect the objective. So this mirror makes a sensible high-multiplicity population representation, but it may yield little new optimization structure. More fundamentally, even a successful mirror would extend a mechanism-design impossibility and characterization result, not chart computational complexity. If the required anchors must literally be complexity-class results, then this paper has none.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that this paper states no computational problem. Theorem 1 is an approximation lower bound for strategyproof mechanisms; Theorem 2 characterizes such mechanisms. The other results are also about strategic properties and approximation guarantees. None gives a running-time or complexity result for an algorithmic problem. The programme’s brief treats that distinction as decisive: continuizing an axiomatic or distortion result does not produce the computational complexity landscape ChoCo is meant to chart.

The proposed mirror of Theorem 1 is plausible. A city with many residents sharing a relatively small set of preferred locations is a credible high-multiplicity scenario, and a mass version of MinMaxP can be defined. But the best version of that mirror still asks for a strategic approximation frontier. Its truthfulness condition also needs care: an individual in an atomless population has zero mass, so their report cannot affect the distribution; requiring protection against positive-mass coalitions gives a meaningful condition, but changes the strategic object. Even if the lower bound transfers and the same frontier holds, that would extend a distortion result, not answer a computational question.

Theorem 2 fares no better for ChoCo. One can ask whether MinMaxP is the unique mass-strategyproof mechanism with the stated guarantees. For rational finite-support populations, the profiles can be represented as finite agent profiles, so much of the paper’s characterization may carry over directly. For a genuinely divisible-mass coalition model, the characterization would need its own formulation and proof. Either route remains a mechanism characterization. Turning it into “compute an optimal strategyproof mechanism” would require specifying a representation of mechanisms and a computational input the paper does not supply. Computing the output of the named line mechanism, by contrast, amounts to finding the extreme reported locations.

I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no plausible continuous population model exists here: the city interpretation is credible, and the mass version could be mathematically coherent. The negative case is narrower but strong for ChoCo: both proposed anchors may support worthwhile mechanism-design work, yet neither supplies a worthwhile continuous computational mirror of a result in this paper.
