What the mirror covers

No result is computationally mirrored; the paper’s structural and impossibility results concern axioms for aggregation rules.

The case FOR (proponent)

The best population-level starting point is a large consultation on a fixed finite agenda: a type is a complete, consistent judgment set, and its mass is the fraction of participants holding exactly those judgments. If the agenda is modest, many participants could share a relatively small number of types. The collective decision would be a complete, consistent judgment set.

But the paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor. Its numbered results—including Theorem 1’s semi-veto-dictatorship, Theorem 2’s semi-dictatorship, Theorem 3’s dictatorship, and Corollary 1’s Arrow result—are axiomatic existence or impossibility claims, not results about computational complexity or algorithms. Corollary 1, proved here, says that for the preference agenda with the paper’s specified relevance relation and privileged propositions, some individual is a dictator. None asserts that a problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or similar.

A mass-based version could ask whether an aggregation rule over distributions of judgment types can satisfy continuous analogues of the paper’s axioms. That would be a continuous axiomatic question, not the computational mirror requested here; the paper provides no objective or algorithmic problem to anchor it. There is also a modeling wrinkle: the dictatorship results can depend on a named individual, while a distribution over judgment types forgets identities. Encoding identity in the types could remove the low-support regime.

So, under the requested anchor rule, there is no honest per-anchor continuous computational problem to hand to a prover. The paper contains no named computational result to mirror.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The consultation example makes a continuous population plausible, but it does not give this paper a computational problem to mirror. The paper defines an aggregation rule \(F:J^n\to J\) and studies which rules satisfy axioms. Its Proposition 1 and Theorems 1–4, including the Arrow corollary, are structural or impossibility results about those rules. None gives an algorithm, a complexity classification, or an optimization problem over a society.

The better population model is clear: let each complete, consistent judgment set be a type, and give each type a rational mass. But the paper’s results depend on the agenda, logical consistency, relevance relation, and universal domain. They do not depend on how mass is distributed across judgment types. To compute an outcome from that distribution, we would need a specified rule. The paper’s majority-rule example is not such a rule for its purposes, since it can violate collective rationality. Choosing a rule and asking for a consistent output closest to weighted majority would introduce a new objective, rather than continuize one of the paper’s results.

The dictatorship results add a modeling difficulty. A named individual can have decisive influence in the finite model, while an individual in a non-atomic population has zero mass. Giving a positive-mass bloc a dictator’s role would be a different axiom and model. One could study continuous analogues of the axioms, or complexity questions about checking the paper’s structural conditions, but the former is axiomatic and the latter does not need a continuous society.

So the negative case is strong for a *continuous mirror of this paper*: it has no named computational result to anchor one, and the natural mass-based variants leave its actual theorems untouched. The broader claim that no worthwhile computational project could ever be built from judgment aggregation is less secure. Such a project might be valuable, but its computational objective would have to come from elsewhere.
