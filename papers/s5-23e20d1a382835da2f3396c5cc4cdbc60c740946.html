What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror targets Theorem 5.2’s NSW premise and fairness guarantee, but the paper’s numbered result is not computational; the type-level exposure averages also lose the identity-level correlations in its match objective.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: this paper has no numbered theorem classifying a problem as polynomial-time solvable or hard. The best anchor is Theorem 5.2, stated in this paper and derived using results from Saito and Joachims [45] and Kroer and Peysakhovich [35]. It is a fairness guarantee, not a complexity theorem. It says that if a policy gives both sides NSW-best recommendations, then for \(K=1\) it is exactly doubly envy-free. For \(K>1\), if the preference probabilities are \(\epsilon\)-similar and \(n=\Theta(m)\), it is doubly envy-free up to additive \(O(\epsilon)\): for every pair on either side, an agent’s utility from their own opportunity is at least their utility from the other agent’s opportunity minus \(O(\epsilon)\). Here, \(\epsilon\)-similarity means that each agent has at least \(K+1\) same-side agents whose relevant preference vectors differ from theirs by at most \(\epsilon\) in every coordinate.

A plausible high-multiplicity regime is a large hiring platform serving recurring applicant and vacancy cohorts. Applicants might share a complete type comprising their qualification, location, availability, and modeled application probabilities to each vacancy type; vacancy types similarly include their criteria and response probabilities. Suppose there are hundreds of thousands of active users but tens or hundreds of such types. Then type mass means the fraction of applicants or vacancies in each type. This is a scenario-specific claim: if the model needs to distinguish every user’s pairwise preferences, the support grows and the mirror loses its advantage. The paper’s dating-platform story need not itself have this regime.

Here is a precise mirror of the computation behind Theorem 5.2. Let \(S\) and \(T\) be finite sets of left- and right-side types, with rational masses \(\mu_s\) and \(\nu_t\) summing to one on each side. Let \(p_{st}\) be the product of the two types’ application probabilities. Use rank percentiles \(y\in[0,1]\), with a nonincreasing examination profile represented as a rational step function on bins \(q=1,\ldots,Q\), whose widths are \(d_q\).

For each \(s,t,q\), let \(x_{stq}\) be the probability that a particular right-side user of type \(t\) appears in bin \(q\) of a left-side type \(s\) user’s recommendation list. Feasibility requires
\[ \sum_q x_{stq}=1\quad\text{for every }s,t, \qquad \sum_t \nu_t x_{stq}=d_q\quad\text{for every }s,q. \]
The first constraint puts every counterpart somewhere in the list; the second fills each rank bin in aggregate. Define \(y_{tsq}\) symmetrically for a right-side type \(t\)’s list of left-side users, with \(\sum_q y_{tsq}=1\) and \(\sum_s\mu_s y_{tsq}=d_q\).

If \(e^R_q\) and \(e^L_q\) are the examination probabilities in bin \(q\), the type-pair exposures are \(a_{st}=\sum_q e^R_qx_{stq}\) and \(b_{ts}=\sum_q e^L_qy_{tsq}\). The per-opposite-population match utilities are
\[ u_s=\sum_t\nu_t p_{st}a_{st}b_{ts}, \qquad v_t=\sum_s\mu_s p_{st}a_{st}b_{ts}. \]
These are the paper’s expected mutual-match utilities, aggregated by type and normalized by the opposite-side population. That normalization preserves envy comparisons and best responses.

I would call the resulting problem Mass-NSW Recommendation Response. Given the type masses, probabilities, rank bins, and a fixed feasible policy \(x\), find a feasible \(y\) maximizing
\[ \Phi_L(x,y)=\sum_s\mu_s\log u_s. \]
A solution is a feasible \(y\) with a certificate of global optimality. The symmetric response problem maximizes \(\Phi_R(x,y)=\sum_t\nu_t\log v_t\) over \(x\) with \(y\) fixed. Since each utility is affine in the response variables when the other side’s policy is fixed, each response maximizes a concave function over a finite transportation polytope. I expect this problem to be tractable, to requested numerical precision under ordinary positive-utility and bit-conditioning assumptions, with size governed by the type supports and rank bins rather than by the number of users.

A mutually NSW-best pair is the natural follow-on: each side’s policy must be optimal for its response problem given the other side’s policy. Under the corresponding mass-based similarity condition—each type has at least \(\kappa\) mass of same-side types within \(\epsilon\) in preference coordinates—and a balanced population-size ratio, the continuous question is whether the paper’s \(O(\epsilon)\) envy guarantee persists. This scaling of the cutoff and similarity condition is a proposed limit version, not a direct corollary of Theorem 5.2. In particular, the theorem’s \(K=1\) branch does not carry over literally when \(K\) is a fixed number of positions in a growing population. The paper’s alternating optimization motivates the follow-on, but does not prove that it converges to a mutually NSW-best pair. I would treat that equilibrium-computation question as open; the concave response problems alone do not settle it.

The mirror covers the NSW optimization premise of Theorem 5.2 and its fairness guarantee. It does not claim that the paper proves a complexity bound, and it does not try to continuize the doubly stochastic recommendation outcomes: those are already randomized in the paper. The continuous object here is the society, represented by masses over complete types.

The weakest point is that the paper’s input allows arbitrary individual-pair probabilities \(p_{ij}\). Replacing those with type-pair probabilities \(p_{st}\) is a real structural restriction, and the empirical datasets in the paper are small rather than high-multiplicity demonstrations. The hiring-cohort scenario makes that restriction plausible, but does not establish it for the authors’ datasets. The positive case rests on the narrower claim that the paper’s reciprocal recommendation problem has a sensible high-multiplicity regime, and that its NSW response computation becomes a genuine continuous-optimization problem there.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 5.2 is a conditional fairness guarantee: if a policy is NSW-best for both sides, then it satisfies the stated envy bound. It does not bound the time needed to find such a policy or classify that computation. The paper’s alternating Frank–Wolfe and Sinkhorn methods are evaluated experimentally; the paper gives no theorem guaranteeing that they find a globally NSW-best policy. So the proposed response optimization is a reasonable new question suggested by the paper, but it is not a continuous version of a named complexity result. That leaves the main ChoCo deliverable without an anchor here.

The hiring-cohort scenario is plausible, and the fact that recommendations are probabilistic does not itself make the society continuous. The harder problem is that the paper’s match objective depends on *which individuals* are paired through the two recommendation policies. For each pair \((a_i,b_j)\), expected matches depend on products such as \(A_i(j,k)B_j(i,\ell)\). Type-level averages of the two factors do not determine the average of their product.

For example, take \(r\) agents on each side, all of one type, with \(p_{ij}=1\) and only the first rank examined. A coordinated policy puts each agent’s paired counterpart first on both sides, producing \(r\) expected matches. A policy with uniform doubly stochastic recommendation matrices produces \(1\) expected match. In both policies, averaging over same-type identities gives first-rank exposure \(1/r\) on each side. Multiplying those averages, as in the proposed mass utility, predicts the diffuse result for both policies. The population masses and type-level exposures cannot distinguish a coordinated matching from diffuse recommendations, even though their match totals differ by a factor of \(r\).

One could add a joint measure over pairwise exposures to retain that alignment. To preserve the theorem’s envy condition as well, the model would need to retain enough individual opportunity information to compare each agent against every other agent. A mass-based average or an almost-everywhere condition would lose the paper’s all-pairs guarantee; a pointwise condition would require carrying an individual-indexed relational structure alongside the type masses. That may define a worthwhile new model, but its computational questions concern those joint recommendation structures, rather than the type-mass response problem proposed in the case for.

This is the strongest negative case I can make, and it has a clear limit. It defeats the proposed type-averaged mirror and weakens the claim that Theorem 5.2 anchors a computational mirror. It does not prove that no continuum model with pairwise couplings could be worthwhile. Repeated hiring cohorts could motivate such a model, and the paper’s future-work discussion of exact or approximation algorithms gives authors a reason to recognize related computational questions. The universal claim that no scenario can produce a worthwhile mirror therefore remains weak.
