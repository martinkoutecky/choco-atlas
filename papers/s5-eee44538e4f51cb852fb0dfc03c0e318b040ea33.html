What the mirror covers

The proposed runoff covers deterministic winner determination under sincere voting; it leaves the paper’s classroom outcomes, errors, strategic votes, and observed frequencies untouched.

The case FOR (proponent)

The honest positive case is narrow. There are no qualifying anchors in this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition asserting a computational result. Its findings that the median-position candidate won twice in the first round and in 70% of second rounds are descriptive results from classroom elections, not named complexity results. The median voter theorem appears as background, and its familiar claim concerns a binary contest. I can’t present any of these as an anchor under your criteria.

The best continuous analogue I can offer, therefore, is unanchored: Continuous Three-Policy Runoff Winner. Let a type be a voter’s ideal public-good level \(g\in\{0,\ldots,10\}\), with utility \(u_g(q)=10-|g-q|\) for policy level \(q\). The society is rational masses \(\mu_g\geq0\), with \(\sum_g\mu_g=1\). Three candidates’ policy levels \(q_1<q_2<q_3\) are part of the instance; the paper’s annex gives \(2,5,8\), while its main text gives \(3,5,8\). Specify tie-breaking as part of the instance. Each type votes for its most-preferred available candidate. If no candidate receives more than half the mass in round one, the two candidates with the most mass advance and the same voting rule determines the runoff winner. The solution is the winner and the exact vote shares in each round. With three candidates and at most eleven types, this is computable by summing the masses type by type; its expected class is tractable.

A plausible regime would be a large local electorate choosing among three proposed levels of a public service, where residents’ ideal levels fall into a small number of shared categories. Millions of residents could then correspond to at most eleven types. This makes the population—not the policy options or outcome space—the continuous object. The authors could reasonably recognize it as a population-scale version of the sincere-voting model they describe. It covers only winner determination under that model, not the experiment’s findings about how often students selected the median candidate, nor its observed errors or strategic votes.

The mirror also suggests follow-up questions: characterize which mass distributions elect the candidate closest to the weighted median, or how much mass must shift to change the winner. But the weakest point is decisive for an anchor-based case: this paper neither poses nor proves a computational problem. The runoff calculation is a natural formalization of its voting setup, not a continuous counterpart to a named computational result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is at the level of what this paper contributes: there is no computational result to mirror. Its findings are observations from classroom elections—such as the median-position candidate winning in 70% of second rounds—not the complexity of a problem or an algorithm for solving one. The proponent is right that there are no qualifying anchors.

The proposed continuous runoff is coherent: with policy levels \(2,5,8\), ideal points \(g\in\{0,\ldots,10\}\), and sincere voting, the winner follows from summing eleven type masses. But that formalization changes the paper’s main question. The paper reports what students did across repeated, randomly reassigned rounds, including errors and strategic votes. The mass model computes a deterministic winner under sincere voting; it does not explain or reproduce those experimental findings.

One could make a more ambitious continuous question about the least mass needed to change the runoff winner, or about strategic persuasion. A large electorate with shared preference categories is a plausible high-multiplicity setting. But those questions require adding an intervention, costs, or a behavioral model that the paper does not study. Expanding the agenda or type space enough to create a broader complexity problem would likewise be a new spatial-voting project, not a continuous version of a result in this article.

So the proposed runoff is a sensible adjacent model, but the case against treating it as a worthwhile mirror is strong: it replaces the paper’s empirical contribution with a tallying problem, while more ambitious computational questions must be supplied from outside the paper. This is not proof that no useful ChoCo project could grow from its voting setup; the negative case is weaker there. It is a case that this paper itself offers no computational result to continue.
