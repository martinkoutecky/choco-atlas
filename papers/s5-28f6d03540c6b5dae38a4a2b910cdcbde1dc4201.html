The case FOR (proponent)

My strongest mirror is Continuous Margin Realizability. An instance is a tournament \(T=(V,E)\) and a rational threshold \(\theta\). Each voter type is a complete ranking \(\pi\) of the options in \(V\); a society is a distribution \(\mu\) over these rankings, with \(\mu_\pi\) the fraction of society holding type \(\pi\). The question is whether there is such a society for which every arc \(u\to v\) of \(T\) is ranked forward by at least a \(\theta\) fraction of the population. Equivalently, maximize \(\min_{u\to v\in E}\sum_{\pi:\,u\prec_\pi v}\mu_\pi\). A solution is a finite list of rankings with rational masses summing to one. For the paper’s five-voter threshold, set \(\theta=3/5\).

The natural regime is a large electorate comparing a fixed slate of options: perhaps millions of participants but relatively few distinct complete ranking types. Paley(43) is best treated as an abstract stress test, not a claim about a literal electorate. For its 43 options, a continuous witness can be represented using at most 904 ranking types: the vector of pairwise support fractions has 903 coordinates, so Carathéodory’s theorem gives that support bound. Thus the model permits a population vastly larger than its number of distinct types, while the five-voter version restricts each mass to a multiple of \(1/5\).

My anchor is Theorem 7.1, proved in this paper. It establishes that \(P=\mathrm{Paley}(43)\) has predictability \(\alpha^{*}(P)=\mathrm{MAS}/C=543/903=181/301>3/5\), yet is not 5-inducible. Consequently, exceeding the \(3/5\) predictability threshold does not guarantee a five-voter realization, and \(N(5)\le 43\) with an explicit witness. In the mirror, the theorem gives a continuous society supporting every arc of \(P\) above \(3/5\), even though no society made from exactly five equally weighted voters does so. The paper proves this using an exact computation of \(\mathrm{MAS}(P)\) and an exhaustive screen of the orders forced by the small slack.

The authors would recognize this as their problem: they define \(\alpha^{*}\) as the fractional distribution-over-rankings version of inducibility, and Theorem 7.1 demonstrates that the fractional and five-voter questions have different answers. I would provisionally expect the general exact problem to be Class B, with difficulty driven by the number of alternatives and their orderings. Its dual pricing step optimizes a weighted count of forward arcs over rankings—the weighted tournament FAS problem, which the paper notes is NP-hard. That is evidence for the expectation, not a hardness proof for \(\mathrm{CMR}_\theta\) itself. Further questions include the complexity of deciding \(\alpha^{*}(T)\ge\theta\) for arbitrary \(T,\theta\), whether exact witnesses can be found efficiently, and which restrictions on tournaments make pricing tractable.

The scope here is just Theorem 7.1’s five-voter threshold gap; it does not extend the paper’s other results. The weak point is also unusually clear: the paper already states and computes this continuous relaxation, including the Paley instance. So this is a strong author-recognizable population mirror, but the worthwhile follow-on is the general complexity landscape, not the introduction of a new model.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

This is a difficult anchor to oppose on modelling grounds. A large electorate ranking 43 options is a coherent high multiplicity regime, and a ranking is a complete voter type. The problem is that the proposed mirror is already the paper’s own continuous model.

The paper defines predictability as
\[ \alpha^{*}(T)=\max_{x\in\Delta(\Pi)}\min_{e\in E(T)} \Pr_{\pi\sim x}[e\text{ is forward in }\pi]. \]
That is exactly the optimization version of Continuous Margin Realizability; asking whether a society supports every arc to threshold \(\theta\) is asking whether \(\alpha^{*}(T)\ge\theta\). The rational distribution \(x\) is already a continuous society over ranking types. The paper formulates this as an LP, computes \(\alpha^{*}\) by column generation with a weighted-MAS separation routine, and gives the exact Paley(43) value. Its new result is the gap between that fractional value and realizability by exactly five equally weighted voters—not the continuous side of the gap.

The best repair is to ask for the complexity of deciding \(\alpha^{*}(T)\ge\theta\) on arbitrary inputs, rather than repeating the Paley(43) calculation. That is a legitimate open computational question, but it broadens a continuous problem the paper already defines and computes; it does not supply a missing continuous mirror of Theorem 7.1. Adding transfer costs or campaigning would produce a different problem, without motivation from this anchor.

So the collision defeats the proposed anchor as a *new* continuization. The negative case is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous work can follow: the paper does not settle the general complexity landscape of its predictability LP, and studying that landscape could be useful. I cannot honestly rule out such a continuation; I can only say it would extend a model already present in the paper, rather than establish a new continuous mirror.
