The case FOR (proponent)

The paper has no numbered computational-complexity result to use as an anchor. Theorem 1 characterizes implementability at a fixed prior; Theorem 2 characterizes robust implementability; Proposition 1 reduces its incentive checks to finitely many beliefs. None classifies a problem as in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise. So there is no qualifying anchor, and I can’t honestly give a per-anchor continuous problem or a trichotomy prediction.

The closest result is Proposition 5, proved in the paper. It says that student-proposing deferred acceptance (SPDA) is ex-post stable and robustly implementable. It also says that any ex-post stable allocation rule that differs from SPDA at a type profile with multiple stable matches fails robustness for some preferences. The paper proves the robustness claim and the converse; SPDA’s ex-post stability follows from the standard deferred-acceptance result. This is a strong result about stable matching and incentives, but it is not a computational result.

There is a plausible follow-on for large school districts: group students by their complete payoff and information-acquisition environment, represent each group’s size as mass, scale school capacities per capita, and ask for a mass assignment that is ex-post stable and robustly implementable when students acquire information about ability. Individual students would still receive one school; assignment fractions would describe the aggregate. The paper’s matching application makes that scenario recognizable, but its formal market is finite, and it neither poses nor analyzes this population-continuum problem. That is the weakest point—and the reason this is a promising extension, not an anchored continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that the paper gives us no computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 characterizes implementation at a fixed prior; Theorem 2 characterizes robust implementation; Proposition 1 reduces incentive checks to finitely many beliefs. None poses a computational problem or analyzes its complexity. Under ChoCo’s scope, that leaves no result in the paper to anchor a complexity mirror.

Proposition 5 is the closest candidate, but its central claim is about which matching rule is robust and stable, not how hard it is to compute an outcome. The proposed school-district scenario is plausible, and scaling capacities with population is a reasonable co-modification. A stronger version could group students by their full preference and payoff environment, give those groups rational masses, and ask for a stable, robust mass assignment. But that asks a new computational question about a continuum matching model. Proposition 5 neither poses that question nor supplies an algorithmic problem whose complexity would carry over.

The type grouping also narrows the application substantially. In the paper, schools’ preferences can depend on the particular student as well as their ability. To aggregate students without losing that information, a type must encode the student’s preferences and their entire profile of treatment across schools. Cloning such a type makes those students indistinguishable to schools; retaining identity-based priority tie-breaks restores the distinctions the aggregation was meant to remove. A type-level model may still be sensible for some cohorts, but its stability notion and matching rule need to be specified for that setting. That is a research programme built from the application, rather than a continuous mirror of Proposition 5.

I would not claim this proves that no continuous matching project could be worthwhile. It does make the negative case strong for this paper: the only proposed mirror is an attractive follow-on, not a mirror of a computational result the authors obtained. The broader claim that no scenario could yield a useful project remains hard to defend; the paper itself gives little basis for that universal conclusion.
