The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t make a positive case that meets the anchor rule. The paper has no numbered result classifying a computational problem as polynomial-time solvable, hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. It studies minimax forecast quality and uses numerical optimization; that is not a complexity result. Its continuity is in the latent state values and forecast probabilities, while the number of experts stays fixed at two.

The nearest results are Theorem 3.2, proved here, which says that for two experts with conditionally independent signals and an unknown binary state space, the log-odds rule with parameter \(0.585\) has worst-case regret at most \(0.025512\); and Proposition 3.3, proved here, which says that with known state space \(\{0,1\}\), the rule with parameter \(0.5168\) has worst-case regret at most \(0.022599\). Theorem 5.1 gives a minimax regret lower bound of \((5\sqrt{5}-11)/8\) when the experts’ marginal forecast distributions are known. These are regret guarantees and bounds, not statements about computational complexity, so I would not use them as anchors.

The most plausible positive direction would be a *new* population version: many forecasters across hospitals or forecasting services, grouped into a small number of types by their complete forecasting setup; \(\mu_t\) would be the share of forecasters of type \(t\); and an aggregator would map the type-labelled distribution of reports to a forecast, judged by worst-case squared regret against an omniscient Bayesian benchmark. That has a sensible high-multiplicity regime, with many forecasters but relatively few types. But the paper’s two-report guarantees do not establish anything about that problem. In the limit, many independent signals could also reveal the latent state and change the benchmark substantially. So this is a plausible extension, not an honest mirror of a named result in the paper, and there is no anchor-backed continuous question to hand to a prover.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper supplies no computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Theorem 3.2 bounds the worst-case regret of a particular log-odds rule; Proposition 3.3 gives another regret bound when the state space is known; Theorem 5.1 lower-bounds minimax regret when marginal forecast distributions are known. Each studies how well an aggregation rule predicts, not the complexity of computing a solution to an encoded problem. The paper’s seven-parameter numerical optimization does not fill that gap: it is not a complexity classification or an algorithm whose running time is analyzed in input parameters.

The proposed many-forecaster version is the strongest possible rescue, but it changes the object being studied. The paper has two distinct experts, each with an unknown information structure, making forecasts about one shared outcome. Replicating experts with the same signal channel creates many independent observations of that outcome; their joint posterior and the omniscient benchmark then depend on the entire population’s reports. That is a new many-expert problem, not a high-multiplicity relaxation of any result here. If types record the full signal channels, supplying the type descriptions risks giving the aggregator information the paper withholds. If types record only the two expert roles, there are still only two types, with no growing multiplicity parameter.

Grouping realized forecast values into types does not solve this: a forecast is a random report generated from an expert’s signal, not a stable description of the expert. And aggregating forecasts across different patients or events would change the target from one shared binary outcome to a collection of outcomes. Either choice defines a different problem.

So none of the three named results survives as an anchor, and the suggested population model is an extension rather than a mirror. That is a strong case against prioritizing this paper for the programme. It does not prove that no worthwhile continuous computational problem could be built from forecast aggregation; the many-expert extension may be worth studying on its own. The negative case is strongest at the paper-to-mirror boundary, and weaker against the universal claim that no scenario could ever produce a worthwhile mirror.
