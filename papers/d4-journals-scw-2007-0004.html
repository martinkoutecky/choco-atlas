The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest nearby result is Theorem 2, proved in this paper: for a strongly connected agenda \(X\), an aggregation rule satisfies universal domain, collective rationality, independence, and unanimity if and only if it is a dictatorship. Corollary 1 applies that result to the preference agenda and derives Arrow’s theorem for strict preferences. These are axiomatic impossibility and characterization results. They say which rules can satisfy a set of conditions; they do not establish a computational complexity result about solving an underlying problem.

There is a plausible population-continuous *axiomatic* question nearby: in a large deliberative body, let each type be a complete, consistent judgment set over a fixed agenda, and let \(\mu_t\) be the share of members with type \(t\). A collective judgment is the output. One could ask whether an aggregation function on these type distributions satisfies continuous versions of the paper’s axioms. That is a genuine continuation of the population, but it introduces a new question, has no objective to optimize, and is not anchored by a computational result in the paper. It also falls outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

So, under the requested anchor criterion, this paper has no qualifying computational result to mirror. Its named results do not support a continuous computational problem that I could honestly hand to a prover as the continuous form of the authors’ problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent is right about a narrow point: Theorem 2 and Corollary 1 are axiomatic results, not complexity theorems. But that rules out a direct computational restatement of those results; it does not establish that no worthwhile continuous computational question can grow from them. The proposed mirror—asking whether an aggregation function on distributions satisfies continuous axioms—is indeed outside ChoCo’s computational scope. There is a more promising step: ask how much a society must change before a concrete aggregation rule produces a consistent collective judgment.

For Theorem 2, take a finite propositional agenda and let each type be a complete, consistent judgment set. A society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over those types. Let \(M(\mu)\) be the propositionwise-majority judgment set, with a specified tie rule, and let \(\gamma(J,J')\) be the cost of shifting mass from type \(J\) to type \(J'\)—for example, the number of judgments changed. Then ask for the minimum-cost mass transfer that makes \(M(\mu')\) consistent. This directly operationalizes the paper’s central conflict between issue-by-issue aggregation and collective rationality. It is a computational optimization problem over a population distribution, with input parameters such as agenda size, support size \(\tau\), and rational bit length \(L\). The theorem’s impossibility result motivates the question; it does not answer its computational version. The paper itself says its results help identify which conditions must be relaxed to make consistent aggregation possible.

Corollary 1 gives a particularly natural specialization. Let types be strict rankings, let the output be the pairwise-majority relation, and charge Kendall distance for moving mass between rankings. Find the least-cost transfer that makes the pairwise-majority relation a transitive order. For a target order \(\sigma\), the requirements that each pair in \(\sigma\) have majority support are linear constraints on the new distribution; optimizing over target orders yields a concrete population-level problem. The possible ranking types number \(m!\), so the algorithmic questions include whether the resulting optimization admits useful separation or pricing methods. In a large electorate, “what fraction of voters, and at what total ranking-change cost, would remove the majority cycle?” is a natural quantity to ask for. It is also exactly the kind of question for which treating voters as named individuals obscures the relevant scale.

These are extensions of the paper’s results, not complexity claims the authors already proved. They require a specified type-change cost and a tractable representation of the agenda, choices a computational study would have to make. But the society is genuinely continuous, and the objective is computational. The case against a *literal* continuous version of Arrow’s theorem is strong; the broader claim that there is no worthwhile continuous mirror does not survive these population-level repair questions.
