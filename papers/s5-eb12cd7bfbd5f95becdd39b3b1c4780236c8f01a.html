The continuous mirror question

Given \(q\) ordered pipeline stations and a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) user types, each with station \(p(t)\), integral bundle utilities \(U_t(k)\), and a convex piecewise-linear per-capita damage function \(D_t\), let each type have positive rational mass \(\mu_t\) with \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\). Choose \(z_{t,k}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_k z_{t,k}=\mu_t\), where \(z_{t,k}\) is the mass of type \(t\) receiving \(k\) units. Set \(F_i=\sum_{t:p(t)\ge i}\sum_k k z_{t,k}\). Find an allocation maximizing \(\sum_{t\in T}\sum_k z_{t,k}U_t(k)-\sum_{t\in T}\mu_tD_t(F_{p(t)})\), and return its allocation, flows, and welfare.

The model it lives in

A fixed pipeline with large cohorts at stations; each complete type records its station, utility schedule, and per-capita damage schedule. The decision variables \(z_{t,k}\) distribute each type’s mass among integral bundles, while \(F_i\) is normalized downstream throughput. With finite bundle ranges and rational piecewise-linear damages, the optimization is an LP.

The objection that survived

The mirror assumes damage is a per-capita burden at each occupied station and scales flow with cohort size; the paper does not motivate that calibration, although the proponent acknowledges it.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a narrow mirror of the paper’s welfare optimization, not of its cooperative-game results. The paper has no named hardness theorem or formal “in P” result. Its closest computational anchor is Theorem 1, proved here in Appendix A.1: for every discrete pipeline problem satisfying the paper’s assumptions on marginal utilities and damages, and for every priority ordering used to break ties, Algorithm 1 returns a welfare-maximizing flow. The algorithm repeatedly gives one unit to an agent with the highest positive net marginal benefit, updates the marginal damages along the flow path, and stops when no positive net benefit remains. That is an exact algorithmic result, but the paper supplies no runtime bound or bit-complexity classification.

My lead mirror is Continuous-Population Pipeline Welfare Maximization. Fix \(q\) ordered pipeline stations and a finite support \(T\) of \(\tau\) complete user types. A type \(t\) specifies its station \(p(t)\), its marginal utility sequence, and its local damage schedule. Its mass \(\mu_t\) is a positive binary rational, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For each type, let \(z_{t,k}\) be the mass assigned an integral bundle of \(k\) units, so \(\sum_k z_{t,k}=\mu_t\). Define \(U_t(k)=\sum_{\ell=1}^k u_{t\ell}\), and let \(D_t\) be the convex piecewise-linear extension of the cumulative marginal damage schedule. The per-capita flow through station \(i\) is
\[ F_i=\sum_{t:p(t)\ge i}\sum_k k z_{t,k}. \]
The problem is to choose \(z\) maximizing
\[ \sum_{t\in T}\sum_k z_{t,k}U_t(k) \;-\; \sum_{t\in T}\mu_tD_t(F_{p(t)}), \]
and return an optimal allocation \(z\), its flows, and its welfare value. The relevant bundle range is finite, as in the paper’s finite-crossing assumption. This normalization treats flow as per-capita throughput and damage as the per-user response to it; multiplying the objective by population size does not change the maximizer.

This is a plausible regime where \(n\) is very large but \(\tau\) is small: for example, many pipeline customers grouped by a modest number of metering districts and demand/damage profiles. Location must be part of the type, since upstream and downstream users face different externalities. The type is otherwise complete: its members share the same utilities and damage schedule. The mass variables permit splitting a type across integral bundles, so type averages can be fractional even though each user receives an integral bundle. If \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), the finite-population version restricts each \(z_{t,k}\) to multiples of \(1/n\); the mirror removes that grid.

The link to Theorem 1 is the underlying group-welfare problem: allocate consumption along the same ordered pipeline while accounting for localized damages. The paper already studies finite agents and discrete bundles; this mirror makes a large cohort at a station explicit rather than treating every user as a separate named agent. It keeps the location-dependent flow externality and the planner’s objective intact. For rational piecewise-linear damage schedules, the mirror is an LP: represent each convex damage function by its affine pieces and use epigraph variables. It should therefore be Class A for this explicit input representation, with running time polynomial in the number of types, bundle levels, damage segments, and input bit length. This is a prediction for the mirror, not a theorem proved by the authors, and it does not claim that their greedy algorithm itself extends.

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s welfare-maximization result only. It does not extend the optimistic and pessimistic games, core allocations, or the Shapley-value result. It also does not establish that discrete hardness dissolves: the paper has no named hardness anchor to transfer.

The natural next questions are whether the LP remains close to the finite high-multiplicity problem when bundle counts must be integer multiples of \(1/n\), and how to compute stable allocations or coalition values for the resulting mass society. Those questions could expose hardness the welfare LP does not.

The soft spot is the population regime. The paper gives one agent per ordered position, whereas this mirror puts many interchangeable users at a station and interprets damages as depending on normalized throughput. That needs a credible pipeline setting and a suitable per-capita damage calibration. Still, station identity preserves the paper’s key asymmetry, and replacing a single user with a cohort that shares that user’s role is a recognizable high-multiplicity version of the same welfare problem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the paper’s one real computational anchor: Algorithm 1 returns a welfare-maximizing flow for the discrete problem. It does not classify the problem’s complexity, and the paper gives no runtime or finite input encoding for its marginal sequences. That weakens the anchor, but does not erase it: an exact optimization algorithm is still a computational result to mirror.

The strongest objection is that position is part of an agent’s type in this model. It determines which flows cause damage to that agent, which users lie downstream, and the path over which consumption creates costs. Keep each position distinct, and a society with many agents still has many types. Combine agents at a position, and the model now needs several choices the paper does not make: whether damage is borne once at a station or by each resident, how pipeline flow and damage scale with population, and whether co-located users share one damage schedule. The proposed objective \(\sum_t \mu_tD_t(F_{p(t)})\) commits to a particular answer: residents share normalized throughput and each bears a per-capita damage cost. That is a coherent new regime, but it takes substantive modeling work to show the authors would regard it as their pipeline problem.

This objection does not defeat the best mirror. A fixed set of stations serving large cohorts with shared demand and damage profiles is plausible, and location can remain part of each complete type. Scaling flow and damages per capita can also be sensible when infrastructure and population grow together. The paper’s model is broad enough that its authors might recognize this as a natural extension. The resulting optimization question is well posed, and its answer being an LP would not make the question unworthy.

So the negative case rests on a real but limited concern: the population axis is not already latent in the paper’s one-agent-per-position model; a mirror must specify a cohort and damage-accounting regime. I cannot honestly turn that into a case that no worthwhile mirror exists in any scenario. The proposed fixed-station cohort model remains a credible survivor.
