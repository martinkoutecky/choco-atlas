The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result of the required kind. Its main result, Theorem 1 (proved here), says that the Slater rule is the only rule satisfying five axioms. Lemmas 1–4, Corollary 1, and Proposition 1 concern axioms and tournament correspondences too; none establishes a complexity classification or algorithm. The introduction points readers to computational studies elsewhere, but does not state their results. So there is no eligible anchor for the requested case.

A plausible *unanchored* mirror would be: given \(m\) alternatives and a finite support of rankings \(R\), each with positive rational mass \(\mu_R\) summing to \(1\), form the pairwise majority tournament \(T_\mu\). Find a linear order minimizing \(\delta(T_\mu,R)\), the Kemeny distance to that tournament. A type is a complete ranking; its mass is its population share; the decision variable is the collective ranking. For example, a very large online electorate could share a small number of ballot templates, with millions of people but only a modest number \(\tau\) of ranking types. The paper defines Slater precisely this way, and its tournamentality condition makes the connection especially direct: the rule depends on the pairwise majority tournament.

That makes the underlying problem a credible high-multiplicity question, but it does not supply the required named computational anchor. Theorem 1 characterizes the rule axiomatically; turning it into a claim about the complexity of finding a Slater ranking would go beyond what that theorem proves. The paper’s scope is therefore its axiomatic characterization, not a computational result whose hardness or tractability can be mirrored.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent supplies no named computational result to anchor. Theorem 1 characterizes the Slater rule through five axioms; the lemmas and corollary support that characterization. None classifies an algorithmic problem. The introduction’s references to computational studies do not change what this paper proves.

The strongest proposed mirror is sensible as a *new* problem: given ranking types and rational masses \(\mu\), compute a Slater ranking from the pairwise-majority tournament \(T_\mu\). But it does not mirror Theorem 1. The theorem’s subject is which correspondence satisfies axioms, while its axioms concern finite profiles and elementary changes. Continuizing those axioms would make the project axiomatic, outside ChoCo’s scope. Asking for computational complexity instead replaces the paper’s result with a different question.

That question also has little population structure for the continuous machinery to act on. Because Slater is tournamental, \(\mu\) matters only through \(T_\mu\): compute the pairwise margins, then solve the Slater ranking problem. This does not make the question invalid, and I would not use the fact that its answer might match the discrete problem as an objection. It does mean the proposed mirror does not expose the kind of mass-transfer, control, or optimization structure that makes the programme’s mirrors compelling.

So the negative case is strong against a *mirror of this paper*: its contribution is axiomatic, not computational. It is weaker against the broader claim that no worthwhile continuous Slater question exists. The weighted-population winner-determination problem is well-posed and could be worth studying on its own; the honest objection is that it is not a computational result from this paper to continuize.
