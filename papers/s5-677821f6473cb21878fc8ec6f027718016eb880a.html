The continuous mirror question

Fix \(r=3\) and \(m\ge4\). Given candidates \(C\), supported types \(t=(A_t,w_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), a unique incumbent winner \(d\), and rational per-capita budgets \(f,b\in[0,1]\), does there exist protected mass \(a_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) with \(\sum_t a_t\le f\) such that no rival can be made strictly higher scoring? For each \(i\ne d\), define \(g_{ti}=w_t(1-\mathbf{1}[i\in A_t]+\mathbf{1}[d\in A_t])\). The condition is that for every \(i\ne d\), \(\mathrm{sc}_{\mu}(i)+\max\{\sum_t g_{ti}y_t: 0\le y_t\le\mu_t-a_t,\ \sum_t y_t\le b\}\le\mathrm{sc}_{\mu}(d)\), where \(y_t\) is the mass bribed from type \(t\) and reballoted to approve \(i\) while excluding \(d\).

The model it lives in

Types are approval sets and voter weights, \(t=(A_t,w_t)\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\). The defender chooses protected mass \(a_t\); the attacker chooses mass \(y_t\) to reballot, at unit per-capita cost, subject to the remaining type masses and attack budget. The question is whether one protected-mass vector of total mass at most \(f\) prevents every rival from exceeding the incumbent after any attack of mass at most \(b\).

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the destructive weighted-protection clause of Theorem 7 for \(r=3\); it leaves the symmetric-\(\$\) clause, constructive protection, and other parameter settings aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is the continuous decision problem solvable in time polynomial in \(m\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\)?
  • Can the defender’s simultaneous constraints across rivals be given an exact tractable formulation?
  • How large can the gap be between the discrete and continuous problems on rational-clone instances?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Theorem 7: for \(r\)-approval, destructive weighted-protection and destructive symmetric-\(\$\)-protection are NP-complete. The paper proves this theorem here. I focus on its first clause: with arbitrarily many candidates, the destructive weighted-protection problem is NP-complete. Voters have integer weights but unit awarding and bribing prices; the attacker tries to make a rival score strictly higher than the incumbent. The paper proves hardness by reducing from bounded-occurrence 3-dimensional matching, and establishes membership using its minmax-vector characterization. This is the strongest anchor for my case because it is a hardness result about protection itself, with candidate comparisons central to the construction.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large federation or cooperative election. Many members may share the same approved slate of three candidates and the same voting-weight class. A type is therefore \(t=(A_t,w_t)\), where \(A_t\) is the three-candidate approval set and \(w_t\) is the voter’s weight. The society is a rational distribution \(\mu\) over its \(\tau\) supported types. For example, millions of members might fall into hundreds or thousands of ballot-and-weight types. Unit protection and bribery costs become costs per unit of population mass, with defense and attack budgets \(f\) and \(b\) expressed as fractions of the population.

Here is the continuous problem I would hand to a prover. Fix \(r=3\), let \(m\ge4\), and let \(d\) be the incumbent’s unique winner. The input is \(C\), the supported types \(t=(A_t,w_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and rational budgets \(f,b\in[0,1]\). Scores are \(\mathrm{sc}_{\mu}(c)=\sum_t\mu_t w_t\mathbf 1[c\in A_t]\). The defender chooses protected masses \(a_t\in[0,\mu_t]\), with \(\sum_t a_t\le f\). The attacker may take masses \(x_{t,B}\ge0\) from unprotected types and change their approval sets to \(B\in\binom C3\), subject to \(\sum_B x_{t,B}\le\mu_t-a_t\) for each type and \(\sum_{t,B}x_{t,B}\le b\). The decision question is whether there exists a protected-mass vector \(a\) for which no allowed attack makes any rival score strictly exceed \(d\). Equivalently, the objective is to minimize \(\sum_t a_t\) subject to that robustness condition, and the decision version asks whether the minimum is at most \(f\).

For a fixed rival \(i\), the attacker can reballot mass from type \(t\) to approve \(i\) and exclude \(d\). The resulting maximum increase in \(i\)’s score over \(d\) is the fractional-knapsack value with item value \(w_t(1-\mathbf 1[i\in A_t]+\mathbf 1[d\in A_t])\), available mass \(\mu_t-a_t\), and total mass budget \(b\). Thus a protected vector is valid exactly when, for every \(i\ne d\), that maximum is at most \(\mathrm{sc}_{\mu}(d)-\mathrm{sc}_{\mu}(i)\). This gives a precise continuous version of the paper’s underlying protection problem, rather than a question about its encoding.

I expect this mirror to be Class C, hard for continuum-specific reasons, though that is a conjecture. Each rival’s attack optimization is a fractional knapsack problem, but the defender must choose one residual-mass vector that controls all rivals at once. That outer interdiction choice may retain substantial combinatorial difficulty. The paper’s 3DM reduction does not establish this: fractional mass can split a triple type, so discrete matching hardness may disappear. Whether the joint protection constraints have a compact polynomial formulation, or remain hard despite that fractionalization, is an open question.

The mirror covers Theorem 7’s destructive weighted-protection result at fixed \(r=3\); it does not claim to mirror the paper’s constructive results, arbitrary-price variants, or the symmetric-\(\$\) clause. Its weakest point is the change in intervention granularity: the source selects whole voters, while this mirror lets the defender and attacker split a type’s mass. That change is consistent with a continuous population and preserves the two-stage protection problem, but it is substantive. A proof of hardness would need a new reduction that survives divisibility; an algorithmic result would need to handle the defender’s simultaneous constraints across rivals.

The next questions are whether this decision problem is polynomial-time solvable in \(m,\tau,L\), whether the attacker’s fractional-knapsack structure yields a useful exact formulation for the defender, and how large the gap can be between the discrete and mass versions on rational-clone instances.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

I cannot make a defensible case that this anchor has no worthwhile continuous mirror. The proponent chose a strong fit: the paper studies anonymous score changes, and many voters can plausibly share the same ballot and weight. In a large federation, for example, protecting a fraction of members with a given ballot is a recognizable version of protecting voters. The move from individuals to masses is natural here.

The most useful objection is narrower. For a fixed protected-mass vector \(a\) and rival \(i\), the attacker’s best move is a fractional knapsack over the remaining masses \(\mu_t-a_t\), with gain
\[ g_{ti}=w_t\bigl(1-\mathbf{1}[i\in A_t]+\mathbf{1}[d\in A_t]\bigr). \]
That attack is easy to evaluate. But the defender needs one vector \(a\) that makes every rival safe, and the attack capacities depend on \(a\). The candidate-wise score inequalities therefore do not immediately give the linear program suggested by ordinary bribery mirrors; the natural formulation is a continuous interdiction problem. This weakens any claim that the mirror is an obvious optimization exercise, but it does not show the question is uninteresting. Whether the defender’s problem is tractable or hard after fractionalization is itself a meaningful computational question.

A stronger mirror can include each type’s award price and bribery price, as well as its ballot and weight. That preserves the paper’s protection setting while making costs part of the complete type description. The paper’s 3DM reduction does not settle this version: its indivisible matching choices may disappear when mass can split. That leaves room for a genuine result about what continuization changes.

So the negative case is weak. The proponent’s mirror is well posed, has a credible high-multiplicity scenario, and is not shown to be already answered by continuous computational work. Its main uncertainty—whether fractional protection makes the problem easier—is a possible result of the research, not a reason to reject the question.
