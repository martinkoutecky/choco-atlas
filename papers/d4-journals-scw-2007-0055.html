The case FOR (proponent)

The paper contains no named computational result. Its numbered Propositions 1–5 establish implications and contradictions among social-choice axioms; none says that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, FPT, or otherwise classifies its complexity. The paper’s main conclusion is an axiomatic impossibility: with four alternatives and an even number of individuals, no social welfare function satisfies Pareto, anonymity, full domain, transitivity, and ISA.

The closest continuous-population analogue would take the 24 rankings of four alternatives as types and a society as a distribution \(\mu\) over those types. One could ask whether a rule \(F\), defined on every such distribution and returning a transitive social ranking, can satisfy continuous versions of Pareto, anonymity, and ISA. That is a coherent question about a continuum of agents, perhaps representing a large electorate with relatively few ballot types. But it is an axiomatic existence question, not a computational one; the paper supplies no algorithmic problem or complexity result to anchor it. Its finite-electorate impossibility also does not by itself settle the question for arbitrary real-valued distributions.

So I can’t offer a qualifying continuous computational problem or classify its expected complexity without inventing a new problem beyond the paper’s results. The strongest honest positive observation is that its electorate can naturally be represented by masses over a fixed set of voter types. The decisive weakness is that the paper studies axioms for aggregation, not the computational questions ChoCo is meant to continuize.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong here: the paper has no computational problem or complexity result to continuize. Its propositions establish an axiomatic impossibility: no social welfare function satisfies Pareto, anonymity, full domain, transitivity, and ISA under the stated conditions. The proofs derive contradictions between requirements on a rule; they do not give an algorithmic task on an election instance.

The proponent’s distribution over the \(24\) rankings is a sensible representation of an anonymous electorate. But it does not supply the missing computational object. The paper studies a rule defined across the whole profile domain; its relevance map describes which parts of a profile determine pairwise social rankings. The direct continuous analogue would ask whether a rule on the distribution simplex can satisfy continuous versions of those axioms. That remains an axiomatic existence question, outside ChoCo’s computational scope.

A stronger attempted rescue would encode a rule and ask an algorithm to verify its axioms, find a violating distribution, or search for a rule in some restricted class. Those could be worthwhile new problems, but the paper specifies neither such a task nor a representation of rules. Choosing one would add the computational problem—and likely determine much of its complexity—rather than mirror a result in the paper.

The case is not airtight against every adjacent project: the natural mass representation could inspire computational questions. But that is the only foothold for the positive case, and it establishes a plausible population model, not a computational mirror of this paper.
