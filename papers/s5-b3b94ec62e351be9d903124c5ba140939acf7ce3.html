The continuous mirror question

Given finite applicant and post type sets with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compatible type pairs, strict rankings for applicant types, and either strict rankings or one all-neighbors tie for each post type, does there exist a measurable one-to-one partial matching \(M\) of the atomless type populations such that, for every feasible alternative \(M'\), the measure of agents who prefer \(M\) to \(M'\) is at least the measure who prefer \(M'\) to \(M\)? Each agent remains matched to at most one partner, and unmatched is worse than any acceptable match.

The model it lives in

Use atomless cohorts of mass \(\mu_t\) for each complete applicant or post type. A matching pairs individual points across compatible cohorts, leaving some unmatched; popularity compares the measure of votes between matchings on the same population, so identity-level couplings must be retained when comparing outcomes.

The objection that survived

A finite type-mass table alone does not capture how the same agents fare under two matchings, so an algorithmic treatment needs a finite representation of those identity couplings; the measurable model defines them, but the case does not give that representation.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirrors cover Theorem 2's mixed-post hardness question and Theorem 1's all-tied-post algorithmic question. They leave aside applicant-side ties and the many-to-one capacity variant.

Open questions for a prover

  • Can the identity couplings between two measurable matchings be represented by a finite type-level formulation that preserves the vote comparison?
  • Does allowing mass to split among partner types destroy the discrete hardness gadgets, or can the continuous mixed-post existence problem still be shown NP-hard?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is to keep the paper’s two-sided notion of popularity and make the *agents* a measure. The paper’s matching edges remain indivisible at the individual level: each agent is matched to at most one partner. What becomes fractional is the amount of each type assigned to each other type.

For a concrete regime, consider a large regional hiring market. Applicants are members of standardized cohorts with the same strict ranking of job categories; vacancies are replicated slots in job families with the same eligibility and preference rules. Some job families rank applicant profiles, while others regard all eligible profiles as equally suitable but prefer being filled to remaining vacant. Let \(\tau\) be the number of distinct applicant and vacancy types, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the population. This fits the paper’s applicants-and-tasks interpretation, and both sides still vote. For the mixed case, both kinds of vacancy need substantial representation, as the paper’s hardness reduction uses linearly many posts of each kind.

Here is a shared model for the mirrors. Give each type \(t\) a rational mass \(\mu_t\), with total mass normalized to \(1\). Each type is a complete description of an agent’s side, acceptable partners, and preferences. Represent the agents of type \(t\) by an atomless interval of measure \(\mu_t\). A matching is a measurable, one-to-one partial pairing of agents across compatible types. Every agent point is either unmatched or has one partner; the mass assigned between two types can be fractional. For two matchings \(M,M'\), count votes by measure: an agent votes for the matching that gives them a better partner, or a partner rather than being unmatched, and abstains if the outcomes are equally ranked. A matching \(M\) is popular when, for every feasible \(M'\), the mass preferring \(M\) is at least the mass preferring \(M'\).

My lead is Mixed-Post Continuous Popular Matching. Its input is finite sets of applicant and post types, rational masses, compatible type pairs, strict rankings of neighboring post types for each applicant type, and, for each post type, either a strict ranking of neighboring applicant types or one tie containing all of them. The question is whether a popular measurable matching exists; a solution is such a matching, with popularity tested against every feasible alternative by the mass-vote definition above.

This mirrors Theorem 2: the paper proves that deciding whether a popular matching exists is NP-complete when applicants have strict preferences and each post either has a strict list or puts all its neighbors in a single tie. The paper’s reduction proves the hardness; membership uses the cited polynomial-time test for a proposed matching. The result holds even when applicant lists have length \(2\) or \(4\), and post lists are either strict lists of length \(2\) or single ties of length \(2\) or \(3\); the reduction requires linearly many strict-list posts and linearly many tied posts. These restrictions give the continuous question a precise anchor rather than a loosened variant of the paper’s problem.

I would initially expect this mirror to be Class B: hardness transfers, because the reduction’s combinatorics live in the compatibility and preference structure encoding the formula, not in a large voter count. But that is a research expectation, not a consequence of Theorem 2. A proof must show that allowing cohorts to split into arbitrary masses does not destroy the gadgets. The key follow-up is whether there is an embedding or a different reduction that survives this divisibility.

A second, narrower mirror is All-Tied-Post Continuous Popular Matching: use the same input and solution definition, but require every post type to tie all its neighboring applicant types. Ask whether a popular mass matching exists and, if so, to produce one. This mirrors Theorem 1, proved in the paper: with strict applicant preferences and every post tying all its neighbors, the discrete problem can be solved in \(O(n^2)\) time, where \(n=|A\cup B|\). I expect the continuous version to be Class A, perhaps through a type-level flow or matching formulation. The paper’s algorithm alone does not give a runtime polynomial in \(\tau\) and the mass bit length. A natural next question is whether its structure yields such an algorithm without expanding rational masses into individual agents.

These mirrors cover only the paper’s one-to-one, two-sided popular-matching existence and computation results, under their respective preference restrictions. They do not continuize the matching outcome: each person still receives at most one partner. The fractional quantity is the mass of people of a type assigned across types, as required by the high-multiplicity model.

My weakest point is the effect of anonymity and divisibility on popularity. The paper’s hardness gadgets use named graph vertices and may rely on whole-vertex assignments; a mass matching can split a type across several partner types. The proposed model retains the paper’s two-sided voting rule and quantifies over all alternative matchings, so the question remains recognizably about their problem. Still, the reduction does not carry over automatically, and the all-tied algorithmic mirror does not settle that issue.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The best case against is that the proposed type-to-type mass table is not enough to represent the paper’s notion of popularity. Popularity compares the *same people* across two matchings. For example, one applicant type can have equal mass assigned to three post types in both matchings, yet a cyclic reassignment can give two thirds of that type a better post and one third a worse one. If those posts are indifferent among applicants and all remain filled, the post side abstains and the reassignment wins. An identity preserving alternative with the same type-level masses produces no such vote margin. So the matching matrix alone does not determine the comparison; a faithful model must retain the joint old and new assignments of agents.

That weakens the proposed mirror of Theorem 1. Its \(O(n^2)\) algorithm relies on discrete matching structure, including alternating paths and the parity classes in the Dulmage–Mendelsohn decomposition. A flow or LP over type-level assignment masses does not inherit that proof, and its expected polynomial running time in the number of types and the mass bit length is unestablished. But this is a gap in the proposed algorithmic story, not a reason the continuous question is ill-posed. One can define popularity over full measurable matchings, and the paper’s repeated applicant and task interpretation makes such a population regime plausible.

The case against Theorem 2 is weaker still. The reduction’s graph vertices encode distinct formula roles. Giving those roles distinct types preserves the type count at roughly the size of the reduction, while allowing each type’s mass to split can destroy the reduction’s all-or-nothing gadget behavior. Thus the discrete NP-completeness result does not establish hardness of the continuous mirror. But that failure is precisely an open modeling and complexity question; it does not make the question unworthy. In a more natural version, repeated applicant cohorts and replicated vacancies share profiles, with masses assigned across compatible types. Mixed ranked and all-tied vacancy types are plausible in that setting, and the resulting model still asks whether a popular matching exists under the paper’s two-sided voting rule.

I cannot honestly make the universal negative case the prompt calls for. The identity-coupling issue defeats the proponent’s simple mass-table formulation, and neither theorem transfers automatically. Yet the faithful measurable-matching formulation remains recognizable, and the paper itself supplies a credible high-multiplicity scenario. Theorem 1 may turn out to have a routine flow-based answer, and Theorem 2’s gadgets may fail under splitting; neither possibility is a sound objection at this stage. The strongest honest conclusion is that the proponent has not yet supplied the right finite representation or an algorithmic result—not that there is no worthwhile continuous mirror.
