The continuous mirror question

Given finitely many types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\), \(\sum_{t\in T}\mu_t=1\), status-quo efforts \(a_t\in[0,1]\), and explicitly encoded rational concave piecewise-linear utilities \(u_t(e,Q)\) that strictly increase in \(Q\), let \(Q^0=\sum_t\mu_ta_t\). Does there exist a nonempty mass selection \(\lambda_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) and efforts \(b_t\in[0,1]\) such that \(Q'=\sum_t\lambda_tb_t\) and \(u_t(b_t,Q')>u_t(a_t,Q^0)\) for every \(t\) with \(\lambda_t>0\)? Decide whether such a deviation exists, equivalently whether the status quo is outside the core.

The model it lives in

A continuum population represented by type masses \(\mu_t\), with type-level efforts as decision variables and per-capita public-good quantity \(Q=\sum_t\mu_tx_t\). Each type’s utility is \(\tilde u_t(x)=u_t(x_t,Q)\); strict positive externalities and concavity make the core test equivalent to the paper’s finite-coordinate model with \(\tau=|T|\).

The objection that survived

The theorem gives a real-RAM bound in terms of convex-optimization calls, so polynomial bit complexity for the stated rational piecewise-linear utility input must be established separately.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 18’s core-membership algorithm with the number of types \(\tau\) replacing the number of named agents; it leaves aside the topological characterization, the Lindahl-equilibrium result, and the approximate-testing discussion.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest anchor is Theorem 18, proved in the paper: given an outcome in an \(n\)-agent public goods economy, a real-RAM algorithm decides whether it is in the core using \(O(n)\) convex optimization problems and \(O(n)\) additional computation. The proceedings version numbers it Theorem 18; the supplied rendering drops the number. This is conditional tractability: the convex optimization problems are the bottleneck, and the paper does not claim an unconditional polynomial-time algorithm for arbitrary utility oracles. The cited hardness results in the introduction concern other game representations; the paper gives no comparable in-paper hardness theorem for this membership problem. ([Bodwin, Theorem 18](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/opus/volltexte/2017/7491/pdf/LIPIcs-ICALP-2017-45.pdf))

A concrete mirror is Finite-Type Atomless Core Membership. Let \(T\) be a finite set of complete agent types, with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\). A type includes its preferences, contribution cost, and its status-quo contribution \(a_t\in[0,1]\). Each type-\(t\) agent has a concave utility \(u_t(e,Q)\), where \(e\) is that agent’s effort and \(Q\) is the per-capita quantity of the public good; holding \(e\) fixed, utility strictly increases with \(Q\). For a finite encoding, take each \(u_t\) to be an explicitly given rational concave piecewise-linear function.

The status quo has \(Q^0=\sum_t\mu_ta_t\), so a type-\(t\) agent currently gets \(u_t(a_t,Q^0)\). A proposed deviation chooses a mass \(\lambda_t\in[0,\mu_t]\) of each type and an effort \(b_t\in[0,1]\) for those members; everyone outside the coalition contributes zero. The resulting public good is \(Q'=\sum_t\lambda_tb_t\). The question is whether there is a nonempty choice of masses and efforts such that \(u_t(b_t,Q')>u_t(a_t,Q^0)\) for every type with \(\lambda_t>0\). Such a tuple \((\lambda,b)\) is a solution certificate; if none exists, the status quo is in the core. Requiring one effort per participating type loses no deviations in this model: by concavity, averaging the efforts of strictly better-off members of a type preserves their total contribution and gives each of them at least their average utility.

This is a population mirror in the programme’s sense. A large city or public-service district might have millions of residents but comparatively few types defined by their full preference-and-cost profile. Mass records the share of residents of each type, and a coalition can include a fraction of that share. The public-good quantity is normalized per capita, a natural scale adjustment as population grows. The problem remains the authors’ core-membership question—whether any coalition can profitably withdraw and act together—not a question about fractional outcomes or lotteries.

I would conjecture Class A, with limited confidence: the theorem’s concavity and positive-externality structure remains, and the type support \(\tau=|T|\) is the natural finite dimension for a high-multiplicity algorithm. The hoped-for analogue would use a mass-peeling argument and run in time polynomial in \(\tau\) and the input length \(L\), perhaps with convex-optimization calls. This is a research target, not a consequence of Theorem 18. In particular, the share-effort terms \(\lambda_tb_t\) complicate the feasible deviation region, so a prover would need a new structural argument.

The weakest point is the algorithm’s peeling step. Theorem 18 removes one individual at a time and terminates after \(n\) rounds. In the mirror, a type can supply any fraction of its mass, so neither deleting one person nor simply deleting a whole type captures the available deviations. That leaves a real possibility of continuum-specific hardness (Class C); discrete hardness does not automatically transfer because fractional type masses change the coalition choices. The mirror still looks worth asking: its population regime and decision question are faithful, even if the answer turns out to mark a boundary of the original algorithm.

The proposed scope is Theorem 18’s core-membership decision problem. The paper’s path-connectedness characterization motivates the algorithm but is not a second complexity anchor, and this mirror makes no claim that its topological proof itself extends to atomless populations. The next questions are whether discrete high-multiplicity instances converge to this model under suitable utility margins, and whether a mass-peeling theorem can replace individual deletion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only anchor the proponent raises is Theorem 18’s core-membership algorithm. The strongest objection is that its parameter \(n\) counts named action coordinates: the input is an arbitrary utility oracle on \([0,1]^n\), and the algorithm repeatedly removes one coordinate. The theorem gives a bound on convex-optimization calls, not bit complexity for an encoded economy. It supplies no fixed type space or rule for how utilities and interactions should scale as the population grows.

That matters for the proposed mirror. A finite-type model with utilities \(u_t(e,Q)\) assumes agents of a type are interchangeable and that others’ contributions affect them through one aggregate \(Q\). The paper’s arbitrary utility function need not have either property. Preserving its full generality would require types to encode dependence on named agents; imposing finite types and aggregate utility instead defines a narrower public-goods model, and explicit piecewise-linear utilities introduce an encoding question the theorem does not answer.

But this objection does not defeat the better mirror. A large district with finitely many preference-and-cost types is a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and core membership remains the same decision question. Even fractional participation is not a fundamental obstacle here: if some members of a type strictly benefit from a deviation, the rest can join using the same effort; their contributions increase \(Q\), which helps under positive externalities. So the proposed mirror is coherent, and the paper’s coordinate-by-coordinate algorithm may transfer to type-level coordinates.

I can’t make the negative case strong without turning “the mirror may be easy” into an objection about its answer, which the brief rules out. The honest negative is that Theorem 18 does not itself define or analyze the population scaling the mirror needs. That leaves a real modeling and encoding gap, but the proposed finite-type public-goods scenario is strong enough that this objection probably does not defeat it.
