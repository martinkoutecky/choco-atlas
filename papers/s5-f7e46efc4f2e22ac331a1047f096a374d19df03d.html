The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case for a continuous mirror is an empirical one. In a large participatory-budgeting population, many residents could share the same complete preference profile—such as district, urban-category preferences, and cost priorities—so the population could be represented by masses \(\mu_t\) over \(\tau\) types, with \(\tau\) much smaller than the number of residents. For 5-Approval, let \(q_t(B)\) be the fraction of type \(t\) choosing each legal five-project ballot \(B\). The project’s vote share is then
\[ S_j=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{B:\,j\in B}q_t(B). \]
This would give the authors a natural way to study population-level rankings and alignment as the population grows.

But the supplied paper contains no named computational result: no numbered Theorem, Lemma, Corollary, or Proposition asserting a complexity or algorithmic claim. Its findings—such as persona prompts increasing vote similarity, or temperature changing diversity—are empirical results, not qualifying anchors. The mass model above is therefore a plausible extension of the experiment, not a continuous mirror of a named result in the paper. There are no anchors here to restate or classify as tractable, hardness-transferring, or continuum-specifically hard.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent concedes the decisive point: this paper states no named computational result to mirror. Its persona, temperature, and presentation-order findings are empirical comparisons of LLM and human votes. They do not ask for an algorithm or analyze the complexity of a computational social-choice problem.

The proposed mass formula, \(S_j=\sum_t\mu_t\sum_{B:\,j\in B}q_t(B)\), is a sensible way to describe collective vote shares in a large population. It does not supply the missing computational question. Given the type masses and ballot distributions, it defines how to aggregate votes; it does not formulate an optimization, control, bribery, or robustness problem whose complexity the paper investigates. Treating the agents as types can make this a useful empirical model, but it does not turn the paper’s findings into a ChoCo mirror.

A stronger proposal might optimize persona assignment or prompt interventions to make the winning projects better align with human preferences across a population. That could be a worthwhile new computational-social-choice question. But the paper does not formulate that task: it measures how prompts affect votes, rather than choosing interventions under a computational objective. Adding such an objective would create a new project, not continue a result in this one.

The negative case is therefore strongest on scope, not on whether a continuous population is sensible. The proposed population model is plausible and could support valuable empirical work. What it cannot rescue is the absence of a computational result in the paper for ChoCo to mirror.
