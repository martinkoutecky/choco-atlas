The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest case is narrow: the paper’s Theorem 2 gives a named approximation lower bound for prediction-based facility mechanisms, and its hard instance already has only four location types. That makes a high-multiplicity population mirror plausible.

Theorem 2 is proved in this paper. It says that for the two-facility game on a line, any deterministic strategy-proof mechanism with bounded robustness has consistency \(\Omega(n)\): even when its location prediction is accurate, its approximation ratio cannot be sublinear in the number of agents. Here “nice” means deterministic, strategy-proof, and bounded-approximation; robustness bounds performance for arbitrary prediction error, while consistency bounds it when the prediction is correct. The proof uses Theorem 1, proved in this paper, and a characterization result cited as Lemma 1 from Fotakis and Tzamos [10]. This is an approximation guarantee lower bound, rather than a P-versus-NP result.

Consider a city arranging two public service facilities along a corridor. There are \(N\) residents, but many live at the same few locations. A location is a complete type: residents there have the same distance cost for every pair of facility locations. The city receives a public forecast of the location distribution, residents report their locations, and a deterministic mechanism chooses the two facilities. This is a recognizable version of the authors’ setting: residents still care about distance to the nearest facility, and the mechanism still faces strategic reports.

Here is a precise mirror, the Mass-Profile Prediction Consistency Frontier. For fixed \(N\), let an admissible society be a finite-support measure
\[ \mu=\sum_{t\in T}\frac{k_t}{N}\delta_t, \qquad k_t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},\quad \sum_{t\in T}k_t=N, \]
where \(t\in\mathbb{R}\) is a location and \(\tau=|T|\) is the number of occupied location types. The public prediction \(\hat\mu\) and the reported society \(\rho\) are measures of the same form. A mechanism \(F_N(\hat\mu,\rho)\) returns an unordered pair of facility locations. Its per-capita social cost is
\[ \overline{\mathrm{SC}}_\mu(\ell_1,\ell_2) = \int \min\{|t-\ell_1|,|t-\ell_2|\}\,d\mu(t), \]
and \(\overline{\mathrm{OPT}}(\mu)\) is the minimum such cost over all facility pairs.

The mass granularity \(\eta=1/N\) preserves the paper’s individual incentive constraint. If \(\mu\) includes a resident of type \(t\), strategy-proofness requires, for every prediction \(\hat\mu\) and every possible misreport \(y\),
\[ \min_j|t-\ell_j| \le \min_j|t-\ell'_j|, \quad (\ell_1,\ell_2)=F_N(\hat\mu,\mu),\quad (\ell'_1,\ell'_2)=F_N(\hat\mu,\mu-\eta\delta_t+\eta\delta_y). \]
Define the frontier \(\Gamma_N(\Lambda)\) as the least worst-case exact-prediction approximation ratio achievable by such a strategy-proof mechanism whose robustness is at most a fixed constant \(\Lambda\). Equivalently, a threshold instance asks whether a mechanism exists with robustness at most \(\Lambda\) and consistency at most a given \(\gamma\); a solution is the mechanism and proofs of those guarantees and its incentive constraints.

The paper’s witness shows why this is a compelling high-multiplicity regime. For even \(N\), use
\[ \mu_{N,\varepsilon} = \frac1N\delta_0+ \left(\frac12-\frac1N\right)\delta_\varepsilon+ \left(\frac12-\frac1N\right)\delta_{1-\varepsilon} +\frac1N\delta_1. \]
It has only four types, while \(N\) can grow. The optimal facilities are at \(\varepsilon\) and \(1-\varepsilon\). Theorem 1’s structural constraint forces a nice mechanism’s facilities either to coincide or to include one at or beyond an extreme location. Theorem 2’s cost comparison then gives an approximation ratio of at least \(N/4\), even with the exact prediction; the odd-\(N\) construction yields the same asymptotic lower bound. Thus the predicted consistency barrier holds with \(\tau=4\), in a society where \(N\) can be much larger than the number of types. The same lower bound applies to mechanisms operating on these histograms: they lift to anonymous mechanisms on labeled profiles, and Theorem 2 applies to that broader mechanism class.

For this mass-grid problem, I expect the approximation barrier to transfer: each admissible histogram is a finite profile expressed as type masses. In the programme’s trichotomy this is closest to Class B, with the qualification that the transferred result is an approximation lower bound, not a computational hardness classification. The paper gives no named P, NP-hardness, or parameterized-complexity result to anchor a stronger claim. This mirror covers Theorem 2’s prediction-consistency result; it does not claim to mirror the paper’s broader mechanism characterization.

The weakest point is also exposed by the witness. The two extreme types each have mass \(1/N\). As \(N\) grows, they disappear in the weak limit, leaving \(\tfrac12\delta_\varepsilon+\tfrac12\delta_{1-\varepsilon}\). The theorem’s incentive obstruction depends on those rare residents, so it does not establish the same bound for a strictly atomless population. The mirror retains the individual report quantum precisely to preserve the authors’ strategy-proofness question. Removing that quantum risks making individual incentives vacuous; replacing them with positive-mass coalition deviations would define a different problem.

Useful follow-up questions are whether the exact frontier \(\Gamma_N(\Lambda)\) can be determined beyond its \(\Omega(N)\) lower bound, and whether a nonvacuous incentive notion for genuinely atomless populations can recover any part of Theorem 2’s conclusion.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The only plausible anchor is Theorem 2. It gives a lower bound on the approximation guarantee of strategy-proof mechanisms with prediction, but it does not analyze the complexity of computing a mechanism’s output. Theorem 1 characterizes mechanisms; Theorems 3–9 establish strategy-proofness or approximation guarantees for proposed mechanisms. Those are characterization and distortion results, not computational-complexity results of the kind ChoCo is charting. A new computational problem could be built around mechanism design here, but it would not be a continuous mirror of a computational result in this paper.

Theorem 2’s mirror also faces a basic choice. Keep a one-person reporting quantum \(1/N\), as the proposed \(\Gamma_N\) does, and the model is a finite \(N\)-agent game written as a histogram. That can be a useful high-multiplicity encoding, and the four-location witness makes the repeated-location scenario plausible. But the quantum remains essential: the bound relies on endpoint residents of mass \(1/N\). Taking a genuine nonatomic population limit removes unilateral influence. A single resident’s report leaves the population measure unchanged, so strategy-proofness imposes no constraint; a mechanism can ignore the prediction and choose optimal facilities for the reported aggregate. The prediction-consistency obstruction then vanishes.

One could preserve strategic force by letting a positive mass move together, but that is a coalition or cohort incentive condition, not the individual strategy-proofness studied in the paper. Alternatively, one can keep the \(1/N\) quantum and study the finite games across values of \(N\); that preserves the theorem, but it does not produce a nondegenerate continuous-population incentive model. These are different models, not repairs to the mirror’s limiting issue.

The proposed frontier \(\Gamma_N(\Lambda)\) is also a global search over mechanisms and their guarantees, rather than a computational task on a given society. The paper gives no complexity question for that synthesis problem. This is the strongest negative case: the witness does support a sensible high-multiplicity regime, so the objection is not that repeated locations are implausible or that the lower bound transfers. It is that the anchor is a mechanism-performance impossibility, and its individual incentive condition either stays finite or loses force in the continuous population limit.
