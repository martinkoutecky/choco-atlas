What the mirror covers

The proposed idea addresses Theorem 1; the anonymity results and Theorem 2's domain-maximality result are also axiomatic and are not covered by a computational mirror.

The case FOR (proponent)

The closest positive hook is Theorem 1, proved in this paper: for preferences over subsets of objects, no voting rule satisfies ontoness, tops-onliness, false-name-proofness, participation, and object neutrality together.

A possible continuous analogue would fit a large online election where many participants share a relatively small set of complete rankings over bundles. A type would be one such ranking, and \(\mu_t\) the fraction of participants holding it. The decision rule would be a functional \(F\) mapping each finite-support rational distribution \(\mu\) to an outcome \(F(\mu)\subseteq\mathcal O\). One could ask whether a functional exists that is onto, tops-only, and object-neutral, and that respects false-name and participation constraints when positive mass of a type is added or removed.

But this is not a valid ChoCo anchor under the requested standard. Theorem 1 is an axiomatic impossibility result, not a result about computational complexity; neither it nor the paper’s other numbered results establishes hardness, tractability, or another complexity classification. And positive-mass entry or withdrawal changes the paper’s individual-voter axioms into group-mass conditions. If individuals instead have zero mass, their entry or withdrawal may leave the distribution unchanged. Either way, the analogue needs new axiom choices and asks an axiomatic question, which the programme places out of scope.

So the paper contains no named computational result to anchor a ChoCo mirror. Theorem 1 is the strongest conceptual hook, but I would not present the continuous existence question as a qualifying mirror of this paper’s results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is the proponent’s only anchor, and it does not survive as a ChoCo mirror. It proves that no voting rule satisfies five axioms on the stated domain; it does not pose a computational problem with an input, an algorithmic task, or a complexity measure. Replacing finite profiles with distributions over rankings still leaves an existence question about axioms. The same is true of the other numbered results: they establish invariance, anonymity, impossibility, or domain maximality, not computational complexity.

The proposed functional \(F(\mu)\) does not fix that mismatch. Requiring it to satisfy mass versions of false-name-proofness and participation changes the paper’s unilateral, one-voter conditions into conditions about adding or removing positive mass. That can be a sensible question about coordinated group entry or withdrawal, but it is a different axiomatic question. Treating each person as infinitesimal instead makes an individual’s entry or withdrawal invisible to \(\mu\). Neither version supplies a computational result to mirror.

A stronger computational reframing could ask whether a represented rule has a profitable mass deviation, how much mass is needed to change its outcome, or whether a rule can be synthesized to meet mass-based axioms. Those could become research problems, but the paper gives no representation of rules or computational task for them. They would be a new agenda built from its setting, not continuizations of its results. The same objection defeats a proposed mirror of Theorem 2: computing a maximal preference domain would still be a new way to study an axiomatic characterization.

The negative case is therefore strong on ChoCo’s stated scope, but it does not establish that no interesting continuous research could grow out of the paper. A population of many voters sharing rankings is plausible, so lack of a high-multiplicity regime is not the objection. The narrower point is that this paper offers no named computational result for a continuous-population analysis to take up.
