The continuous mirror question

Given finite buyer and seller type sets with rational masses totaling one on each side, rational expected values and costs under posterior \(\pi\), and per-capita externality coefficients, choose feasible match masses \(x_{as}\geq 0\) with \(\sum_s x_{as}\leq\alpha_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{as}\leq\beta_s\). For a rational threshold \(K\), decide whether there is a feasible \(x\) such that \(\sum_{a,s}x_{as}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[u_{as}-v_{as}]-\sum_{a,s,a',s'}x_{as}x_{a's'}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c_{as}^{a's'}]\geq K\).

The model it lives in

Let \(a\) and \(s\) range over complete buyer and seller types, including the information, valuation, cost, and externality data used by the assignment problem. The rational masses \(\alpha_a\) and \(\beta_s\) sum to one on each side; \(x_{as}\) is the mass matched across those types. The objective is expected surplus, with externality coefficients normalized per capita so the interaction term remains meaningful at population scale.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers the efficient allocation objective used within Theorem 1. It leaves the theorem’s incentive guarantee and Theorem 2’s asymptotically small aggregate rewards aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • For variable type support, is this rational quadratic mass-assignment problem polynomial-time solvable or NP-hard?
  • Which restrictions on the externality coefficients make the objective tractable?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a narrow positive lead, but no qualifying computational anchor under your rule. The paper gives no numbered result about an algorithm, running time, or computational hardness. The assignment choice is defined by a quadratic assignment maximization, but the paper treats finding its maximizer as part of the mechanism; it does not analyze the cost of computing it.

The closest named result is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It says that for any assignment problem with interdependent valuations, there are first-stage rewards and second-stage beliefs under which truthful signal reports followed by truthful reports of buyers’ valuation and externality functions form a sequential dominant strategy assessment. The result establishes implementability, not efficient computation of the assignment. Theorem 2, also proved here, says that for a conditionally independent sequence of assignment problems satisfying the paper’s signal-informativeness condition, there is a threshold \(\hat n\) such that every \(n>\hat n\) admits such an assessment; for every fixed \(k\), rewards can be chosen so their aggregate is \(O(n^{-k})\) for every signal profile. That is an asymptotic incentive and payment result, not a complexity result. I would not present either as a computational anchor.

The best *candidate* mirror is the allocation optimization that Theorem 1’s mechanism must perform. Consider many regional network operators matched to channel blocks. Let \(A\) be a finite set of complete operator types and \(S\) a finite set of complete channel-provider types. A type includes everything the problem uses: signal, state-dependent valuation or cost, and the full externality schedule. Let \(\alpha_a\) and \(\beta_s\) be their rational population masses, each side totaling one. Let \(u_{as}(\theta)\) be type \(a\)’s value for type \(s\), \(v_{as}(\theta)\) the public cost, and \(c_{as}^{a's'}(\theta)\) the externality on an \((a,s)\) match from an \((a',s')\) match. Given a rational posterior \(\pi\), choose assignment masses \(x_{as}\geq0\), with row sums at most \(\alpha_a\) and column sums at most \(\beta_s\), to maximize

\[ \sum_{a,s} x_{as}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[u_{as}-v_{as}] \;-\; \sum_{a,s,a',s'} x_{as}x_{a's'}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c_{as}^{a's'}]. \]

Call the decision version Interdependent Continuum Assignment: given these binary-rational data and a threshold \(K\), is there a feasible mass assignment \(x\) whose objective is at least \(K\)? The output is a feasible matching of type mass, not a mechanism or a fractional outcome for an individual. The \(1/n\)-style normalization implicit in the per-capita externality coefficients makes the interaction term well-defined at population scale; that is a co-modification of the model, not the paper’s stated scaling.

The scenario is plausible if many regional units and channel blocks recur across a large market, so the number of agents greatly exceeds the number of complete types. It is less plausible for a market of a few named national firms. My tentative expectation is continuum-specific hardness: divisible flows do not remove the generally nonconcave quadratic objective, but the paper supplies no hardness proof, and divisibility means discrete QAP hardness does not transfer automatically. Useful follow-ups would be to identify tractable structure in the externality tensor and to determine whether this mass-optimization problem has a polynomial algorithm or is NP-hard.

This covers only the computation of the efficient assignment underlying Theorem 1. It does not mirror Theorem 1’s incentive guarantee or Theorem 2’s small-reward result. In a genuinely atomless population, one individual’s report has zero effect on aggregate assignments, so unilateral incentive claims can become vacuous; preserving the paper’s strategic content would require a separate, explicit group-deviation model. The weak point, then, is substantial: the proposed optimization is recognizable as the allocation problem the authors use, but the paper does not report a named computational result about it, and the mechanism’s implementation result is not itself a complexity theorem.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper has no numbered computational result to continuize. Theorem 1 proves that a truthful strategy profile can be supported by rewards and beliefs; Theorem 2 proves that, under its large-market assumptions, the aggregate rewards can become arbitrarily small. Neither says how hard it is to find an assignment, construct the mechanism, or compute its payments. The strongest negative case starts there: a complexity question about the mechanism’s allocation step would be a new analysis of a primitive the paper takes as given, not a mirror of a computational result it establishes.

Theorem 1’s strategic content also changes sharply in a population limit. Its claims concern named buyers who report signals and valuation-externality functions, and receive VCG transfers based on their effect on the assignment. A mass model can preserve aggregate assignment and welfare, but it does not by itself preserve those individual deviations and transfers. Treating a positive-mass type as the strategic player would instead ask about deviations by a whole cohort. That could be an interesting mechanism-design question, but it changes the players and incentive claim.

Theorem 2 is a harder fit. It studies finite populations with conditionally independent private signals and strictly positive rewards whose aggregate tends to zero as \(n\) grows. In the exact population limit, an individual has zero mass, so the aggregate signal distribution does not respond to that person’s report and the rewards vanish. Preserving the result would require specifying how individual truthfulness survives that limit, or changing the deviation unit or reward scaling. Either move asks a new question beyond the theorem’s asymptotic payment result.

The best counterproposal is the type-mass quadratic program for efficient assignment. It is a natural continuum model if operators, providers, and their externalities fall into a fixed set of recurring types. But the paper does not analyze the complexity of its finite quadratic assignment problem, and the proposed mass program is a new complexity target: with a fixed type set it has an explicit finite-dimensional quadratic objective, while modeling arbitrary identity-specific externality schedules can make the type set grow with the population. Normalizing the externalities and imposing type-level symmetry may yield a worthwhile adjacent project, but those choices supply the model and computational question that the paper itself leaves open.

That is the limit of the negative case. I cannot rule out that the type-mass assignment problem is worth studying; the spectrum example makes it plausible. The stronger conclusion is narrower: the paper supplies no computational result for a continuous mirror to answer, and its central incentive results do not pass directly to mass populations without changing what is being asked.
