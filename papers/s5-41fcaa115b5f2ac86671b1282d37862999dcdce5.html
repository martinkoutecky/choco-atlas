The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is limited by a formal problem: the paper contains no qualifying anchor. The best salvageable case is a research question suggested by the paper’s discussion of probabilistic serial, fractional matching, and allocation. I would call it Continuum Probabilistic Assignment. This is not a qualifying anchor, but it is a plausible mirror of the paper’s underlying matching problem.

An instance consists of a finite set \(S\) of schools, a finite set \(T\) of complete preference-and-eligibility types, a rational population distribution \(\mu\in\mathbb{Q}^{T}\), and rational per-capita school capacities \(q_s\) with \(\sum_{s\in S}q_s=1\). Type \(t\) represents mass \(\mu_t\) of students; all information relevant to the mechanism, including priorities or eligibility classes, is part of \(t\).

The decision variable is an aggregate assignment \(x=(x_{t,s})\), where \(x_{t,s}\) is the mass of type \(t\) assigned to school \(s\). The continuous probabilistic-serial rule runs on this society: every unit of mass consumes its most-preferred school at unit speed, and moves to its next available school whenever the current school reaches capacity. A solution is the exact event sequence and resulting matrix \(x\), satisfying

\[ \sum_{s\in S}x_{t,s}=\mu_t \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{t\in T}x_{t,s}=q_s. \]

The objective is to compute the mechanism’s aggregate assignment, not to introduce lotteries over already-continuous outcomes. Each infinitesimal student still receives one integral school; \(x_{t,s}\) records the mass of such assignments. The fractional object is therefore forced by population aggregation, while the society—not merely the outcome—is the continuous object.

A credible regime is a large school district or centralized housing market with hundreds of thousands of participants but relatively few preference, eligibility, and priority types. The scenario is not that every real student has an identical ranking; rather, it is a high-multiplicity market in which many students share the same ranking and all mechanism-relevant attributes. That is a sensible instance regime even if small bespoke matching markets are not.

The paper’s authors would likely recognize this as a population version of their probabilistic matching and time-sharing agenda. It also separates the mirror from the paper’s native use of continuity: the paper mainly makes outcomes probabilistic or divisible while retaining a finite society. This mirror makes the society continuous and interprets the resulting fractional assignment as mass, not necessarily as a lottery.

I would expect this problem to be Class A. An event-driven algorithm need only process school-exhaustion events and update the currently selected school of each type, giving a polynomial-time procedure in \(m\), \(\tau\), and the input bit length. Further questions would concern computing welfare-optimal assignments under fairness constraints, constructing finite lottery decompositions of \(x\), and controlling the outcome by transferring mass between types.

The weakest point is decisive: this is a plausible new computational problem inspired by the paper, not a theorem-backed mirror of a named result in the paper. Moreover, the paper’s central contribution is a case for probabilistic outcomes, whereas the ChoCo mirror must treat population mass as primary. Without importing a numbered result from one of the cited papers, the positive case can establish plausibility but cannot satisfy the programme’s anchor requirement.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the mirror is decisive at the programme’s gate: this paper contains no qualifying computational result. It is a manifesto and survey. Its only computational-complexity passage says that computing convex combinations can be difficult and cites Aziz et al. (2014); it proves no theorem about that problem. The paper’s actual claims concern fairness, strategyproofness, stability, welfare, and the desirability of randomisation. Therefore no continuous-population problem can be a mirror of a named computational result of this paper. It can only be a new ChoCo problem inspired by its agenda.

The proposed Continuum Probabilistic Assignment illustrates the problem. With explicitly listed types, rational masses, and capacities, it is essentially the usual probabilistic-serial execution compressed by type. Each type changes schools only finitely many times, and the next exhaustion event can be computed from the current aggregate rates. That may be a sensible high-multiplicity regime—the objection is not that school districts cannot contain many students of each mechanism-relevant type—but it is a computation of an already-described fractional assignment mechanism, not a population version of a theorem in this paper. The paper does not formulate this input, output, or complexity question.

One could make a more ambitious proposal: study a continuum version of random serial dictatorship, maximal lotteries, fractional matching, or the complexity of decomposing aggregate assignments into lotteries. Those could be worthwhile ChoCo projects. But they would be mirrors of the external papers cited in this chapter, or new problems extracted from its research agenda, not mirrors of results established here. In the case of random serial dictatorship especially, a continuum priority order requires an additional fluid-limit or priority-process model; choosing one is legitimate, but it is new modelling work rather than a canonical continuization of this chapter.

Nor can the gap be repaired by saying that the chapter’s probabilistic outcomes secretly provide the anchor. Its continuity is primarily in lotteries, time shares, and fractional assignments while the society remains finite. That is precisely the outcome-space continuity that ChoCo distinguishes from population continuization. Adding a distribution \( \mu \) over types may produce a valid high-multiplicity formulation, but it does not retroactively turn the chapter’s general advocacy into a computational result.

The universal claim that no scenario could yield a worthwhile continuous-population problem is stronger than the evidence permits: a well-designed school-district, matching, or lottery-computation problem might indeed be valuable. The honest negative conclusion is narrower but sufficient here: this paper offers no named computational anchor, and the proposed replacement is a new research direction rather than a mirror of the paper.
