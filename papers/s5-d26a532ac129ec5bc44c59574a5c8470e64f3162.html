The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying named anchor in this paper. It contains no numbered theorem, lemma, corollary, or proposition establishing that a computational problem is hard or tractable. Its results are empirical: for example, it reports an HSX forecast error of 30.96% and proposes a regression-based validity test. Those are not computational-complexity results, so I cannot honestly restate them as anchors or provide one required mirror question per anchor.

The strongest positive case I can make is an unanchored candidate based on the paper’s central mechanism: prices aggregate participants’ assessments of future outcomes. A possible question is *Continuum VSM Equilibrium Forecast*. An instance would specify finitely many outcomes \(\Omega\) and securities \(I\), each security’s rational payoff \(d_i(\omega)\), and a finite set of trader types \(T\) with rational masses \(\mu_t\). Each type has a belief over outcomes, an initial cash and share endowment, and a rational concave piecewise-linear utility function over terminal wealth. The question is to compute prices and type-level holdings that form an \(\varepsilon\)-equilibrium: each type’s chosen holdings are within \(\varepsilon\) of its best response at those prices, and aggregate holdings clear the market within \(\varepsilon\). Prices would supply the forecast, using the paper’s payoff transformation.

A plausible regime is a recurring online business forecast market with many participants, organized into a relatively small number of genuinely similar information, belief, and endowment types. The paper reports that HSX had more than 725,000 registered participants and over 15,000 active traders per day, across a set of movie securities. That supports a large-population setting, but it does not establish a small type support: if participants’ beliefs or relevant information differ individually, \(\tau\) could be close to the number of traders. The paper’s description of participants trading on dispersed assessments gives the mirror some economic plausibility, though the authors might recognize it as a model of their price-aggregation mechanism rather than a continuous version of their empirical forecasting problem.

For the restricted, price-taking equilibrium question above, I would expect tractability when the utility and market assumptions preserve a convex optimization formulation. That is a conjecture about this newly specified problem, not a result of the paper. It does not mirror the paper’s forecast-accuracy comparisons or validity test, and it does not show that continuization resolves any discrete hardness. The weakest point is therefore fundamental: the paper supplies a plausible population and mechanism to model, but no named computational result for the mirror to inherit.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The paper offers no computational result to anchor a mirror. It reports forecast errors, compares forecasts with experts, and proposes a statistical validity test. None asks whether an intervention or decision problem is tractable. The proponent’s equilibrium question is therefore a new model inspired by the paper, not a continuous version of one of its results.

That new model has a plausible large population, but the paper gives no evidence that its participants form a small set of complete types. Their dispersed information is central to the proposed price aggregation. A type that preserves the relevant information, beliefs, endowments, and trading incentives may be nearly unique to each participant, making \(\tau\) approach the number of traders. Grouping participants more coarsely risks erasing the information the market is meant to aggregate. HSX’s large trader count establishes population size, not low type support.

The proposed equilibrium also replaces the paper’s actual object. Its markets use order flow, market makers or double auctions, trading rules, and evolving prices; the paper studies those designs empirically. A static price-taking equilibrium over type-level holdings does not capture that process or predict whether its prices will match realized outcomes. A richer model could retain trading dynamics, but would need a model of individual information and actions and would become a different research project. Optimizing market design would be another possible project, but the paper does not pose that computational problem either.

So the strongest affirmative case is a potentially worthwhile continuous-market model, not a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper. The negative case is weakest where it must be: one could construct a recurring forecasting market with genuinely few participant types and study its equilibrium computation. The paper supplies no basis for those assumptions, and its empirical questions do not become computational social choice questions merely by replacing traders with masses.
