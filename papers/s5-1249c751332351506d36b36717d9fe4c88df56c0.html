The continuous mirror question

Given candidates C, supported types T with rational masses mu_t, candidate-probability vectors pi_t, a rational type-influence kernel K_{st}, target c^\star, and rational campaign budget kappa, choose seed masses x_t with 0\le x_t\le mu_t and sum_t x_t\le kappa to maximize the increase in expected margin of victory under the population-limit PLTR dynamics. Specifically, with z_t(a)=sum_s K_{st}a_s, use a_t^0=x_t and a_t^{r+1}=x_t+(mu_t-x_t)\min\{1,z_t(a^r)\}, then apply the paper’s PLTR preference update using the limiting active masses; determine the maximum margin increase, or decide whether it is at least a given rational q.

The model it lives in

An atomless electorate divided into exchangeable role types, with mass mu_t per type, a shared influence kernel K_{st}, and a campaign decision allocating seed mass x_t. Uniform voter thresholds yield active-mass dynamics, and the objective is the paper’s expected increase in the target’s margin of victory.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers Theorem 1’s constructive PLTR hardness question. It leaves the R-PLTR approximation results and the destructive-control results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror I believe is constructive election control in PLTR over an atomless, type-structured population. It keeps the paper’s central question: how should a controller allocate a campaign budget to increase a target candidate’s margin, given uncertain voter preferences and social influence?

Here a type is a cohort with the same candidate-probability vector \(\pi_t\), the same threshold law, and the same pattern of influence on every other cohort. Let \(T\) be the supported types, with population masses \(\mu_t\geq 0\) summing to \(1\). A nonnegative kernel \(K_{st}\) describes the influence from source type \(s\) on each member of type \(t\), with \(\sum_s K_{st}\mu_s\leq 1\). The input also gives the candidates, target \(c^\star\), and campaign budget \(\kappa\), all in binary rational form.

The decision variable is a seed-mass vector \(x\), where \(0\leq x_t\leq\mu_t\) and \(\sum_t x_t\leq\kappa\). Thus \(x_t\) is the fraction of the whole population directly reached in cohort \(t\). If \(a_t^r\) is the active mass of type \(t\) after round \(r\), its incoming influence is \(z_t(a^r)=\sum_s K_{st}a_s^r\). With the paper’s uniform thresholds, the population-limit LTM can be written
\[ a_t^0=x_t,\qquad a_t^{r+1}=x_t+(\mu_t-x_t)\min\{1,z_t(a^r)\}. \]
The limiting active masses determine the PLTR preference update: active mass of type \(t\) changes its candidate probabilities by the paper’s Eq. (1), using \(z_t(a)\) as its incoming active influence; inactive mass keeps \(\pi_t\). The score \(F(c,x)\) is then the resulting expected score per unit population mass. Let \(c\) be the leading opponent before the campaign and \(\hat c(x)\) the leading opponent after it. The objective is exactly the paper’s increase-in-margin objective:
\[ \operatorname{MoV}(x)=F(c,0)-F(c^\star,0) -\bigl(F(\hat c(x),x)-F(c^\star,x)\bigr). \]
Call this Role-Continuum PLTR Control: given the instance above, choose a feasible \(x\) maximizing \(\operatorname{MoV}(x)\). Its decision version asks, for rational \(q\), whether some feasible seed allocation achieves \(\operatorname{MoV}(x)\geq q\).

The regime is a large electorate divided into exchangeable cohorts: for example, large political communities with similar estimated support, susceptibility, and exposure to other communities. There are \(N\) voters and \(\tau=|T|\) cohort types, with many voters per type; the budget is a population share. The finite paper instead has one node per voter and selects individual seed nodes. In the mirror, users within a cohort are indistinguishable under the model, while the influence kernel records their shared type-level exposure. This preserves the campaign, uncertain preferences, and margin objective. Probabilistic preferences in the paper are not themselves the continuization: the population and the seed decision are now masses.

My anchor is Theorem 1, proved in this paper. It states that an \(\alpha\)-approximation for constructive election control in PLTR yields an \(\alpha\beta\)-approximation for Densest-\(k\)-Subgraph, for a positive constant \(\beta<1\). The authors prove this by reducing an arbitrary Densest-\(k\)-Subgraph instance to a PLTR network: graph adjacency becomes influence, the seed budget is \(k\), and the constructed campaign’s margin objective tracks the number of edges induced by the selected vertices up to constant factors. The theorem therefore transfers Densest-\(k\)-Subgraph’s approximation barriers to their finite-node problem. The paper gives the resulting conditional consequence that, under Gap-ETH, PLTR election control has no \(n^{-o(1)}\)-approximation. The theorem is proved here; the Densest-\(k\)-Subgraph hardness bounds it invokes are prior results.

I expect the role-continuum problem to remain hard, provisionally as a continuum-specific hard case. Its kernel can retain complicated interactions among types, and its campaign still optimizes a nonlinear margin after threshold-driven diffusion. But I would not claim that Theorem 1 already proves this: its reduction chooses discrete seed nodes, whereas the mirror permits fractional seed mass within each type. Whether that added freedom preserves the Densest-\(k\)-Subgraph gap is a central question for the prover. The mirror therefore asks whether the finite hardness dissolves under mass allocation, or whether the interaction structure sustains hardness even in the continuum.

The authors could plausibly recognize this as their problem: it retains their constructive objective and PLTR update while replacing a large exchangeable population with a type distribution. The continuum model makes a co-modification to diffusion: individual edges become a type-level influence kernel, and independent uniform thresholds become exact fractions in the population limit. That is part of specifying a population mirror, not an attempt to make the campaign question easier.

The weak point is that realistic cohorts may not have identical exposure profiles. If the types must encode each voter’s exact network position, then \(\tau\) can grow with \(N\), and the high-multiplicity advantage becomes less compelling. More directly, the theorem’s reduction uses individual graph structure, and fractional seeds may defeat its gap. The mirror is strongest in a genuine many-users-per-role regime; the main open question is whether its fractional optimization remains hard there. This case covers Theorem 1’s constructive PLTR hardness only; it does not claim a mirror for the paper’s R-PLTR approximation algorithm or its destructive-control results.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

Theorem 1 is a genuine computational result, so the case against cannot start by saying there is nothing to mirror. The strongest objection is that its hardness lives in the network’s vertex identities and adjacency. A voter type for this problem must capture preferences, susceptibility, and influence relationships. Two voters with the same candidate probabilities and degree can still have different effects on campaign spread because their neighbors, and the paths from them, differ. Replacing those relationships with average influence can change the active set and the margin.

That puts pressure on the proposed mirror. Its recurrence is exact for a population in which every member of a type receives the same influence from the active mass of each other type. That is a coherent dense, homogeneous network model, but it is an additional structural assumption. To preserve an arbitrary graph like the one used in Theorem 1, one generally needs a separate type for each distinct network role; in the reduction, that can mean \(\tau\) grows with \(n\). Preserving its seed budget also means a budget of \(k\) voters becomes a population share \(k/n\). The theorem’s hard instances therefore do not establish hardness for a fixed-type population limit.

A more faithful repair would model repeated copies of small network motifs, keeping each copy’s internal paths and threshold process. That preserves more of the paper’s mechanism, but if copies do not interact, campaign effects reduce to choosing among a finite menu of actions per copy. If they do interact, their cross-copy exposure must also be represented; a type kernel then needs to capture enough structure that the original aggregation problem returns. Neither repair supplies an obvious, broad high-multiplicity regime for the theorem’s arbitrary-network hardness.

This is a case against treating Theorem 1 as strong evidence for a useful continuous mirror. It does not, though, defeat the best block-population mirror. A large electorate divided into genuinely homogeneous communities, with rational type-level influence and a population-share campaign budget, gives a finite, well-defined optimization problem; the paper’s LTM dynamics and margin objective remain recognizable. The authors could plausibly regard it as their problem in a dense-network regime. The negative case is weak precisely there: I cannot honestly argue that no such scenario is worthwhile. The sharper conclusion is that the proponent has described a promising new regime, not shown that Theorem 1’s network-hardness result survives as its continuous-population mirror.
