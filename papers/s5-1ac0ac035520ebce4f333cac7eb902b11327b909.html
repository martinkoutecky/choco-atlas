The continuous mirror question

Given rational masses for finitely many applicant and position types, their preferences over counterpart types, acceptable type pairs, a feasible baseline mass matching \(M\), and \(\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\ge 1}\cup\{\infty\}\), decide whether there is a feasible mass matching \(N\) whose mass of agents strictly preferring their assignment in \(N\) is at least \(\alpha\) times the mass strictly preferring their assignment in \(M\), and return such an \(N\) when it exists. Use the paper’s convention that the unchanged comparison counts as \(1\)-more-popular, and require positive improvement when \(\alpha>1\).

The model it lives in

Represent each side by rational masses of complete preference types, with each person receiving at most one match. Split each type’s mass by its baseline partner type, then use transition-mass variables from baseline to new partner types; couple the two sides so every new type-pair has equal mass at both ends. The plus and minus vote masses and feasibility constraints are linear, so the question is LP feasibility and witness construction, with no separate optimization objective.

What the mirror covers

The selected mirror covers Theorem 1’s static \(\alpha\)-improvement problem. The proponent also states a dynamic mass-recourse question for Corollary 2, but its proposed bound is unproved; the other maintenance bounds, lower bounds, and voting-path results are not covered by the selected mirror.

Open questions for a prover

  • Give an explicit LP over baseline-to-new partner-type transition masses and prove that its feasible points are realizable as measurable matchings, including unmatched agents and ties.

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is a continuous version of the paper’s dynamic near-popular matching problem, anchored in Corollary 2 (proved here). It says that under vertex arrivals and departures, for any \(k>0\), DeferredResolution maintains a \((\Delta+k)\)-unpopular matching with an amortized \(O(\Delta^2+\Delta^3/k)\) changes per round. The result keeps the paper’s central tradeoff: tolerate a larger unpopularity factor to reduce how much the assignment must change.

A plausible high-multiplicity setting is a large labor market with recurring cohorts: many applicants and positions, but relatively few categories defined by attributes such as field, region, and contract. Members of each type share the same preferences over counterpart types and the same acceptable matches. Let \(\mu_a\) and \(\nu_b\) be the rational masses of applicant type \(a\) and position type \(b\). A matching is a measurable assignment, represented in aggregate by masses \(x_{ab}\ge 0\), with \(\sum_b x_{ab}\le\mu_a\) and \(\sum_a x_{ab}\le\nu_b\). An aggregate allocation can be realized by assigning different subsets of each cohort to different counterpart types; each individual still receives at most one match.

The continuous dynamic problem. The instance gives finite type sets, their preference orders over counterpart types, the acceptable type pairs, initial masses and a sequence of cohort arrivals and departures. At each update, the algorithm must output a feasible matching. For any two matchings \(M,N\), define \(P_+(N,M)\) and \(P_-(N,M)\) as the masses of agents who prefer their assignment in \(N\) or \(M\), respectively. The unpopularity factor is the supremum of \(P_+(N,M)/P_-(N,M)\) over feasible \(N\), with the usual conventions when the denominator is zero.

The question is to maintain matchings with \(\rho(M_t)\le\Delta_T+k\), where \(\Delta_T\) is the maximum number of acceptable counterpart *types*, while bounding cumulative recourse by \(O((\Delta_T^2+\Delta_T^3/k)\sum_t\delta_t)\). Here \(\delta_t\) is the mass entering or leaving at update \(t\), and recourse is the mass of continuing individuals whose partner changes. The output is a sequence of measurable matchings and the resulting recourse bound. I expect this type-based fluid problem to be tractable if the paper’s potential argument extends from counts to masses. That is a research question, not a consequence of Corollary 2. The further questions are whether type-level indifferences preserve the convergence argument, and whether the bound can be proved with dependence on support size and input bit length.

A clean supporting anchor is Theorem 1 (proved here). For a graph with preferences that may contain ties, where every listed neighbor is at least as preferred as being unmatched, it says that for any matching \(M\) and any \(\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}\), one can decide in polynomial time whether \(\rho(M)\ge\alpha\), and compute an \(\alpha\)-more-popular matching when one exists.

Its continuous mirror is the Mass-\(\alpha\)-Improvement problem. Given rational type masses, acceptable type pairs, preferences over counterpart types, a baseline matching \(M\), and rational \(\alpha\) (or \(\infty\)), decide whether a feasible matching \(N\) exists with \(P_+(N,M)\ge\alpha P_-(N,M)\), and return it if so. For \(\alpha>1\), require \(P_+(N,M)>0\), as in the paper’s convention that an unchanged matching counts as only \(1\)-more-popular.

This is a finite LP problem. Split each type’s mass into subcohorts according to its baseline counterpart type, including an unmatched subcohort. Each candidate match between subcohorts contributes a fixed amount to the plus/minus vote masses, according to the two types’ rankings. Feasibility and the vote condition are linear; the refined type sets and the LP have size polynomial in \(\tau\), the number of positive-mass types, and in the rational input length \(L\). I expect this mirror to be in Class A. It is not a claim that the continuous version newly resolves a discrete hardness result: the discrete anchor is already in P. Its value is that the algorithm operates on mass data and may avoid expanding a high-multiplicity electorate into individuals.

The corresponding social model is natural: millions of applicants and positions can be described as fractions of preference-and-compatibility classes, and the question “can a different matching win by a factor \(\alpha\) in votes?” retains the paper’s underlying voting comparison. The population becomes continuous; the matching remains an assignment problem. The authors explicitly allow ties for Theorem 1, which helps this mirror. A fractional allocation is also implementable as a measurable assignment of people to partners, rather than giving any individual a fractional job.

The paper offers no numbered NP-hardness or completeness result to anchor a hardness-transfer mirror. It cites hardness results in related work, but those are not numbered computational theorems of this paper, so I would not use them as anchors. Nor would I add Theorem 3’s \(\Omega(n)\) maintenance lower bound as a third mirror: its construction depends on a long path of individually distinguished partners, and a fixed-support type model does not obviously preserve that structure.

My weakest point is the dynamic mirror’s degree parameter. In the paper, \(\Delta\) counts individual neighbors. In a dense market, an agent may be adjacent to everyone in several large counterpart cohorts, so that degree grows with population size. The proposed \(\Delta_T\) counts acceptable counterpart types instead; this is a sensible type-based co-modification, but it is not the paper’s parameter, and Corollary 2 does not establish the stated mass-recourse bound. The static Theorem 1 mirror is firmer: it has an exact mass-vote formulation and a direct LP route. The dynamic mirror remains credible as a question, but its acceptance depends on whether the authors and referee regard type-level preferences and type-degree as preserving the problem they study.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against the dynamic mirror is that Corollary 2’s guarantee is tied to atomic graph updates and counts changes to individual matching edges. A type-mass update changes a whole bundle of possible matches at once. To define recourse, one must also specify how much of each old assignment persists through the update. The proposed \(\Delta_T\) and mass-change bound are sensible choices, but the corollary proves neither; an integral potential argument does not automatically establish them.

That objection weakens against a better formulation. With fixed preference types, an update can add, remove, or relabel a specified mass, and recourse can be measured by the mass of continuing agents whose partners change. Assignment-state flows can track that mass. The resulting problem preserves the paper’s central question—how to balance popularity against reassignment—and the authors could reasonably recognize it as theirs. The missing bound is a research question, not a reason to dismiss the mirror.

The static mirror of Theorem 1 is even harder to reject. It needs a little care: aggregate matchings alone do not say which agents improved, so the model should split each type by its baseline partner and track reassignment between those subcohorts. Once that is done, the vote condition and matching constraints are linear, with polynomially many variables in the type support. This makes the answer look immediate and likely tractable. But an easy Class A result is still a valid continuous computational question under the programme’s criteria; ease does not make the mirror out of scope.

So I cannot make a strong case that neither anchor has a worthwhile mirror. The dynamic version is under-specified until its update and recourse measures are fixed, and its proposed bound is unsupported. The static version seems routine. Those are real limitations on the case for this paper, but they do not defeat better formulations of either mirror. The honest negative case is weak, especially against the Mass-\(\alpha\)-Improvement anchor.
