The case FOR (proponent)

I can’t name an anchor that meets the requirement. The paper’s named results are about stochastic dynamics, not computational complexity: Theorem 1 proves that each agent’s urn proportion converges almost surely; Theorem 3 proves that, on a connected graph, all agents’ limiting proportions agree almost surely; and Theorem 4 proves that, for \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), the common limit has full support on \([0,1]\). Lemma 6 establishes attainability of every equilibrium point. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, fixed-parameter tractable, or similar. The beta-distribution statements are conjectures, and the simulations do not supply a named computational result.

There is a plausible *future* computational question in the paper’s stated strategic-disinformation motivation: on a large platform with a few communication and campaign-cost types, choose what fraction of each type to target, minimizing campaign cost while raising the chance of a false consensus past a threshold. The type masses would be population fractions. But the paper defines neither that intervention problem nor its continuum dynamics, so this would be a new research question, not a continuous mirror anchored in a result of this paper.

The main obstacle is that the graph and the randomness are central to the proved results. Passing to type masses could change the interaction structure and may wash out the fluctuations responsible for Theorem 4’s full-support limit. Thus this is not evidence that no continuous mirror exists; it is the reason I cannot honestly provide the requested per-anchor continuous problem or predict its complexity class from this paper.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent’s concession is the strongest point against a mirror: none of the paper’s named results is a computational result. Theorem 1 proves almost-sure convergence, Theorem 3 proves consensus on a connected graph, and Theorem 4 proves full support of the limiting belief. Lemma 6 proves attainability. None defines an algorithmic input and output or studies complexity. The paper’s central work is stochastic approximation and asymptotic analysis. Under ChoCo’s stated scope, there is no computational result here to continuize.

The best continuum versions of Theorems 1 and 3 still run into the graph. An agent’s update depends on its actual neighbors, so a valid type must include enough network structure to determine those interactions. For an arbitrary sparse graph, that can make the number of types grow with the number of agents, erasing the high-multiplicity benefit. Coarser types such as “degree two” or “received a truthful signal” do not determine the dynamics. A finite-type mixing model can close the equations, but its population limit averages out individual draws and produces deterministic mean-field dynamics. Studying convergence and consensus there is a natural analytic question; the paper supplies no computational problem for ChoCo to classify.

Theorem 4 and Lemma 6 offer the sharpest obstruction to a continuous mirror. Their substance is stochastic: finite agents make independent draws, and a sequence of those draws can move the process toward any consensus level. With a diffuse population and positive-mass neighborhoods, those independent draws average to their conditional means. The full-support random consensus and the finite-step attainability argument then disappear. Preserving them requires retaining finite neighborhoods, which retains agent-level structure, or adding aggregate noise, which introduces a new mechanism. Either choice weakens the case that this is a continuous-population version of the proved result.

The proposed strategic-disinformation problem is the best repair. The paper explicitly names that as future work, and choosing a mass of agents to influence could be a coherent computational problem. But the paper specifies no intervention, cost, budget, or optimization objective. Adding those would produce a potentially worthwhile follow-on project; it would not mirror any of the four results raised. It also shifts the object from consensus analysis to network control.

So the negative case is strong against treating this paper as an anchor for ChoCo: the named results are analytic, and the most natural population limit removes the stochastic feature that motivates the paper. It is weaker as a universal claim. A carefully designed strategic control problem on a network with repeated agent roles might be worthwhile. The paper gives a motivation for asking it, rather than a computational result whose continuous mirror it already defines.
