What the mirror covers

No result in the paper supplies a qualifying computational anchor. The random-preference theorem is not mirrored; the \(O(n^2)\) stable-husband algorithm is credited to Gusfield.

The case FOR (proponent)

I don’t see a compliant numbered anchor for a continuous-complexity mirror. The paper’s central result is printed only as “Theorem” and proves that, under independent uniformly random preference rankings, a fixed girl has between \(c\ln n\) and \(C\ln n\) stable husbands with probability tending to \(1\), for any \(c<\tfrac12\) and \(C>1\). That is a probabilistic result about the size of an algorithm’s output, not a complexity classification. The numbered lemmas analyze the proposal process; they do not establish that a problem is in P, hard, or otherwise classified. The \(O(n^2)\) stable-husband algorithm mentioned in the introduction is attributed to Gusfield, not proved here as a numbered result.

The closest plausible mirror would use two finite sets of preference types, one for each side, with rational masses. A solution would be a mass-matching matrix \(x\) whose row and column sums equal those masses. Call it stable when no pair of types has positive mass on both sides assigned to partners they like less than each other. Given a target type \(a\), the problem would be to return every type \(b\) for which some stable mass matching has \(x_{ab}>0\). A large cohort market with many agents sharing each complete ranking over counterpart categories is a plausible high-multiplicity regime for that question.

That question preserves the paper’s underlying stable-matching object, but it does not preserve the theorem’s key quantity: the paper counts individual husbands under random rankings over individuals, while the mirror counts partner types. With a fixed number of types, that count cannot grow as \(\ln n\); letting the number of types grow changes the random model. So this is a possible new research question, not an honest anchor-based continuous version of the paper’s result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a mirror is that this paper supplies no computational-complexity result to mirror. Its theorem is a probabilistic asymptotic statement: for random rankings, a particular girl has between \(c\ln n\) and \(C\ln n\) stable husbands with high probability. The lemmas analyze that random process. The paper’s stable-husband algorithm is shown correct, while the \(O(n^2)\) algorithm is credited to Gusfield in the introduction. None of these is a numbered result classifying the complexity of a computational problem. Under ChoCo’s stated test, that absence is decisive.

The proposed mass-matching query is coherent and could motivate a separate project. Its best version still faces a choice. Give every person a singleton type of mass \(1/n\), and the model retains the named preferences and the query about one girl’s husbands; it has encoded the finite instance as a measure, without giving the paper’s theorem a continuous-complexity counterpart. Group repeated complete preference types, and a stable mass matching can report partner *types* for a cohort. It cannot report the distinct named husbands of one girl. A zero-mass tagged girl does not appear in the coupling; giving her positive mass makes her a divisible cohort and changes the query.

So the negative case is strongest at the boundary between a mirror and an adjacent research problem: continuous stable matching may be worth studying, but the candidate mirror does not continue this paper’s computational result. I cannot rule out every worthwhile continuous stable-matching model. The honest claim is narrower: this paper offers a random-output theorem and an algorithmic background discussion, not a computational-complexity result for the continuization programme to take forward.
