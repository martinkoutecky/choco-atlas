Why no mirror

The proposed lower-quota and common-quota mass models are coherent high-multiplicity extensions that the authors would likely recognise. However, Theorems 4 and 8 only certify correctness of IP encodings; this paper does not use them to solve instances or prove a complexity, approximation, or parameterized result. Consequently, no anchor satisfies the required computational bit.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers the lower-quota and common-quota formulations in Theorems 4 and 8; it leaves stable score-limits, paired applications, combined-feature models, and externally cited hardness results unmirrored.

Open questions for a prover

  • Is stable-feasibility for nested \(\mathcal P\) solvable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), \(|\mathcal P|\), and the input length?
  • For non-nested \(\mathcal P\), does NP-hardness persist with repeated types and bounded \(\tau\), or does fractional mass yield a polynomial algorithm?
  • Can feasible mass assignments be rounded to finite stable matchings with controlled quota violations?

The case FOR (proponent)

There is a credible positive case, centred on the paper’s admission problems with quota constraints. The mirror is not “fractional matching” in the abstract: it is the same college-admissions problem with the applicant population replaced by masses of identical applicants.

The natural regime is a centralized admission system with \(N\gg\tau\): perhaps a national university or teacher-training intake with hundreds of thousands of applicants, a moderate number \(m\) of programmes, and only \(\tau\) distinct complete profiles. A type includes the applicant’s entire application set, ranking of colleges, and score at every college. Thus two applicants have the same type only if they are indistinguishable to the model. The mass \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of applicants of type \(t\), and every infinitesimal applicant still receives either one college or no college. Fractional \(x_{tj}\) records only the aggregate assignment of a type.

My lead anchor is Theorem 4. It is proved in this paper, although it is not itself an NP-hardness theorem. It says that the College Admissions problem with lower quotas is exactly represented by the integer program consisting of:

  • the constraint that each applicant is assigned to at most one college;
  • binary college-opening variables \(o_j\), with

\[ o_j\ell_j\le \sum_i x_{ij}\le o_j u_j; \]

  • stability constraints for open colleges, requiring an applicant who is not assigned to \(c_j\) or a more preferred college to face \(u_j\) applicants with strictly higher score at \(c_j\);
  • group-stability constraints for closed colleges, requiring that fewer than \(\ell_j\) applicants would be willing to attend a closed college.

The theorem concludes that the integer solutions of constraints (1), (14), (15), and (16) correspond exactly to stable matchings with lower quotas. The paper separately states in Section 3.2 that finding such a stable matching is NP-hard, citing Biró, Fleiner, Irving and Manlove [6]; that hardness claim is cited from elsewhere, not proved as a numbered theorem here.

The corresponding continuous problem is:

Lower-Quota Stable Admission\(_\infty\). An instance consists of colleges \(C\), a finite type set \(T\), rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), an application set \(A_t\subseteq C\), a ranking \(\rho_t\) over \(A_t\), scores \(s_t(j)\), and per-capita lower and upper quotas \(\ell_j,u_j\). Find a stable mass assignment \(x_{tj}\ge0\) and opening decisions \(o_j\in\{0,1\}\), or report that none exists.

The assignment must satisfy
\[ \sum_{j\in A_t}x_{tj}\le \mu_t \]
for every type and
\[ o_j\ell_j\le q_j:=\sum_t x_{tj}\le o_j u_j \]
for every college.

For an open college \(j\), define the mass of type \(t\) not assigned to \(j\) or a better college by
\[ r_{tj} = \mu_t-\sum_{k\in A_t:\rho_t(k)\le \rho_t(j)}x_{tk}. \]
If \(r_{tj}>0\), then \(j\) must be full with higher-scoring applicants:
\[ \sum_{v:s_v(j)>s_t(j)}x_{vj}=u_j. \]
For a closed college, use the high-multiplicity limit of the paper’s group-stability condition:
\[ \sum_{t:j\in A_t} \left( \mu_t-\sum_{k\in A_t:\rho_t(k)<\rho_t(j)}x_{tk} \right) \le \ell_j. \]
The last inequality is the normalized limit of the finite condition “at most \(\ell_jN-1\) applicants can form a blocking group.” One could instead adopt a strict continuum convention; choosing the closure convention makes the finite-to-continuous bridge explicit.

This is recognisably the authors’ problem. It retains the lower-quota institution, the decision to open or close a college, applicant rankings, college priorities, pairwise stability, and group stability. Only the population has been continuized, while quotas have been scaled per capita. The objective is feasibility: produce a stable admission outcome or prove that none exists. Minimizing unmatched mass would be a natural extension, but is not needed for the mirror.

My expectation is that the unrestricted problem is a Class C candidate, though this is precisely something to investigate rather than assert. The fractional population may dissolve the integrality-driven part of the discrete hardness, while the open/closed decisions and lower-quota group constraints remain combinatorial. If the cited discrete reduction can be reproduced with repeated applicant types and fractional assignments, hardness would instead transfer and make this Class B. With the opening pattern fixed, or with lower quotas removed, I would expect a Class A algorithm based on flow, generalized deferred acceptance, or a linear formulation. The resulting questions are substantive: does hardness persist for bounded \(\tau\)? Is there an FPT algorithm in \(\tau\) or in the number of lower-quota colleges? Can a continuous solution be rounded to a finite stable matching with controlled quota violations?

The second anchor is Theorem 8, also proved in this paper. It concerns common quotas, under the paper’s no-ties assumption. It states that constraints (1), (2), and (19), together with (20)–(22) and (23)–(24), describe exactly the stable matchings with common quotas. In substance:

  • applicants are assigned at most once;
  • individual college quotas are respected;
  • every common-quota set \(C_p\) satisfies

\[ \sum_{i,j\in C_p}x_{ij}\le u_p; \]

  • score-limit variables \(t_p\) ensure that an admitted applicant meets the relevant individual and common score limits;
  • auxiliary variables \(y_i^p\) ensure that a rejected applicant who is not assigned to a better college fails at least one relevant common-quota threshold;
  • variables \(f_p\) and constraints (23)–(24) force the score limit of an unfilled college or common quota to be zero.

The theorem concludes that feasible integer solutions of this formulation correspond exactly to stable matchings with common quotas. The paper also reports, citing [6], that nested common-quota systems admit efficient generalized Gale–Shapley algorithms, whereas non-nested systems may have no stable solution and finding one is NP-hard.

The continuous counterpart is:

Common-Quota Stable Admission\(_\infty\). An instance consists of the same type distribution and rankings, individual quotas \(u_j\), and a family \(\mathcal P\) of common-quota sets with per-capita capacities \(u_P\). The family includes singleton sets \(\{j\}\). For each \(P\), applicants have a common priority score \(s_t(P)\), with no ties.

Find masses \(x_{tj}\ge0\) satisfying
\[ \sum_{j\in A_t}x_{tj}\le\mu_t, \qquad \sum_t x_{tj}\le u_j, \qquad \sum_{j\in P}\sum_t x_{tj}\le u_P. \]

The assignment is stable if, whenever a positive mass of type \(t\) is not assigned to \(j\) or a more preferred college, some quota set \(P\ni j\) is full with applicants of strictly higher priority:
\[ \sum_{h\in P}\sum_{v:s_v(P)>s_t(P)}x_{vh}=u_P. \]
The singleton \(P=\{j\}\) gives ordinary college stability; larger \(P\) gives the common-quota blocking condition. The output is a stable mass assignment or \(\mathrm{NO}\).

This is a particularly clean mirror because it preserves the paper’s score-limit interpretation while making only the applicant population continuous. In a nested \(\mathcal P\), I would expect Class A: the type-level version of generalized deferred acceptance should process masses rather than individual applicants, and score limits act as the natural dual variables. For arbitrary non-nested \(\mathcal P\), I would expect a Class C boundary, unless the discrete hardness reduction can be implemented with repeated types, in which case it becomes Class B. That distinction directly asks where the hardness lives: in individual applicant integrality or in the combinatorics of the quota-set system.

These mirrors cover only the lower-quota and common-quota results. I would not stretch them to cover the paper’s combined lower/common-quota model, because the authors themselves say that the stability notion becomes problematic there. Paired applications could also be continuized, with \(x_{t,\{j,k\}}\) consuming mass at both colleges, but that would be a separate question rather than a necessary part of this case.

The weakest point is the lower-quota boundary convention. In the finite model, the condition is \(\ell_jN-1\), while after normalization the distinction between \(<\ell_j\) and \(\le\ell_j\) disappears asymptotically. A referee could reasonably demand that the continuum notion specify which convention is intended. That is a real modelling choice, not a fatal objection: the common-quota mirror avoids it, and the lower-quota question can state its convention explicitly.

A second limitation is empirical rather than conceptual: actual admission data may contain nearly unique score vectors, making \(\tau\) close to \(N\). The mirror is strongest for standardized, recurring cohorts with score bands and preference templates. That scenario is entirely plausible for the paper’s stated applications, but it should be presented as an instance regime, not as a claim that every university admissions market is high-multiplicity.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest negative point is that the proposed anchors are not computational results of this paper. Theorem 4 and Theorem 8 prove that particular finite, binary integer programmes encode stable matchings. Neither gives a complexity classification, an algorithmic bound, an approximation result, or a parameterized result. The NP-hardness statements are imported from Biró et al. [6] and Ronn, rather than proved as results of this paper. Thus the proposed continuous questions are not mirrors of the theorems’ computational content; they are new high-multiplicity matching problems inspired by the formulations.

For Theorem 4, the proposed mass model is nevertheless coherent. A national admissions system with repeated preference-and-score profiles is a legitimate high-multiplicity regime, and per-capita quotas are a defensible co-modification. The boundary between “fewer than \(\ell_jN\)” and “at most \(\ell_jN-1\)” can be specified in the continuum, and fractional type masses can represent aggregate assignments while each infinitesimal applicant receives one college. So the objections from rounding, splitting, or ties do not defeat the model.

They do, however, show what the result would actually be: a newly defined mixed discrete–continuous feasibility problem, with binary college-opening decisions and mass assignments. The paper supplies no theorem whose behaviour this question would continue. Calling it a mirror of Theorem 4 therefore overstates the connection. The cited NP-hardness could motivate studying the new problem, but it cannot serve as the paper’s own computational anchor.

Theorem 8 has the same problem, despite being the cleaner candidate. Its score-limit IP is again a correctness characterization, not a complexity result. The proposed common-quota continuum is a sensible model: applicant types can be complete preference-and-score profiles, quota sets can be retained, and repeated applicant profiles are plausible in a national admissions system. But the computational content would come from a new choice of type-level stability and a new input regime, not from anything established by Theorem 8. The nested and non-nested complexity facts are likewise cited from earlier work.

The stronger version of the positive case therefore survives the obvious attacks. One cannot dismiss these models because applicants are individually named, because quotas must scale, because the answer might become easier, or because high-multiplicity matching has prior literature. Nor can one claim that the relevant combinatorics being institutional rather than demographic makes the question worthless; that would violate the programme’s own rules.

Consequently, I cannot honestly defend the universal claim that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists in any scenario. The negative case can reject the proponents’ anchoring: they have presented plausible new high-multiplicity problems, not continuous versions of named computational results from this paper. But the common-quota—and probably also the lower-quota—model is a legitimate population continuization. The honest verdict is therefore “weak negative on anchoring and novelty,” not “no worthwhile mirror.”
