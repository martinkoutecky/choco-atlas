The continuous mirror question

Given candidates \(C\), rankings \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_\tau\) with positive rational masses \(\mu_i\) summing to \(1\), and an output cap \(q\), output \(\min\{q, |\arg\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{L}(C)} \sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i d_{KT}(\pi,\sigma_i)|\}\) distinct rankings attaining the minimum.

The model it lives in

A society is a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\tau\) complete-ranking types. A candidate ranking \(\pi\) has per-capita cost \(\mathrm{K}_\mu(\pi)=\sum_i\mu_i d_{KT}(\pi,\sigma_i)\); the task is to output up to \(q\) distinct minimizers. Theorem 5 suggests parameterization by unanimity width \(w\) and \(q\), with rational arithmetic whose bit length depends polynomially on the mass encoding length \(L\).

The objection that survived

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers distinct optimal aggregation in Theorem 5 and the candidate-parameterized distinct aggregation of Theorem 2. It leaves aside the parameter comparisons, Mallows experiments, and most approximate-enumeration results.

Open questions for a prover

  • What is the parameterized complexity of exact distinct-optimum enumeration on support-encoded rational societies beyond the paper’s parameters \(m\) and \(w+q\), particularly when the support size \(\tau\) is the main small parameter?

The case FOR (proponent)

My lead is Continuous Distinct Kemeny Aggregation. An instance is a candidate set \(C\) and a distribution \(\mu\) supported on \(\tau\) complete rankings \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_\tau\), with positive binary-rational masses summing to one, plus an output cap \(q\). Define the per-capita Kemeny score of a ranking \(\pi\) by
\[ \mathrm{K}_\mu(\pi)=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\mu_i\,d_{KT}(\pi,\sigma_i). \]
The task is to output \(\min\{q,\lvert\arg\min_\pi \mathrm{K}_\mu(\pi)\rvert\}\) distinct minimizing rankings. A solution is that list of rankings; each must have minimum expected Kendall–Tau distance.

The regime I find convincing is a very large platform or repeated survey in which millions of people rank the same fixed shortlist, but many submit the same complete ballot. A type is one such ranking; its mass is the fraction of the population submitting it. For example, the population may have millions of members but only hundreds of positive-mass ballot types. This is high multiplicity in the paper’s exact sense: for this problem, agents with the same ranking are indistinguishable. The score is per-capita disagreement, not a fractional ballot or a fractional output ranking.

My first anchor is Theorem 2, proved in this paper. It gives an \(O^*(2^m r^{O(1)})\)-time algorithm for DISTINCT KEMENY RANKING AGGREGATION: given \(m\) candidates, a profile, a score bound, and a requested number \(r\), output up to \(r\) distinct rankings within the bound that respect unanimity. It also establishes that the distinct bounded-score and distinct optimal variants are FPT parameterized by \(m\), since at most \(m!\) rankings can be output. Its subset dynamic program compares rankings using pairwise disagreement costs. In the mirror, those costs become rational masses: the penalty for putting \(a\) above \(b\) is the total mass of types that put \(b\) above \(a\). The same dynamic program can therefore work with exact rational costs, in time \(O(2^m q^{O(1)}\operatorname{poly}(\tau,L))\), where \(L\) is the mass encoding length. That is a concrete tractable parameter slice, not a claim of polynomial time for unrestricted Kemeny aggregation.

A second, independently useful anchor is Theorem 5, also proved here. It gives an \(O^*(2^{O(w)}r)\)-time algorithm for DISTINCT OPT KEMENY RANK AGGREGATION, parameterized by the paper’s unanimity width \(w\) and the requested output count \(r\). Here \(r\) is the output cap, not the number of input voters. For the continuous mirror, form the unanimous partial order from the positive-mass types and use the paper’s unanimity-width parameter: the pathwidth of the graph of pairs not unanimously ordered. The question is again to output up to \(q\) distinct minimizers of \(\mathrm{K}_\mu\). The theorem’s dynamic program uses pairwise costs and that unanimity structure, so rational masses appear to fit its computation with polynomial dependence on \(\tau\) and \(L\). This is a second Class A parameterization of the same continuous problem, by \(w+q\).

The unrestricted mirror should be expected to remain hard, by hardness transfer, rather than to become easy just because the population is continuous. The paper’s introduction cites prior Kemeny hardness, including hardness with only four input rankings; that is background from elsewhere, not a numbered hardness theorem in this paper. A finite profile with counts \(n_i\) maps to \(\mu_i=n_i/N\), and its Kemeny score is \(N\mathrm{K}_\mu(\pi)\), so the optimal rankings are exactly the same. Replicating each ballot type preserves that objective while making the cohorts arbitrarily large. Thus the paper’s numbered results give tractable parameterized slices, while the general problem inherits the discrete hardness. I would not claim a continuum-specific hardness result here.

This mirror covers the paper’s distinct optimal Kemeny aggregation results, especially Theorems 2 and 5. It does not attempt to continuize the paper’s Mallows experiments, its parameter comparisons, or the rankings being output. The continuous object is the population distribution over ballot types.

The weakest point is also clear: for rational masses, the optimizer is identical to the optimizer of a finite profile after multiplying by a common denominator. So this is a succinct high-multiplicity representation of Kemeny aggregation, not a new kind of ranking objective, and the results’ gains come from small \(m\) or small unanimity width rather than from large population size alone. Also, the repeated-ballot regime is plausible for some fixed-shortlist applications but not when nearly every person submits a unique ranking. I still think the mirror holds: it asks a well-defined computational question on a population represented by type masses, and its weighted pairwise costs preserve precisely the structure the paper’s algorithms use. One further question is which parameterizations remain useful when \(\tau\) is large; small support alone is unlikely to suffice given the cited hardness with few rankings.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is that the proposed mirror is very close to weighted Kemeny aggregation, already a computational problem. For a distribution \(\mu\), the per-capita objective is just a weighted sum of Kendall–Tau distances. If its rational masses have common denominator \(D\), then
\[ D\,\mathrm K_\mu(\pi)=\sum_i (D\mu_i)d_{KT}(\pi,\sigma_i), \]
so the continuous and integer-weighted instances have exactly the same optimal rankings. Weighted Kemeny work already studies this objective computationally; for example, Kenyon-Mathieu and Schudy give a weighted generalization of their tournament feedback-arc-set PTAS for Kemeny–Young aggregation ([ECCC report](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/eccc-reports/2006/TR06-144/index.html)). If the programme wants a new objective or a tractability gain caused by fractional population, this mirror may be too incremental.

That objection does not defeat either anchor. Theorem 2’s subset dynamic program can use rational pairwise penalties, and Theorem 5’s unanimity structure is unchanged by positive masses; its dynamic program can likewise compare exact rational costs. Their parameterized enumeration questions are not answered merely by knowing weighted Kemeny aggregation or a PTAS. The population representation also matters computationally when the support is small and the multiplicities are large: the common denominator may represent far more voters than are explicitly listed in the input.

Nor do the other proposed objections hold. The paper has named algorithmic results; identical rankings are a sensible type in a large survey or election; the objective does not track individual voters; and the normalized score remains nontrivial as population size grows. Discrete-to-continuous identity is useful here as a dictionary, not a reason to dismiss the question. Existing weighted work makes the novelty case less clean, but it is not enough to show that this paper’s model is already the continuous-population model or that the distinct-optimum parameterizations are already settled.

So I cannot honestly defeat both anchors. The negative case is weak precisely where it needs to be strongest: these are valid population-mass formulations, and their algorithmic questions survive the best objection, which is that the objective has close weighted-profile precedents.
