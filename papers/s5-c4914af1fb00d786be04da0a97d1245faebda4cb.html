The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest positive case is conditional. A credible continuous-population mirror exists, but this paper supplies no qualifying computational anchor: none of Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Theorem 3, Lemma 1, or Lemma 2 asserts NP-hardness, membership in P, fixed-parameter tractability, or any comparable complexity classification. The cited results are game-theoretic and statistical, not computational-complexity results. Thus, strictly speaking, the paper has zero eligible anchors.

The best provisional anchor is nevertheless Theorem 2, proved in this paper. In model II, there are an odd number \(n\ge5\) of voters, each receiving two independent private binary signals, with symmetric accuracy \(q\in(1/2,1)\), a prior \(\pi\ne1/2\), and majority rule. Let \(S_i\in\{0,1,2\}\) be the number of \(1\)-signals received by voter \(i\). A strategy is sincere when it selects the alternative with the higher posterior probability; it is informative when \(v_i(0)=A\) and \(v_i(2)=B\); and it is rational when the strategy profile is a Bayesian Nash equilibrium. Theorem 2 states that sincere voting cannot be both informative and rational. This is proved here, not cited.

A natural population mirror would be a Continuous Type-Block Jury Equilibrium problem. The two alternatives are \(A\) and \(B\), the state prior is \(\pi\), and the population is a continuum of total mass \(1\). A type is the complete signal statistic \(s\in\{0,1,2\}\). Conditional on state \(A\), the type masses are \(\mu^A=(q^2,2q(1-q),(1-q)^2)\); conditional on state \(B\), they are \(\mu^B=((1-q)^2,2q(1-q),q^2)\). A type-level action \(x_s\in[0,1]\) is the fraction of type-\(s\) mass voting for \(A\); each individual still casts an integral vote. In state \(\theta\), \(A\) wins by quota \(\rho\) exactly when \(\sum_s\mu^\theta_s x_s\ge\rho\), with \(\rho=1/2\) for majority rule.

Sincerity is defined from the posterior \( \Pr(A\mid s)=\frac{\pi\mu^A_s}{\pi\mu^A_s+(1-\pi)\mu^B_s} \). A type-block equilibrium requires that no positive-mass type cohort can switch its voting mass from \(A\) to \(B\), or vice versa, and increase its posterior expected payoff from the collective outcome. The problem is: given \(\pi,\mu^A,\mu^B,\rho\), decide whether the sincere profile is informative and a type-block equilibrium; if not, output a profitable type and deviation.

This is a genuine population continuization: the active object is the mass of each information type, not a fractional outcome or a noise distribution. The intended regime is a very large jury, referendum electorate, or delegated information panel with millions of agents but only three relevant information types, so \(n\gg\tau\) with \(\tau=3\). The same construction extends to model III by adding the public-signal coordinate to the type.

For the paper’s fixed three-type family, I would expect this problem to be tractable: equilibrium verification requires only finitely many posterior and quota comparisons, and the mass formulation is a constant-dimensional system of linear inequalities. The interesting further questions are whether Theorem 2’s impossibility survives this mass-deviation equilibrium, whether a standard atomless equilibrium instead makes every individual non-pivotal, and whether the finite-population and continuum limits commute.

The weakest point is serious: type-block deviations are not the individual Bayesian Nash deviations used in Theorem 2. Under the standard atomless-game notion, one voter has zero influence on the outcome, so the paper’s pivotality argument largely disappears. Thus this is best presented as a faithful continuous population extension of the paper’s information-aggregation problem, not as a direct continuous version of its theorem.

So the positive case is: the paper has a plausible high-multiplicity population mirror, and Theorem 2 gives it a clear behavioral target. But it is not a fully compliant ChoCo computational case, because the source contains no named computational-complexity result.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The negative case is unusually strong because the paper has no eligible computational anchor. Theorem 1, Theorem 2, Theorem 3, and Lemmas 1–2 are claims about Bayesian best responses, equilibrium, and statistical accuracy. None classifies a computational problem, gives an algorithm, or proves complexity. A new equilibrium-verification problem invented around Theorem 2 therefore cannot be presented as the computational continuation of a result in this paper.

More importantly, Theorem 2 does not concern type masses. Its force comes from a finite individual’s positive probability of being pivotal. When \(d=(n-1)/2\) other voters vote for \(A\), voter \(j\) can condition her action on that event and infer information about the other voters’ signals. That is the entire source of the contradiction between sincere and rational voting.

In the proposed continuum, conditional on the state, the masses of the three signal types are fixed almost surely:

\[ \mu^A=(q^2,2q(1-q),(1-q)^2),\qquad \mu^B=((1-q)^2,2q(1-q),q^2). \]

A single voter has measure zero and cannot change the aggregate vote or cross the quota. Thus the pivotal event on which Theorem 2 conditions disappears. Every individual action is payoff-irrelevant, so under the standard nonatomic Nash concept sincere voting is an equilibrium only vacuously: every voting profile is. The theorem’s strategic object has not been continuized; it has vanished.

The proposed “type-block equilibrium” does not repair this. Requiring a positive-mass signal cohort to deviate treats that cohort as a single coordinated player. In the paper, voters sharing a signal are separate players who do not coordinate their deviations. Replacing them by three strategic blocks changes the player set, information structure, and solution concept. If blocks retain positive influence, the model is a finite game among blocs with continuous bookkeeping; if each voter remains infinitesimal, no unilateral deviation is influential. There is no version that both preserves the paper’s Bayesian-Nash argument and remains a genuine continuum of voters.

Theorem 3 has the same defect. A public signal can be retained as a common shock, but after it is observed the continuum again has deterministic private-signal masses. Adding the public-signal coordinate merely creates finitely many deterministic regimes; it does not restore individual pivotality. Theorem 1 and the two lemmas are even less promising: with two or three types they reduce to finite posterior and threshold comparisons, not a population-scale computational problem.

One could study finite-\(n\) corrections, pivotality probabilities, or a central-limit scaling around the continuum. That would be a legitimate asymptotic study, but the interesting object would then be the vanishing finite-population fluctuation, not the continuous society itself. Alternatively, one could let the number of signal types grow and formulate a new distributional mechanism-design problem. That might be worthwhile research, but it would no longer mirror any named result here; its computational structure would come from the encoding of signal types and aggregation rules, not from continuizing this electorate.

So the proponent’s own concession is decisive under ChoCo’s gate: there is no computational result to mirror, and the only proposed behavioral anchor loses its defining mechanism in the atomless limit. I cannot prove that no imaginative new Bayesian mass-player model could ever be useful, but such a model would be a new problem rather than a worthwhile continuous mirror of this paper.
