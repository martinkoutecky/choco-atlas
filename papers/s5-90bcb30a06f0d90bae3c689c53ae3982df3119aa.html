The continuous mirror question

Given a finite rational metric \((X,d)\), candidate stops \(C\subseteq X\), budget \(k\), and supported route types \(t=(a_t,b_t)\) with rational masses \(\mu_t>0\) summing to \(1\), under null transit cost, determine the complexity of finding \(Y\subseteq C\), \(|Y|\leq k\), minimizing \(\sum_{t}\mu_t\min\{d(a_t,b_t),d(a_t,Y)+d(b_t,Y)\}\), as a function of \(m=|C|\), support size \(\tau\), and input bit length \(L\); take \(d(x,\emptyset)=+\infty\).

The model it lives in

Route types are origin-destination pairs carrying population masses \(\mu_t\); the selected stops remain a discrete set \(Y\), and the objective is per-capita travel cost \(\sum_t\mu_t c_t(Y)\).

The objection that survived

The hardness reduction uses one route type per \(k\)-median datapoint, so it establishes no hardness result for fixed small \(\tau\); this narrows the complexity claim but does not defeat the variable-support mass problem.

fatal: False

What the mirror covers

The selected mirror covers Proposition 1’s minimum-cost optimization and its hardness transfer; Theorem 1 offers a second mirror through core fairness over endpoint masses. The ECA and \(\lambda\)-Hybrid guarantees and the empirical evaluations are not covered by the stated questions.

Open questions for a prover

  • How does the optimization complexity depend on \(m,\tau,L\), especially when \(\tau\) is small?
  • Can weighted Greedy Capture be formally shown to give the stated core guarantee for rational endpoint masses in time polynomial in \(m,\tau,L\)?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest mirror is the paper’s transit stop problem with a continuous population of recurring riders. Let each type \(t=(a_t,b_t)\) be a complete origin–destination description, and let \(\mu_t\) be its rational share of the population, with \(\sum_t\mu_t=1\). For this paper’s model, riders with the same endpoints have the same costs. A plausible regime is a large commuter or shuttle population with many repeated trips: \(N\) riders but only \(\tau\ll N\) route types. The chosen stops remain a discrete subset of candidates; only the population becomes a distribution. Rational discrete populations map directly to this model, and rational type distributions can be realized by a finite population using a common denominator.

Lead: Continuous Minimum-Cost Transit Stop Placement. Given a finite metric, candidate stops \(C\), a budget \(k\), and the supported route types with masses \(\mu_t\), choose \(Y\subseteq C\), \(|Y|\le k\), minimizing \(\bar c_\mu(Y)=\sum_t\mu_t c_t(Y)\). For the paper’s null-transit-cost restriction, \(c_t(Y)=\min\{d(a_t,b_t),d(a_t,Y)+d(b_t,Y)\}\). This is the per-capita version of the paper’s total-cost objective; multiplying it by population size does not change the minimizing placement.

The anchor is Proposition 1: unless \(P=NP\), no polynomial-time algorithm computes a minimum-cost TrSP solution, even with null transit costs. The authors prove this in Appendix A by reducing from \(k\)-median, whose hardness they cite. In the published PDF this is Proposition 1; the supplied transcription omits the numeral on this restatable block and uses Proposition 1 for a later result, so I follow the published numbering. [Paper PDF](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/fair-transit.pdf)

The same reduction works for the mass objective: give each constructed route type mass \(1/n\), so the continuous objective is the discrete total cost divided by \(n\), and scale the decision threshold accordingly. Replicating each type \(q\) times gives a finite realization with \(N=q\tau\) riders and leaves the type distribution and optimum unchanged. I expect this mirror to remain NP-hard—Class B, with the hard combinatorics in candidate-stop selection and the metric. That makes it a useful boundary case: continuization gives a natural aggregate model, but does not promise that every optimization problem becomes easier.

Second question: Continuous \((2,\rho)\)-Core Transit Placement. Given the same route distribution and null transit costs, output a feasible stop set \(Y\) such that, for every alternative stop set \(Z\subseteq C\), \(\mu(\{t:\rho c_t(Z)<c_t(Y)\})<2|Z|/k\). This is the mass form of \((2,\rho)\)-core: no group with mass at least \(2|Z|/k\) can all improve by the factor \(\rho\) using \(Z\).

The anchor is Theorem 1, proved in the paper: if a feasible \(Y\) is \(\rho\)-proportionally fair in the clustering instance induced by the agents’ endpoints, then \(Y\) is a \((2,\rho)\)-core solution. The theorem’s proof turns endpoint guarantees into a guarantee for each route by pairing its two endpoints. [Paper PDF](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~haziz/fair-transit.pdf) In the continuous mirror, give each route’s two endpoints mass \(\mu_t\), for total endpoint mass \(2\). A weighted Greedy Capture procedure can work with this endpoint measure; the paper itself notes that its capture rule can deactivate an exact fractional endpoint quota. With the known \((1+\sqrt2)\)-PF guarantee for Greedy Capture, the expected result is a polynomial-time \((2,1+\sqrt2)\)-core placement, with running time in \(m,\tau\), and the input bit length \(L\). This is a Class A mirror, though the weighted algorithm and proof would need to be written down formally.

These are questions the authors should recognize: their agents are already described by route pairs, their costs depend on those pairs, and their fairness claims already compare coalition sizes to \(n/k\). In the mirror, those counts become masses. The core question does require a careful formulation: “there exists a nonblocking individual” is not the right continuum condition, so I state it as a bound on the mass of strict improvers. This preserves the paper’s coalition logic without turning the outcome into fractional stops or moving continuity into the costs or alternatives.

The scope here is the exact-cost hardness result and the null-transit core guarantee. The paper’s Helena data, with many distinct route records, does not itself establish a regime with \(\tau\ll N\); the stronger case is a recurring commuter or fixed-route shuttle population with repeated origin–destination types. That is the main weakness of the mirror’s motivation, alongside the need to formalize the weighted fairness algorithm. The hardness result still transfers cleanly, and the endpoint-mass construction gives a concrete, plausibly tractable fairness question. Further work could ask whether the coalition-size factor \(2\) can be improved in this regime, and how efficiently continuous core guarantees can be checked.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is about how often the useful regime occurs, not whether these mirrors are well-posed. The minimum-cost mirror is the weighted objective \(\sum_t \mu_t c_t(Y)\). Appendix A’s \(k\)-median reduction gives a hardness result for it, but the construction assigns a separate route type to each input datapoint, so \(\tau=N\). That says nothing about whether the problem simplifies when many riders share a route type. In the Helena data, exact origin–destination pairs appear unlikely to compress much; coarsening noisy locations into shared types can change costs and fairness comparisons. That limits the empirical case. But recurring trips on a fixed shuttle provide a plausible high-multiplicity regime, and the remaining stop-selection problem is a genuine weighted optimization question. Its hardness would be a boundary result, which the programme explicitly values. I cannot turn this anchor into a sound objection.

The core mirror is harder to oppose. In the null-transit setting, give each route type’s two endpoints mass \(\mu_t\). Suppose a coalition has route mass \(s\geq 2|Z|/k\), where \(Z\) is a deviating stop set. Endpoint proportional fairness implies that, for each stop in \(Z\), the mass of endpoints that strictly prefer it is less than \(2/k\). Their union therefore has mass less than \(2|Z|/k\leq s\). Since the coalition has endpoint mass \(2s\), more than \(s\) endpoint mass remains good for every stop in \(Z\). If no positive mass of routes had both endpoints good, at most \(s\) endpoint mass could be good. Thus some positive route mass has both endpoints good, reproducing the pairing step behind Theorem 1. A weighted Greedy Capture procedure appears to supply the needed endpoint guarantee, with rational masses handled through the finite support. Its weighted proof should be made explicit, but the mirror looks both sensible and tractable.

A stronger fairness mirror could also apply the same mass substitution to ECA: grow cost thresholds until a candidate pair serves mass at least \(2/k\). The paper’s proof is based on coalition thresholds and costs, so this too looks like a plausible weighted extension rather than a conceptual mismatch. The best negative point is therefore that several proposed results may lift with little new work, while the paper’s measured data may offer limited type compression. Under ChoCo’s criteria, neither establishes that the questions are unworthy. My honest assessment is that the case against is weak, especially against the continuous core mirror.
