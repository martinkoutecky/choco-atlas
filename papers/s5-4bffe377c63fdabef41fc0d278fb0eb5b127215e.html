The continuous mirror question

Given a finite premise agenda \(\Phi\), an initial distribution \(\mu\) over complete, consistent judgment types, reserve masses \(\rho_t\) by type, a rational mass budget \(\kappa\), and a consistent, complement-free target \(J\), is there a recruitment vector \(z\) with \(0\le z_t\le\rho_t\) and \(\sum_t z_t\le\kappa\) such that every formula in \(J\) is accepted by the continuous \(\mathrm{UPQR}_{1/2}\) outcome on \(\mu^z_t=(\mu_t+z_t)/(1+\sum_t z_t)\)? A literal assigned to \(\Phi_1\) is accepted when its mass share is greater than \(1/2\); a literal assigned to \(\Phi_2\) is accepted when its share is at least \(1/2\).

The model it lives in

Types are complete, consistent judgment sets over \(\Phi\); the input lists the positive initial and reserve masses as binary rationals. The decision variables are recruited masses \(z_t\), bounded by \(\rho_t\) and total budget \(\kappa\); the objective is to make all target formulas enter the rule's outcome. The quota conditions are linear in \(z\), with strict inequalities for target literals in \(\Phi_1\) and weak inequalities for those in \(\Phi_2\), so feasibility is decidable in polynomial time by maximizing a shared slack for the strict constraints.

What the mirror covers

The mirror covers exact control by adding judges under \(\mathrm{UPQR}_{1/2}\), the branch used to establish Theorem 4's hardness; it leaves ordinary Hamming-distance control and the paper's deleting- and replacing-judge results aside.

Open questions for a prover

  • How closely does the continuous answer correspond to the discrete problem when all masses are restricted to multiples of \(1/n\), and what rounding guarantees follow from positive quota margins?

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest case is narrow: continuous exact control by adding judge mass under \(\mathrm{UPQR}_{1/2}\).

The anchor is Theorem 4, proved in this paper by a reduction from X3C. It says that \(\mathrm{UPQR}_{1/2}\) is resistant to both EXACT CONTROL BY ADDING JUDGES and CONTROL BY ADDING JUDGES. In the exact problem, the input gives an agenda, an initial profile \(T\), a pool \(S\) of judges who may be added, a bound \(k\), and a consistent, complement-free desired judgment set \(J\). The question is whether some \(S'\subseteq S\), with \(|S'|\le k\), makes \(J\) a subset of the collective outcome. The other variant asks whether adding such a subset strictly reduces the Hamming distance to \(J\). The paper’s X3C construction uses an agenda of premises only, so the exact-control result already concerns the part of judgment aggregation my mirror models.

My lead mirror is Continuous Exact Control by Adding Judge Mass under \(\mathrm{UPQR}_{1/2}\). An instance has a finite, complement-closed agenda \(\Phi\) consisting of premises; an initial society \(\mu\) of total mass \(1\); a reserve pool with available mass \(\rho_t\) for each type \(t\); a rational mass budget \(\kappa\); and a desired consistent, complement-free judgment set \(J\). A type \(t\) is a complete, consistent judgment set over \(\Phi\). Thus agents of the same type agree on every judgment the problem uses. The input lists only types with positive initial or reserve mass, with their binary rational masses.

The action is to recruit mass \(z_t\) of each reserve type, where \(0\le z_t\le\rho_t\) and \(\sum_t z_t\le\kappa\). The resulting society is
\[ \mu^z_t=\frac{\mu_t+z_t}{1+\sum_t z_t}. \]
The question is whether some such action makes every formula in \(J\) part of the \(\mathrm{UPQR}_{1/2}\) outcome. In the continuous quota rule, a literal assigned to \(\Phi_1\) is accepted when its mass share is greater than \(1/2\); a literal assigned to \(\Phi_2\) is accepted when its share is at least \(1/2\). This carries over the paper’s tie convention: at an exact tie, the \(\Phi_2\) literal is selected. A solution is the recruited mass vector \(z\). The control objective is exact achievement of \(J\) within the mass budget.

A plausible regime is a large, recurring policy consultation or membership vote on a fixed agenda: many participants, but comparatively few distinct complete response patterns. The organizer can recruit additional participants from an eligible pool whose available mass by response pattern is known. The type is the participant’s full judgment set; mass represents a fraction of the initial population. For example, \(N\) may be in the hundreds of thousands while the observed and recruitable support contains only \(\tau\) judgment types. That is the regime in which asking whether a target judgment can be induced by recruiting, say, \(2\%\) of the population is more natural than counting named individuals. It is the paper’s adding-judges question with its control variable expressed in the population’s natural units.

This mirror is expected to be tractable. For each target literal, the quota condition becomes a linear inequality. For \(\phi\in J\cap\Phi_1\), it is
\[ 2\sum_{t:\phi\in t}(\mu_t+z_t)>1+\sum_t z_t, \]
and for \(\phi\in J\cap\Phi_2\), replace \(>\) with \(\ge\). Together with the reserve and budget bounds, these give a linear feasibility problem with \(O(\tau)\) variables. The strict inequalities can be handled by maximizing a shared slack and asking whether it can be positive. So the continuous decision problem is solvable in polynomial time in the agenda size, support size, and input bit length. The discrete reduction’s choice of a subset of judges becomes a choice of fractional mass by type; the target is still the same judgment outcome.

Further questions follow naturally: how the continuous answer relates to the original problem when masses are restricted to multiples of \(1/n\); whether a positive margin from quota thresholds gives useful rounding guarantees; and how the same model behaves for deletion, replacement, or type-dependent recruitment costs. The ordinary Hamming-distance version of Theorem 4 is a nearby candidate, but I would not claim it here: the exact-target problem is already a precise mirror of the theorem’s exact branch.

The weakest point is that the paper’s motivating institutions—juries and arbitration panels—are often small, and their members may not be fractionally recruitable. The case therefore depends on a different, credible instance regime, such as a large consultation with repeated judgment patterns, rather than on those examples themselves. There is also a deliberate idealization in treating all agents of a type as interchangeable. That is exactly the high-multiplicity assumption, and here it preserves the paper’s formal problem: each type records the complete judgment information the aggregation rule uses. The mirror covers Theorem 4’s exact adding-control result, not the paper’s full study of control.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against Theorem 4’s mirror is institutional. The paper’s examples are juries and arbitration panels, where the number of decision makers is small and each appointment is indivisible. A continuous version needs a different setting: for example, an organizer recruiting participants for a large consultation, with the recruitable population known by complete judgment type. That is plausible, but it changes the institutional picture from appointing judges to recruiting a mass electorate. It also assumes the organizer can recruit arbitrary fractions of each type.

That objection does not defeat the better mirror. The theorem’s reduction uses an agenda of premises only, and its target concerns which formulas enter the outcome—not who receives an assignment or how named judges fare. In a large consultation with a fixed agenda, participants sharing a complete judgment set are genuinely interchangeable for the rule. Their masses and recruitable reserves are therefore meaningful inputs. The proposed quota conditions are linear in the recruited masses, including with the rule’s stated tie convention. The parity issue at an exact half share calls for choosing a mass-rule convention; it does not make the question ill-posed.

The model’s simplicity is the remaining negative point: exact control becomes linear feasibility, so this particular mirror may offer less technical depth than problems whose continuous formulations require a nontrivial pricing algorithm or convex optimization. But that is a concern about the project’s payoff, not a case that the mirror is unworthy. The programme explicitly seeks tractable continuous mirrors, and Theorem 4 provides a named computational result whose discrete choice structure is replaced by a well-defined mass-control question. I cannot make a strong universal case against it: the panel examples fit poorly, but the consultation regime is credible enough that this anchor survives.
