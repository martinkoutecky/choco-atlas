The case FOR (proponent)

There is a plausible continuous economic research direction here, but I can’t make it a qualifying ChoCo case: the paper contains no named computational result. It proves existence and uniqueness properties of an equitable mechanism, not that a computational problem is in P, NP-hard, or otherwise has a complexity classification. So there is no eligible anchor from which to state one continuous computational problem per result.

The closest named results are Proposition 6 and Corollary 9, both proved in this paper. Proposition 6 says that if every feasible allocation rule has an incentive-feasible mechanism that gives each agent type the same interim utility, then an interim incentive equitable mechanism exists; all such mechanisms give agents the same interim utilities, and strict concavity makes the mechanism unique. Corollary 9 establishes that this conclusion applies when agents have non-exclusive information. These are substantive existence results, but neither analyzes the complexity of finding or recognizing such a mechanism. The paper’s example of nonexistence with exclusive information is also economic analysis, not a named complexity result.

The strongest positive direction I see is a new continuum-agent version of their mechanism problem. Take a large population of households with a finite support of complete economic types—preferences and private-information characteristics—and let \(\mu_\theta\) be the share of type \(\theta\). Give the population per-capita supplies of goods, and let a mechanism assign bundles from reports and the reported population profile. Ask whether there is a feasible, interim incentive-compatible mechanism that is interim incentive efficient and gives each type the same interim-equivalent share \(\lambda\) of the reference bundle. Many households sharing a small number of types is a credible regime, and scaling supplies with population size is a natural co-modification. The paper’s egalitarian-equivalence question would remain recognizable.

That is a candidate for a new economic continuation, not a continuous mirror anchored by this paper’s computational results. The hardest modeling issue is preserving meaningful individual incentive constraints when each agent has negligible mass; the paper’s finite profile of agents and its continuum population would need a careful bridge. My weakest point, and the decisive one under your criteria, is that the paper never asks the computational question ChoCo is meant to chart.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against a ChoCo mirror is that the paper never states a computational problem. Proposition 6 proves existence and essential uniqueness of interim incentive equitable mechanisms under an implementability condition; Corollary 9 shows that non-exclusive information guarantees that condition. Neither classifies the complexity of finding a mechanism, testing existence, or deciding implementability. The paper’s example of nonexistence under exclusive information is economic analysis, not a complexity result.

The best mirror one could construct would take a large population with finite support over complete economic types, scale resources per capita, and ask for an algorithm to compute an interim incentive equitable mechanism—or decide whether one exists. A narrower version would do this under non-exclusive information, perhaps with rational, finitely represented utilities and priors. That is a coherent economic computational problem. But it supplies the computational task, input representation, and population regime that the paper does not: the paper works with a fixed finite set of named agents, arbitrary concave utility functions, and a prior over their joint type profiles. Its proofs establish an existence claim over allocation rules; they do not define an encoded input or an algorithmic output whose complexity can be mirrored. Choosing a finite utility language and asking for a runtime would begin a new algorithmic project, not continue a computational result in this paper.

The distinction matters for both anchors. Proposition 6’s mechanism-computation question would be new even when its hypothesis holds. Corollary 9 does not add an algorithmic result; it identifies an information structure in which the existence theorem applies. And the exclusive-information example could inspire a separate decision problem, but it cannot rescue the paper as a source of a named computational result.

There is a plausible continuous economic research direction here. One could model type cohorts, per-capita feasibility, and report-dependent allocations, then specify how individual incentives work when each person has negligible mass. That bridge may be possible, but it must settle how the prior and information structure are represented and how a mechanism is encoded. Even granting a successful bridge, its value would come from defining and studying that new mechanism-design problem—not from continuizing a computational question the paper already asks. Existing work on high-multiplicity populations would support the plausibility of such a regime, but would not supply the missing anchor.

So I would reject this as a ChoCo mirror, while keeping the broader possibility open: the paper could motivate worthwhile continuous economic computation, but the evidence here does not show that it has a continuous computational mirror.
