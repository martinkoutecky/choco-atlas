What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers only fully transferable stability in Theorem 3.3; it leaves the non-transferable and partial-transfer stability results aside.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive lead is the fully transferable case, centered on Theorem 3.3. It is proved in this chapter, with the proof drawing on ideas attributed to Afriat and Bresis. The theorem says that a matching is stable exactly when it is efficient—its total pair value is at least that of every other bijection; equivalently, it is cyclically monotone; equivalently, there are cuts \(u_i,v_{j'}\) with \(u_i+v_{j'}\ge\theta_{ij'}\) for every pair, equality on matched pairs, and minimum total cuts among feasible cuts. The chapter uses this optimization characterization in its existence argument.

There is an important qualification: the paper gives no named complexity result, no hardness result, and no runtime analysis. Theorem 3.3 is the nearest named computational anchor, as an optimization characterization; whether it meets your anchor rule depends on whether its use in the existence proof counts as “computes with it.” Theorem 3.2 is a cited rank-optimality result for the men-proposing Gale–Shapley algorithm, not a complexity claim. So this is a qualified lead, not a claim that the chapter proves a problem is in P.

The continuous problem I would put forward is FT Stable Mass Matching. Its instance has finite sets of men’s and women’s types \(A\) and \(B\), rational mass distributions \(\mu\) and \(\nu\), each summing to one, and a rational total surplus \(\theta_{ab}\) for each type pair. A type fully specifies the agent’s relevant utility profile; agents within a type are indistinguishable for this problem. Find a mass assignment \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) with row sums \(\sum_b x_{ab}=\mu_a\) and column sums \(\sum_a x_{ab}=\nu_b\), maximizing \(\sum_{a,b}\theta_{ab}x_{ab}\). A solution also supplies per-capita cuts \(u_a,v_b\) such that \(u_a+v_b\ge\theta_{ab}\) for all pairs and equality whenever \(x_{ab}>0\). Those cuts certify stability; the mass assignment says how much of each type is matched to each other type.

A plausible regime is a large, recurring two-sided market—say, cohorts of workers and employers with transferable compensation—where many participants share the same relevant utility profiles. Headcount \(N\) can greatly exceed the number of supported types \(\tau_A+\tau_B\). Each infinitesimal unit of mass still receives one partner; \(x_{ab}\) records the aggregate mass of such matches. This is a direct population continuization of the chapter’s fully transferable matching problem, and the authors would likely recognize it: Theorem 3.3 explicitly connects that problem to optimal transport.

I expect this mirror to be tractable: it is a transportation linear program with \(\tau_A\tau_B\) variables, so standard exact LP methods give a polynomial-time algorithm in the support sizes and input bit length. There is also a clean high-multiplicity dictionary: for rational masses, scale by a common denominator to obtain integer type counts; conversely, a discrete matching induces a mass assignment. The transportation polytope’s integrality gives the same optimum after scaling. This is not a case where discrete hardness dissolves—the chapter supplies no hardness anchor to begin with.

The soft spot is the anchor itself: Theorem 3.3 is a structural optimization theorem, not a computational result, and the resulting mass problem is the familiar transportation formulation. If the anchor rule requires an explicit complexity theorem or computational use of an encoding, this paper has no qualifying anchor. If Theorem 3.3 is admitted as the optimization-formulation exception, the mirror is precise and faithful, but its scope is limited to the fully transferable result; it does not mirror the chapter’s partial-transfer stability questions or make a broader complexity claim.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this chapter has no named computational result to continuize. Theorem 3.3 characterizes fully transferable stability as maximum weight matching, cyclic monotonicity, and an optimality condition for cuts. It gives no complexity claim, input encoding, or runtime. Theorem 3.1 is an existence result, Theorem 3.2 is a rank-optimality result, and the remaining named results concern stability and existence. The proponent correctly identifies Theorem 3.3 as the nearest anchor, but its use in an existence argument does not turn it into a computational theorem.

The proposed FT Stable Mass Matching is the strongest mirror. For finite supports \(A\) and \(B\), it is the finite-support Kantorovich transportation problem: choose \(x_{ab}\ge 0\) with the given marginals to maximize \(\sum_{a,b}\theta_{ab}x_{ab}\). With the payoff table explicit, this is a transportation LP with \(|A||B|\) variables, solvable by standard exact algorithms. The chapter itself connects its FT characterization to Monge–Kantorovich theory. A continuous-population formulation here would restate an established optimization problem, rather than open a complexity question the chapter left unresolved.

The high-multiplicity dictionary reinforces that concern. For rational masses, scaling by a common denominator gives integer supplies and demands; the transportation polytope is integral, so an optimal mass plan lifts to a discrete matching at that scale. This is a useful correspondence, yet it leaves no new algorithmic phenomenon to investigate in this model. The case against the mirror does not rest on its P answer being dull. It rests on the finite-support mass problem already having the standard transportation formulation and algorithm.

A more ambitious mirror could ask about mass stability under the chapter’s partial-transfer or fake-promise definitions. That would be a new computational question, but the chapter proves no computational result for those definitions to anchor it, and changing to aggregate types would require choosing and defending a new stability notion. I cannot establish that no useful version could ever be formulated. The negative case is strongest for the actual proposed FT anchor: it is outside the programme’s computational-result gate, and its most faithful mass formulation is already standard optimal transport.
