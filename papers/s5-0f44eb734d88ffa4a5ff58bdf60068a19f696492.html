What the mirror covers

The calculation applies the paper’s subscriber-uniform indicator to viewer types; it does not mirror the axiomatic characterizations or establish continuous versions of them.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest positive case is a Twitch like audience with millions of viewers and a fixed catalogue of streamers. For the subscriber uniform indicator, a viewer’s complete type is their subscription price and the subset of streamers they watched. In the paper’s case study, prices are normalized to \(1\); with \(19\) streamers, there are at most \(2^{19}-1\) nonempty viewing patterns. The roughly five million unique viewers can therefore be represented by a distribution \(\mu\) over a bounded set of types. Here \(\mu_t\) is the fraction of viewers with pattern \(A_t\) and price \(p_t\).

A natural supplementary problem is Continuous Subscriber Uniform Relevance Computation: given \(m\ge3\), a finite support of types \((A_t,p_t)\), and rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), compute the per capita relevance of every service,
\[ r_i=\sum_{t:\,i\in A_t}\mu_t\,\frac{p_t}{|A_t|}. \]
The output is the exact relevance vector; multiplying by population size gives total attributed revenue. This is plainly the same subscriber uniform relevance calculation over a population distribution, and it takes \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations for support size \(\tau\). With fixed \(m=19\), the number of possible types stays bounded as the audience grows.

The closest named result is Theorem 4, proved here: an indicator satisfies composition, nullity, and evenness if and only if it is the subscriber uniform indicator. Those properties have a natural population interpretation: combine viewer groups by adding their contributions; assign zero relevance to unwatched services; and treat services equally when the same viewers consume them. The continuous formulation is a plausible high multiplicity rendering of that relevance problem.

But Theorem 4 is not an eligible anchor under the requested rule. It characterizes an indicator by axioms; it does not state that a computational problem is in P, hard, or belongs to another complexity class. The same is true of Theorems 1–3 and Proposition 1: they give axiomatic characterizations or property checks, not computational results. So, strictly, this paper supplies no eligible anchors, and the supplementary computation above cannot be presented as an anchor backed by a named complexity result.

That is also the weakest point in the positive case. The authors would likely recognize the mass model as their relevance calculation for a large audience, but with rational masses it can be expanded into a finite audience with repeated types, and the formula is already direct. It offers a credible population mirror, yet it reveals little about the computational complexity landscape the programme is meant to chart. The paper’s continuous consumption values and revenue allocations do not change that: its subscribers remain a finite set.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The proponent has found a sensible high multiplicity regime: with 19 streamers and fixed prices, viewers can be grouped by the subset they watch. That makes a continuous society a natural description of the audience. It does not, however, give this paper a computational result to mirror. Theorem 4—and Theorems 1–3—characterize indicators by axioms. None states a complexity result or presents an algorithmic problem. Extending such a characterization to distributions would be an axiomatic extension, outside ChoCo’s scope.

The best computational candidate is the proposed relevance calculation. For subscriber-uniform relevance, its per capita value is
\[ r_i=\sum_{t:\,i\in A_t}\mu_t\frac{p_t}{|A_t|}. \]
Given the listed support, this is a direct sum, computable for all services in \(O(m\tau)\) arithmetic operations. The other indicators likewise reduce to sums of type-level contributions or aggregate totals. The continuum gives these formulas a population interpretation, but adds no optimization, separation problem, or meaningful complexity question. A more elaborate algorithmic task—say, choosing an indicator or optimizing a revenue policy—would introduce a problem the paper does not study.

So the objection is not that viewers cannot be grouped into types, nor that the resulting question is already “done” because a high multiplicity version is possible. The issue is that the strongest mirror is only formula evaluation, while the paper’s actual results are normative characterizations. That is a strong reason to pass on this paper as a ChoCo complexity case. The universal claim that no worthwhile computational question could ever be built around streaming revenue allocation would be harder to defend; it would be a new research direction, not a mirror of these results.
