The case FOR (proponent)

There is no qualifying named computational anchor in this paper under your rule. Theorem 1 is a lattice property of market-clearing prices; Lemma 2 identifies buyer-optimal prices with VCG prices; and Theorem 2 proves incentive compatibility. None asserts that a computational problem is in P, hard, or has another complexity classification. Section 2 describes the Hungarian method and says prices are easy to compute, but that algorithmic claim is not a numbered result.

The strongest nearby mirror is a typed, high-multiplicity assignment market. Let buyers have finitely many valuation types \(T\), with rational masses \(\mu_t\) summing to \(1\), and let item classes \(K\) have rational per-capita supplies \(b_k\) summing to \(1\). Each type \(t\) has values \(v_{tk}\) for the item classes. Ask for a mass assignment \(x_{tk}\) and prices \(p_k\) such that
\[ \sum_k x_{tk}=\mu_t,\qquad \sum_t x_{tk}=b_k, \]
and positive assignment to \(k\) means \(k\) maximizes \(v_{tk}-p_k\) for type \(t\). Among market-clearing prices, choose a buyer-optimal one minimizing \(\sum_k b_kp_k\). A solution is the prices and a compatible mass assignment.

The natural regime is a large market with many buyers but relatively few repeated valuation vectors—for example, buyers choosing among standardized item classes. Type-level assignments may be fractional even though each buyer receives one item; the per-capita supply is the corresponding co-modification of the model. This problem looks tractable: compute a welfare-maximizing transportation allocation, then select buyer-optimal prices among optimal dual solutions. That gives a plausible Class A mirror of the computation underlying the paper’s mechanism, not a new complexity result.

The nearest named result is Theorem 2 (proved here): assigning items according to a maximum-value matching and charging buyer-optimal market-clearing prices is incentive compatible. That is the paper’s substantive claim, but its continuum version has a real weakness: a single agent of zero mass cannot change the aggregate prices. Any incentive-compatibility claim for such an agent therefore needs a specified individual-level allocation rule, and may reduce to the simpler fact that truthful buyers receive a utility-maximizing item at fixed prices. So the assignment-and-pricing model is a credible positive mirror, but it does not furnish the required named computational anchor or preserve the theorem’s strategic content on the same terms.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest objection is that this paper offers no named computational result for ChoCo to mirror. Theorem 1 is a lattice property, Lemma 2 identifies two price definitions, and Theorem 2 proves incentive compatibility. Section 2 describes an efficient Hungarian-style algorithm, but does not establish a complexity classification. That leaves no result here about hardness, tractability, or the complexity of a pricing problem.

The best continuous model is coherent: buyers have finitely many valuation types with masses, and item classes have per-capita supplies. A mass assignment is then a capacitated assignment problem. But that models the computation of a market-clearing allocation and prices, not the paper’s main claim about truthful reporting. The paper already gives an LP and a standard algorithm for the finite assignment problem; the continuous model supplies new population and supply assumptions, rather than a computational problem this paper studies.

Theorem 2’s strategic content is particularly hard to preserve. Its VCG price compares the welfare of other buyers with and without one named buyer and one assigned item. In an atomless population, one buyer’s report does not change the distribution, so that leave-one-out externality disappears. A price-taking version can still be incentive compatible: at fixed prices, a truthful buyer chooses an item maximizing their true utility. But that is a different and much simpler claim, not the continuum counterpart of the paper’s VCG equivalence. Treating a positive-mass type as a strategic player preserves price impact only by changing the question to coalition or bloc deviations.

So the negative case is strong against a faithful continuous mirror of the paper’s main theorem, and against presenting this paper as a computational-complexity contribution. It is weaker against the standalone typed assignment model: that is a sensible continuous problem, and this paper’s matching framework could motivate it. I cannot honestly rule out its being worthwhile as a separate ChoCo example; the case against rests on the gap between that new problem and the result this paper actually proves.
