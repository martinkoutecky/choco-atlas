Why no mirror

The paper has a direct and meaningful continuous-population mirror: ranking types carry mass \(\mu\), and stable-lottery optimization remains well-defined. However, every proposed anchor is explicitly a distortion or tightness theorem, while the paper gives no named algorithmic, hardness, approximation-complexity, or parameterized result. The mirror is therefore natural but fails the required computational-result gate.

What the mirror covers

The proposed mirror covers computation of \(g\)-stable and stable-\(k\) lotteries over a ranking distribution, together with their metric-distortion guarantees and the continuous analogue of the matching lower-bound construction; it does not turn the paper's analytic theorems into computational results.

The case FOR (proponent)

The strongest honest verdict is a qualified yes: the paper has a very natural continuous-population mirror, and its stable-\(k\) special case gives a clean tractable continuous problem. However, the paper contains no named NP-hardness, membership-in-\(\mathrm P\), FPT, or similar computational-complexity result. Thus it does not satisfy the programme’s strict anchor requirement. Its named results are distortion theorems.

The supplied transcription suppresses theorem numerals, so I refer to the paper’s labels rather than inventing numbers.

The lead mirror is Continuous \(g\)-Stable Metric-Distortion. Let \(C\) be the candidate set, \(T\) the set of complete rankings of \(C\), and let \(\mu\in\Delta(T)\) be the fraction of the electorate of each ranking type. A type is a complete reported preference ranking; the unknown metric is an adversarial latent state, exactly as in the paper’s ordinal-information model.

For \(D\in\Delta(C)\), define

\[ \operatorname{rank}_t(a;D) = \sum_{b:a\succ_t b}D(b) \]

and

\[ H_{\mu,g}(D) = \max_{a\in C} \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t\, g\!\left(\operatorname{rank}_t(a;D)\right). \]

The continuous problem is:

Given \(C\), an explicit rational distribution \(\mu\), and \(g\), output a lottery
\[ > D^g_\mu\in\arg\min_{D\in\Delta(C)}H_{\mu,g}(D), > \]
together with an exact or \(\varepsilon\)-optimality certificate. The quality of the output is its worst-case metric distortion over all metric scenarios consistent with the ranking distribution \(\mu\).

This is directly the paper’s \(g\)-stable lottery problem with the finite average over voters replaced by the population integral. The action is the lottery \(D\); the objective is the minimax activation score; the population mass is \(\mu_t\).

The main anchor is Theorem \(\ref{thm:main}\), proved in this paper. Its full content is that there exists a continuous nondecreasing function \(g:[0,1]\to[0,1]\), with \(g(0)=0\) and \(g(1)=1\), such that every \(g\)-stable lottery has metric distortion at most \(2.13713\) in every election. The paper obtains this from a feasible pair \((g,W)\), verified by exact rational computation after heuristic search.

The theorem transfers cleanly to the continuous problem: every expectation over voters becomes \(\sum_t\mu_t\), and the stationarity and biased-metric arguments remain valid. Thus the continuous rule should also satisfy

\[ \sup_{\mathcal M} \frac{\operatorname{SC}_{\mathcal M}(D^g_\mu)} {\min_{c\in C}\operatorname{SC}_{\mathcal M}(c)} \le 2.13713. \]

I would expect the computation of the paper’s particular \(g\)-stable lottery to sit on a possible Class-A/Class-C boundary. For convex choices of \(g\), it is convex optimization. For the paper’s specially optimized piecewise-linear \(g\), the paper gives no algorithm or convexity guarantee, so exact global computation could be continuum-specific hard. There is no discrete hardness result here from which hardness could transfer.

The cleanest algorithmic anchor is Theorem \(\ref{thm:dist-sl}\), proved here. It states that every stable \(k\)-lottery has distortion at most

\[ 1+2\lambda_k, \qquad \lambda_k= \frac{1+(k-1)(k+1)^{-1/(k-1)}}{k}, \]

with \(\lambda_1=1\); in particular, stable \(2\)-lotteries and stable \(3\)-lotteries have distortion \(7/3\).

Its continuous mirror is Continuous Stable-\(k\) Lottery Computation:

Given \(\mu\) and an integer \(k\ge 1\), output
\[ > D_{\mu,k}\in > \arg\min_{D\in\Delta(C)} > \max_{a\in C} > \sum_{t\in T}\mu_t > \left( > \sum_{b:a\succ_t b}D(b) > \right)^k. > \]
Return \(D_{\mu,k}\) and its optimal objective value. The resulting lottery is evaluated by worst-case metric distortion over all metrics consistent with \(\mu\).

For fixed \(k\), this is a convex optimization problem: each rank is linear in \(D\), \(x\mapsto x^k\) is convex, and the maximum can be handled with an epigraph variable. With the type support explicitly listed, an \(\varepsilon\)-optimal solution should be computable in time polynomial in \(m\), \(\tau\), the input bit length, and \(\log(1/\varepsilon)\). This is the strongest genuine Class-A mirror available in the paper.

The appendix theorem \(\ref{thm:lower-bound-stable-k}\), also proved here, strengthens the case semantically: for every integer \(k>1\), the supremum of metric distortion over all elections and stable \(k\)-lotteries is exactly \(1+2\lambda_k\). Its construction already speaks in population fractions \(1-r\) and \(r\), with uniformly distributed permutation types. Those fractions can be taken as \(\mu\) directly, and the limiting construction gives the same bound in the continuous model. So continuization does not trivialize the problem or artificially improve its guarantee.

The regime is plausible: a large election with a fixed slate of candidates and many recurring preference templates—party-line voters, repeated constituencies, demographic preference classes, or large-scale preference-elicitation data—has \(n\gg\tau\). The paper itself is especially compatible with this regime because voter identities never matter: every result depends only on averages of functions of rankings. The finite profile already maps to \(\mu_t=n_t/n\), and the continuous version removes irrelevant replication.

The weakest point is also clear. Since the paper’s rule and analysis depend only on the empirical distribution of rankings, a referee may regard the mirror as a high-multiplicity reformulation rather than a new computational problem. Moreover, the paper does not prove a runtime bound for computing its optimized \(g\)-stable lottery. Therefore I would not claim that this paper supplies a full ChoCo-style complexity result. I would claim instead that it supplies an unusually convincing model mirror, with a provably tractable stable-\(k\) subfamily and a well-defined open computation problem for the \(2.13713\) rule.

The case AGAINST (opponent, writing after the proponent)

The strongest case against is a strict one: this paper supplies no computational result to mirror. Its named contributions—Theorem \(\ref{thm:main}\), Theorem \(\ref{thm:dist-sl}\), and Theorem \(\ref{thm:lower-bound-stable-k}\)—are distortion guarantees and tightness results, not complexity, algorithmic, approximation, or parameterized-complexity theorems. The proposed computational questions are new problems suggested by the definitions, not mirrors of results proved in the paper.

For Theorem \(\ref{thm:main}\), the proposed problem

\[ \min_{D\in\Delta(C)} \max_{a\in C}\sum_t\mu_t\,g(\operatorname{rank}_t(a;D)) \]

is perfectly well-defined. But the distortion theorem itself transfers immediately: every finite voter average in the proof can be replaced by \(\sum_t\mu_t\). Asking for an exact or approximate algorithm for the paper’s particular piecewise-linear \(g\) would be a worthwhile new computational project, but it is not a computational consequence of the theorem. The paper neither gives such an algorithm nor establishes its complexity.

The stable-\(k\) theorem is no stronger as an anchor. Its continuous formulation can indeed be written as

\[ \min_{D,z}\ z \]

subject to

\[ \sum_t\mu_t \left(\sum_{b:a\succ_t b}D(b)\right)^k\le z \quad\text{for every }a\in C, \]

together with \(D\in\Delta(C)\). For fixed \(k\), this is a routine convex program. That makes it a legitimate Class-A baseline, but the convex algorithm is supplied by standard optimization rather than by a computational result in the paper. The theorem being mirrored is still only the \(1+2\lambda_k\) distortion bound.

The appendix lower-bound theorem is even less suitable as a computational anchor. Its construction already uses population fractions and permutation types, so it strongly supports the semantic plausibility of a continuous society. But it proves only that the distortion guarantee is tight. It gives no computational task beyond evaluating a supplied lottery on a supplied distribution.

The usual substantive objections also fail. Voters are not individuated by identity, location, or arrival time; all relevant quantities depend only on the empirical distribution of complete rankings. Mass remains meaningful, the objective does not track who receives anything, and the appendix already exhibits natural high-multiplicity regimes. Nor would it be legitimate to object merely that the continuous answer is unchanged or that high-multiplicity voting is already known.

Thus the negative case establishes only that this is a poor paper for the programme’s strict “named computational result” gate. It does not honestly establish that no worthwhile continuous mirror exists. In fact, the stable-\(k\) formulation is a clean and natural continuous-population optimization problem. The universal claim therefore fails; the strongest defensible verdict is “semantic mirror yes, computational anchor no.”
